Tangent equations and adjoint outputs

Compute how an observed final state changes when an initial state or a model parameter is perturbed.

Differentiate the evolution

Let \(y'=f(t,y,\alpha)\) with smooth \(f\) and \(y(0)=y_0(\alpha)\). Assume that the solution exists and depends differentiably on \(\alpha\) throughout the observation window. Differentiating the equation along the solution gives \(S'=f_y S+f_\alpha\) and \(S(0)=y_0'(\alpha)\), where \(S=\partial y/\partial\alpha\). The first term propagates state changes through the dynamics and the second injects the direct parameter effect. Losing the initial derivative gives the wrong sensitivity whenever a parameter also changes the initial preparation.

For an output \(Q=\phi(y(T),\alpha)\), the chain rule gives \(Q_\alpha=\phi_y S(T)+\phi_\alpha\). Each parameter requires its own tangent column. Many observations can then reuse the propagated columns. Scaling matters because a derivative with respect to milliseconds is numerically different from the derivative with respect to seconds. Multiplying by a stated parameter perturbation converts the derivative back into a predicted observation change that can be checked directly.

A rate parameter and a final measurement

Take \(y'=-\alpha y,\ y(0)=2\). Then \(S'=-\alpha S-y,\ S(0)=0\). Multiplying by \(e^{\alpha t}\) leaves \((e^{\alpha t}S)'=-2\), hence \(S=-2te^{-\alpha t}\). At \(\alpha=0.5\) per second and \(T=2\) s the sensitivity of final amplitude is \(-4/e\approx-1.47152\) amplitude units per inverse second. Raising the decay rate by 0.01 per second predicts a decrease of 0.014715. The exact change is \(2e^{-1}(e^{-0.02}-1)\approx-0.01457\); the discrepancy is quadratic in the parameter perturbation.

Testing a tangent code by differences requires controlling both the ODE integration error and subtraction error. Use several perturbation sizes. With a forward difference the truncation error normally decreases linearly at first, until numerical noise in subtracting two nearly equal outputs dominates.

Run one dual equation for a scalar output

Define a column vector \(z\) backward in time by \(-z'=f_y^Tz,\ z(T)=\nabla_y\phi\). Differentiating \(z^TS\) cancels the two terms containing \(f_y\). Integrating what remains gives

\[Q_\alpha=\phi_\alpha+z(0)^T y_0'(\alpha)+\int_0^T z^T f_\alpha\,dt.\]

For the preceding final-amplitude observation \(z=e^{-\alpha(T-t)}\). Its product with \(f_\alpha=-y\) is the constant \(-2e^{-\alpha T}\), whose integral reproduces the tangent result. One adjoint trajectory can provide gradients with respect to many parameters for this single output. Its coefficients require the forward trajectory at earlier times, so storing or reconstructing that trajectory is part of the numerical cost. If the desired derivative is of the discrete numerical update itself, differentiating its specific steps and interpolation choices can matter at finite time resolution.

A cancellation at a particular observation time

Compare observing the final amplitude with observing the lost amplitude \(Q=y(0)-y(T)\). If the rate is the only parameter, changing the observation reverses the sign of the computed rate sensitivity. If instead the unknown is the common initial amplitude, the direct derivative of \(y(0)\) is one and the propagated derivative is \(e^{-\alpha T}\). The final derivative of the loss is \(1-e^{-\alpha T}\), tending to zero as the observation window shrinks. Omitting the direct term would predict a large negative initial sensitivity for a loss that is identically zero at zero time.

Differentiate the time at which a model switches

If a trajectory switches dynamics at a threshold, the event time itself depends on the parameter. Let a pre-event solution \(y^-(t,p)\) meet \(G(t,y,p)=0\) at \(\tau(p)\). Denote by \(S^-\) its parameter derivative at fixed time, evaluated on the nominal event. Differentiating the event equation gives

\[\tau_p=-\frac{G_y S^-+G_p}{G_t+G_y f^-}.\]

The denominator is the rate at which the trajectory crosses the threshold. This formula requires a transversal crossing and a locally unchanged event sequence. At a grazing contact the derivative may diverge or cease to exist, and a perturbed trajectory can encounter a different number of events.

Assume the state itself is continuous while the right-hand side changes from \(f^-\) to \(f^+\). The total parameter change of the event state is \(S^-+f^-\tau_p\). But the post-event tangent at a fixed clock time must remove the amount of post-event evolution gained or lost by shifting the start time, giving \(S^+=S^-+(f^--f^+)\tau_p\). This jump in a derivative of a continuous state is sometimes surprising. Comparing trajectories taken at the same physical clock time is different from comparing their states at their respective event times.

Estimate the final position after a velocity switch

Let a cart start at zero and travel at constant positive speed \(p\) until it reaches position one. It then travels at fixed speed 0.5. Observe its position at \(T=2\), and examine \(p=1\), so the event occurs at \(\tau=1\). The pre-event position is \(pt\) and its tangent at fixed time is \(S^-=t=1\). Using \(G=y-1\) gives \(\tau_p=-1/p^2=-1\). Since \(f^-=1\) and \(f^+=0.5\), the tangent immediately after the switch becomes \(1+(1-0.5)(-1)=0.5\). Subsequent evolution at the fixed speed adds no further parameter source.

Check directly: as long as the event precedes the observation, \(y(T,p)=1+0.5(2-1/p)\). Its derivative at one is indeed 0.5. At \(p=1.01\) the exact final position is approximately 1.5049505 instead of 1.5. The first-order prediction is 1.505. Carrying the pre-event tangent unchanged through the switch would predict 1.51, nearly doubling the response. Resetting it to zero because the event position is fixed would miss the change entirely.

At \(p=0.5\) the switch is exactly at the observation endpoint. Parameters below this value give \(y(T)=2p\), whereas parameters above it use the switched expression. Both one-sided derivatives happen to equal two here because the two velocities match at this specific threshold value. Their second derivatives differ. Changing the post-event speed to 0.25 makes even the first derivatives disagree at the boundary. An apparent smooth result in one carefully chosen switching case cannot justify ignoring event timing throughout a parameter study.

A finite-difference sensitivity test should consequently check which events occurred on both perturbed trajectories. Agreement in total simulated duration and integrator tolerance is insufficient if the jump sequence changed. Recording event times together with the reported output makes it possible to distinguish an error in the reset law from a derivative that has ceased to exist across the selected parameter interval.

Propagate a reset depending on the event location

If a switching event resets the state by \(y^+=R(t,y^-,p)\), differentiate the reset along the moving event before returning to a fixed clock time. The result is \(S^+=R_yS^-+R_p+(R_t+R_y f^--f^+)\tau_p\). This reduces to the continuous-state formula when \(R\) is the identity. The occurrence time must still come from the pre-event trajectory, since the post-event state generally does not satisfy the original trigger condition.

Modify the cart example so the trigger position is \(H(p)=1+0.2(p-1)\), and the indicated position is reset to \(R=0.3y^-+0.2\) before it increases at speed 0.5. At \(p=1\), \(\tau=H/p=1\) and \(\tau_p=0.2-1=-0.8\). The pre-event fixed-time tangent is one. The reset gives \(S^+=0.3+(0.3-0.5)(-0.8)=0.46\). The indicated final position at time two is \(0.3H+0.2+0.5(2-H/p)\), exactly one at the nominal parameter. Differentiating this expression gives \(0.3(0.2)-0.5(0.2-1)=0.46\), independently verifying the sign and the event-time correction. Simply differentiating the reset map at the nominal crossing position would give 0.3 and lose the varying length of the post-event trajectory.
