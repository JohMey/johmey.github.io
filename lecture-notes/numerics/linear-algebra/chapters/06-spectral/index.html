Eigenvalues, modes and spectral gaps

Estimate a dominant vibration or diffusion mode, explain the role of eigenvalue separation and use a residual to check a computed eigenpair.

What an eigenproblem measures

In an undamped mechanical model \(M\ddot u+Ku=0\), insert \(u=v\cos(\omega t)\). Nonzero amplitudes require \(Kv=\omega^2Mv\). The normal frequencies are square roots of generalised eigenvalues and the mode shapes are determined only up to scale and sign. For symmetric positive definite \(M\), substituting \(w=M^{1/2}v\) gives the ordinary symmetric operator \(M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}\). This step explains why mass weighting matters when comparing shapes from two different meshes.

For an ordinary symmetric matrix \(A\) with orthonormal eigenvectors, a normalised trial vector \(v=\sum c_i u_i\) has Rayleigh quotient \(\rho=v^TAv=\sum_i\lambda_i c_i^2\). Hence it lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues. Near an isolated mode its value can be much more accurate than the vector direction: shifting a small amount of amplitude into another orthogonal mode changes the quotient by a term proportional to the square of that amplitude.

Select a mode by repeated application

The power iteration repeatedly forms \(Av_k\) and normalises it. Before normalisation, \(A^kv_0=\sum c_i\lambda_i^k u_i\). If one eigenvalue has strictly largest magnitude and the initial vector overlaps its eigenspace, the ratio of any competing component to the leading one gains a factor \((\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k\). Normalisation avoids overflow while preserving those ratios. A negative leading eigenvalue can cause sign changes between successive normalised vectors. Comparing their raw difference then misrepresents convergence.

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(4,2)\) and a starting vector proportional to \((1,1)^T\), the ratio of the second component to the first is \(1/2\) after one multiplication and \(1/4\) after two. A vector proportional to \((4,1)^T\) has Rayleigh quotient \((4\cdot16+2)/17=66/17=3.88235\). After four steps the ratio is \(1/16\), and the quotient is \(1026/257=3.99222\). Check that the quotient error decreases roughly fourfold per additional step, although the directional error decreases only twofold.

Use shifts to find another part of the spectrum

Power iteration selects magnitude. To find a mode near a target \(\sigma\), apply the inverse of \(A-\sigma I\) implicitly by solving a linear system each iteration. The eigenvectors are the same and the eigenvalues are \(1/(\lambda_i-\sigma)\). For eigenvalues 2 and 4 with \(\sigma=2.1\), the transformed magnitudes are 10 and about 0.526. The eigenvalue near 2 dominates for a suitable starter. A fixed shift permits factor reuse, but choosing it exactly equal to an eigenvalue makes the shifted solve singular.

Test a candidate pair by forming \(r=Av-\rho v\) with \(\|v\|_2=1\). In the orthonormal basis, \(\|r\|^2=\sum_i(\lambda_i-\rho)^2 c_i^2\). At least one eigenvalue must lie within \(\|r\|\) of \(\rho\). This is a numerical certificate for a symmetric problem. To certify a particular eigenvector, the gap to neighbouring eigenvalues also matters. At a repeated eigenvalue every normalised linear combination of its modes is valid. Comparing subspaces or projecting one span onto the other is then more meaningful than matching individual component signs.

Which boundary modes are physical?

An elastic assembly suspended in free space admits rigid translations, and in three dimensions also rigid rotations when its geometry allows them. In a correctly assembled model these motions cost zero strain energy, so the stiffness matrix has a nullspace and some generalised eigenvalues are zero. Attempting unshifted inverse iteration on that stiffness matrix cannot locate a lowest positive vibration by simply inverting the operator. One can instead constrain rigid motions consistently or project into the mass-orthogonal complement of their span.

For a two-mass system with unit masses and a unit spring connecting them, \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Translation \((1,1)^T\) has zero squared frequency; opposite displacement \((1,-1)^T\) has squared frequency two. Grounding the first mass eliminates translation but changes the second mode to an oscillator with frequency one. Projection leaves the original free-system vibration at \(\sqrt2\). These are physically different comparisons; the treatment of a nullspace determines which frequency an eigenvalue calculation is actually reporting.

Convert an eigenpair residual into an angle bound

Given a real symmetric matrix \(A\), a unit-vector approximation \(v\) and a scalar \(\theta\), write its residual \(r=Av-\theta v\). Expand \(v=\sum_j c_jq_j\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis. The squared residual is \(\sum_j(\lambda_j-\theta)^2|c_j|^2\). This identity always implies that at least one eigenvalue lies within \(\|r\|\) of \(\theta\). It does not by itself show whether the reported vector represents one particular physical mode. Two almost coincident eigenvalues can produce a tiny residual for many different mixtures of their eigenvectors.

To bound leakage out of a target eigenspace, let \(\gamma\) be a lower bound on the distance from \(\theta\) to every eigenvalue outside that space. The squared residual is at least \(\gamma^2\sum_{\rm outside}|c_j|^2\). Consequently the sine of the angle between \(v\) and the target space is at most \(\|r\|/\gamma\). This result assumes a symmetric or Hermitian operator under the chosen inner product. For a mass-weighted mechanical problem, transform to mass-normal coordinates or perform the argument in the proper mass inner product. An ordinary Euclidean angle between unscaled coordinates may have little relevance to vibrational energy.

Resolve a cluster more reliably than either member

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(10,10.02,30)\), and \(v=(1,1,0)^T/\sqrt2\). Its Rayleigh quotient is 10.01. Multiplication gives \(r=(-0.01,0.01,0)^T/\sqrt2\), of length 0.01. Relative to the cluster near ten this residual is small, yet the angle from the first coordinate eigenvector is 45 degrees. Using only the other low eigenvalue as a separation gives \(\gamma=0.01\), so the bound on that individual angle has sine at most one and provides no useful orientation guarantee.

Now take the target as the entire first two-dimensional subspace. Its gap from the reported quotient to the outside eigenvalue is \(30-10.01=19.99\). The same residual implies leakage no greater than \(0.01/19.99\approx0.000500\). In fact the true leakage of this particular vector is exactly zero. If the measured eigenvalues are squared angular frequencies, the two low modes have nearly the same frequency but can mix strongly under a small asymmetric manufacturing perturbation. Their combined subspace may remain very stable and may therefore be the more sensible object to compare with measurements.

A laboratory comparison can project independent excitation patterns onto the computed cluster and compare the total energy in that cluster. Such a result is invariant under rotations of its basis, which a simple table of pairwise eigenvector differences is not. If the gap to the rest of the spectrum closes as well, the subspace estimate becomes weak. Measuring the residual alone cannot repair that missing separation information.

For example the sum of squared projections of the excitation \((1,0,0)^T\) onto the first two computed orthonormal modes is one for any orthonormal basis of the exact low cluster. Individual projections can range between zero and one as the basis rotates. A test based on cluster projection assesses the reproducible quantity without requiring a uniquely labelled first mode.
