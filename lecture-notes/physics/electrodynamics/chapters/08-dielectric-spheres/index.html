An inclusion in a uniform field

Place a homogeneous dielectric sphere of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon\) in vacuum. Distant electrodes produce a static uniform field \(E_0\mathbf e_z\) before insertion. Take the dielectric to be linear, isotropic and free of mobile charge. Bound charge can develop at its surface even though the object is electrically neutral. The monopole measurement from the previous lecture consequently misses the material response. The dipole moment provides a measurable alternative.

In each constant-permittivity region Gauss’s law for \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) give Laplace’s equation. The background potential is \(-E_0r\cos\theta\), an angular pattern of degree one. A spherical interface does not couple different angular degrees. The allowed radial functions for this degree are \(r\) and \(r^{-2}\). Finiteness at the centre prohibits a singular interior term. Matching the imposed field far away fixes the growing exterior term.

\[V_{\rm in}=-Ar\cos\theta,\qquad V_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2.\]

Solve at the surface

Continuity of voltage gives \(A=E_0-B/a^3\). It also guarantees continuity of tangential field, as a tangential displacement compares points along the same surface potential. Normal displacement is continuous in the absence of free surface charge. Differentiating radially changes the sign and supplies a factor of two for the decaying term, so \(\epsilon A=\epsilon_0(E_0+2B/a^3)\). Elimination gives

\[A=\frac{3\epsilon_0}{\epsilon+2\epsilon_0}E_0,\qquad B=a^3\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_0}{\epsilon+2\epsilon_0}E_0.\]

The uniform internal field polarizes the sphere along the field direction. Its bound surface charge is \(\sigma_b=(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)A\cos\theta\). Opposite hemispheres cancel in total charge. Their separation gives an induced dipole \(p=4\pi\epsilon_0B\). Equivalently integrate the uniform polarization over the volume. Both constructions give the same dipole, one from exterior voltage and one from microscopic charge displacement. When \(\epsilon=\epsilon_0\) the exterior perturbation vanishes. A very large positive permittivity suppresses the internal field and approaches the conductor result.

Predict a voltage difference

Take \(a=2.00\) mm, \(\epsilon=4\epsilon_0\) and \(E_0=30.0\) kV/m. The internal field is \(15.0\) kV/m, and \(B=1.20\times10^{-4}\) V m². Multiplication by \(4\pi\epsilon_0\) gives \(p=1.34\times10^{-14}\) C m. At an axial probe 10.0 mm from the centre the sphere increases voltage relative to the uniform-field baseline by \(B/r^2=1.20\) V. The opposite axial probe sees a shift of \(-1.20\) V. A differential measurement doubles the signal and removes a common voltage offset.

For the assumed uniform applied field the exterior dipole solution is exact at every point outside the sphere. A nearby field-producing electrode can spoil that simplification. A field that varies across the particle contains higher angular orders in its local expansion and their induced responses survive at the surface. In an experiment, repeating the measurement with a smaller sphere tests the predicted \(a^3\) law while reducing sensitivity to field curvature.

The assumption of real, constant permittivity is experimentally testable. A slowly varying applied field should give a dipole that follows the field with negligible lag. If the measured signal shifts in phase while frequency is raised, the response must be described by a frequency-dependent complex polarization. A static voltage estimate alone cannot supply that extra timescale.

Build an effective permittivity from many inclusions

A single dielectric sphere in a smooth applied field develops a dipole. A dilute suspension of such spheres can therefore alter the bulk capacitance of a sample. Suppose identical spheres of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon_i\) occupy a small fraction \(f\) of a matrix with permittivity \(\epsilon_m\). The matrix is homogeneous, both materials are isotropic, and no conducting path or interfacial free charge is present. Each sphere sees nearly the matrix field when it is far from all neighbours. The single-sphere boundary calculation gives an excess dipole \(p=4\pi\epsilon_m a^3 b E_m\), with \(b=(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_m)/(\epsilon_i+2\epsilon_m)\).

The number of inclusions per unit sample volume is \(n=f/(4\pi a^3/3)\). At first order the excess polarization density is \(np=3f\epsilon_m b E_m\). The difference between \(E_m\) and the sample-average field is itself first order in \(f\), so replacing one by the other in this correction changes only higher-order terms. Comparing average displacement with \(\epsilon_{\rm eff}E\) gives

\[\epsilon_{\rm eff}=\epsilon_m[1+3fb+O(f^2)] .\]

This is a dilute effective-medium prediction. The absolute radius cancels at fixed volume fraction because each dipole scales as \(a^3\) while number density scales as \(a^{-3}\). The cancellation requires particles small compared with the scale of the imposed field. Aggregation, closely spaced pairs and a large occupied fraction can change local fields and produce a nonlinear dependence on concentration.

Measure a composite using two capacitor readings

A parallel-plate measurement cell has electrode area \(25.0\) cm² and gap 1.00 mm. Filling it with matrix alone gives \(\epsilon_m=2.00\epsilon_0\), hence \(C_m=44.3\) pF after subtraction of stray capacitance. Disperse spheres with \(\epsilon_i=8.00\epsilon_0\) at \(f=0.0200\). The contrast factor is \((8-2)/(8+4)=0.500\). Consequently \(\epsilon_{\rm eff}/\epsilon_m\simeq1+3(0.0200)(0.500)=1.030\). The expected reading is \(45.6\) pF, an increase of approximately \(1.33\) pF.

A second sample with the same volume fraction but twice the sphere radius should give the same leading result. A systematic change might indicate settling, aggregation, finite-gap effects or frequency-dependent interface polarization. In the equal-permittivity limit the contrast factor vanishes and no capacitive signal should remain. In the high-permittivity limit \(b\) approaches one, so the fractional increase saturates at approximately \(3f\) to first order. This bounded prediction gives a useful check when a simple arithmetic mixing rule appears to predict an arbitrarily large capacitance increase from an arbitrarily dilute concentration.

The hypothesis that the suspension is dilute can be tested without a microscopic field probe. Make a concentration series by adding material with known volume at fixed particle size. The predicted initial slope of relative capacitance versus volume fraction for the numerical pair is \(3b=1.50\). A linear fit restricted to the lowest concentrations estimates the contrast parameter and hence the inclusion permittivity if the matrix is known. Strong curvature at higher concentration then measures the breakdown of the independent-inclusion approximation. Extrapolating that curvature back as a new single-particle dielectric constant would blend collective and material effects.
