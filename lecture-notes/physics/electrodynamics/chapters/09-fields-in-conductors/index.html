Charge relaxation and magnetic diffusion

What relaxes when a field is applied?

A metal plate inserted in a static electric field rapidly rearranges its free surface charge. A metal plate subjected to a changing magnetic field can carry eddy currents for an appreciable time. The two observations concern different field equations. Model a uniform material using \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\), treating the coefficients as constants over the frequencies used.

Free charge conservation gives \(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\). Within the uniform bulk, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon\) gives \(\partial_t\rho=-(\sigma/\epsilon)\rho\). A prepared bulk charge perturbation therefore decays exponentially with characteristic time \(\tau_q=\epsilon/\sigma\). The derivation says nothing about the disappearance of total charge from an isolated body. Charge can move to its boundary, where the bulk equation with constant coefficients across the interface no longer applies. Extremely short formal times in good metals also probe frequencies beyond the constant-conductivity model.

Curl fields obey a different evolution

Use Ampère’s law without displacement current when \(\omega\epsilon\ll\sigma\) and the frequency lies within the material model. Combining \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\sigma\mathbf E\) with Faraday’s law gives \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=-\mu\sigma\partial_t\mathbf B\). Because magnetic flux has zero divergence, the left-hand side is \(-\nabla^2\mathbf B\). Thus

\[\partial_t\mathbf B=D_m\nabla^2\mathbf B,\qquad D_m=\frac1{\mu\sigma}.\]

A spatial Fourier component with wavenumber \(k\) decays as \(\exp(-D_mk^2t)\). Long spatial scales persist longer because the electric field required to change a large magnetic flux drives stronger circulating currents. Dimensional analysis gives a diffusion time of order \(\mu\sigma L^2\), with a geometric factor determined by the actual boundaries.

An alternating field outside a copper block

Take a tangential field at the plane \(x=0\) bounding a conductor occupying \(x>0\). Substitute \(B(x,t)=\mathrm{Re}\{B_0e^{i\omega t-\kappa x}\}\) into diffusion. The admissible square root of \(\kappa^2=i\omega\mu\sigma\) has positive real part,

\[\kappa=(1+i)/\delta,\qquad \delta=\sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)} .\]

At depth \(\delta\) the amplitude has dropped to \(1/e\) and the phase lags by one radian. These are independent observables for testing the semi-infinite, constant-property solution. The surface excitation sets the boundary amplitude. It need not equal the field that an external coil would generate with the metal absent.

For copper take \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\) S/m, \(\mu=\mu_0\) and \(f=1.00\) kHz. Then \(\delta=2.09\) mm. At \(x=4.00\) mm the field amplitude is \(0.148\) of its surface value and the phase lag is \(1.91\) rad. Raising frequency by a factor of four halves the penetration depth. If a plate thickness is comparable to \(\delta\), fields from the opposite face also affect the solution and a second boundary condition must replace the one-sided decay condition. This geometric check matters as much as the conductivity value when using an amplitude ratio to infer a material property.

The oscillating solution also predicts current concentrated near the surface, since Ampère’s law makes the current density proportional to the local spatial derivative of magnetic field. As frequency rises, less of the metal cross-section transports the alternating current at a given field amplitude. This mechanism helps explain why a conductor’s measured AC resistance can exceed its DC value even when the local conductivity is unchanged.

Relate surface fields to the power dissipated in a metal

The skin depth gives the distance over which current flows, yet a circuit measurement usually returns an impedance or a heating rate. Use the conducting half-space \(x>0\) and time convention \(e^{i\omega t}\). Choose magnetic field along \(z\), with phasor \(H_z=H_0e^{-(1+i)x/\delta}\). From Ampère's law without displacement current, the only current component is \(J_y=-\partial_xH_z=(1+i)H_z/\delta\). Ohm’s law therefore fixes the tangential electric field immediately inside the conductor.

\[Z_s=\frac{E_y(0)}{H_z(0)}=\frac{1+i}{\sigma\delta},\qquad R_s=\mathrm{Re}\,Z_s=\sqrt{\frac{\pi f\mu}{\sigma}} .\]

The surface resistance \(R_s\) is expressed in ohms even though neither field in this ratio is a terminal voltage or current. Current integrated over depth is a sheet current with units A/m equal to the surface magnetic amplitude. The real part of the surface impedance relates mean heating per unit area to this current, while the imaginary part describes reversible magnetic energy storage near the surface.

For peak phasors, mean inward Poynting flux is \(\mathrm{Re}[E_yH_z^*]/2=R_s|H_0|^2/2\). The same result must follow from local heating. Insert \(|J_y|^2=2|H_0|^2e^{-2x/\delta}/\delta^2\) into \(\int_0^\infty |J_y|^2/(2\sigma)\,dx\). The integral supplies \(\delta/2\), reproducing the surface formula. This equality is a sensitive check on the distinction between amplitude decay over \(\delta\) and squared-amplitude decay over \(\delta/2\).

Estimate loss in a microwave wall

Take copper with constant \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\) S/m, \(\mu=\mu_0\) at \(f=10.0\) MHz. The skin depth is \(\delta=20.9\) μm and \(R_s=1/(\sigma\delta)=0.825\) mΩ. A nearly uniform tangential peak magnetic field of \(80.0\) A/m illuminates a wall patch of area \(0.0200\) m². Surface heating density is \(0.000825(80.0)^2/2=2.64\) W/m², corresponding to \(52.8\) mW for the patch. If the supplied field were an RMS amplitude the division by two would be inappropriate.

Increasing frequency fourfold at constant peak magnetic field doubles the loss and halves the skin depth. A coating 10 μm thick at the original frequency is less than one skin depth, so a calculation assuming all current decays within that coating is unreliable; substrate conductivity and the second interface matter. A very thick low-loss conductor can instead be represented by the surface condition without resolving the field throughout its interior. A numerical mode solver using that substitution should recover both the inferred loss and its square-root frequency dependence.

The complex sign in the impedance also makes a phase prediction. For the stated time convention the surface electric field leads the tangential magnetic field by 45 degrees. Squaring the electric amplitude alone and dividing by the bulk resistivity would give a volumetric rather than an areal heating rate. To obtain an areal quantity one must integrate over its penetration profile. A measured phase substantially different from 45 degrees can indicate a magnetic permeability with appreciable losses, a finite film thickness, or a displacement current no longer negligible compared with conduction.

A calibrated surface-field phase measurement tests this condition independently of absolute field amplitude.
