Radiation from a small electric dipole

Relate a time-dependent electric dipole moment to its radiation pattern, total power and the conditions under which a far-field detector measures them.

From potentials to a distant detector

An alternating current in a short conductor moves positive and negative charge relative to one another. A current probe at the source and a field probe well outside it measure different aspects of the same charge motion. Consider a localised neutral source of size \(d\), oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\) in vacuum. Assume \(d\ll\lambda=2\pi c/\omega\) so that phase changes across the source are small. The leading electric multipole is its dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho(\mathbf r',t)d^3r'\).

A signal emitted at source time \(t_r=t-r/c\) reaches an observation point a distance \(r\) away at time \(t\). Keeping the leading distant term of the retarded vector potential gives \(\mathbf A\simeq\mu_0\int\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t_r)d^3r'/(4\pi r)\). By charge continuity the current integral equals \(\dot{\mathbf p}\). One sees this by differentiating the integral defining a component of \(\mathbf p\), substituting \(-\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J\) for \(\partial_t\rho\) and integrating by parts. No surface term remains for a localised source whose current vanishes at the enclosing boundary.

Extract the transverse radiation field

Curling the potential differentiates both \(1/r\) and the retarded time. The former produces a term falling as \(1/r^2\). Differentiating \(t_r\) produces a term proportional to \(\ddot{\mathbf p}/(cr)\), which dominates sufficiently far away. The resulting magnetic field is transverse to the direction of observation. The radiative electric field can be written as the transverse vector \(\hat{\mathbf r}\times(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}(t_r))/(4\pi\epsilon_0 c^2r)\). The magnetic field is \(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf E/c\).

Take \(\mathbf p=p_0\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf z}\). The electric amplitude is proportional to \(\omega^2p_0\sin\theta/r\), where \(\theta\) is the angle to the dipole axis. Along the axis no transverse component survives. In the equatorial plane the radiation is strongest. These conclusions concern the energy-carrying far field. The electrostatic dipole field and the induction field decay with higher inverse powers of radius and may dominate a probe whose distance is small on the wavelength scale.

Integrate over a sphere

Use the plane-wave expression for the cycle-averaged Poynting flux locally in the far zone. Multiplying by \(r^2\) gives the power per unit solid angle. Integration uses \(d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\) and \(\int \sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3\). For a peak dipole moment the result is

\[\frac{d\langle P\rangle}{d\Omega}=\frac{\omega^4 p_0^2}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}\sin^2\theta,\qquad \langle P\rangle=\frac{\omega^4p_0^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.\]

The sphere has more area at larger radius while the intensity falls as \(r^{-2}\), so total power is independent of the chosen far-field radius. At fixed dipole moment, doubling frequency increases the radiated power sixteenfold. If the experiment instead fixes the amplitude of current through a short element, the resulting dipole moment falls with increasing frequency because current is the time derivative of charge. The change in what is controlled matters.

Check an angular scan

A calibrated small dipole radiates 3.0 mW in total. At \(r=10\) m in its far zone, the equatorial mean intensity is \(3\langle P\rangle/(8\pi r^2)=3.58\) µW/m². At 30° from the axis it is one quarter of that value, 0.895 µW/m². Treating the source as isotropic would conceal both the axial null and the enhanced equatorial intensity. A dipole probe receiving the field must also be correctly polarised to measure the predicted amplitude.

Move a correctly oriented equatorial probe from 10 to 20 m. Its measured electric amplitude should halve and its measured power per area should fall by four. Failure of this scaling could indicate reflections, inadequate distance from the source or a directional response in the probe. Combining this distance test with an angular scan distinguishes a radiation measurement from a mapping of the near electrostatic field.

An oscillating electric dipole with fixed orientation emits linearly polarized radiation at a generic far-field point. The electric field points along the projection of the dipole axis onto the plane transverse to the line of sight. Turning a linear receiving antenna through a right angle can therefore remove its signal even at a location of maximum emitted intensity. To distinguish antenna misalignment from a source-pattern null, repeat the scan with two orthogonal receiving orientations and add the received powers.

Radiation from a driven bound charge

A dipole need not be fed by a wire. An incident optical field can drive a bound charge, which reradiates part of the incident power. Model a charge of magnitude \(q\) and effective mass \(m\) attached to a harmonic restoring force \(-m\omega_0^2x\). Expose it to a spatially uniform sinusoidal electric field of peak amplitude \(E_0\) at angular frequency \(\omega\). Uniformity here requires the oscillator size to be small compared with the wavelength. Include linear damping rate \(\gamma\) through the term \(m\gamma\dot x\). Steady harmonic response gives a dipole amplitude

\[|p|=\frac{q^2E_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}} .\]

The radiated power of a sinusoidal electric dipole with peak moment \(p\) is \(\omega^4|p|^2/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)\). Divide by the incident plane-wave intensity \(\epsilon_0c E_0^2/2\) to obtain a scattering cross section. Well below resonance, where the restoring term dominates, define the static polarizability \(\alpha_0=q^2/(m\omega_0^2)\) in SI units. Then

\[\sigma_{\rm sc}\simeq \frac{k^4}{6\pi}\left|\frac{\alpha_0}{\epsilon_0}\right|^2,\qquad k=\omega/c.\]

The strong fourth-power dependence originates in the dipole's radiating acceleration. The amplitude of the induced moment varies little in the low-frequency regime, but twice differentiating it in time produces a field amplitude proportional to \(\omega^2\). Squaring that field gives the fourth power. Near an internal resonance the polarizability itself varies rapidly and this simple colour dependence no longer applies.

Predict the ratio and magnitude of scattered light

Suppose a small isotropic object has \(\alpha_0/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=1.00\times10^{-30}\) m³, and both 450 nm and 650 nm light are far from its absorption bands. At 450 nm, \(k=1.40\times10^7\) m⁻¹. Substitution gives \(\sigma_{\rm sc}= (8\pi/3)k^4(10^{-30}\,{\rm m^3})^2\simeq3.18\times10^{-31}\) m². At 650 nm the cross section is smaller by \((650/450)^4=4.35\), about \(7.32\times10^{-32}\) m². Equal incident irradiances therefore give that same ratio of total scattered powers.

The angular pattern remains dipolar, so a small detector also needs a known collection angle and polarization geometry. At a direction parallel to the incident electric polarization the ideal driven dipole has a null, irrespective of wavelength. A distribution of particle sizes with some dimensions comparable to wavelength can fill such a null and alter the colour ratio. Comparing angular scans and wavelength scans separates these two experimental predictions rather than attributing every departure to a changed number of scatterers.

An ideal absorption-free dielectric particle still removes some power from the original forward beam by redirecting radiation. For a dilute ensemble one might infer scattering cross section from the weak reduction in collimated transmitted power, provided light scattered into the detector aperture is excluded. If the material also absorbs, the reduction measures extinction and is larger than scattering alone. Measuring total scattered power and forward-beam attenuation separately then distinguishes reradiated energy from internal dissipation. Comparing measurements at several concentrations checks whether neighbouring objects remain independent scatterers or instead interfere appreciably across the ensemble.

The polarizability here is in SI units. Tabulated polarizability volumes must first be multiplied by \(4\pi\epsilon_0\) before substitution in the SI cross-section expression.
