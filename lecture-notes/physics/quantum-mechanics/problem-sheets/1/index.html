Give two packets the same density

Two packets have wavefunctions \(f(x)e^{ikx}\) and \(f(x)e^{-ikx}\), where \(f\) is real and normalised. Compare their densities and currents. For electrons with \(k=2.0\times10^8\) m⁻¹ and local density \(4.0\times10^5\) m⁻¹, find both signed currents. Does either current by itself give the chance of finding a particle inside a chosen interval?

Differentiate the phase of each packet

Both densities are \(f^2\), while their phase gradients are \(+k\) and \(-k\). The speeds entering the flux are \(\pm\hbar k/m_e=\pm2.32\times10^4\) m/s, giving currents \(\pm9.27\times10^9\) s⁻¹ at the stated point. The probability within an interval instead comes from integrating the density. Its rate of change requires the difference of the currents at both ends. A large numerical rate per second can describe a normalised packet passing a point in a very short time.

Determine an unknown step

A beam with kinetic energy 9.0 eV approaches an upward step. Its reflected probability fraction is 0.040. Infer the ratio of wave numbers, the step energy and the transmitted amplitude for unit incident amplitude. Then recover the transmitted probability from current.

Invert the matching conditions

For an upward step below the incident energy \(r\) is positive, so \(r=0.20\). The ratio \(q/k=(1-r)/(1+r)=2/3\) gives \((E-V_0)/E=4/9\) and \(V_0=5.0\) eV. Displacement continuity gives \(t=1.20\). The current fraction is \((2/3)(1.20)^2=0.96\), closing the balance with \(R=0.04\). The step energy in this inverse calculation depends on having specified the sign of the step.

Infer a tunnelling decay length

At fixed incident energy a wide rectangular barrier transmits \(2.0\times10^{-4}\) of incident particles. Increasing its width by 0.20 nm reduces that fraction to \(2.0\times10^{-5}\). Estimate \(\kappa\), the field-amplitude decay length and \(V_0-E\) for an electron. State which part of the inference would fail for a thin barrier.

Use the ratio to eliminate the prefactor

For \(\kappa L\gg1\) the ratio is \(e^{-2\kappa\Delta L}=0.10\), so \(\kappa=\ln10/(0.40\,\mathrm{nm})=5.76\) nm⁻¹ and \(1/\kappa=0.174\) nm. With \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV·nm², \(V_0-E=0.0381(5.76)^2=1.26\) eV. In a thin barrier the complete hyperbolic-sine matching expression is needed; taking a log ratio then need not yield a width-independent slope.
