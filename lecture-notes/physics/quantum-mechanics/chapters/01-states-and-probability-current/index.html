Wave packets and probability current

Normalise a quantum wave packet and derive a local balance law that predicts the passage of probability through a detector plane.

Specify a preparation

Prepare many independent particles of the same mass in the same one-dimensional state and record one position from each preparation. The frequencies in small position bins reveal a probability density. Quantum theory assigns a complex wavefunction \(\psi(x,t)\) to a pure state. The probability for \(a<x<b\) is \(\int_a^b|\psi|^2dx\). The integral over the whole line equals one for a normalised packet. Thus \(|\psi|^2\) has units of inverse length and can numerically exceed one without any probability exceeding one.

A single position histogram fixes the modulus of the wavefunction but contains no direct information about its phase. A global phase is unobservable, whereas a phase that varies across space changes the momentum distribution and subsequent evolution. Two preparations with the same initial histogram can reach a detector at different times. To predict how probability moves we need the phase information together with an equation of motion.

Obtain a continuity equation

For a particle in a real scalar potential \(V(x,t)\), the Schrödinger equation is \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-(\hbar^2/2m)\partial_{xx}\psi+V\psi\). Multiply by \(\psi^*\), subtract the conjugate equation multiplied by \(\psi\) and use the product rule. The two potential terms cancel because \(V\) is real. The result takes the conservative form

\[\partial_t|\psi|^2+\partial_x j=0,\qquad j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*\partial_x\psi-\psi\partial_x\psi^*\right).\]

Integrated between two fixed points, the rate of change of probability inside equals the inward current at the left minus the outward current at the right. In this one-dimensional normalisation \(j\) has units of inverse seconds. Vanishing current at both infinities ensures that a normalised state stays normalised even if the shape of its density changes appreciably.

Writing \(\psi=\sqrt\rho\,e^{i\varphi}\) gives \(j=(\hbar/m)\rho\,\partial_x\varphi\) where the phase is regular. For an initially real packet the current vanishes at that instant. A spatial phase ramp \(e^{ikx}\) leaves \(\rho\) unchanged yet gives current \(\hbar k\rho/m\). Current is a signed net flux. Interpreting every crossing of a point as a first arrival requires additional information about the detector and possible returns.

Count momenta as well as positions

Use the Fourier convention \(\psi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int\tilde\psi(k)e^{ikx}dk\). Parseval’s identity preserves the normalisation. The probability in a wave-number interval is \(|\tilde\psi(k)|^2dk\), with momentum \(p=\hbar k\). A Gaussian packet having density variance \(\sigma_x^2\) at preparation can be written as \(\psi=(2\pi\sigma_x^2)^{-1/4}\exp[-(x-x_0)^2/(4\sigma_x^2)+ik_0x]\). Fourier transforming this Gaussian gives mean momentum \(\hbar k_0\) and standard deviation \(\hbar/(2\sigma_x)\). Tighter initial localisation broadens the momentum distribution.

For a free particle the plane-wave components advance with \(\omega(k)=\hbar k^2/(2m)\). Different wave numbers give different group velocities. An initially minimal, unchirped Gaussian spreads to \(\sigma_x(t)=\sigma_x\sqrt{1+[\hbar t/(2m\sigma_x^2)]^2}\). Transferring this expression to an initially focused or phase-curved packet requires its position–momentum correlation as well as its initial width.

Predict a free-flight measurement

An electron has initial width 100 nm and mean wave number \(1.0\times10^8\) m⁻¹. Its mean speed is \(\hbar k_0/m_e=1.16\times10^4\) m/s and its velocity spread is about 579 m/s. After 1.0 ns the centre has moved 11.6 µm. For the unchirped preparation the width grows to \(\sqrt{(0.100\,\mu\mathrm m)^2+(0.579\,\mu\mathrm m)^2}=0.588\) µm. The packet is well inside the nonrelativistic regime.

The solution predicts two independent observables: the motion of the centre and the broadening about that centre. Reversing the phase ramp reverses the former while leaving the latter unchanged. The initial position histograms for the two preparations are identical. A downstream record is needed to discriminate their motion.

One can measure spreading by repeating the preparation and recording the position distribution after several flight times. For an initially uncorrelated position and momentum, the free-particle variance is \(\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_x^2(0)+t^2\sigma_p^2/m^2\). This follows from the operator equation \(x(t)=x(0)+p(0)t/m\). A known initial position–momentum covariance adds a term linear in time and can even make the packet contract initially. Measuring a minimum downstream width alone therefore does not establish the width or the momentum uncertainty at release.

Infer current from two interfering momentum components

An interference pattern can make density strongly position dependent even when probability current is uniform. Put a free particle on a ring of circumference \(L\) and use periodic boundary conditions. Two states with wavenumbers \(+k\) and \(-k\), where \(k=2\pi n/L\) and \(n\) is a nonzero integer, have the same energy \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\). A normalized superposition has wavefunction \(\psi=[a e^{ikx}+be^{-ikx}]e^{-iEt/\hbar}/\sqrt L\) with \(|a|^2+|b|^2=1\). Integration around the ring makes the two opposite travelling waves orthogonal.

Squaring the amplitude gives a density containing a constant term and a spatial fringe, \(\rho=[1+2\mathrm{Re}(ab^*e^{2ikx})]/L\). To find current, differentiate \(\psi\) before taking the imaginary part of \(\psi^*\partial_x\psi\). The two diagonal products contribute with opposite signs. The cross terms in that product are real, so their contribution to current vanishes. Thus

\[j=\frac{\hbar k}{mL}(|a|^2-|b|^2),\qquad \partial_t\rho=-\partial_xj=0 .\]

The relative weights determine the net rate of passage past a fixed point; the relative phase shifts the fringe positions without changing that rate. Equal weights give a standing wave with stationary nodes and zero current. An unequal pair can have a stationary density pattern with nonzero circulating current, because every segment loses probability at one end at exactly the rate it gains it at the other.

Distinguish two preparations with one density pattern

Take an electron on an ideal ring of circumference \(1.00\) μm with \(n=2\), so \(k=1.257\times10^7\) m⁻¹. Choose \(a=\sqrt{0.80}\) and \(b=\sqrt{0.20}\) real. Fringe spacing is \(\pi/k=0.250\) μm and normalized density ranges from \(0.20/L\) to \(1.80/L\). The velocity scale is \(\hbar k/m_e=1.46\times10^3\) m/s. The probability current is this speed times \(0.60/L\), or \(8.73\times10^8\) s⁻¹. These units describe crossings per unit time for a normalized one-dimensional state rather than probability density per metre.

Interchange the weights assigned to clockwise and anticlockwise components while keeping their relative phase fixed and real. Density everywhere on the ring is identical but current reverses sign. A position histogram alone therefore cannot resolve which preparation was made. A momentum measurement over repeated preparations would instead return the two momenta with swapped probabilities. For a physical charged particle, multiplication by its charge gives the circulating electric current, whose sign is also reversed relative to probability current for an electron. A weak magnetic probe couples to that circulation rather than to the fringe visibility.

A relative phase change offers another comparison. Multiplying the anticlockwise amplitude by \(e^{i\alpha}\) moves the density pattern around the ring by \(\alpha/(2k)\) with the sign fixed by the chosen wave convention. At \(\alpha=\pi\) a previous maximum becomes a minimum, yet neither the probabilities of the two momentum outcomes nor the current changes. Conversely replacing the coherent preparation by a random mixture with the same two momentum probabilities leaves the circulating current intact while removing the interference fringes. Current, momentum populations and position fringes are therefore independent pieces of information about even this simple state.

Follow a freely spreading wave packet

A freely evolving minimum-uncertainty Gaussian starts at rest with position width \(\sigma_0\). Its momentum spread is \(\hbar/(2\sigma_0)\). Free motion gives \(\sigma(t)^2=\sigma_0^2+[\hbar t/(2m\sigma_0)]^2\) in the absence of an initial position–momentum correlation.
