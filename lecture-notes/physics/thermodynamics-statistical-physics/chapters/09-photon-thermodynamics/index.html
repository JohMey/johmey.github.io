Thermal radiation in a cavity

Count electromagnetic states, derive the temperature scaling of radiation energy and connect a cavity measurement to emitted power.

Photons in thermal equilibrium

An empty cavity with walls at temperature \(T\) contains electromagnetic radiation. The walls absorb and emit photons, so equilibrium does not constrain their number. The chemical potential of equilibrium photons is zero. Each mode of angular frequency \(\omega\) carries a thermal mean energy \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) above its temperature-independent vacuum energy. The spectrum follows by multiplying this occupation by the number of modes in a frequency interval.

Take a large box of volume \(V\) and periodic boundary conditions for counting. One state per polarization occupies a reciprocal-space cell \((2\pi)^3/V\). A thin spherical shell contributes \(4\pi k^2dk\) times two independent transverse polarizations. Using \(\omega=ck\) gives the state density per volume \(\omega^2/(\pi^2c^3)\). Hence

\[u_\omega(T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}.\]

At low frequency the mean mode energy approaches \(k_BT\) as in classical equipartition. Extending that classical formula to arbitrarily high frequencies would produce an infinite integral. Quantum occupation suppresses these modes exponentially when a single photon costs much more than the thermal energy.

Energy, pressure and flux

Integrate over positive \(\omega\), putting \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\). The integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) gives energy density \(u=aT^4\) with \(a=\pi^2k_B^4/(15\hbar^3c^3)\). Pressure follows from radiation momentum: a photon carries momentum \(E/c\), while crossing a plane weights directions by their projected speed. Evaluating the normal momentum flux over isotropic directions introduces \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\), hence \(p=u/3\).

A small hole in a black cavity admits only outgoing directions. The half-sphere average of the normal speed gives net emitted flux \(cu/4\), which is the Stefan–Boltzmann law \(\sigma T^4\) with \(\sigma=ac/4\). The different factors of three and four describe different angular averages and observables. A wall facing another large black surface also receives thermal radiation, so its net radiative heat loss depends on the fourth powers of both temperatures.

Observe cooling power

A 1.00 cm² aperture faces a large black surrounding enclosure at 300 K. The cavity temperature is 600 K. The outgoing power is \(\sigma A T^4=0.735\) W while incoming power is \(0.0459\) W. The difference, \(0.689\) W, must be replaced to maintain temperature if all other thermal exchanges are negligible. The interior radiation energy density is only \(9.81\times10^{-5}\) J/m³; repeated replenishment at the walls sustains the outgoing flux.

A wavelength spectrometer and a frequency spectrometer plot different spectral densities. To convert use \(u_\lambda=u_\nu|d\nu/d\lambda|\), since both must assign the same energy to corresponding intervals. Merely substituting \(\nu=c/\lambda\) into a density per hertz shifts the plotted maximum incorrectly. The integrated energy and the predicted net aperture power are independent of which spectral coordinate is chosen.

The photon equation of state also predicts a reversible adiabatic temperature change. Write \(U=aVT^4\) and \(p=aT^4/3\), then set \(dU=-p\,dV\). Collecting terms gives \(dT/T=-dV/(3V)\), or \(TV^{1/3}=\mathrm{constant}\). Halving the linear dimension of a cubical equilibrium radiation cavity would double its temperature. This scaling agrees with the shift of every electromagnetic mode frequency under uniform contraction.

Determine the entropy carried by equilibrium radiation

A beam emitted through a small black-cavity opening carries entropy as well as energy. For large equilibrium photon systems, extensivity gives the Euler relation \(U=TS-pV+\mu N\). The photon chemical potential is zero and \(p=u/3\), where \(u=U/V=aT^4\). Solving for entropy density gives \(s=(u+p)/T=4aT^3/3\). The same result follows independently by integrating \(dS=C_VdT/T\) at fixed volume, since \(C_V=4aVT^3\).

Inside the cavity the radiation is isotropic. Photons leaving through a small aperture sample each outgoing direction in proportion to its projected velocity, giving \(c/4\) times the interior density for either energy or entropy. Thus the emitted energy flux is \(\sigma T^4\) and entropy flux is \((4/3)\sigma T^3\). The latter exceeds energy flux divided by \(T\) by a factor of \(4/3\). Opening the cavity to a colder environment is an irreversible emission process, not the reversible transfer of an equal amount of heat through a material boundary at the source temperature.

Consider a device intercepting ideal black radiation from a hot reservoir at \(T_h\) while exchanging a counterpropagating equilibrium field with an environment at \(T_0\). The net incoming energy flux per area is \(\sigma(T_h^4-T_0^4)\) and the corresponding net entropy flux is \((4/3)\sigma(T_h^3-T_0^3)\). A reversible converter must return this entropy to the ambient reservoir, requiring at least \(T_0\) times the net entropy flux as rejected heat. The remaining energy is the maximum possible work for these specified radiation streams,

\[\frac{\dot W_{\max}}A=\sigma T_h^4\left[1-\frac43\frac{T_0}{T_h}+\frac13\left(\frac{T_0}{T_h}\right)^4\right].\]

Evaluate an optical energy and entropy budget

Take a 1.00 cm² aperture, \(T_h=600\) K and \(T_0=300\) K. Outgoing energy is \(0.735\) W and incoming ambient radiation \(0.0459\) W. Net energy input is therefore \(0.689\) W. The net entropy supply is \((4/3)\sigma A(600^3-300^3)=1.43\times10^{-3}\) W/K. Returning this to 300 K requires at least \(0.429\) W, leaving approximately \(0.260\) W available as work. The result is about 35.4 percent of the hot aperture's outgoing radiation power.

As \(T_h\) tends to \(T_0\), net energy and net entropy transport vanish and the maximum work vanishes as well. A grey, directional or spectrally filtered source may have different relationships between intensity and entropy from these two ideal black streams. Using only a bolometer reading and a colour temperature to infer available work is therefore justified only if the assumed angular and spectral distributions are also credible.

A check on the limiting expression is to compare it with an ideal material heat engine fed at \(T_h\). Such an engine can in principle extract a fraction \(1-T_0/T_h\) of a reversible heat input. In the optical problem the incoming stream already has a specified entropy-to-energy ratio. Delivering it to a converter cannot undo the entropy generated by producing that stream in the first place. This accounts for the smaller work fraction at the same two nominal temperatures and makes clear which boundary is being used when assigning a maximum work to emitted radiation.
