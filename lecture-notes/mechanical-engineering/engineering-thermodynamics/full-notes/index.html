Chapter 1 · State, process and the first law for a closed system

Distinguish properties from path-dependent transfers and determine heat and work for a piston-cylinder process.

Decide what crosses the boundary

Draw a boundary around the gas in a piston cylinder. If no gas crosses its surface, the system is closed even though the piston moves. A rigid vessel is a special closed system with fixed volume. An insulated wall prevents heat transfer; a shaft or electrical lead can still transmit work through it. These descriptions impose different constraints and should be identified before choosing an equation.

At equilibrium a simple compressible substance can be described using independent state variables such as temperature and specific volume. Pressure \(p\), volume \(V\), temperature \(T\) and internal energy \(U\) are properties. An equation of state relates some of them. For a dilute ideal gas \(pV=mRT\), where \(R\) is the specific gas constant. Always use absolute pressure and absolute temperature in this relation. Two initial and final states determine changes in properties, but do not specify the heat and work transfers between them.

Construct the process energy balance

Take work done by the system as positive and heat entering it as positive. The first law is \(\Delta U+\Delta KE+\Delta PE=Q-W\). For a stationary vessel ignore changes in kinetic and gravitational energy of the system as a whole. A moving piston of area \(A\) against external pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\) performs incremental work \(p_{\rm ext}A\,dx=p_{\rm ext}dV\). For a quasistatic mechanically reversible motion the boundary pressure can be represented by the gas pressure throughout the process.

If gas expands from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) at constant external pressure, \(W=p_{\rm ext}(V_2-V_1)\). If it instead follows a reversible isothermal ideal-gas path, inserting \(p=mRT/V\) and integrating gives \(W=mRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\). Both expressions have units of energy. Their different volume dependence makes a pressure-volume diagram useful: the area under each pressure path has a direct work interpretation when its boundary-force assumptions hold.

Heat air below a freely moving piston

A weight-loaded, frictionless piston keeps 0.50 kg of ideal air at 150 kPa absolute while a heater raises its temperature from 300 to 360 K. Use \(R=0.287\) and \(c_v=0.718\) kJ/(kg K), treated as constant. The initial and final volumes are \(mRT/p=0.287\) and 0.3444 m³. Boundary work is \(150(0.0574)=8.61\) kJ. For this gas the internal-energy change is \(mc_v\Delta T=0.50(0.718)(60)=21.54\) kJ.

The required heat is \(Q=\Delta U+W=30.15\) kJ. The same result follows from \(mc_p\Delta T\) because for this constant-pressure ideal-gas process with only boundary work \(c_p=c_v+R=1.005\) kJ/(kg K). The extra heat above the internal-energy increase moved the piston weight. Using \(Q=mc_p\Delta T\) without inspecting the constraint would give the wrong heat input to a rigid tank.

Compare a rigid-wall experiment

Lock the piston at its initial volume before heating an identical mass from 300 to 360 K. Now \(W=0\), so only 21.54 kJ of heat is needed. The final pressure is \(150(360/300)=180\) kPa. Equal temperature changes in an ideal gas imply equal changes in internal energy, even though final pressures and energy transfers differ. For a real fluid near a phase boundary, constant ideal-gas heat capacities may fail; use tabulated state properties or a suitable equation of state. In either case the first-law accounting is unchanged once the initial and final internal energies and the actual boundary work are known.

A different path between specified volumes

An ideal gas initially at 300 kPa and 0.020 m³ expands quasistatically and isothermally to twice its initial volume. Since \(pV=p_1V_1\), work out is \(W=p_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)=6\ln2=4.16\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Ideal-gas internal energy depends only on temperature, so \(\Delta U=0\) and heat entering equals 4.16 kJ. A freely expanding ideal gas in an insulated rigid outer vessel can reach the same final temperature and occupied volume while doing zero boundary work. The state variables alone do not record which process occurred.

Determine heat and work along a measured compression path

A measured pressure–volume trace contains more information than its endpoints. For some slow piston processes an empirical polytropic law \(pV^n=\text{constant}\) fits the path. Here \(n\) is a fitted process exponent and need not equal the gas heat-capacity ratio. Assume a closed cylinder with negligible kinetic and potential energy change, a quasistatic pressure load and a calorically perfect ideal gas. Work out of the gas is the area \(W=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}p\,dV\), which is negative for compression. Substitute \(p=p_1V_1^n V^{-n}\) and integrate:

\[W=\frac{p_2V_2-p_1V_1}{1-n}\quad(n\ne1),\qquad W=p_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)\quad(n=1).\]

The logarithmic case is the continuous limit as \(n\) approaches one. The ideal-gas equation also gives \(T_2/T_1=(V_1/V_2)^{n-1}\), so \(\Delta U=mc_v(T_2-T_1)\). With \(Q-W=\Delta U\) and \(pV=mRT\), the heat input is \(Q=m[c_v+R/(1-n)](T_2-T_1)\) for \(n\ne1\). Setting \(n=\gamma=c_p/c_v\) eliminates \(Q\). This inference relies on the ideal-gas, constant-property, quasistatic assumptions. An arbitrary irreversible compression with the same endpoints cannot be called isentropic from the fitted exponent alone.

Recover a cylinder heat loss from three measurements

A test traps air at \(p_1=100\,\mathrm{kPa}\) absolute, \(V_1=0.0100\,\mathrm{m^3}\), \(T_1=300\,\mathrm K\), then compresses it to \(V_2=0.0040\,\mathrm{m^3}\). The fitted exponent is 1.25. Using \(R=0.287,\ c_v=0.718\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\), the trapped mass is \(p_1V_1/(RT_1)=0.01161\,\mathrm{kg}\). The predicted pressure is \(100(2.5)^{1.25}=314.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\), and \(T_2=300(2.5)^{0.25}=377.2\,\mathrm K\). Note that absolute pressure is required in both the fit and the gas law. A pressure transducer referenced to atmosphere would need its reference restored.

The gas does \(W=[314.4(0.0040)-100(0.0100)]/(1-1.25)=-1.03\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Its internal energy rises by \(0.01161(0.718)(77.2)=0.644\,\mathrm{kJ}\), giving \(Q=\Delta U+W=-0.386\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Compression work enters the gas while part leaves as heat through the cylinder wall. If the process were isothermal at 300 K, the same volume change would require only \(-W=0.916\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and the full amount would leave as heat. A mid-stroke measurement gives a stronger check than the end pressure: the fitted law predicts \(100(0.010/0.007)^{1.25}=156.2\,\mathrm{kPa}\) at 7.0 litres. A substantial discrepancy would favour direct numerical integration of the measured trace over using a single exponent.

An apparent exponent can also be estimated directly from pressure ratios: \(n=\ln(p_2/p_1)/\ln(V_1/V_2)\). Compare estimates from the first and second halves of the stroke. Agreement means the same log–log slope describes both halves, while disagreement may indicate changing heat transfer, leakage or phase lag between the pressure and position sensors. It does not identify which of those mechanisms is responsible. As a separate check, suppose a water jacket gains the estimated 0.386 kJ each stroke and the compressor completes ten such strokes per minute. The mean heat removal from these strokes would be \(0.386(10)/60=0.0643\,\mathrm{kW}\). A jacket measurement of the complete machine includes intake and exhaust processes and mechanical friction as well, so experimental comparison requires either an isolated compression interval or a balance around the rest of the cycle.

A change in operating speed can also change the fitted exponent because it changes the time available for heat exchange during a single compression stroke.

Chapter 2 · Open systems, enthalpy and steady-flow devices

Use an inlet-outlet balance to distinguish shaft work, kinetic-energy conversion and pressure reduction.

Account for fluid entering and leaving

A closed-system piston can be followed with its gas. A continuously operating turbine has fresh fluid crossing its boundary every second. Enclose the hardware and its internal flow in a stationary control volume. Flowing mass carries internal energy \(u\), kinetic energy \(v^2/2\) and gravitational energy \(gz\) per kilogram. Upstream pressure also does work pushing fluid in, while downstream pressure opposes its exit. Per unit mass this flow work is \(p/\rho\), motivating the specific enthalpy \(h=u+p/\rho\).

Adding all incoming and outgoing energy rates gives, for one inlet, one outlet and constant stored energy,

\[\dot Q-\dot W_s=\dot m\left[h_2-h_1+(v_2^2-v_1^2)/2+g(z_2-z_1)\right].\]

Here \(\dot W_s\) is shaft power out of the control volume. Pressure work at the flowing boundaries is already contained in enthalpy; adding it once more would count it twice. One inlet and outlet have the same steady mass rate even when their volumetric flow rates differ dramatically because gas expands.

Read the dominant energy conversion

In a short, insulated nozzle there is no shaft, the elevation difference is small and falling enthalpy raises velocity. The property \(h+v^2/2\) is then constant, often called stagnation enthalpy. In an insulated turbine with small inlet and outlet speeds, the enthalpy drop instead appears mainly as positive shaft work. An insulated compressor takes work in, raising enthalpy. An insulated throttling valve has neither shaft power nor a large net change of bulk kinetic energy between distant ports, so its inlet and outlet enthalpies are approximately equal. These components can all drop pressure during expansion, yet their energy effects differ.

For ideal-gas air with nearly constant \(c_p\), an enthalpy difference may be approximated by \(c_p\Delta T\). Keeping h in kJ/kg while using \(v^2/2\) in J/kg is a common factor-of-one-thousand error. At 100 m/s the kinetic energy is 5 kJ/kg. That estimate often decides whether an inlet velocity may be omitted from a turbine work calculation.

Evaluate heat loss and exhaust speed

A turbine passes 2.0 kg/s of gas from \(h_1=820\) to \(h_2=600\) kJ/kg. Inlet speed is 40 m/s, exhaust speed 160 m/s and the casing loses 10 kW of heat. Elevations are equal. The gas loses \(2(820-600)=440\) kW of enthalpy flow. Its kinetic-energy rate grows by \(2(160^2-40^2)/2000=24.0\) kW. The unit delivers \(440-24-10=406\) kW shaft power. Counting all 440 kW as shaft output would overstate the result by both the residual exhaust energy and the heat leak.

A separate nozzle using gas at \(h_1=820\) kJ/kg and 40 m/s could reach \(h_2=800\) kJ/kg without a shaft if its speed rose to \(\sqrt{40^2+2(20\,000)}=204\) m/s. This estimate is an energy condition. The required area ratio, pressure drop and possibility of choking also depend on compressible-flow relations.

Extend the same balance to a mixer

Two insulated water lines mix at negligible speed change. One supplies 0.20 kg/s at 360 K and the other 0.30 kg/s at 300 K. A mass balance gives 0.50 kg/s at exit. With equal, constant \(c_p\) and no shaft, summing the two inlet enthalpy rates yields an outlet temperature \([0.20(360)+0.30(300)]/0.50=324\) K. The temperature lies between the inlet values, closer to the higher mass rate. The mixer conserves energy while creating entropy. The next lecture explains how both statements can hold simultaneously and why reversing the mixer without another effect is impossible.

Interpret a measured stagnation temperature

For an insulated diffuser with negligible shaft work, reducing speed from 200 to 40 m/s raises static enthalpy by \((200^2-40^2)/2=19.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). For air with \(c_p=1.005\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) that corresponds to a 19.1 K static temperature rise. Stagnation enthalpy \(h+V^2/2\) stays constant even if internal friction produces entropy. A stationary temperature probe that slows the arriving air may approach a stagnation reading, so comparison with a predicted static temperature requires knowing where and how the measurement was taken.

Minimise compression work by selecting an intermediate pressure

A continuous air supply can be compressed in two stages and cooled at intermediate pressure. For a steady adiabatic compressor with negligible kinetic and potential changes, shaft work input is the stream enthalpy rise. With an ideal gas, constant \(c_p,\gamma\), and a reversible stage, \(w=c_p T_{\rm in}[(p_{\rm out}/p_{\rm in})^a-1]\), where \(a=(\gamma-1)/\gamma\). This follows by integrating \(ds=c_p\,dT/T-R\,dp/p=0\), then applying \(dh=c_p\,dT\). If both stages use the same isentropic efficiency, divide each ideal rise by that efficiency.

Assume complete intercooling to the original inlet temperature \(T_1\), no pressure losses and prescribed first and last pressures. Put \(r_1=p_i/p_1,\ r_2=p_3/p_i\), so \(r_1r_2=p_3/p_1=r\). Total ideal work is \(c_pT_1[r_1^a+(r/r_1)^a-2]\). Differentiate with respect to \(\ln r_1\); the derivative is \(c_pT_1a(r_1^a-r_2^a)\). The sum has a minimum at equal ratios, giving intermediate pressure \(p_i=\sqrt{p_1p_3}\). This is a geometric mean of absolute pressures. A gauge-pressure ratio would give a completely different design.

Compare two cooled stages with a single compression

Supply air at 100 kPa and 300 K and deliver it at 900 kPa absolute. For \(\gamma=1.4,\ c_p=1.005\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\), an ideal single stage gives \(T_3=300(9^{0.2857})=562.0\,\mathrm K\) and work about 263 kJ/kg. Two equal-ratio stages operate through 300 kPa. Each reaches \(300(3^{0.2857})=410.6\,\mathrm K\) before its outlet or intercooler, requiring \(1.005(110.6)=111.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Total work is about 222.4 kJ/kg, roughly 15.5% lower. The intercooler must remove 111.2 kJ/kg between the stages under the ideal complete-cooling assumption.

With equal stage efficiencies of 0.80, each real enthalpy rise becomes 139.0 kJ/kg and the intermediate discharge temperature is about 438 K. Cooling back to 300 K then removes 139.0 kJ per kilogram, with a corresponding load of 27.8 kW at 0.20 kg/s. The equal-ratio choice survives because both stage work expressions acquired the same multiplier. An intercooler that can cool only to 330 K increases the weight of second-stage compression work; the new optimum shifts towards a larger first-stage pressure ratio. A design comparing only compressor shaft power would also need to account for coolant pumping, fan power and pressure losses before choosing the number of stages.

For an incomplete intercooler the optimal shift can be calculated. Take stage two inlet temperature \(T_m=330\) K independently maintained and identical efficiencies. The variable portion of total work is proportional to \(T_1r_1^a+T_m(r/r_1)^a\). Differentiating with respect to log first-stage ratio gives \(T_1r_1^a=T_mr_2^a\), or \(r_1/r_2=(330/300)^{1/a}=1.40\). At overall ratio nine, \(r_1=\sqrt{9(1.40)}=3.54,\ r_2=2.54\). Compressing farther while the air is colder is energetically preferable within this simplified comparison. These ratios presume the cooler can maintain 330 K over its revised duty and that both discharge temperatures stay within their allowable ranges. A compressor map may impose further flow and speed limits beyond the thermodynamic minimum.

At fixed pressure endpoints, the measured intermediate pressure alone would thus be insufficient for diagnosing whether the cooler or a compressor caused an observed power increase.

Problem sheet 1 · Energy accounts for stored and flowing matter

Draw a boundary, identify work and heat with consistent signs, and test the effect of changing the boundary.

A gas under a spring-loaded piston

A closed gas expands from 0.010 to 0.016 m³. The resisting absolute pressure rises linearly from 160 to 280 kPa. Its internal energy increases by 0.900 kJ. Compute the boundary work and the heat entering the gas. If the ambient pressure is 100 kPa, how much of the boundary work is available after displacing the atmosphere?

Work, heat and the atmosphere

The area under the straight pressure–volume line is the mean ordinate times the volume interval. With quasistatic motion, \(W=(160+280)(0.006)/2=1.320\,\mathrm{kJ}\). The first law with work positive out gives \(Q=\Delta U+W=2.220\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Displacing the atmosphere consumes \(100(0.006)=0.600\,\mathrm{kJ}\). The remaining 0.720 kJ raises the piston or compresses the spring. Subtract atmospheric work when assessing useful work, but use absolute pressure for the closed gas energy balance.

Is the exit speed consistent?

Air enters a steady insulated nozzle at 360 K and 30 m/s, and leaves at 325 K. Take constant specific heat 1.005 kJ/(kg K). Find the exit speed and the exit area for 0.24 kg/s if the exit density is 1.08 kg/m³. Explain what changes if the nozzle loses 3 kJ/kg as heat at the same temperatures.

Balance and continuity

Per unit mass, \(V_2^2=V_1^2+2c_p(T_1-T_2)=900+2(1005)(35)=71250\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Thus \(V_2=266.9\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The area is \(\dot m/(\rho_2 V_2)=8.33\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). Losing 3000 J/kg reduces \(V_2^2\) by 6000, giving 255.4 m/s. Energy that leaves the control volume as heat is unavailable to accelerate the stream.

Filling an evacuated rigid receiver

An insulated, initially evacuated tank is filled from a large air line at 300 K. The final mass is 0.80 kg. Assume perfect gas, \(c_p=1.005\), \(c_v=0.718\) kJ/(kg K), negligible kinetic energy in the final tank, no shaft work. Find the final energy and temperature. Why would a closed, constant-volume calculation yield a different interpretation?

Integrate the unsteady balance

Each kilogram carries inlet enthalpy 301.5 kJ/kg. Integrating over filling gives \(U_2=m_2 h_{\rm in}=241.2\,\mathrm{kJ}\) with a common zero-energy convention. Hence \(T_2=h_{\rm in}/c_v=419.9\,\mathrm K\). The temperature rise accompanies the flow work carried in with the gas. A closed balance that follows the entering parcel must explicitly include the work that pushes the parcel into the receiver.

Chapter 3 · Entropy, irreversibility and useful-work loss

Check a proposed process with the second law and quantify the effect of a finite temperature difference.

Energy bookkeeping is not sufficient

In an insulated two-body experiment, heat lost by one body is gained by the other. That energy balance alone would allow heat to move spontaneously from a colder body to a hotter one. Entropy supplies the missing direction constraint. It is a property of a state. For a simple equilibrium system a reversible differential heat transfer defines \(dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T\). An irreversible process between the same endpoints has the same entropy difference; the actual heat transfer divided by the body temperature need not equal that difference.

For a closed system, the integrated entropy balance reads \(\Delta S=\int \delta Q/T_b+S_{\rm gen}\), where \(T_b\) is the absolute temperature at the boundary where the heat crosses. Entropy generation satisfies \(S_{\rm gen}\ge0\). A system can lose entropy by exporting it in heat or in a mass stream. A negative change in the entropy of a single hot body therefore need not violate the second law.

Calculate a property change with a convenient path

For a calorically perfect ideal gas, combine \(T\,ds=du+p\,dv\) with \(du=c_v\,dT\) and \(p=RT/v\). This gives \(ds=c_v\,dT/T+R\,dv/v\). Replacing \(v\) using the ideal-gas law yields the alternative \(ds=c_p\,dT/T-R\,dp/p\). Integrate between the initial and final equilibrium states. These are property relationships, even when the actual path between states includes friction, throttling or free expansion.

An ideal gas passing through an insulated valve with equal inlet and outlet speeds has \(h_2=h_1\), so its temperatures are equal in the constant-\(c_p\) approximation. Dropping air from 400 to 100 kPa gives \(s_2-s_1=-0.287\ln(100/400)=0.398\) kJ/(kg K). With no heat transfer or other streams, the positive entropy change per kilogram is entirely generated. A reversible insulated expansion turbine would generally produce a lower outlet temperature and shaft work.

Trace entropy across two reservoirs

An insulated conductor transfers 600 W from a reservoir at 400 K to another at 300 K. Its stored energy and entropy are steady. The hot reservoir loses entropy at \(600/400=1.50\) W/K, while the cold one gains \(600/300=2.00\) W/K. The total generation rate is 0.50 W/K. Heat rate is the same at both ends; entropy transfer in that heat differs because the temperatures differ.

\[\dot S_{\rm gen}=\dot Q\left(\frac1{T_c}-\frac1{T_h}\right).\]

If the surroundings defining the available-work reference are at \(T_0=300\) K, the corresponding destruction of available work is \(T_0\dot S_{\rm gen}=150\) W. For these particular reservoirs a reversible engine supplied with 600 W at 400 K could have produced \(600(1-300/400)=150\) W before rejecting the remainder. The conductor instead rejects all 600 W to the cold sink. Energy did not disappear; an opportunity for work was lost.

Locate sources and choose a boundary

A heat exchanger can have little external heat leak yet generate appreciable entropy internally through finite temperature differences and pressure losses. Write \(\dot S_{\rm gen}=\sum_{\rm out}\dot m s-\sum_{\rm in}\dot m s-\sum_j\dot Q_j/T_{b,j}\) for steady operation. Both streams belong inside the same control volume when testing internal heat exchange. If the boundary encloses only one stream, heat crossing the wall carries entropy with it and must appear explicitly. The largest reduction in entropy generation per extra unit of area or pumping effort is a design question. The thermodynamic balance identifies losses without alone determining the economically preferred geometry.

Compare the conditions on a valve and a turbine

Air passes through a steady insulated throttling valve from 400 to 100 kPa with negligible changes of speed. With no shaft work the first law gives equal inlet and outlet enthalpy. For ideal air this implies equal temperature. Its entropy change follows from \(\Delta s=c_p\ln(T_2/T_1)-R\ln(p_2/p_1)=0.287\ln4=0.398\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\). At 0.10 kg/s the entropy generation is 0.0398 kW/K. An ideal adiabatic turbine undergoing the same pressure drop has constant entropy and delivers work as its temperature falls. Pressure ratio alone therefore cannot determine the temperature change across an unfamiliar device.

Obtain a work bound for two finite bodies

The reversible efficiency \(1-T_c/T_h\) describes operation between reservoirs held at fixed temperatures. If the two bodies are finite, each increment of transferred heat changes the temperatures and the instantaneous efficiency. Consider two incompressible blocks in a common insulated enclosure. Their heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\) are taken as constant. A reversible engine operates between them and exports mechanical work until no temperature difference remains. The working fluid returns to its starting state after each incremental cycle, so conservation of entropy for this reversible composite system determines the final temperature.

\[C_h\ln(T_f/T_h)+C_c\ln(T_f/T_c)=0,\qquad T_f=T_h^{C_h/(C_h+C_c)}T_c^{C_c/(C_h+C_c)}.\]

Energy conservation gives maximum work \(W_{\max}=C_hT_h+C_cT_c-(C_h+C_c)T_f\) for consistent internal-energy references. The reversible final temperature is a weighted geometric mean. If the two blocks are simply put in thermal contact without work output, first-law conservation instead gives the weighted arithmetic mean. The arithmetic mean is larger for unequal initial temperatures; additional energy remains as sensible heat after irreversible contact. These different final temperatures matter when comparing two ways of using the same finite resources.

In a real engine running at finite heat-transfer rates there are temperature differences between each block and the working fluid. Total entropy then increases. For a specified common final temperature the entropy change of the blocks is fixed by state properties; measuring it indicates the departure from the ideal reversible construction without needing the working-fluid cycle.

Compare direct contact and a reversible engine quantitatively

Take a hot assembly of heat capacity 2.0 kJ/K at 450 K and a cooler assembly of 1.0 kJ/K at 300 K. Solving the log balance gives \(T_f=(450^2\,300)^{1/3}=393.1\,\mathrm K\). The hot assembly releases about \(2(450-393.1)=113.8\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and the cold one receives 93.1 kJ. Their difference gives approximately 20.7 kJ of maximum work. Acting as though the initial temperatures stayed at 450 and 300 K throughout would assign an incorrectly high efficiency to later parts of the transfer.

By direct contact the common temperature would be \((2\times450+300)/3=400\,\mathrm K\). The entropy generated is \(2\ln(400/450)+\ln(400/300)=0.0521\,\mathrm{kJ/K}\). If instead a device delivers a measured 12 kJ of work before the blocks reach a common temperature, energy fixes that temperature as \((1200-12)/3=396\,\mathrm K\). The measured process then generates \(2\ln(396/450)+\ln(396/300)=0.0220\,\mathrm{kJ/K}\), a positive value between the two limiting cases. Entropy generation multiplied by an environmental temperature gives destroyed exergy relative to that environment; comparing the finite-body work outputs also requires acknowledging their different final stored energies.

The geometric-mean result has an informative limiting case. If the cold assembly is so large that \(C_c/C_h\) tends to infinity, its temperature remains effectively fixed, and the engine work reduces to \(C_h[(T_h-T_c)-T_c\ln(T_h/T_c)]\). This is the integral of the reversible work recovered as the finite hot block approaches a constant sink. It is smaller than applying the initial Carnot efficiency to all of the hot block’s released heat.

Chapter 4 · Thermodynamic cycles, refrigeration and phase change

Analyse a vapour-compression cycle from supplied state properties and compare its performance with a reversible bound.

Account for transfers over a complete cycle

A cyclic machine returns a working fluid to its initial state. Its internal energy and entropy changes over one complete cycle are zero. Heat and shaft work can nevertheless have nonzero net values. For an engine receiving \(Q_h\), rejecting positive magnitude \(Q_c\) and delivering work \(W_{\rm out}\), the first law gives \(W_{\rm out}=Q_h-Q_c\). Thermal efficiency is \(W_{\rm out}/Q_h\). A refrigerator instead consumes work to remove heat from a cold space. Its useful effect is cooling, so its performance measure is \(\mathrm{COP}_R=Q_c/W_{\rm in}\).

Between reservoirs at absolute temperatures \(T_h,T_c\) a reversible cycle transports equal entropy \(Q_h/T_h=Q_c/T_c\). Combining this condition with the first law gives \(\eta_{\rm rev}=1-T_c/T_h\) for an engine and \(\mathrm{COP}_{R,\rm rev}=T_c/(T_h-T_c)\) for a refrigerator. The latter can exceed one because cooling duty includes transported heat. A heat pump uses \(Q_h\) as useful output and has \(\mathrm{COP}_{HP}=\mathrm{COP}_R+1\) for the same cycle.

Follow the refrigerant once around the loop

Label the compressor inlet 1, discharge 2, condenser outlet 3 and expansion-valve outlet 4. Assume steady operation, negligible kinetic and elevation changes and insulated compressor and valve. The evaporator absorbs \(q_c=h_1-h_4\) per kilogram, the compressor consumes \(w=h_2-h_1\), the condenser rejects \(q_h=h_2-h_3\) and throttling preserves enthalpy \(h_4=h_3\). Enter enthalpies from a consistent refrigerant table or supplied state data. A constant ideal-gas heat capacity cannot represent latent heat across a two-phase region.

At saturation a mixture at quality \(x\) has \(h=(1-x)h_f+xh_g\), where \(x\) is vapour mass fraction and \(h_f,h_g\) are saturated liquid and vapour enthalpies at the same pressure. A throttle can create vapour as the pressure falls even though no external heat enters the valve. The mixture redistributes its enthalpy between phases at the new saturation temperature.

Determine cooling, power and flash fraction

For an illustrative refrigerant take enthalpies \(h_1=390\), \(h_2=430\), \(h_3=h_4=250\) kJ/kg. At evaporator pressure the saturated values are \(h_f=200,\ h_g=390\) kJ/kg. With mass flow 0.060 kg/s, the evaporator removes \(0.060(390-250)=8.40\) kW, the compressor needs 2.40 kW and the condenser rejects 10.80 kW. The powers satisfy \(8.40+2.40=10.80\) kW, and cooling COP is 3.50. The valve outlet quality is \((250-200)/(390-200)=0.263\): just over a quarter of the mass is already vapour when it enters the evaporator.

Suppose that at the actual discharge pressure an isentropic compression would give \(h_{2s}=420\) kJ/kg. The compressor isentropic efficiency is \((h_{2s}-h_1)/(h_2-h_1)=30/40=0.75\). Losses raise the required shaft power and increase condenser duty at unchanged evaporator inlet and outlet conditions.

Distinguish a property assumption from a bound

If heat is removed from a 268 K reservoir and rejected to a 308 K reservoir, the reversible refrigeration COP is \(268/(308-268)=6.70\). A measured COP of 3.50 lies below that limit. Real heat exchangers need finite temperature differences, compressors generate entropy and valves destroy expansion work. Reducing the required temperature lift improves the reversible limit. The working fluid’s actual pressures and enthalpies must be redetermined to estimate a real COP change. A cycle sketch and a numbered state table prevent accidentally using a compressor outlet temperature as the external hot-reservoir temperature in the bound.

Follow the energy after the compressor stops

Consider a cold cabinet held at constant temperature by a refrigerator. If it receives a 100 W heat leak and a 25 W electric fan dissipates power inside it, the evaporator must remove 125 W in steady operation. At cooling COP 2.5 the compressor draws 50 W, and the condenser releases \(125+50=175\) W into the room. If both the condenser and the cabinet exterior exchange heat with that room, its net thermal gain from the refrigerator system is \(175-100=75\) W. This equals the total electrical input to fan and compressor. Boundary selection explains why the condenser duty and the net heating of the entire room have different values.

Derive a gas-turbine efficiency from pressure ratio

A gas turbine running on a closed idealised Brayton cycle provides a useful counterpart to a refrigerating cycle. Model compression and expansion as isentropic ideal-gas processes with constant heat capacities. Heat is supplied and rejected at constant pressure. Let \(T_1\) be compressor inlet, \(T_2\) compressor outlet, \(T_3\) turbine inlet and \(T_4\) exhaust, and set \(r_p=p_2/p_1=p_3/p_4\). For \(a=(\gamma-1)/\gamma\), the isentropic relations give \(T_2/T_1=T_3/T_4=r_p^a\). The heat quantities per kilogram are \(q_{\rm in}=c_p(T_3-T_2)\) and \(q_{\rm out}=c_p(T_4-T_1)\).

Rewriting the latter difference as \((T_3-T_2)/r_p^a\) yields the ideal thermal efficiency \(1-r_p^{-a}\). This simple rise with pressure ratio does not mean that available shaft work rises indefinitely for a fixed permissible turbine inlet temperature. Net specific work divided by \(c_p\) is \(T_3(1-1/y)-T_1(y-1)\) with \(y=r_p^a\). Differentiating gives \(T_3/y^2-T_1=0\), so maximum work at fixed end temperatures occurs at \(y=\sqrt{T_3/T_1}\). When compression heats the gas all the way to \(T_3\), external heat input and net output both vanish in the ideal model.

Calculate the fraction of turbine output consumed by compression

Let \(T_1=300\,\mathrm K,\ T_3=1200\,\mathrm K,\ r_p=8,\ \gamma=1.4\), and \(c_p=1.005\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\). Then \(y=1.811\), \(T_2=543.4\,\mathrm K\), and \(T_4=662.5\,\mathrm K\). Compressor work is 244.7 kJ/kg, turbine work is 540.2 kJ/kg and net output is about 295.6 kJ/kg. The back-work ratio is \(244.7/540.2=0.453\), and the thermal efficiency is 44.8%. The ideal maximum-work pressure ratio would be \(2^{1/a}=11.31\) for these temperature limits.

Now specify compressor isentropic efficiency 0.82 and turbine efficiency 0.87 at the same pressure ratio and inlet temperatures. Actual compressor outlet is \(300+(543.4-300)/0.82=596.9\,\mathrm K\) and actual turbine outlet is \(1200-0.87(1200-662.5)=732.3\,\mathrm K\). The net work becomes \(1.005[(1200-732.3)-(596.9-300)]=171.7\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Heat supplied is \(1.005(1200-596.9)=606.1\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving efficiency 28.3%. A relatively small fractional degradation in each machine causes a larger fractional drop in net power because compression consumes a large portion of gross turbine work. If a recuperator is considered, the actual exhaust being hotter than the compressor discharge offers a possible heat-recovery driving temperature difference.

The actual exhaust and compressor temperatures also quantify the limit of regeneration. If both streams have the same heat capacity rate and an idealised recuperator has effectiveness 0.70, the compressed stream can gain \(0.70(732.3-596.9)=94.8\) K before entering the external heater. Its required heat input falls by \(1.005(94.8)=95.3\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), to about 511 kJ/kg. If turbine and compressor pressure ratios, inlet temperatures and work remain unchanged, thermal efficiency rises to \(171.7/511=33.6\%\). Check both exchanger terminal temperatures to ensure heat still flows in the intended direction. Pressure drop in an actual recuperator can reduce net work, while the lower fuel requirement might still justify the component. A recuperator cannot improve the ideal cycle at an operating condition where turbine exhaust is already colder than compressor delivery.

Counterflow heat exchange here transfers energy internally, so the cycle energy account includes the reduced external heating requirement and leaves the recuperator transfer out of its net external balance.

Chapter 5 · Steady conduction, thermal resistance and insulation

Derive the heat leak through a cylindrical wall and identify when increasing insulation thickness can increase heat transfer.

Turn heat flux into a temperature field

Energy moves through a stationary homogeneous solid down its temperature gradient. For isotropic conduction Fourier’s law is \(\mathbf q=-k\nabla T\), where \(\mathbf q\) is heat flux in W/m² and \(k\) is thermal conductivity. A steady one-dimensional wall with no internal heat generation passes the same heat rate at every cut. Heat flux is uniform only when the area of the cuts is uniform. That distinction matters for pipes and spherical shells.

Let an inner cylindrical surface at radius \(r_i\) be warmer than the outer one at \(r_o\). Assume a long concentric wall of length \(L\), constant \(k\), negligible end loss and axisymmetry. Fourier’s law gives \(\dot Q=-2\pi rLk\,dT/dr\), with outward rate taken positive. The area grows with radius, so the magnitude of \(dT/dr\) decreases outward even though the heat rate is unchanged.

Derive and combine thermal resistances

Separate and integrate over the cylindrical wall. This yields \(T_i-T_o=\dot Q\ln(r_o/r_i)/(2\pi k L)\). The cylindrical conduction resistance is the factor multiplying \(\dot Q\). At an outer convection boundary \(\dot Q=h\,2\pi r_oL(T_o-T_\infty)\), giving another resistance between the surface and the bulk surroundings. For a prescribed inner surface temperature,

\[\dot Q=\frac{T_i-T_\infty}{\ln(r_o/r_i)/(2\pi kL)+1/(2\pi r_oLh)}.\]

For multiple concentric layers, add the conduction resistance of each. The drops across series resistances sum because the same rate passes through them all. Poor contact between layers can add another temperature jump. A parallel heat leak bypassing the insulation carries part of the total heat under a common temperature difference, so its conductance adds instead.

Estimate the heat leak from a warm pipe

Take \(L=1.0\) m, \(r_i=0.020\) m and \(r_o=0.040\) m. The insulation has \(k=0.050\) W/(m K) and the surroundings have \(h=10\) W/(m² K), \(T_\infty=293\) K. The inner insulation surface is held at 353 K. The conduction resistance is \(\ln2/[2\pi(0.050)(1)]=2.206\) K/W, and the outer film resistance is \(1/[10(2\pi)(0.040)(1)]=0.398\) K/W. Total heat loss is \(60/(2.206+0.398)=23.0\) W per metre of pipe.

The outer surface is \(293+(23.0)(0.398)=302.2\) K. The rest of the 60 K difference is within the insulation. The heat flux at the outer surface is half that at the inner surface, because the heat-transfer area is twice as large. Assigning the outer circumference to the entire wall in a flat-wall formula would miss this variation. When thickness is very small relative to radius, expanding the logarithm recovers the plane-wall approximation.

Explain the critical radius

Increasing \(r_o\) makes the conduction resistance grow but makes the convection resistance shrink. At fixed \(T_i,T_\infty,h,k\), differentiating the total resistance gives \(dR/dr_o=1/(2\pi Lk r_o)-1/(2\pi Lh r_o^2)\). Its minimum occurs at \(r_c=k/h\); that radius maximises heat loss. Here \(r_c=0.0050\) m, already below the bare pipe radius, so adding this insulation reduces loss from the outset. A much thinner wire could initially lose more heat because its exposed area increases faster than the wall resistance. This conclusion assumes the same external coefficient as the diameter changes. If natural convection or radiation changes appreciably, the boundary model and its temperature dependence must be reconsidered.

Recover an unknown contact resistance

A 0.010 m² test sandwich passes 8 W at steady state between measured outer face temperatures of 50 and 40 °C. Its measured total resistance is \(10/8=1.25\,\mathrm{K/W}\). The two known solid layers together account for 0.75 K/W, leaving 0.50 K/W attributable to the interface if other parasitic paths are small. The area-normalised contact resistance is \(R''=A R=0.0050\,\mathrm{m^2K/W}\), corresponding to conductance \(1/R''=200\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\). A pair of thermocouples on opposite sides of the interface should then show a 4 K temperature difference. That independent measurement helps distinguish contact resistance from an unaccounted external heat loss.

Derive the temperature field along a cooling fin

A fin adds area for convection but must first conduct heat from its base to that area. Treat a straight fin with constant cross-sectional area \(A_c\), exposed perimeter \(P\), conductivity \(k\) and uniform film coefficient \(h\). Its base is held at \(T_b\) and surrounding air at \(T_\infty\). Assume the temperature is nearly uniform across each fin section; a small transverse Biot number supports this approximation. Take the tip as adiabatic, appropriate when tip area is small or insulated. Define excess temperature \(\theta=T-T_\infty\).

A slice \(dx\) loses heat \(hP\theta\,dx\) to convection. Its decrease in axial heat rate must supply that loss. Fourier’s law \(Q_x=-kA_c\,d\theta/dx\) then yields \(\theta''-m^2\theta=0\), where \(m=\sqrt{hP/(kA_c)}\). The boundary conditions are \(\theta(0)=\theta_b,\ \theta'(L)=0\). Using a hyperbolic cosine referenced to the insulated end gives

\[\theta(x)=\theta_b\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)},\qquad Q_b=\sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b\tanh(mL).\]

If the whole fin stayed at base temperature, heat removal would be \(hPL\theta_b\). The ratio \(\eta_f=\tanh(mL)/(mL)\) is the fin efficiency under the stated tip model. A very long fin can have low efficiency although it removes more heat than a short one; efficiency measures use of installed area, while total heat removal measures cooling duty.

Decide whether doubling fin length is worthwhile

An aluminium fin has rectangular section 20 mm by 2 mm, length 50 mm and conductivity 180 W/(m K). Air at 25 °C flows over it with \(h=35\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\), and the base is at 75 °C. Thus \(A_c=4.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2},\ P=0.044\,\mathrm m,\ \theta_b=50\,\mathrm K\). The transverse length \(A_c/P=0.909\,\mathrm{mm}\) gives \(hA_c/(kP)=0.000177\), consistent with small cross-sectional gradients. The fin parameter is \(m=\sqrt{35(0.044)/[180(4\times10^{-5})]}=14.6\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\); hence \(mL=0.731\) and \(\tanh(mL)=0.624\).

The base heat rate is \(\sqrt{35(0.044)(180)(4\times10^{-5})}(50)(0.624)=3.29\,\mathrm W\). Fin efficiency is \(0.624/0.731=0.854\). Doubling length makes \(mL=1.46\) and raises heat removal to about \(5.27\tanh(1.46)=4.73\,\mathrm W\). Twice the material therefore gives only 44% more cooling. The original covered base area by itself would convect \(35(4\times10^{-5})(50)=0.070\,\mathrm W\), so the fin still offers a large improvement. Adding several fins can change the film coefficient through crowding, and finite contact resistance can lower the effective base temperature. Those effects must be checked before multiplying a single-fin heat rate by the number installed.

A surface thermometer offers a second way to test the heat-transfer model. The insulated-tip solution gives \(T(L)=25+50/\cosh(0.731)=64.1^\circ\mathrm C\). If a thin thermocouple instead measures nearly 75 °C all along a fin while the base still sheds only the predicted few watts, the effective film coefficient may be smaller than assumed. A much colder measured tip could indicate strong local tip convection or poor base contact. Interpret a profile together with the base heat rate: several combinations of conductance, base temperature and film conditions can reproduce one measured temperature, but they need not reproduce both the profile and the total cooling duty.

The root of the fin offers a particularly direct simultaneous measurement of axial temperature gradient and supplied heat rate.

Chapter 6 · Thermal diffusion and transient surface cooling

Use diffusion time, Biot number and penetration depth to choose between a uniform-body model and a spatial temperature field.

Allow energy to accumulate in the material

A surface thermocouple responds rapidly to a cold wash, while the interior of a thick part can remain hot. To predict the time needed to cool the centre, steady thermal resistance alone is insufficient. Enclose a differential slice of material. Incoming conductive heat minus outgoing heat plus local generation equals the rate of change of its stored energy. Substituting Fourier’s law in a homogeneous stationary solid gives

\[\rho c_p\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=k\nabla^2T+\dot q_v,\qquad \alpha=\frac{k}{\rho c_p}.\]

Thermal diffusivity \(\alpha\) in m²/s compares a material’s ability to spread heat with its ability to store it. A large conductivity does not alone guarantee fast adjustment if volumetric heat capacity is also large. Without heat generation, a length \(L\) gives a characteristic diffusion time \(t_d=L^2/\alpha\). The dimensionless Fourier number \(Fo=\alpha t/L^2\) measures how long diffusion has acted relative to that scale.

Decide whether the interior stays nearly uniform

At a convecting boundary \(-k\partial T/\partial n=h(T_s-T_\infty)\). Comparing the resistance of the interior with a fluid film leads to \(Bi=hL_c/k\), often using \(L_c=V/A_s\) for a lumped model. If this ratio is small, commonly below about 0.1 as a first engineering check, the internal temperature difference is small compared with that across the film. Integrating energy over a uniform body then gives \(\rho c_p V\,dT/dt=-hA_s(T-T_\infty)\) and \((T-T_\infty)/(T_0-T_\infty)=e^{-t/\tau}\), with \(\tau=\rho c_pV/(hA_s)\).

The Biot and Fourier numbers answer different questions. A small Fourier number says a disturbance has penetrated only a short distance compared with \(L\). A small Biot number says the boundary transfers heat slowly enough for internal gradients to remain small. Choose the length for each comparison explicitly, especially for a slab cooled on one side rather than both.

Solve a sudden prescribed-temperature change

Consider initially uniform material occupying \(x>0\) whose surface is suddenly held at \(T_s\), with its far interior still at \(T_i\). Neglect internal generation and use constant properties. The initial and boundary conditions have no geometric length scale. Try the similarity coordinate \(\eta=x/(2\sqrt{\alpha t})\) and set \(\Theta=(T-T_s)/(T_i-T_s)\). Differentiating and substituting into the diffusion equation yields \(\Theta''+2\eta\Theta'=0\), with derivatives in \(\eta\). Integrating once gives \(\Theta'=C e^{-\eta^2}\). The limits \(\Theta(0)=0,\ \Theta(\infty)=1\) give \(\Theta=\operatorname{erf}(\eta)\).

At \(x=0\) the imposed surface temperature holds exactly. At a fixed depth and very early time \(\eta\) is large, so the temperature tends to its initial value. The distance of any fixed fractional temperature change grows as \(\sqrt{t}\). Quadrupling exposure time doubles that distance, a scaling quite different from a body travelling at a constant speed.

Predict depth and validate the time window

A thick solid initially at 373 K is held at 293 K at its surface. With \(\alpha=1.0\times10^{-5}\) m²/s and \(t=10\) s, \(2\sqrt{\alpha t}=0.020\) m. At depth 10 mm, \(\eta=0.50\) and \(\operatorname{erf}(0.50)=0.5205\), giving \(T=293+80(0.5205)=334.6\) K. At 20 mm, \(\operatorname{erf}(1)=0.8427\) gives 360.4 K. The surface has cooled by 80 K while the deeper sensor has cooled by only 12.6 K.

At 40 s the 20 mm sensor reaches the same fraction previously measured at 10 mm. This prediction assumes the remote boundary has not begun to influence the measurement and that the surface temperature is actually prescribed. A finite slab or a convective wash may require a finite-domain diffusion solution instead. Record surface and interior histories to distinguish uncertain boundary conditions from uncertain diffusivity.

Interpret a penetration depth

If two embedded sensors are at 2 and 6 mm in the same semi-infinite specimen, the deeper sensor reaches the same normalised temperature at nine times the shallow sensor’s time because \(x/\sqrt{\alpha t}\) must match. An observed factor much smaller than nine can indicate a heat leak along a probe or a far boundary that has begun to influence the temperature field.

Resolve a slab with a finite surface film

A prescribed surface temperature and a uniformly cooling lump are two useful limits. A finite slab exchanging heat through a finite film generally satisfies neither. Let \(L\) be half-thickness, \(x=0\) the midplane, and \(T_i\) the initial uniform temperature. Both sides suddenly see fluid at \(T_\infty\) with constant \(h\). Using \(X=x/L,\ Fo=\alpha t/L^2\) and \(\Theta=(T-T_\infty)/(T_i-T_\infty)\), the diffusion equation becomes \(\Theta_{Fo}=\Theta_{XX}\), with symmetry \(\Theta_X(0)=0\) and film condition \(-\Theta_X(1)=Bi\,\Theta(1)\), where \(Bi=hL/k\).

Seek a separated mode \(\Theta=\cos(\lambda X)e^{-\lambda^2Fo}\). Its derivative vanishes at the midplane. Imposing the convective surface condition gives \(\lambda\tan\lambda=Bi\). The uniform initial condition fixes modal amplitudes by cosine orthogonality on \(0<X<1\):

\[\Theta=\sum_n A_n\cos(\lambda_n X)e^{-\lambda_n^2Fo},\qquad A_n=\frac{4\sin\lambda_n}{2\lambda_n+\sin(2\lambda_n)}.\]

For \(Bi\ll1\), the smallest root has \(\lambda_1^2\simeq Bi\), its shape approaches a constant, and \(A_1\simeq1\). The decay becomes \(e^{-Bi\,Fo}\), exactly the lumped time scale because \(Bi\,Fo=h t/(\rho cL)\). For a large film coefficient the first root tends towards \(\pi/2\), corresponding to a prescribed surface temperature. The same boundary-value model thus recovers both limiting behaviours.

Predict when the core and surface meet different targets

A 40 mm thick flat slab with \(k=2.0\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\) and \(\alpha=1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) is cooled on both sides by \(h=50\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\). It starts at 100 °C in fluid at 20 °C. Here \(L=0.020\) m and \(Bi=0.50\). The first root of \(\lambda\tan\lambda=0.5\) is 0.6533 and \(A_1=1.070\). After 600 s, \(Fo=1.50\). The first term gives centre excess ratio \(1.070\exp[-(0.6533)^2(1.50)]=0.564\), hence \(T_c=65.1^\circ\mathrm C\). At the surface multiply by \(\cos(0.6533)=0.794\), giving about 55.9 °C. Higher roots are strongly attenuated at this Fourier number.

A process requiring the centre to fall below 50 °C needs centre ratio \((50-20)/(100-20)=0.375\). Solve \(Fo=\ln(1.070/0.375)/0.6533^2=2.46\), or approximately 984 s. By contrast a lumped estimate gives \(Fo=-\ln(0.375)/0.5=1.96\), about 785 s. It underestimates the core cooling time by over three minutes because the film and internal gradients both matter. An early-time surface prediction requires more modes than this late-time centre criterion. In particular the first term alone gives a centre ratio above unity at time zero, signalling that the remaining terms are necessary to reconstruct the original uniform state.

If product quality depends on average energy content rather than the hottest spot, average the first-mode shape over half the thickness. Its factor \(\int_0^1\cos(\lambda_1 X)dX=\sin\lambda_1/\lambda_1=0.930\) gives mean excess ratio \(0.564(0.930)=0.525\) after 600 s. The predicted bulk temperature is therefore about 62.0 °C, between centre and surface as expected. A calorimetric measurement made after removing and mixing the material is closer to the mean than to either point thermometer. A decision to stop cooling based on such a reading can therefore satisfy an average enthalpy requirement while leaving the centre several kelvins above a local temperature limit.

The surface flux can be checked against the rate of thermal energy loss. At 600 seconds each face transfers \(50(55.9-20)=1795\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\) to the air. Per square metre of plan area the slab therefore loses approximately 3590 W. The volumetric heat capacity obtained independently from \(k/\alpha\) is \(2.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{J/(m^3K)}\). Multiplying by total 0.040 m thickness gives an areal capacity of 80000 J/(m² K), and a predicted instantaneous mean cooling rate \(3590/80000=0.0449\,\mathrm{K/s}\). Differentiating the one-term mean temperature gives the same decay rate to within rounding.

This independent balance is especially useful if measurements suggest two different film coefficients on opposite sides. An unequal pair of measured fluxes cannot be fitted by a strictly symmetric half-slab model even if it reproduces one centre temperature. The full-thickness conduction problem then requires both surface boundary conditions; simply replacing them by their arithmetic mean may match total heat over one interval while shifting the hottest location away from the midplane.

Chapter 7 · Phase equilibrium and steam quality

Locate a two-phase state from two independent measurements and use mass fractions to find its volume and energy.

Temperature can stay fixed while heat enters

In an open pan at a fixed ambient pressure, liquid water begins to boil at a particular temperature. Additional heat can then convert liquid to vapour while the temperature remains close to that value. Specifying both saturation temperature and saturation pressure of a pure substance does not locate a unique mixture state. Along the coexistence curve they are linked, and the fraction of material in each phase supplies the missing information. Outside that curve pressure and temperature can usually be used as independent coordinates for a simple compressible substance.

Define quality \(x=m_g/(m_f+m_g)\) as the vapour mass fraction, using subscripts \(f\) for saturated liquid and \(g\) for saturated vapour. A property such as volume or enthalpy is additive over the two masses when interfacial contributions are negligible. Writing \(V=m_f v_f+m_gv_g\) and dividing by total mass gives the mixing relation. The same derivation applies to specific internal energy, enthalpy and entropy.

\[v=v_f+x(v_g-v_f),\qquad h=h_f+xh_{fg},\qquad h_{fg}=h_g-h_f .\]

Quality lies between zero and one in a saturated mixture. A calculated value beyond either endpoint signals a single-phase state or inconsistent measurements. Temperature does not interpolate: both phases in equilibrium have the same temperature.

Distinguish vapour mass from space occupied

A closed vessel contains 2.00 kg of saturated water and steam at approximately 100 kPa in 0.500 m³. Use the supplied rounded values \(v_f=0.00104,\ v_g=1.694\,\mathrm{m^3/kg}\), \(h_f=417.5,\ h_{fg}=2258\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The measured specific volume is 0.250 m³/kg. Quality follows as \((0.250-0.00104)/(1.694-0.00104)=0.1471\). Thus vapour mass is 0.294 kg while 1.706 kg remains liquid.

The vapour occupies \(0.2941(1.694)=0.4982\,\mathrm{m^3}\), almost the entire vessel. The liquid occupies roughly 0.0018 m³. Specific enthalpy is \(417.5+0.1471(2258)=749.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Specific internal energy follows from \(u=h-pv=749.6-100(0.250)=724.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) when using kPa and m³/kg. A level indicator measures occupied volume; using its reading directly as a mass fraction would give a large error.

Connect latent heat to the coexistence curve

For two pure phases in equilibrium their specific Gibbs free energies are equal. Along the coexistence line this equality persists, so \(dg_f=dg_g\). From \(dg=v\,dp-s\,dT\) one obtains \(dp_{\rm sat}/dT=(s_g-s_f)/(v_g-v_f)=h_{fg}/[T(v_g-v_f)]\). At about 373 K using the same values, the slope is \(2258/[373(1.693)]=3.58\,\mathrm{kPa/K}\). A small increase of boiling pressure by 3.6 kPa therefore raises saturation temperature by about one kelvin locally. Larger changes require integrating the changing properties or reading a saturation table rather than treating this local slope as a constant.

Predict flash after throttling

A saturated liquid with inlet enthalpy 500 kJ/kg passes through an insulated valve to the pressure of the drum above. With negligible work and velocity change, enthalpy remains 500 kJ/kg. The downstream quality is therefore \((500-417.5)/2258=0.0365\). Only about 3.7% of the water flashes by mass, but the large specific volume of the vapour can make its occupied volume dominate the downstream pipe.

Trace a saturated mixture along a constant-volume path

The drum example determines one state. If a sealed rigid vessel is heated, its pressure and temperature change together along saturation while its specific volume remains fixed. Provided equilibrium is maintained and no other phase appears, the quality at every pressure is \(x=(v-v_f)/(v_g-v_f)\). The energy equation for the vessel is \(Q=\Delta U\) when it does no boundary or shaft work. Replacing internal-energy change by enthalpy change would add the spurious \(V\Delta p\) term of a rigid container.

Quality does not change solely because latent heat has entered. Differentiate \(v=(1-x)v_f+xv_g\) along the saturation curve at fixed \(v\). This gives

\[\frac{dx}{dT}=-\frac{(1-x)\,dv_f/dT+x\,dv_g/dT}{v_g-v_f}.\]

Saturated liquid usually expands as temperature rises while saturated vapour specific volume decreases over the ordinary water range. The sign therefore depends on the initial mixture and local property slopes. A vessel containing almost all liquid by volume can, under some conditions, follow the saturated-liquid boundary on heating rather than drying out. Determine the actual phase region from the property data after each change. When \(v\) lies outside the interval between \(v_f\) and \(v_g\), a saturated quality formula is no longer applicable.

Find how much energy changes phase in a sealed vessel

Take 1.00 kg of water in a rigid 0.300 m³ vessel. Use rounded saturation data at 100 kPa: \(v_f=0.00104,\ v_g=1.694\,\mathrm{m^3/kg},\ u_f=417.4,\ u_g=2506\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). At 200 kPa use \(v_f=0.00106,\ v_g=0.8857,\ u_f=504.5,\ u_g=2529\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The volume bounds contain 0.300 m³/kg at both pressures. Initial quality is \((0.300-0.00104)/(1.694-0.00104)=0.1766\); final quality is \((0.300-0.00106)/(0.8857-0.00106)=0.3379\). Thus about 0.161 kg additionally evaporates.

Mixture internal energies are \(417.4+0.1766(2506-417.4)=786.3\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) and \(504.5+0.3379(2529-504.5)=1188.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving heat input about 402 kJ. Merely multiplying evaporated mass by one latent heat would overlook the sensible energy changes in each phase and the variation of saturation properties. As an independent check, \(\Delta H=\Delta U+V\Delta p\) exceeds the required heat by \(0.300(200-100)=30.0\,\mathrm{kJ}\). If additional heating brings saturated vapour specific volume down to 0.300 m³/kg, the last liquid disappears. Beyond that state the fixed-volume path enters the superheated region and one must determine temperature using a single-phase equation of state or superheated-vapour data.

The vapour volume fraction in the second state is \(m x v_g/V=0.3379(0.8857)/0.300=0.998\) approximately. Although about one third of the mass is vapour, almost every part of the vessel volume is vapour-filled. A level probe that detects a very small liquid volume is consistent with the presence of substantial residual liquid mass. This has a direct consequence for rounding: replacing \(v_f\) by zero only gives a modest error in calculated quality here, but using that same approximation to predict the remaining liquid depth gives a nonsensical zero. Retain the finite liquid volume whenever the question concerns an interface height or the mass recovered through a bottom drain.

The units of the property table matter just as much as the phase labels when predicting a small liquid inventory.

Chapter 8 · Gas mixtures, humidity and condensation

Translate relative humidity into a water mass balance and determine how much condensate a cooling coil removes.

Choose a quantity that survives condensation

A ventilation duct can carry an unchanged mass flow of dry air while water evaporates into it or condenses out. It is therefore convenient to express water content and mixture energy per kilogram of dry air. Model the dry air and water vapour as ideal gases sharing a common temperature and total volume. The partial pressure of each gas is the pressure it would exert if it occupied this volume alone. Dalton’s law gives \(p=p_a+p_v\).

Relative humidity \(\phi=p_v/p_{\rm sat}(T)\) describes proximity to saturation at the current temperature. It is not the mass fraction of water. For humidity ratio \(w=m_v/m_a\), divide the two ideal-gas mass equations. The common temperature and volume cancel, leaving a ratio of molar masses multiplied by the partial-pressure ratio.

\[w=\frac{M_v}{M_a}\frac{p_v}{p-p_v}\simeq0.622\frac{p_v}{p-p_v},\qquad w_{\rm sat}(T)=0.622\frac{p_{\rm sat}(T)}{p-p_{\rm sat}(T)} .\]

Before any water condenses during cooling at constant total pressure, the humidity ratio and vapour partial pressure remain fixed. Relative humidity rises because saturation pressure falls. The dew point is the temperature at which saturation pressure equals that fixed vapour pressure. Once that point is crossed, further cooling can remove water.

Find water removal and the associated cooling duty

Air enters a coil at 30 °C, 100 kPa and 60% relative humidity, and leaves saturated at 12 °C. Use \(p_{\rm sat}(30)=4.24\) kPa and \(p_{\rm sat}(12)=1.40\) kPa. The inlet vapour partial pressure is 2.544 kPa, giving \(w_1=0.01624\) kg water per kg dry air. The outlet ratio is \(w_2=0.00883\). For dry-air flow 0.50 kg/s the condensate flow is \(0.50(0.01624-0.00883)=0.00371\,\mathrm{kg/s}\), about 13.3 kg/h.

Using a 0 °C liquid-water reference, approximate moist-air enthalpy per kilogram dry air by \(h=1.005T+w(2501+1.86T)\) kJ/kg dry air with \(T\) in °C. The duct streams carry about 71.7 and 34.3 kJ/kg dry air. Condensate leaving at 12 °C carries \(h_l\simeq4.18(12)=50.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Energy conservation gives heat removed per kilogram dry air as \(h_1-h_2-(w_1-w_2)h_l\), approximately 37.0 kJ/kg dry air, or 18.5 kW.

Separate humidity ratio from relative humidity

Suppose the outlet stream is reheated to 20 °C without adding water. Its vapour pressure remains 1.40 kPa at the same total pressure. Since \(p_{\rm sat}(20)=2.34\,\mathrm{kPa}\), the new relative humidity is 59.8%. Neither evaporating water nor drying the air was required during this reheat. A sensor reporting 60% at both the inlet and reheated outlet therefore describes two air streams containing rather different masses of water. The coil water balance, written on the dry-air basis, explains the apparent coincidence.

Bracket an unknown dew point

At the original inlet the vapour pressure was 2.544 kPa. Suppose a saturation table gives 2.487 kPa at 21 °C and 2.644 kPa at 22 °C. Linear interpolation over this short interval puts the dew point at \(21+(2.544-2.487)/(2.644-2.487)=21.36^\circ\mathrm C\). A coil surface warmer than this value cannot start condensing the entering air locally under the stated conditions.

Balance an adiabatic spray chamber

A direct evaporative cooler behaves differently from a refrigerated cooling coil. Water is added to an airstream, the dry-bulb temperature falls, and the humidity ratio increases. Assume steady operation, negligible shaft work and heat leakage, unchanged total pressure, and no liquid droplets carried out. Express enthalpy per kilogram dry air. The dry-air flow is conserved; added liquid flow per kilogram dry air equals \(w_2-w_1\). The resulting energy balance is

\[h_1+(w_2-w_1)h_{\ell,\rm in}=h_2.\]

Use the same enthalpy reference for liquid and moist air. If the added water has low liquid enthalpy on that reference, the moist-air enthalpy is approximately constant. Often the recirculated liquid is near the leaving-air temperature; the more accurate energy equation above can be used without that approximation. An ideal infinitely long saturated contactor has \(\phi_2=1\), but a finite cooler generally leaves unsaturated air. An outlet temperature or a contact effectiveness is needed in addition to the balances unless saturation is specified.

With constant heat capacities one may write \(h_2=c_{pa}T_2+w_2(h_{g0}+c_{pv}T_2)\). Substituting this form into the balance and grouping the unknown \(w_2\) shows explicitly which measured quantities are needed. The process cannot cool below the adiabatic saturation limit for the given inlet state and water supply merely by increasing the spray rate.

Check outlet humidity and water consumption

A cooler receives air at 35 °C and 100 kPa with inlet humidity ratio 0.0080 kg/kg dry air and dry-air flow 0.60 kg/s. Its measured outlet temperature is 23 °C and liquid makeup enters at 20 °C. Use \(h=1.005T+w(2501+1.86T)\) kJ/kg dry air and \(h_\ell=4.18T\) kJ/kg. The inlet carries \(1.005(35)+0.008[2501+1.86(35)]=55.70\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) dry air; liquid enters with \(h_\ell=83.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\).

The outlet equation is \(55.70+(w_2-0.0080)83.6=1.005(23)+w_2[2501+1.86(23)]\), giving \(w_2=0.01297\,\mathrm{kg/kg}\) dry air. Thus liquid consumption is \(0.60(0.01297-0.0080)=0.00298\,\mathrm{kg/s}\), or about 10.7 kg/h. The outlet vapour pressure is \(100(0.01297)/(0.622+0.01297)=2.04\,\mathrm{kPa}\). At a supplied saturation pressure of 2.81 kPa for 23 °C, this corresponds to about 73% relative humidity, consistent with a finite contact process.

Treating air as a dry sensible stream would suggest a cooling duty of roughly \(0.60(1.005)(35-23)=7.24\,\mathrm{kW}\) removed from the cabinet. In this adiabatic device most of the sensible decrease supplies evaporation inside the boundary; no corresponding cooling-coil duty exists. A downstream process sensitive to water content may therefore react very differently to evaporative cooling and cooling plus dehumidification, even when both report the same outlet dry-bulb temperature.

An independent check can be made by weighing the makeup reservoir over a stable ten-minute test. At the predicted consumption it should lose about \(10.7/6=1.78\) kg, provided no liquid is stored in the spray circuit during that interval. A greater measured loss together with the same outlet humidity suggests entrained droplets, drainage or leakage rather than necessarily more evaporation. A humidity probe downstream of an effective droplet separator and a separate liquid-drain measurement distinguish those paths and make the water balance close.

Chapter 9 · Useful work and exergy destruction

Evaluate the useful-work potential of a steady stream and distinguish energy conservation from the destruction of useful-work potential.

Specify the surroundings before rating a resource

A warm stream contains energy relative to a chosen reference. The amount that could become useful work depends on the surroundings available to receive heat. If stream and environment reach mutual thermal and mechanical equilibrium, there is no further physical work potential associated with their difference. This equilibrium is the physical dead state at \(T_0,p_0\). Exclude chemical reactions here and consider a steady flow with constant composition.

Imagine a reversible device that takes a stream from its current state to the dead state while interacting thermally only with the environment. The steady first law relates delivered work to changes in stream enthalpy, kinetic and potential energy, and heat. The reversible entropy balance gives heat received from the environment \(q_0=T_0(s_0-s)\). Eliminating that heat gives the specific flow exergy

\[\psi=(h-h_0)-T_0(s-s_0)+V^2/2+g(z-z_0).\]

Use consistent units for the kinetic and potential terms. The entropy contribution reduces the fraction of enthalpy that a reversible device can turn into useful work. At the dead state, with the same velocity and elevation as the reference, that work potential vanishes.

Cool a liquid to the environment

Take a stream of liquid water at approximately ambient pressure, 360 K, with negligible motion and elevation change. It flows at 0.20 kg/s towards a large environment at 300 K. Assume \(c_p=4.18\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) constant. Integration gives \(h-h_0=c_p(T-T_0)\) and \(s-s_0=c_p\ln(T/T_0)\). The stream’s specific physical exergy is therefore \(4.18[60-300\ln(360/300)]=22.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Its enthalpy difference is much larger, 250.8 kJ/kg.

Cooling all the way to 300 K makes at most \(0.20(22.2)=4.44\,\mathrm{kW}\) of useful work available, even though the stream releases 50.16 kW of heat. Direct heat exchange with the environment delivers no shaft work. The environment entropy gain is \(50.16/300=0.1672\,\mathrm{kW/K}\), while the stream loses \(0.20(4.18)\ln(360/300)=0.1524\,\mathrm{kW/K}\). The difference, about 0.0148 kW/K, is the entropy production rate. Multiplication by \(T_0\) gives the lost 4.44 kW of work potential.

Include heat crossing another temperature boundary

Heat \(Q\) supplied reversibly by a reservoir at a uniform boundary temperature \(T_b\) carries exergy \(Q(1-T_0/T_b)\). This follows by comparing its energy with the environmental heat required to discharge the associated entropy \(Q/T_b\). A heat input of 10 kW from a 600 K reservoir in a 300 K environment carries 5 kW of exergy. A transfer from a finite cooling stream has changing source temperature and requires integration, as in the water example. Irreversibility in the device destroys exergy at rate \(T_0\dot S_{\rm gen}\); directing heat to a useful secondary process can retain part of the exergy that direct cooling would destroy.

Include the work potential of pressure

For an ideal gas already at \(T_0\), an elevated pressure still gives physical flow exergy \(RT_0\ln(p/p_0)\). Air at 300 K and twice environmental pressure has about \(0.287(300)\ln2=59.7\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) of pressure-related work potential although its ideal-gas enthalpy equals that at the dead state.

Derive the least work needed to heat a finite stream

Heat pumps are commonly compared by heat delivered per unit of electrical work. A second-law comparison additionally accounts for the temperature at which heat is delivered. Consider a steady stream heated from \(T_i\) to \(T_o\) at nearly environmental pressure, with constant heat capacity rate \(C=\dot m c_p\). A reversible heat pump extracts heat from surroundings at \(T_0\) and delivers it through a temperature-matched sequence of infinitesimal condensers. For one increment \(dQ=C\,dT\) at stream temperature \(T\), the reversed Carnot relation gives \(dW_{\min}=dQ(1-T_0/T)\).

Integrating over the stream yields \(W_{\min}=C[(T_o-T_i)-T_0\ln(T_o/T_i)]\). This is the increase of its physical flow exergy, and is smaller than evaluating the Carnot work factor at the final high temperature for the whole heat duty. A device that condenses at a single temperature above the warmest outlet generates entropy during delivery to the colder portions. The result assumes that the source of heat is the environment at \(T_0\); a colder source would introduce an additional resource requirement.

For steady equipment receiving only electricity and environmental heat, with negligible parasitic flows, a meaningful second-law efficiency is the stream’s exergy increase divided by actual electrical input. Electric power is entirely work potential, whereas a kilowatt of heat near room temperature carries only a small fraction of a kilowatt of exergy.

Compare a heat pump with a resistance heater

Water flows at 0.10 kg/s from 293 K to 333 K in a 293 K environment. With \(c_p=4.18\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\), heat duty is \(0.418(40)=16.72\,\mathrm{kW}\). The exergy increase is \(0.418[40-293\ln(333/293)]=1.05\,\mathrm{kW}\) approximately. This relatively small figure reflects the modest average temperature lift. A well-insulated heat pump drawing 4.20 kW from the electrical supply has heating COP \(16.72/4.20=3.98\) and exergy efficiency about 25%. Its surroundings provide 12.52 kW of heat under the stated energy balance.

A resistance heater supplying the identical water service draws 16.72 kW when its losses to the room are negligible. It is nearly 100% efficient on the simple electrical-energy-to-heat basis, yet its exergy efficiency is only about 6.3%. For the heat pump the implied destruction of work potential is \(4.20-1.05=3.15\,\mathrm{kW}\); for resistance heating it is about 15.67 kW. Electricity used by a circulating pump, external heat leaks or a source below environmental temperature belongs in the appropriate device boundary before drawing a practical comparison. The heat-duty equality ensures that the machines are judged against the same delivered temperature service.

For the resistance case the entropy balance can be checked without using the exergy formula. Electrical work carries no entropy across the control surface, and assume no heat leakage. The stream increases its entropy at \(0.418\ln(333/293)=0.0535\,\mathrm{kW/K}\). Multiplying by the 293 K environment gives 15.7 kW destroyed, consistent with the difference between input work and stream exergy gain. This agreement depends on keeping the heater and heated stream in the same chosen boundary.

Chapter 10 · Steam power cycles and turbine efficiency

Close a Rankine cycle energy balance and predict how turbine irreversibility changes power, condenser duty and exhaust quality.

Follow a kilogram through the loop

A simple steam plant pumps condensed water to boiler pressure, heats it to steam, expands it through a turbine and condenses it at low pressure. Water is nearly incompressible in the pump, while vapour occupies a much greater specific volume during expansion. A reversible steady adiabatic device satisfies \(dh=v\,dp\) because \(Tds=dh-v\,dp\) and \(ds=0\). Pressure increase therefore costs little work per kilogram when the fluid is liquid. This is one reason for condensing the working fluid before pumping it.

Number the saturated liquid leaving the condenser as state 1, the pump outlet as 2, the turbine inlet as 3 and the exhaust as 4. Neglect changes in kinetic and potential energy. Using positive magnitudes for pump work input and turbine work output gives

\[w_p\simeq v_1(p_2-p_1),\quad w_t=h_3-h_4,\quad q_{\rm in}=h_3-h_2,\quad \eta_{\rm th}=(w_t-w_p)/q_{\rm in}.\]

The exhaust pressure is set by the low-pressure side. An ideal turbine exit at that pressure has \(s_{4s}=s_3\); a real adiabatic turbine delivers a smaller enthalpy drop. Its isentropic efficiency is \(\eta_t=(h_3-h_4)/(h_3-h_{4s})\).

Calculate an ideal and an actual expansion

Use illustrative rounded state data. Condenser pressure is 10 kPa, with \(h_1=191.8\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), \(v_1=0.00101\,\mathrm{m^3/kg}\), \(s_f=0.649,\ s_{fg}=7.50\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) and \(h_{fg}=2393\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Boiler pressure is 4000 kPa and superheated inlet properties are \(h_3=3214\,\mathrm{kJ/kg},s_3=6.77\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\). The ideal pump requires \(0.00101(4000-10)=4.03\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), so \(h_2=195.8\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\).

At the low pressure, the ideal exhaust quality is \(x_{4s}=(6.77-0.649)/7.50=0.816\), giving \(h_{4s}=191.8+0.816(2393)\simeq2145\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). With turbine efficiency 0.85 the actual exit enthalpy is \(3214-0.85(3214-2145)=2305\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The corresponding quality is \((2305-191.8)/2393=0.883\). At fixed inlet and exhaust pressure this less efficient turbine leaves more enthalpy in the exhaust, and therefore a higher quality inside the two-phase region.

Interpret the condenser and generator duties

The actual turbine delivers about 909 kJ/kg. Subtracting pump work leaves approximately 905 kJ/kg net shaft work. Boiler heat input is \(3214-195.8=3018\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving thermal efficiency about 30.0%. The condenser rejects \(2305-191.8=2113\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), which closes the cycle energy balance within the rounding of the property data. At 2.0 kg/s, the idealised net mechanical output is about 1.81 MW before generator and auxiliary losses.

A lower condenser pressure generally increases the available expansion work for a fixed turbine inlet. It can also lower exhaust quality in the ideal expansion, increasing liquid droplet loading on the later blades. Evaluating such a change requires new saturation properties and a fresh state calculation. Treating the exhaust as an ideal gas would omit the phase fraction precisely where it affects both the energy balance and turbine operating requirements.

Estimate the consequence of a pump loss

If the pump’s isentropic efficiency falls to 0.70, actual input rises from 4.03 to \(4.03/0.70=5.76\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The turbine work remains the same under the specified inlet and outlet assumptions. Boiler duty falls by 1.73 kJ/kg because its feed arrives with more enthalpy. Both effects should enter the revised efficiency, even though pump work is small compared with the turbine output.

Derive the extraction fraction of an open feedwater heater

In a regenerative steam cycle, part of the flow is withdrawn from the turbine to preheat the boiler feedwater. This raises the average temperature of external heat input but reduces the mass continuing through the low-pressure turbine. Draw the control volumes per kilogram entering the boiler. Let fraction \(y\) be extracted at intermediate pressure and enthalpy \(h_b\), and let the remaining \(1-y\) pass to the condenser. A first pump returns the condensed fraction to the extraction pressure at enthalpy \(h_c\). In an insulated open heater the streams mix at the same pressure to a single liquid outlet of enthalpy \(h_f\).

Mass and energy balances reduce to \(y h_b+(1-y)h_c=h_f\), hence \(y=(h_f-h_c)/(h_b-h_c)\). This fraction must lie between zero and one for passive mixing at the stated states. A second pump then raises the full boiler flow to boiler pressure. If turbine inlet and final exhaust enthalpies are \(h_3,h_4\), work per kilogram admitted to the boiler is

\[w_t=(h_3-h_b)+(1-y)(h_b-h_4),\qquad w_p=(1-y)w_{p1}+w_{p2}.\]

The first stage always carries the whole flow, as does the second pump. Only the low-pressure turbine, condenser and first pump carry the reduced mass. Applying the factor \(1-y\) to the entire turbine drop is a common bookkeeping mistake. Actual component efficiencies can be included through the enthalpies used in the same balances.

Quantify the exchange between specific work and thermal efficiency

Use an illustrative cycle with \(h_3=3300,\ h_b=2780,\ h_4=2300\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Condensate leaves at 192.0 kJ/kg and requires \(w_{p1}=0.5\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) to reach the heater pressure, so \(h_c=192.5\). Mixed feedwater leaves at \(h_f=640\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) and the second pump requires 5.0 kJ/kg. The extraction fraction is \((640-192.5)/(2780-192.5)=0.1730\). About 83% of the boiler flow therefore reaches the low-pressure exhaust.

Turbine work is \(520+0.8270(480)=917.0\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Pump input is \(0.8270(0.5)+5=5.41\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving net 911.5 kJ/kg. Boiler input is \(3300-(640+5)=2655\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), or 34.3% thermal efficiency. Condenser duty, calculated independently from the remaining stream, is \(0.8270(2300-192)=1743.3\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), matching the difference between heat input and net work after rounding.

As a simplified comparison, let all steam expand to the same exhaust state and let a single pump require 5.5 kJ/kg with no regeneration. The appropriate selection depends on which physical resource sets the constraint.

For a boiler limited to 10 MW of heat supply, the regenerative cycle can process \(10000/2655=3.77\,\mathrm{kg/s}\) of boiler flow and supply about 3.43 MW net. Without extraction the same heat limit permits only \(10000/3102.5=3.22\,\mathrm{kg/s}\), yielding approximately 3.21 MW net. These power outputs reverse the ranking of work per kilogram because the limiting quantity has changed from mass flow to heat input.

Chapter 11 · Heat-exchanger effectiveness and finite capacity rates

Derive the counterflow effectiveness relation and determine outlet temperatures when neither stream temperature is fixed.

Set the maximum possible heat rate

In a two-stream heat exchanger, the hot temperature falls as the cold temperature rises. Treat each stream as single phase with constant heat capacity rate \(C=\dot m c_p\) in watts per kelvin. Neglect heat leakage, axial conduction and changes in kinetic energy. A steady overall balance gives \(Q=C_h(T_{hi}-T_{ho})=C_c(T_{co}-T_{ci})\). Performing this balance alone leaves both outlet temperatures and the transferred heat rate unknown.

The stream with smaller capacity rate would undergo the larger temperature change for a given heat rate. Even in an ideal counterflow exchanger it cannot pass the inlet temperature of the other stream at its nearest end. Thus \(Q_{\max}=C_{\min}(T_{hi}-T_{ci})\). Effectiveness is \(\varepsilon=Q/Q_{\max}\), which immediately gives both outlet temperatures once the heat transfer law fixes \(\varepsilon\).

Apply a balance to an area element

Let \(A\) increase along the hot stream and write the local temperature difference \(\Delta=T_h-T_c\). An element transfers \(dQ=U\Delta\,dA\). In counterflow both temperature profiles decrease when followed in the hot-stream direction, giving \(dT_h=-dQ/C_h\), \(dT_c=-dQ/C_c\). Hence \(d\ln\Delta/dA=U(1/C_c-1/C_h)\). Integrate over area and express each end difference in terms of inlet temperatures and total heat rate using the stream balances.

\[NTU=UA/C_{\min},\quad C_r=C_{\min}/C_{\max},\quad \varepsilon=\frac{1-\exp[-NTU(1-C_r)]}{1-C_r\exp[-NTU(1-C_r)]}.\]

The sign in the differential balance follows from the chosen spatial direction. In parallel flow the cold stream warms along that coordinate and the local temperature difference decays more rapidly. For equal capacity rates the counterflow formula has a removable zero over zero. Its limit is \(NTU/(1+NTU)\), which can also be obtained from the constant temperature difference in that special case.

Predict both outlet temperatures

A counterflow cooler receives a hot stream at 90 °C with \(C_h=2.0\,\mathrm{kW/K}\) and a cold stream at 20 °C with \(C_c=1.0\,\mathrm{kW/K}\). Its conductance \(UA\) is 1.50 kW/K. Thus \(C_r=0.5\), \(NTU=1.5\), and the exponential is \(e^{-0.75}=0.4724\). Effectiveness is 0.6908. Consequently \(Q=0.6908(1.0)(90-20)=48.4\,\mathrm{kW}\), \(T_{ho}=90-48.4/2=65.8^\circ\mathrm C\) and \(T_{co}=20+48.4=68.4^\circ\mathrm C\).

The cold outlet is warmer than the hot outlet. They are at opposite physical ends, so this does not imply a local reversal of heat flow. The end differences are \(90-68.4=21.6\) K and \(65.8-20=45.8\) K, both positive. Their logarithmic mean is approximately \((45.8-21.6)/\ln(45.8/21.6)=32.2\,\mathrm K\), independently recovering \(Q=UA\Delta T_{\rm lm}\simeq48.3\,\mathrm{kW}\). This local check catches errors caused by swapping the terminal temperatures of a counterflow exchanger.

A smaller stream capacity rate raises NTU when the exchanger conductance is held fixed, yet it can reduce heat transfer in watts because less thermal capacity passes each second. For inlet temperatures 350 and 290 K and \(C_r=0.60\), use the counterflow expression to predict the effectiveness at NTU 1.5 and 3.0. Identify which side controls an exchanger’s conductance

The effectiveness relation uses a single conductance \(UA\), but a designer may need to determine whether the hot film, cold film or fouling dominates it. Write the total thermal resistance in K/W as \(R_{\rm tot}=1/(h_hA_h)+R_w+R_f+1/(h_cA_c)\). Areas must be kept explicit if the two sides use different reference areas. Suppose a test can vary hot-side flow while holding cold-side flow, wall resistance and deposit condition fixed. Let a supported correlation in the tested region imply \(h_h\propto\dot m_h^{\,n}\). With \(m_r=\dot m_h/\dot m_{\rm ref}\), the relation takes the linear form

\[(UA)^{-1}=R_{\rm fixed}+R_{h,\rm ref}\,m_r^{-n}.\]

This is the basis of a Wilson-plot style separation. Each measured \(UA\) can be obtained from balanced heat duty and the correct logarithmic mean temperature difference. The intercept estimates the combined fixed resistance; the slope estimates the hot-film resistance at reference flow. One set of measurements alone does not separate cold film from wall and deposits individually. Temperature-dependent material properties and changes in the opposite stream can spoil the assumed linear relation.

Treating \(UA\) as proportional to flow whenever convection rises would ignore the series resistances left in its path. As \(m_r\) becomes large the hot-film term tends to zero, leaving an asymptote \(1/R_{\rm fixed}\) rather than unbounded conductance. Pumping power typically rises over this same change of flow.

Use a flow sweep to distinguish fouling from film resistance

A clean counterflow rig at reference hot-side mass flow has \(UA=500\,\mathrm{W/K}\), giving total resistance \(0.00200\,\mathrm{K/W}\). At twice the flow it has \(UA=600\,\mathrm{W/K}\), or \(0.001667\,\mathrm{K/W}\). Over this interval use measured exponent \(n=0.80\), so \(2^{-0.8}=0.5743\). Subtracting the two equations gives \(R_{h,\rm ref}=(0.00200-0.001667)/(1-0.5743)=0.000783\,\mathrm{K/W}\). The combined fixed resistance is \(0.001217\,\mathrm{K/W}\). Even an ideal hot film could raise conductance only to about 822 W/K under the unchanged fixed-side assumptions.

A later test at reference flow gives \(UA=400\,\mathrm{W/K}\), so total resistance has grown to 0.00250 K/W. If a second flow sweep confirms the same hot-film slope, the added fixed resistance is 0.00050 K/W. For a heat-transfer area of 2.0 m² on the chosen basis this corresponds to fouling resistance \(R_f''=0.0010\,\mathrm{m^2K/W}\). If instead the slope has changed, assigning the entire difference to deposits is unwarranted; changed flow distribution or fluid properties may contribute. Temperature measurement also matters strongly when the two terminal differences are close to sensor uncertainty. Compare the hot and cold enthalpy duties before fitting the conductance and retain the residual across all measured flow settings.

A third clean test can challenge the fitted exponent. At four times reference hot flow, the predicted resistance is \(0.001217+0.000783(4^{-0.8})=0.001475\,\mathrm{K/W}\), or \(UA\simeq678\,\mathrm{W/K}\). A systematic departure that grows at high flow could reflect changing cold-side properties, thermocouple bias or failure of the hot-film correlation. Two calibration points always fit a two-parameter straight line; the independent third point actually tests the assumed dependence.

Chapter 12 · Thermal radiation and enclosure exchange

Use surface emission and reflected irradiation to derive the net heat exchange between parallel grey surfaces.

Separate what a surface emits from what leaves it

A hot surface transfers energy across a vacuum through electromagnetic radiation. A black surface emits total hemispherical power \(E_b=\sigma T^4\) per unit area, using absolute temperature. A diffuse grey opaque surface emits a fraction \(\varepsilon E_b\) and reflects a fraction of incident irradiation \(G\). Under the grey equilibrium-property assumptions, absorptivity equals emissivity and reflectivity is \(1-\varepsilon\). The total radiation leaving the surface, called radiosity, is then \(J=\varepsilon E_b+(1-\varepsilon)G\).

Net radiative heat flux is \(q''=J-G\). Eliminate the unknown irradiation using the radiosity relation. For a surface of area \(A\) and \(0<\varepsilon<1\), this gives \(Q=(E_b-J)/[(1-\varepsilon)/(A\varepsilon)]\). The denominator behaves like a resistance between the blackbody emission potential and the surface radiosity. It vanishes for an ideal black surface, whose outgoing radiation is set solely by its temperature.

Couple two large facing plates

Consider two large parallel diffuse grey plates of equal area with negligible edge losses. Each plate sees only the other, so \(G_1=J_2\) and \(G_2=J_1\). The net rate from 1 to 2 is \(A(J_1-J_2)\). Combine that space resistance \(1/A\) with the surface resistances of the two plates in series. After cancellation the net flux is

\[q''=\frac{\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)}{1/\varepsilon_1+1/\varepsilon_2-1}.\]

The fourth powers cannot generally be evaluated using Celsius temperatures, even though a simple temperature difference has the same magnitude in kelvins and degrees Celsius. For finite or angled surfaces, a view factor describes how much of one surface’s outgoing radiation reaches the other. Equal area alone does not imply a view factor of one.

Add a freely floating radiation shield

Take \(T_1=500\,\mathrm K\), \(T_2=300\,\mathrm K\), both plate emissivities 0.80 and \(\sigma=5.670\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W/(m^2 K^4)}\). The blackbody difference is \(3084\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\). Division by \(1/0.8+1/0.8-1=1.5\) gives \(2056\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\). Insert an opaque thin shield with emissivity 0.10 on both faces and negligible temperature drop through its thickness. There are now two radiation gaps, each with denominator \(1/0.8+1/0.1-1=10.25\).

At steady state the shield exchanges equal heat flux with both plates and stores no energy. With equal gap denominators it follows that \(T_s^4=(T_1^4+T_2^4)/2\), so \(T_s\simeq433\,\mathrm K\). The total resistance denominator is 20.5 and heat flux drops to about 150 W/m². The floating shield temperature is higher than the arithmetic average of the plate temperatures. Solid supports connecting the shield to either plate would add conductive paths and change its temperature and the total heat leak. This explains why a low measured radiative load alone may not determine the performance of an insulated assembly.

Compare radiation with a convection coefficient

For black exchange, factor \(T_1^4-T_2^4=(T_1-T_2)(T_1+T_2)(T_1^2+T_2^2)\). For the original grey plates the corresponding effective coefficient is \(2056/(500-300)=10.28\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\). It can be compared directly with a convective coefficient for the same temperature difference, but its value changes when either absolute temperature changes.

Combine view-factor reciprocity with grey-surface resistances

Two large parallel plates have equal area and see one another completely. For opposite-sized diffuse grey surfaces in a two-surface enclosure, exchange through the intervening space depends on view factor \(F_{12}\), the fraction of energy leaving surface 1 that directly reaches surface 2. Reciprocity requires \(A_1F_{12}=A_2F_{21}\). A convex inner body entirely surrounded by a shell has \(F_{12}=1\); radiation leaving the larger concave shell need not all reach the inner body because some hits the shell again.

Net transfer through the space is \(Q=A_1F_{12}(J_1-J_2)\), with radiosities \(J_1,J_2\). Combine this relation with both surface resistance expressions and eliminate the radiosities. For uniform isothermal surfaces that can each be represented by one radiosity, the result is

\[Q=\frac{\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)}{(1-\varepsilon_1)/(A_1\varepsilon_1)+1/(A_1F_{12})+(1-\varepsilon_2)/(A_2\varepsilon_2)} .\]

For concentric long cylinders with negligible ends \(F_{12}=1\) and \(A_1/A_2=r_1/r_2\). The rate per inner area simplifies to \(\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)/[1/\varepsilon_1+(A_1/A_2)(1/\varepsilon_2-1)]\). As the outer area becomes very large its surface resistance has little effect on transfer per unit inner area. When the two areas approach equality the familiar parallel-surface denominator is recovered. This area weighting matters when a shiny outer enclosure surrounds a relatively small part.

Separate radiation and support conduction in a vacuum vessel

A long heated cylinder has outer radius 10 mm and active length 0.50 m, concentric with a cylindrical vacuum jacket of inner radius 40 mm. Take \(T_1=400\,\mathrm K,\ T_2=300\,\mathrm K,\ \varepsilon_1=0.70,\ \varepsilon_2=0.20\), and ignore end exchange. Areas satisfy \(A_1/A_2=0.25\), and \(A_1=2\pi(0.010)(0.50)=0.03142\,\mathrm{m^2}\). The effective denominator is \(1/0.70+0.25(1/0.20-1)=2.429\). The blackbody flux difference is \(5.670\times10^{-8}(400^4-300^4)=992.3\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\), giving radiative loss \(992.3(0.03142)/2.429=12.8\,\mathrm W\).

Two solid supports each have area 4.0 mm², length 20 mm and mean conductivity 15 W/(m K). Conduction through both is \(2(15)(4.0\times10^{-6})(100)/0.020=0.60\,\mathrm W\), so holding the inner cylinder at its stated temperature requires approximately 13.4 W. A measured heater power of 15 W would leave about 1.6 W unaccounted for, potentially from end radiation, electrical leads, residual gas or emissivity uncertainty. If the jacket is blackened to \(\varepsilon_2=1\), the radiation rises to \(992.3(0.03142)(0.70)=21.8\,\mathrm W\). Improving absorption of the outer wall increases the heat loss from the inner component even though neither temperature changes.

An intercept test can distinguish temperature-dependent pathways more effectively than a single operating point. At a small excess \(\Delta T\) around 300 K, radiative loss varies approximately as \(4\sigma T^3 A_1\Delta T/[1/\varepsilon_1+(A_1/A_2)(1/\varepsilon_2-1)]\). The original cylinder has linearised radiative conductance about \(4(5.67\times10^{-8})(300)^3(0.03142)/2.429=0.0792\,\mathrm{W/K}\), compared with 0.0060 W/K for its two supports. At large temperature differences the effective radiation conductance rises with absolute temperature while ideal support conduction stays linear. Recording heater power at several balanced temperatures lets those trends be tested, provided the surface emissivities and support conductivity do not themselves vary significantly over the range.

A measurement with electrical power off also establishes any background sensor offset before estimating these comparatively small near-ambient conductances from a temperature difference.

Applied study

Compare the experimental runs

Follow energy through an open system

A heat exchanger recovers energy from a warm process stream to preheat an incoming cold stream. A turbine also cools a stream, yet delivers shaft work. A steady-flow control volume distinguishes the mechanisms. Neglect changes in kinetic and potential energy and let heat into the control volume and shaft work out be positive. For one inlet and one outlet the first law reads \(\dot Q-\dot W=\dot m(h_{\rm out}-h_{\rm in})\), where \(h\) is specific enthalpy. For an ideal gas with constant specific heat, or a liquid stream with constant specific heat and negligible pressure change, \(h_{\rm out}-h_{\rm in}=c_p(T_{\rm out}-T_{\rm in})\).

Draw a boundary around an insulated exchanger containing both streams. With no shaft work, any heat given up by the hot stream must appear in the cold stream. Define heat-capacity rates \(C_h=\dot m_hc_{p,h}\) and \(C_c=\dot m_cc_{p,c}\), measured in W/K. The positive rate of internal heat transfer is

\[\dot Q_{\rm ex}=C_h(T_{h,i}-T_{h,o})=C_c(T_{c,o}-T_{c,i}).\]

The smaller capacity rate limits the possible transfer to \(\dot Q_{\max}=C_{\min}(T_{h,i}-T_{c,i})\). Energy balance alone gives one equation for the two outlet temperatures. The missing information is how much surface and thermal conductance connect the streams.

Derive the counterflow temperature profile

Let \(x\) increase in the hot-stream direction. The cold stream enters at the other end. Per unit length, a strip of wall of width \(w\) transfers \(Uw(T_h-T_c)\). Assume steady operation, constant overall coefficient \(U\), no external losses and negligible axial conduction. A slice balance gives \(dT_h/dx=-Uw(T_h-T_c)/C_h\) and \(dT_c/dx=-Uw(T_h-T_c)/C_c\). Both slopes are negative in this coordinate. A cold parcel warms as it moves towards decreasing \(x\).

Take \(C_h=C_{\min}\), \(C_r=C_h/C_c\) and \(\mathrm{NTU}=UA/C_h\), with transfer area \(A=wL\). Subtract the slice balances. The local temperature difference at \(L\) divided by its value at zero is \(\exp[-\mathrm{NTU}(1-C_r)]\). Substitute the two stream balances above to eliminate the outlet temperatures. The resulting effectiveness is

\[\varepsilon=\frac{\dot Q_{\rm ex}}{\dot Q_{\max}}=\frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{NTU}(1-C_r)}}{1-C_r e^{-\mathrm{NTU}(1-C_r)}}.\]

With equal capacity rates the difference is constant along the wall, and the limiting effectiveness is \(\mathrm{NTU}/(1+\mathrm{NTU})\). With a vanishing area there is no exchange. These limits provide simple checks on the algebra.

Worked example · Two water channels

Use \(T_{h,i}=350\) K, \(T_{c,i}=290\) K, \(C_h=0.0054\) W/K and \(C_c=0.0090\) W/K. The wall and fluid films together give \(U=90\) W/(m² K) over \(A=0.045(0.002)=9.0\times10^{-5}\) m². Thus \(C_r=0.60\), \(\mathrm{NTU}=1.50\), and the exponential factor is \(e^{-0.60}=0.5488\). The effectiveness is 0.6727 and \(\dot Q_{\rm ex}=0.6727(0.0054)(60)=0.2180\) W.

The hot outlet is \(350-0.2180/0.0054=309.64\) K, and the cold outlet is \(290+0.2180/0.0090=314.22\) K. These outlets lie at opposite ends. The local hot-to-cold differences at the ends are \(350-314.22=35.78\) K and \(309.64-290=19.64\) K, both positive. For a body cooling in time against a reservoir, the same conductance enters a different energy balance. Study transient cooling of a solid.
