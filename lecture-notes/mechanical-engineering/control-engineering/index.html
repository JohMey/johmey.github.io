Chapters

  1. From a balance law to a measured plant

    Predict a transient from conservation of energy, then use measurements to decide whether a first-order model is credible.

  2. Transfer functions and connections between systems

    Build a model of a sensor-actuator chain from individual balances and determine which responses a measurement can reveal.

  3. Closed-loop poles, damping and performance

    Translate a time-domain requirement into acceptable poles and find when additional gain destabilises a higher-order plant.

  4. Frequency response, disturbance rejection and robustness

    A loop that tracks slowly changing commands may amplify a disturbance at another frequency. Work from the sinusoidal response to a defensible choice of bandwidth.

  5. States, reachability and estimation

    Two mechanisms can produce the same output at one instant and different outputs an instant later. Determine what the actuator can change and what a sensor history can reveal.

  6. Sampling, saturation and commissioning

    Carry a continuous-time design onto a sampled actuator, predict where its operating assumptions fail, and distinguish an observed final value from an equilibrium.

  7. Following poles as a controller gain changes

    Use the geometry of the characteristic equation to discover which transient specifications one adjustable gain can meet.

  8. Uncertainty envelopes and robust stability

    Turn a bounded discrepancy between plant and model into a frequency-by-frequency test of the feedback design.

  9. Optimal state feedback and the Riccati equation

    Choose how heavily to penalise tracking error and actuator effort, then derive the feedback gain that minimises that stated cost.

  10. Estimating hidden states from noisy measurements

    Propagate both the state estimate and its uncertainty, and derive how much of an unexpected sensor reading to trust.

  11. Interacting actuators, pairing and decoupling

    Predict how closing one loop changes the apparent gain of another, and identify a physically expensive actuator combination.

  12. Finite-horizon control with real actuator constraints

    Build a small predictive controller from a sampled model and determine when the requested target is physically reachable.

Applied study

Design feedback for a tracking requirement and examine how loop gain changes speed, offset and stability.

Turn a machine balance into a plant model

A speed controller drives a motor against viscous drag. The shaft has inertia \(J\), drag coefficient \(b\), angular speed \(\omega\) and commanded torque \(u\). With a constant opposing load torque \(d\), the balance is \(J\dot\omega=u-b\omega-d\). Without feedback a step in load changes the steady speed by \(-d/b\). An engineer who can measure speed can command more torque as soon as the speed falls.

Divide by \(b\). With output \(y=\omega\), time constant \(\tau=J/b\) and static gain \(K=1/b\), the unloaded plant becomes \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\). A heating element with finite heat capacity and heat loss has the same first-order form under constant material properties. Transfer functions or step-response measurements can therefore guide controller design before the full machine is available.

Choose the loop gain from a tracking requirement

A proportional controller applies \(u=K_p(r-y)\) for a reference \(r\). Substitution gives \(\tau\dot y+(1+KK_p)y=KK_pr\). If \(1+KK_p>0\), the closed loop settles with time constant \(\tau_{\rm cl}=\tau/(1+KK_p)\). Following a step from rest,

\[y(t)=\frac{KK_p}{1+KK_p}r\,(1-e^{-t/\tau_{\rm cl}}),\qquad e_\infty=\frac{r}{1+KK_p}.\]

The steady error supplies the command that balances the drag. For a normalised model with \(K=2\), \(\tau=20\) s, \(r=1\) and \(K_p=4\), the loop gain is 8. The output tends to \(8/9=0.8889\) with time constant 2.222 s. Keeping the steady error within 5% of the setpoint requires \(1/(1+2K_p)\le0.05\), hence \(K_p\ge9.5\). At the instant of a unit step this higher gain asks for 9.5 units of actuator effort. A drive limited to 5 units initially saturates, so the linear prediction for the rise time must be revised.

Reject a load disturbance with integral action

For the motor with a constant load, proportional control settles at \(y=(K_pr-d)/(b+K_p)\). Adding \(K_i\int (r-y)\,dt\) to the command lets the controller supply constant torque even at zero error. If the loop settles and the actuator has sufficient reserve, a bounded steady integrator state requires \(r-y=0\).

The extra state changes stability and damping. For the normalised plant and a PI controller, the closed-loop characteristic polynomial is \(\tau s^2+(1+KK_p)s+KK_i\). At \(K=2\), \(\tau=20\) s, \(K_p=4\) and \(K_i=1\) per second, its roots are \(-0.225\pm0.222i\) per second. Both decay, but a disturbance can now excite an oscillation. A gain that removes steady error can change the transient response substantially.

Problem class

A motor picks up a load

Take \(b=0.50\) N·m·s/rad, \(K_p=2.0\) N·m·s/rad and reference speed 10 rad/s. A 1 N·m load is added after the motor has settled. Find the two steady speeds and the torque an integral controller would need to restore 10 rad/s.

Balance torque before and after the load step

Before the load, \(y=20/2.5=8.0\) rad/s. After it, \(y=(20-1)/2.5=7.6\) rad/s. At the desired speed the drag is 5 N·m, so the restored steady state needs a total of 6 N·m from the motor. At zero error the integral term supplies that torque.

Design for critical damping

Keep the normalised plant and \(K_p=4\). Find the integral gain for a repeated pole. Predict the effect of doubling this integral gain on the pole locations.

Find the poles from the characteristic polynomial

Critical damping requires \((1+KK_p)^2-4\tau KK_i=0\), giving \(K_i=81/160=0.50625\) per second. The pole is \(s=-9/40=-0.225\) per second. Doubling \(K_i\) leaves the real part unchanged and gives an imaginary part of magnitude 0.225 per second. The poles now form a complex pair.
