Angular momentum and work in a rotor

Convert a change of swirl into shaft torque

A fluid element can gain energy in a rotating impeller even if its inlet and outlet are at the same elevation. The rotor exerts a torque that changes the angular momentum carried by the flow. Draw a fixed control volume around the rotor. In a steady, single-inlet, single-outlet approximation, with representative mass-flow-weighted tangential velocities \(V_{\theta1},V_{\theta2}\), the axial torque on the fluid is \(\dot m(r_2V_{\theta2}-r_1V_{\theta1})\). Positive tangential velocity points in the direction of shaft rotation.

Multiplying by shaft angular speed converts torque to power. Divide by the mass flow to obtain the Euler specific work. Writing blade speeds \(U_i=\omega r_i\) avoids confusion between the angular velocity of the hardware and the velocity components of the fluid.

\[w_E=U_2V_{\theta2}-U_1V_{\theta1},\qquad H_E=w_E/g .\]

A pump adding energy has positive \(w_E\) under this convention. A turbine removes energy from the fluid and develops shaft output corresponding to a decrease in the fluid’s angular momentum. A stationary guide vane may change swirl while doing no shaft work because its angular speed is zero.

Relate the blade angle to the exit flow

At each station absolute velocity is the vector sum of local blade velocity \(U\) and velocity \(W\) relative to the moving blade. The throughflow component of \(V\) equals that of \(W\) when the blade motion is purely tangential. For an ideal radial impeller with backward-curved blades, define outlet angle \(\beta_2\) from the backward tangent towards the radial throughflow. Then \(W_{\theta2}=-V_{r2}\cot\beta_2\) and \(V_{\theta2}=U_2-V_{r2}\cot\beta_2\). State this angle convention before reading a drawing; other conventions change the algebraic appearance.

Predict work and distinguish hydraulic delivery

Let the outer radius be 0.10 m, rotor speed 1800 rpm, outlet radial velocity 3.0 m/s, exit blade angle 30°, and inlet swirl zero. The tip speed is 18.85 m/s; the ideal absolute exit swirl is \(18.85-3\cot30^\circ=13.65\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Hence specific work is 257.4 J/kg and head \(257.4/9.81=26.24\,\mathrm m\). For water at 8.0 L/s the mass flow is about 8.0 kg/s. Rotor torque on the fluid is \(8(0.10)(13.65)=10.92\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), and work rate is 2.06 kW.

If measurements show an actual delivered head of 21 m at this flow, hydraulic output is \(8(9.81)(21)=1.65\,\mathrm{kW}\). The ratio of actual head to the ideal blade-following estimate is 0.800. It combines departures from the ideal exit triangle with hydraulic losses in this simplified comparison. Mechanical bearing loss would additionally enter a comparison with measured shaft input power. If inlet guide vanes add positive inlet swirl, the subtracted \(U_1V_{\theta1}\) term reduces specific work for the same exit conditions.

Connect the triangle to pump affinity

For geometrically similar operation at unchanged impeller diameter, a fixed ratio \(V_r/U\) holds the ideal outlet triangle shape constant. Flow then scales with shaft speed, head with speed squared and hydraulic power with speed cubed. A real installation with static lift need not move between similar operating points when speed changes.

Derive work from a jet in a moving bucket

A radial pump uses an impeller to add angular momentum to a confined flow. An impulse turbine reverses the sequence: a stationary nozzle first converts pressure head to a high-speed free jet, which transfers momentum to buckets at nearly ambient pressure. Let jet speed \(V_j\) be tangential at the runner and bucket speed \(U\) positive in that direction. Relative inlet speed is \(V_j-U\). Suppose the bucket turns this relative stream through angle \(\pi-\beta\) and its exit relative speed magnitude is \(k(V_j-U)\), with \(k\le1\) accounting for relative-flow loss. The exit tangential absolute speed is \(V_{\theta2}=U-k(V_j-U)\cos\beta\).

A succession of buckets intercepts the full continuous nozzle mass flow \(\dot m=\rho Q\), unlike one isolated vane travelling away from a jet. Neglect leakage, windage and mechanical losses. Tangential runner force is \(\dot m(V_j-V_{\theta2})\), hence shaft work per kilogram and hydraulic efficiency relative to incoming jet kinetic energy are

\[w=U(V_j-U)(1+k\cos\beta),\qquad \eta_j=\frac{2U(V_j-U)(1+k\cos\beta)}{V_j^2}.\]

Differentiating with respect to speed at fixed inlet jet and exit geometry gives \(U=V_j/2\) for maximum power. At zero bucket speed force can be large but shaft power is zero. At bucket speed equal to jet speed there is no relative inlet stream to turn and therefore no useful momentum transfer. These two limits expose an incorrectly drawn exit velocity triangle.

Compare torque at the design and off-design speeds

A laboratory nozzle delivers 12 L/s of water at 30 m/s to a runner of pitch radius 0.20 m. Adopt \(k=0.85\) and \(\beta=15^\circ\), so \(1+k\cos\beta=1.821\). At optimum speed \(U=15\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\omega=75\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), about 716 rpm. The runner force is \(12(30-15)(1.821)=328\,\mathrm N\), torque 65.6 N m, and power \(15(328)=4.92\,\mathrm{kW}\). The jet supplied \(12(30^2)/2=5.40\,\mathrm{kW}\), giving efficiency 91.0% under these idealised runner assumptions.

Load the shaft until bucket speed drops to 9 m/s. The force rises to \(12(21)(1.821)=459\,\mathrm N\) and torque rises to 91.8 N m, but power drops to \(9(459)=4.13\,\mathrm{kW}\). Increased measured torque therefore does not demonstrate improved conversion without the speed measurement. The exit tangential velocity becomes \(9-0.85(21)\cos15^\circ=-8.24\,\mathrm{m/s}\), indicating the bucket now reverses some absolute tangential momentum. Maintaining the assumed jet flow requires sufficient nozzle head independently of shaft loading; a restricted supply may change both jet velocity and flow rate during the test.

At ideal design speed the leaving water still has a radial or transverse velocity because \(\beta\) is nonzero. Its relative exit transverse component is \(k(V_j-U)\sin\beta=3.30\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The absolute tangential component is \(15-0.85(15)\cos15^\circ=2.69\,\mathrm{m/s}\), giving residual jet kinetic energy about 9.1 J/kg. The rest of the gap between inlet kinetic energy and shaft work includes dissipation associated with \(k<1\). It would be incorrect to attribute the entire efficiency shortfall to measured tangential exit velocity alone. Traversing velocity components or collecting the discharge separately provides a diagnostic of losses within the bucket.
