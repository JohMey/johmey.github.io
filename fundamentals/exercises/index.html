A common clock for cooling and reaction

A metal part cools in air at 20 °C. It starts at 68 °C and reaches 44 °C in 6 min. In a separate experiment, a well-mixed reversible reaction approaches an equilibrium concentration of 0.20 mol/L. Its measured concentration falls from 0.80 to 0.50 mol/L in 90 s. Assume constant parameters and a single exponential relaxation in each experiment.

What should you plot to make the two experiments fall on the same curve? Use the measurements to predict when the metal reaches 26 °C and when the concentration reaches 0.275 mol/L.

Work through the time and amplitude scales

Subtract the equilibrium value first. The temperature excess goes from 48 to 24 K and the concentration excess from 0.60 to 0.30 mol/L. Both measurements therefore give a half-life. Write \(y=(X-X_{\rm eq})/(X_0-X_{\rm eq})\) and \(s=t/t_{1/2}\). The two sets of points fall on \(y=2^{-s}\).

The target excesses are 6 K and 0.075 mol/L. Each is one eighth of its initial value, so three half-lives must pass. The cooling time is 18 min and the reaction time is 270 s. A later point far from the same curve would give you a reason to revisit the constant-parameter assumption.

A heated bar with a gap at the support

A straight steel bar of length 1.0 m and cross-section 100 mm² is fixed axially at its left end. Its right end lies 0.30 mm from a rigid stop. Take \(E=200\) GPa and \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{K}^{-1}\). The temperature rises uniformly by 50 K. Assume small strain, elastic behaviour and lateral support against buckling.

Sketch axial stress against temperature rise. When does the bar first touch the stop? Find the final displacement and the force on the stop. After heating, the part cools in air at its original temperature. Its excess temperature halves every six minutes. When does the bar lose contact, and what clearance opens six minutes later?

Determine the contact point and reaction

Before contact there is no axial force, and the right end moves by \(\alpha L\Delta T\). It closes the 0.30 mm gap at \(\Delta T=0.00030/(12\times10^{-6}\times1.0)=25\) K. From that point the end remains 0.30 mm from its initial location. The additional 25 K of heating produces a compressive strain relative to free expansion.

Taking tension as positive, \(\sigma=-E\alpha(50-25)=-60\) MPa. The graph is horizontal at zero up to 25 K, then falls with slope −2.4 MPa/K. At 50 K the bar pushes on the stop with a force of \(60\times10^6\times100\times10^{-6}=6000\) N. The stop unloads when the excess temperature returns to 25 K, after six minutes of cooling. Six minutes later the excess is 12.5 K and the bar extends freely by 0.150 mm, leaving a clearance of 0.150 mm.

Two systems with the same period

A rigid air column of length 1.0 m supports a longitudinal standing wave with half a wavelength between its ends. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. An ideal LC circuit contains a 10 µF capacitor. Choose the inductance so that the circuit oscillates at the acoustic frequency. If the air column is then doubled in length, what change to the inductance restores the match?

Match the frequencies and test the scaling

The standing wave has wavelength \(2L=2.0\) m, hence \(f=c/(2L)=170\) Hz. In the circuit, charge obeys \(\ddot q+q/(\mathcal L C)=0\), so \(2\pi f=1/\sqrt{\mathcal L C}\). With \(C=10^{-5}\) F, the required inductance is \(\mathcal L=1/[(2\pi\cdot170)^2 C]=0.0876\) H.

Doubling the air-column length halves its frequency to 85 Hz. The inductance must increase fourfold to about 0.351 H. The air column sets a round-trip travel time through its length. The circuit sets an energy-exchange time through the square root of inductance times capacitance.
