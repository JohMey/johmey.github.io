Why does a discharging capacitor reverse polarity?

Follow the exchange of electric and magnetic energy and predict when the capacitor voltage changes sign.

Prerequisites Kirchhoff’s voltage law and second-order differential equations.

Follow the energy in a discharging loop

Connect a charged capacitor to an inductor and resistor in series, then leave the circuit without a source. As the capacitor empties, the current grows. When the capacitor first reaches zero voltage, the inductor still carries current. That current continues to move charge, giving the capacitor the opposite polarity.

Assume ideal, constant components and neglect propagation time along the wires. Let \(q\) be the charge on the plate that starts positive and define \(i=\dot q\). Discharge then gives a negative current in this convention. Summing the voltages round the loop gives \(L\dot i+Ri+q/C=0\), where \(L\) is inductance, \(R\) resistance and \(C\) capacitance. Multiplying by \(i\) gives the energy balance

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{Li^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{2C}\right)=-Ri^2.\]

The first term stores magnetic energy and the second stores electric energy. The resistor dissipates power whenever current flows. Reversing the sign of the charge is therefore compatible with a decrease in total stored energy.

Find the condition for a reversal

Substitute \(i=\dot q\) in the loop law to get \(L\ddot q+R\dot q+q/C=0\). Trying \(q=e^{st}\) gives \(s^2+2\alpha s+\omega_0^2=0\), with \(\alpha=R/(2L)\) and \(\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}\). If \(\alpha<\omega_0\), the roots are \(-\alpha\pm i\omega_d\), where \(\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\alpha^2}\). Charge oscillates as the amplitudes decay.

For an initial voltage \(V_0\) and zero current, \(q(0)=CV_0\) and \(\dot q(0)=0\). The cosine coefficient in the real solution is \(CV_0\). Enforcing the zero initial slope sets the sine coefficient, giving

\[\frac{q(t)}{CV_0}=e^{-\alpha t}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right).\]

Differentiating gives

\[i=-CV_0(\omega_0^2/\omega_d)e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega_dt).\]

The voltage reaches its first negative minimum when the current returns to zero at \(t=\pi/\omega_d\). At this instant the residual energy is back in the capacitor.

Further reading · RLC Series Circuits (OpenStax, University Physics II §14.6).

Predict the first reversal and the negative peak

Take \(L=10\) mH, \(C=100\) µF, \(R=2.0\) Ω and \(V_0=10.0\) V. The initial charge is \(1.00\) mC. The angular frequencies are \(\omega_0=1000\) rad/s and \(\omega_d=\sqrt{1000^2-100^2}=995.0\) rad/s, since \(\alpha=100\) s⁻¹.

To locate the polarity reversal, set \(q/C=0\). The exponential factor stays positive, so the bracket in the charge solution must vanish. This gives

\[\tan(\omega_dt)=-\omega_d/\alpha.\]

The first positive solution is in the second quadrant, at \(t=[\pi-\arctan(\omega_d/\alpha)]/\omega_d=1.679\) ms. The capacitor is momentarily empty and the inductor continues to drive current.

The current first returns to zero after \(\pi/995.0=3.157\) ms. Then

\[q/C=-10e^{-100(0.003157)}=-7.29\,\mathrm{V}.\]

A full period takes 6.315 ms. At that time the voltage is positive again, at \(+5.32\) V. The successive extrema show how much oscillation survives each cycle.

Tutorial exercises

Account for the missing energy

Calculate the heat released in the resistor between connection and the first negative peak. Explain how the answer can be found without knowing the entire current history.

Show the solution

At both endpoints \(i=0\), so the inductor stores zero energy. Initially \(CV_0^2/2=5.00\) mJ. At the first negative peak the capacitor holds \(C(7.29\,\mathrm V)^2/2=2.66\) mJ. The energy balance assigns the difference, 2.34 mJ, to \(\int Ri^2\,dt\). Squaring the voltage counts the energy of either polarity.

Choose a resistance for critical damping

Keep the original \(L\), \(C\), initial voltage and initial current. Find the boundary resistance at which oscillations disappear and determine the sign of \(q\) there.

Show the solution

The characteristic roots coincide at \(\alpha=\omega_0\), so

\[R_\mathrm{crit}=2\sqrt{L/C}=20.0\,\Omega.\]

The repeated root gives

\[q=(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}.\]

The initial conditions fix \(A=CV_0\), \(B=\omega_0CV_0\). Hence \(q=CV_0(1+\omega_0t)e^{-\omega_0t}\) stays positive and tends to zero as energy dissipates.
