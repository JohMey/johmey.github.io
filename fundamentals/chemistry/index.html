What sets a reaction’s equilibrium and how quickly does it approach it?

Use a species balance to distinguish the final composition of a reversible reaction from its rate of approach.

Prerequisites Species balances and first-order differential equations.

Count what the reaction conserves

A sealed vessel initially contains only A. Molecules of A convert to B, and B can convert back to A. If the composition stops changing, are molecules still reacting? Consider a well-mixed batch at fixed temperature and volume. Each conversion exchanges one molecule for one molecule. Write \(a\) and \(b\) for the concentrations in mol/m³.

Suppose each A molecule has the same probability per unit time \(k_f\) of converting, and each B molecule has reverse rate constant \(k_r\). Both constants have units s⁻¹. The numbers converting per unit volume and time are therefore \(k_f a\) and \(k_r b\). Counting the gains and losses gives

\[\dot a=-k_f a+k_r b,\]
\[\dot b=k_f a-k_r b.\]

Add the equations. Every term cancels, so \(a+b=c_T\) stays at its initial value. This conservation law follows from the one-to-one reaction and the closed, fixed-volume vessel.

Separate the endpoint from the approach

Use \(b=c_T-a\) to eliminate one unknown. Then \(\dot a=k_r c_T-(k_f+k_r)a\). At equilibrium \(\dot a=0\), which fixes \(a_* = k_r c_T/(k_f+k_r)\) and \(b_* = k_f c_T/(k_f+k_r)\). Both reactions continue at equal rates.

Subtract the equilibrium value by writing \(y=a-a_*\). The constant term cancels and \(\dot y=-(k_f+k_r)y\). Separating variables gives

\[a(t)=a_*+[a(0)-a_*]e^{-t/\tau},\]
\[\tau=\frac{1}{k_f+k_r}.\]

The ratio \(k_f/k_r=b_*/a_*\) fixes the final composition. The sum sets the relaxation time. After one \(\tau\), the remaining distance to equilibrium is \(1/e\) of its initial value.

How long until the mixture is close to equilibrium?

Take \(a(0)=1.00\) mol/m³, \(b(0)=0\), \(k_f=1.2\) s⁻¹ and \(k_r=0.4\) s⁻¹. Conservation fixes \(c_T=1.00\) mol/m³. The equilibrium split is \(a_*=0.25\), \(b_*=0.75\) mol/m³, and \(\tau=1/1.6=0.625\) s. At \(t=\tau\), \(a=0.25+0.75/e=0.526\) mol/m³.

To complete 95% of the change, set the remaining fraction to 0.05. Solving \(e^{-t/\tau}=0.05\) gives

\[t=\tau\ln 20=1.87\,\mathrm{s}.\]

At that moment \(a=0.2875\) mol/m³. Specifying a tolerance gives “time to equilibrium” a measurable meaning.

Tutorial exercises

Infer both rates from one experiment

A second vessel has equilibrium ratio \(b_*/a_*=4\). The distance from equilibrium halves in 2.00 s. Determine the two rate constants.

Show the solution

The half-time gives \(k_f+k_r=\ln 2/(2.00\,\mathrm s)=0.3466\) s⁻¹. Since \(k_f=4k_r\), \(5k_r=0.3466\) s⁻¹. Thus \(k_r=0.0693\) s⁻¹ and \(k_f=0.2773\) s⁻¹. Measuring only the final composition would leave their common scale undetermined.

Restart from the product side

Use the rates from the worked example. Fill the vessel with pure B at 1.00 mol/m³. Predict the initial slope of \(a\) and its concentration after one relaxation time.

Show the solution

Initially \(\dot a=k_r b=+0.40\) mol/(m³·s). At \(t=\tau\), \(a=0.25+(0-0.25)e^{-1}=0.158\) mol/m³. The sign of the initial slope reverses when preparing pure B. The total concentration and rates still select the same equilibrium composition.
