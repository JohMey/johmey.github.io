Project · Can two displacement sensors distinguish soft and stiff modes?

A pair of spring-mounted platforms forms a small inverse experiment. Use energy, conditioning, factorisation and modal residuals to decide what can be inferred from a measured displacement.

Problem sheet 7 · Integrative project

Assemble the stiffness from a physical experiment

Each platform is attached to a wall by a spring of stiffness \(k=1\) N/m. A coupling spring between platforms has stiffness \(c=49.5\) N/m. Start with the extensions of all three springs, write twice the energy and identify \(K\). Predict the two eigenvectors before computing its eigenvalues.

Work through the solution

The extension of the coupling is \(x_2-x_1\). Thus \(2U=k(x_1^2+x_2^2)+c(x_2-x_1)^2\) and the diagonal entries are \(k+c=50.5\), with off-diagonals \(-c=-49.5\). In-phase motion leaves the coupling untouched and has eigenvalue 1. Opposing motion stretches it and has eigenvalue 100. The 2-norm condition number is 100.

Solve one load and verify the result independently

Apply \(f=(1,0)\) N. Solve in modal coordinates, then calculate the elimination pivots and the diagonal of the Cholesky factor and recover the same displacements by a separate route. Which part of \(x\) is most difficult to infer from imperfect displacement measurements?

Work through the solution

The load is half the sum of \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\). Divide by their stiffnesses to get \(x=(0.505,0.495)\) m. The first pivot is 50.5, the second is

\[\det K/50.5=100/50.5=1.980198.\]

Eliminating the first row yields this second pivot with right-hand side \(49.5/50.5\). These elimination pivots are the diagonal of \(D\) in an \(LDL^T\) factorization. The positive-diagonal Cholesky factor has their square roots on its diagonal, about 7.106 and 1.407. Back substitution agrees. The difference of the displacements is only 0.01 m. Estimating the large coupling stiffness from that difference is sensitive to small sensor errors.

Change the energy and test the diagnosis

Divide \(K\) by 50.5 to give a matrix with unit diagonal. Enter the new off-diagonal into the chapter on positive pivots to the nearest slider value and compare the minimum across slices. Next use the scaling experiment with equal diagonal stiffnesses and the absolute coupling. Explain the residual slow direction.

Work through the solution

The normalised coupling is about \(-0.9802\). Its second pivot is \(1-0.9802^2\simeq0.0392\). The almost-flat valley runs along the in-phase motion. Flipping the sign of the second coordinate changes the sign of the coupling and preserves eigenvalues. Diagonal preconditioning cannot separate the scales because the diagonal terms are already equal; a basis that separates the two physical modes can.

Transfer the conclusion to a reported frequency

A unit-mass trial mode is tilted 3° away from the soft mode. Predict its Rayleigh quotient and the frequency bias. Would a two-decimal-place frequency match identify the trial shape?

Work through the solution

For eigenvalues 1 and 100 the quotient is \(1+99\sin^2(3^\circ)\simeq1.2712\) s⁻². The angular frequency is about 1.1275 rad/s, an error close to 12.8%. At an independently verified angular frequency of 1.00 rad/s to the nearest 0.01, this two-mode model requires \(1+99\sin^2\theta<1.005^2\), hence \(|\theta|<0.58^\circ\) up to the arbitrary eigenvector sign. The reading bounds the tilt but does not determine its value or sign. A smaller eigenvalue gap would weaken that bound.
