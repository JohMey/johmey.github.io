Chapter 1 · Conditioning and backward error

Distinguish an inaccurate measurement from an inaccurate solve by tracing how each changes the answer to a small equilibrium problem.

From first principles

Derive the relative error in a linear solve

Suppose measured data \(b\) determine unknowns through \(Ax=b\), with \(A\) square and nonsingular. Keep the coefficients fixed and change only the measured data by \(\delta b\). Subtracting the two equations removes the unperturbed solution exactly,

\[A(x+\delta x)-Ax=\delta b,\]
\[\delta x=A^{-1}\delta b .\]

Choose a vector norm and its induced matrix norm, defined as the largest stretch of a unit vector. By that definition \(\|\delta x\|\leq\|A^{-1}\|\|\delta b\|\), while \(\|b\|\leq\|A\|\|x\|\). For nonzero \(b\) and \(x\), combine these two inequalities to express both errors as fractions,

\[\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|}\leq\|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}.\]

The product is the normwise condition number. It concerns the problem even when the solver is exact. For a computed solution one can instead insert its measured residual as the data perturbation. The norm bound allows the most sensitive possible orientation of that perturbation, so a particular residual can give a considerably smaller error.

Derivation and applications

Bound each recovered coordinate using instrument tolerances

Sensors often have different tolerances expressed in different physical units. A perturbation ball based on an overall norm discards this information. Assume \(A\) is known and invertible, and let the calibrated uncertainty of reading \(j\) be an independent interval \(|\delta b_j|\leq t_j\). Here independent means all sign combinations are admissible, without a probabilistic assumption. Writing the solution perturbation row by row gives

\[\delta x_i=\sum_j (A^{-1})_{ij}\delta b_j .\]

The triangle inequality provides an upper bound. It is also attainable for a specified coordinate: choose the perturbation signs to agree with the signs of that row. Thus the smallest symmetric enclosing interval for that coordinate is

\[\max_{|\delta b_j|\leq t_j}|\delta x_i|=\sum_j |(A^{-1})_{ij}|t_j=(|A^{-1}|t)_i .\]

One can evaluate the same quantity for a reported linear output \(z=c^Tx\) without forming the whole inverse. Solve one transposed system \(A^Tw=c\); then the output is \(w^Tb\) and its extreme uncertainty follows from the same sign argument,

\[|\delta z|_{\max}=|w|^Tt,\]
\[A^Tw=c .\]

The maximizing perturbations for different coordinates may conflict. A vector of separate coordinate bounds therefore describes an enclosing box; its corners need not be reachable simultaneously. Calibration information tying two errors to the same bias would change the admissible set and can substantially alter the bound.

Worked example

Bound a difference from two calibrated sensors

Take readings \(b_1=x_1+x_2\) and \(b_2=x_1-x_2\), with tolerances \(0.020\) and \(0.080\) in common units. Reconstructing \(x_2=(b_1-b_2)/2\) gives worst error \(0.050\), attained when the two readings move in opposite directions. For \(x_1+x_2=b_1\) the error bound is only \(0.020\). Adding the two individual coordinate bounds would yield \(0.100\) and miss their shared dependence on the data.

Second application

Choose the more effective recalibration

A second reconstruction reports \(z=0.1b_1+4b_2\), with tolerances \(0.50\) and \(0.050\). Its worst error is \(0.250\). Halving the tolerance of the first sensor reduces it to \(0.225\), while halving the second reduces it to \(0.150\). The second recalibration yields a fourfold larger reduction despite its smaller raw tolerance.

OneSimulation laboratory · Separate algebraic error from mesh error

Determine which perturbations a machine can represent

A computed residual is assembled from rounded operations. To interpret a small residual we need a scale for each rounding. In a normalized binary system with \(p\) significant bits, including the leading one, positive numbers in \([2^e,2^{e+1})\) have the form \(2^e(1+k\,2^{1-p})\). Increasing the integer \(k\) by one advances by \(2^{e+1-p}\). Thus absolute spacing grows with the size of the number. At a power of two, the neighbours on the two sides even have different spacings.

Rounding to nearest incurs at most half a spacing. Dividing by the smallest magnitude \(2^e\) in that interval gives the relative bound \(u=2^{-p}\), called unit roundoff. For a normal result of suitable magnitude one can write \(\operatorname{fl}(z)=z(1+\delta)\), \(|\delta|\leq u\). In binary64, \(p=53\). The distance from one to its larger neighbour is \(2^{-52}\), twice the unit roundoff. Subnormal numbers maintain a constant absolute spacing towards zero. The uniform relative estimate above does not extend through zero or to overflows.

Suppose an instrument adds the increments \(2^{-53}\) twice to a unit baseline. The exact result is \(1+2^{-52}\). With round-to-nearest, ties-to-even, \(1+2^{-53}\) is halfway between one and its successor. The lower endpoint has even trailing significand, so it rounds to one. Adding the second increment separately hits the same tie again. Grouping the increments first produces the representable \(2^{-52}\). Adding this to one then advances one spacing. Identical mathematical data can therefore yield different rounded sums.

Now take \(1+2^{-52}\) and subtract one. The difference \(2^{-52}\) is represented exactly, even though it is tiny relative to the original numbers. Cancellation becomes damaging when nearby operands already contain error relative to their difference. Recording the precision, order of accumulation and scale of the data lets us distinguish this effect from sensitivity of the underlying linear system.

Two questions about a reported solution

Suppose you determine two unknown forces by balancing their components in two directions. If the directions are almost parallel, the second measurement repeats much of the first. A machine can solve both written equations very accurately while the inferred forces vary considerably from trial to trial. To understand this, separate the map from data to physical answer from the algorithm used to evaluate that map. Conditioning describes the map locally near the supplied data. Stability describes the calculation that produced an answer from those data.

Let \(A\) be a nonsingular real matrix, let \(Ax=b\) and suppose that only the right-hand side changes. Subtract \(Ax=b\) from \(A(x+\delta x)=b+\delta b\). The result \(A\delta x=\delta b\) says that \(A^{-1}\) maps the measurement disturbance into the answer. In a consistent norm, \(\|\delta x\|\leq\|A^{-1}\|\|\delta b\|\). To compare percentages rather than units, use \(\|b\|\leq\|A\|\|x\|\) and divide through by \(\|x\|\). For nonzero \(b\) this gives

\[\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|}\leq\|A\|\|A^{-1}\|\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}.\]

The product is the condition number in the specified norm. Rotating the disturbance relative to the singular vectors changes the actual amplification, so a worst-case bound alone cannot tell which measurement caused trouble.

Resolve the sensitive direction

Consider \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1.001\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(b=(2,2.001)^T\). The exact answer is \((1,1)^T\). Subtract the first measurement from the second. The difference, 0.001, is exactly 0.001 times the second unknown. If the second measurement increases by 0.0001, its inferred value increases to 1.1. The first unknown must fall to 0.9 to preserve the first measurement. Neither individual equilibrium equation looks unstable by itself. It is their near redundancy that makes the difference sensitive.

The infinity norm makes the scale explicit. The largest absolute row sum of \(A\) is 2.001. Its inverse is \(1000\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1.001&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), whose largest row sum is 2001. The relative data change is \(0.0001/2.001\) while the relative solution change is 0.1. Multiplying the data change by the condition number gives about 0.200, a valid upper bound. Had the two measurements increased equally, their difference would have stayed fixed and the same amplification would not occur.

Ask which equations the machine solved

For a computed vector \(\hat x\), form \(r=b-A\hat x\). In exact arithmetic \(\hat x\) solves \(A\hat x=b-r\) exactly. Consequently \(\|r\|/\|b\|\) is a relative backward error when only \(b\) may change. In our example the vector \((0.9,1.1)^T\), tested against the original right-hand side, has a small residual in the second row. It is both an accurate answer to a nearby set of measurements and a poor estimate of the original forces.

If the rows carry different physical units, an unscaled residual can let one equation dominate the judgement. Scale each balance by a stated characteristic magnitude or report the residuals separately. Recompute them from the original data when possible. Feeding the same rounded intermediate quantities back into a consistency check may merely confirm the internal arithmetic. For a sensitive inverse map, improving the accuracy of the input or measuring an independent direction can accomplish more than tightening the solver tolerance.

Allow uncertainty in the coefficients

If directions as well as forces are measured, the perturbed equations are \((A+\delta A)(x+\delta x)=b+\delta b\). Keeping first-order terms gives \(A\delta x\simeq\delta b-\delta A\,x\). Uncertainty in a coefficient therefore contributes an effective disturbance proportional to the magnitude of the state it multiplies. Reporting only uncertainty in the measured right-hand side can underestimate the final error.

For a scalar calibration \(a x=b\), logarithmic differentiation gives \(\delta x/x\simeq\delta b/b-\delta a/a\). If both measurements are 1% too large with the same sign, their errors cancel exactly in the ratio. Opposite 1% changes give \(1.01/0.99-1\simeq2.02\%\). Componentwise measurement correlations affect typical errors even though a norm bound has to allow the worst admissible combination. In force reconstruction this is a reason to keep calibration information with the measured balances.

Compute a condition number for a particular observable

Suppose a linear solve is used only to predict \(q=c^Tx\), rather than every component of the state. Let \(A\) be fixed and nonsingular. Perturb the load by \(\delta b\); the output changes by \(c^TA^{-1}\delta b\). Solve the dual system \(A^Tz=c\). Then the exact change is \(z^T\delta b\), and Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(|\delta q|\le\|z\|_2\|\delta b\|_2\). Equality is possible by choosing a load error parallel to \(z\). The absolute condition number of this observable with respect to this load norm is consequently \(\|z\|_2\). It can be much smaller than the worst amplification over all outputs.

The error model matters to the result. If each source instead has its own bounded relative uncertainty \(|\delta b_i|\le\epsilon |b_i|\), the worst change is \(\epsilon\sum_i|z_i b_i|\). The bound is attained by choosing the signs of the perturbations to make every term reinforce the same output change. For independent mean-zero source errors with covariances collected in \(\Sigma\), the variance is \(z^T\Sigma z\), generally smaller than a worst-case bound. A worst-case condition number and a statistical standard deviation answer different questions even when evaluated using exactly the same dual solution.

Which of two coupled readings can be predicted?

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1.02\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and load \(b=(2,2.02)^T\). Subtracting the equations gives \(0.02x_2=0.02\), and back-substitution gives \(x=(1,1)^T\). For a prediction of the sum \(q_s=x_1+x_2\), the dual vector is \(z_s=(1,0)^T\): the first equation already reports that sum. For the difference \(q_d=x_1-x_2\), subtract twice the inferred \(x_2\) from the first equation. This gives \(q_d=101b_1-100b_2\), so \(z_d=(101,-100)^T\). These two physically simple outputs depend on the same pair of source readings in strikingly different ways.

With each reading uncertain by at most 0.001 in absolute units, the sum can change by at most 0.001. The difference can change by as much as 0.201 when the errors have opposite signs. With a single common offset of 0.001 in both readings, however, the difference changes by only 0.001 because the two large terms nearly cancel. The nominal difference is zero, so relative error in that output is undefined. An absolute tolerance or a physically chosen reference amplitude provides a meaningful accuracy requirement.

A designer considering improved load cells should therefore ask whether calibration drift is shared or independent and whether the useful output is a sum or a difference. Reporting just the matrix condition number gives no way to recover either piece of information. If the coefficients of \(A\) are uncertain as well, the effective right-hand side also contains the term \(-\delta A\,x\) to first order. Its direction can be entirely different from plausible measurement errors in \(b\).

A direct numerical perturbation illustrates the worst bounded case. Increasing \(b_1\) by 0.001 and decreasing \(b_2\) by 0.001 gives \(x_2=0.90\) and \(x_1=1.101\). The output difference has changed by 0.201 exactly, demonstrating that the sign choice in the componentwise bound is achievable.

Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,s)\) and take a unit residual \(r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). Compute the correction solving \(A e=r\). Its size depends on alignment even though the residual norm remains exactly one.

Chapter 2 · Elimination, pivoting and factorisation

Build an LU factorisation by hand, reuse it for a second load and identify when an innocent-looking elimination step loses useful digits.

From first principles

Make one coefficient vanish without changing the solution

Consider a pair of linear balances \(ax+by=f\) and \(cx+dy=g\). Every solution of both also satisfies their linear combinations. Conversely, adding a multiple of one equation to another is reversible by subtracting the same multiple. For \(a\ne0\), choose the multiplier \(m=c/a\) so that subtracting \(m\) times the first equation from the second removes \(x\),

\[(d-mb)y=g-mf,\]
\[x=(f-by)/a .\]

The same two operations can be stored as a matrix factorization. The lower triangle records the multiplier and the upper triangle records the remaining coefficients,

\[\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\m&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d-mb\end{pmatrix}.\]

The second pivot \(d-mb\) must also be nonzero for a unique solution. Further right-hand sides can reuse both factors because neither depends on \(f\) or \(g\). When the first pivot is zero one can try exchanging rows. Exchanging rows to obtain a larger pivot can also reduce rounding errors in the multiplier.

Derivation and applications

Eliminate a variable from a symmetric quadratic energy

For a symmetric stiffness matrix the elimination pivot has a mechanical meaning. Consider the quadratic energy of two displacement coordinates \(u,v\), with stiffnesses \(a,d\), coupling \(c\), and applied loads \(f,g\). Stationarity with respect to each displacement gives the two linear balance equations. Assume the first stiffness \(a>0\),

\[E(u,v)=\tfrac12 a u^2+cuv+\tfrac12 d v^2-fu-gv,\]
\[\partial_u E=au+cv-f .\]

At a prescribed \(v\) the energy is minimized at \(u=(f-cv)/a\). Complete the square in \(u\) before solving the remaining balance. The reduced energy retains both the coupling-induced stiffness change and the force transmitted from the eliminated coordinate,

\[E=\frac a2\left(u-\frac{f-cv}{a}\right)^2+\frac12\left(d-\frac{c^2}{a}\right)v^2-\left(g-\frac{cf}{a}\right)v-\frac{f^2}{2a}.\]

The second elimination pivot is the curvature \(s=d-c^2/a\). For a stable equilibrium with a unique response one needs \(s>0\). The same result follows by expanding the symmetric factorization

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\c/a&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&s\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&c/a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Small \(s\) means one combined motion changes the energy very little. A large resulting displacement can therefore be a property of the model even if the elimination is performed accurately.

Worked example

Compute the effective load after eliminating a support

Let \(a=4\), \(c=-2\), \(d=3\) in \(\mathrm{N/m}\), with loads \(f=2\) and \(g=1\,\mathrm N\). The retained stiffness is \(3-4/4=2\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Its effective load is \(1-(-2)(2)/4=2\,\mathrm N\), so \(v=1\,\mathrm m\) and \(u=(2+2)/4=1\,\mathrm m\). Substitution gives original loads \(4-2=2\) and \(-2+3=1\).

Second application

Diagnose loss of stability without computing eigenvalues

With \(a=9\), \(d=4\), and \(c=6\) the second pivot vanishes. The trial motion \(u=-2v/3\) has zero quadratic energy for every \(v\). Changing only \(c\) to \(6.3\) gives \(s=4-6.3^2/9=-0.41\). Minimizing over \(u\) at \(v=2\) and zero load now yields \(E=s v^2/2=-0.82\), demonstrating an unstable direction in the supposed stiffness model.

Eliminate an unknown without changing the solution

Gaussian elimination replaces one equation by itself minus a multiple of another. In exact arithmetic this is reversible: add the same multiple back. In the first column choose a nonzero pivot \(a_{11}\) and form multipliers \(l_{i1}=a_{i1}/a_{11}\) for the rows below it. Subtract \(l_{i1}\) times row one from row \(i\). All lower entries in column one vanish, and the remaining unknowns satisfy a smaller system. Applying the same operations in the same order to the load vector is essential.

Repeating the argument produces an upper triangular matrix \(U\). The inverse row additions can be collected into a unit lower triangular matrix \(L\) containing the multipliers. If rows have been permuted, the relation is \(PA=LU\), where \(P\) records those exchanges. To solve for a new right-hand side, first solve \(Ly=Pb\) by forward substitution, then \(Ux=y\) by backward substitution. The factorisation depends on the matrix alone. This is valuable in static analysis when the material and supports stay fixed and only the applied loads change.

Factor once and load twice

Consider \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1&0\\1&3&1\\0&1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Subtract half the first row from the second to get \((0,2.5,1)\). Subtract 0.4 times the new second row from the third to get \((0,0,1.6)\). Thus \(U\) has diagonal \(2,2.5,1.6\) with ones immediately above, and \(L\) has multipliers 0.5 and 0.4 immediately below its unit diagonal. This matrix needs no pivot exchange. Multiplying the two factors recovers the original entries and gives a quick arithmetic check.

For the load \(b=(1,2,3)^T\), forward substitution yields \(y=(1,1.5,2.4)^T\). Back substitution starts with \(x_3=2.4/1.6=1.5\), then gives \(x_2=(1.5-1.5)/2.5=0\) and \(x_1=(1-0)/2=0.5\). A second load \(b=(0,1,0)^T\) gives \(y=(0,1,-0.4)^T\) and \(x=(-0.25,0.5,-0.25)^T\). Both calculations are short once the triangular factors are available.

Eliminate a chain in two linear-time sweeps

In a chain of nearest-neighbour balances, row \(i\) contains only a left coefficient \(a_i\), diagonal \(d_i\), and right coefficient \(c_i\). Subtracting a multiple of the preceding, already reduced row removes the left coefficient and updates just one diagonal and one load. Write the reduced pivot and load as \(p_i,g_i\). The first pair is \(p_1=d_1,\ g_1=b_1\). Thereafter the multiplier, pivot and load satisfy

\[m_i=a_i/p_{i-1},\qquad p_i=d_i-m_ic_{i-1},\qquad g_i=b_i-m_ig_{i-1}.\]

The last equation gives \(x_n=g_n/p_n\). Proceed backwards with \(x_i=(g_i-c_ix_{i+1})/p_i\). This is the Thomas algorithm. Each row costs a fixed number of operations and only three coefficient diagonals need storage. On repeated load cases the pivots and multipliers depend only on the original coefficient matrix and can be retained. Symmetric positive definiteness guarantees positive pivots here. An arbitrary nonsingular tridiagonal matrix can still have a zero leading pivot and require pivoting.

A four-node diffusion chain

With conductance one between successive nodes and fixed end values 1 and 2, the four internal balances have diagonal two, neighbours minus one and load \((1,0,0,2)^T\). The reduced pivots are \(2,3/2,4/3,5/4\). The corresponding loads are \(1,1/2,1/3,9/4\). The last unknown is \((9/4)/(5/4)=9/5\). One step back gives \((1/3+9/5)/(4/3)=8/5\), then \(7/5\) and \(6/5\). Each of the five temperature differences is one fifth, as conservation without an interior source requires. A dense factorisation would return the same field but would obscure why a million-node chain is computationally tractable.

Why exchange rows?

Consider \(10^{-5}x+y=1\) and \(x+y=2\). The exact solution is \(x=1/(1-10^{-5})\), \(y=2-x\). Eliminating with the first coefficient gives multiplier \(10^5\). The reduced equation contains the difference between numbers of size \(10^5\). In arithmetic rounded to three significant digits, both the new coefficient and right-hand side round to \(-1.00\times10^5\). Back substitution gives \(y=1\), then \(x=0\). Substitution in the second original equation exposes a residual of one.

Swap the rows first. The multiplier is now \(10^{-5}\), and the reduced equation rounds to \(y=1.00\). Back substitution in the first pivot equation gives \(x=1.00\), close to the exact answer. Partial pivoting chooses the largest absolute available coefficient in the pivot column, bounding the magnitudes of the multipliers by one. It usually limits numerical growth, though it cannot remove sensitivity already present in the original matrix.

For a dense matrix of size \(n\), the triangular factorisation takes order \(n^3\) arithmetic operations and substitutions take order \(n^2\) per load. Sparse discretisations can be much cheaper, but eliminating an unknown may create new nonzero entries between its neighbours. This fill depends on ordering. Ordering for small fill and pivoting for accuracy answer different questions, and production solvers may need both.

Use symmetry when the physics supplies it

For a symmetric positive definite stiffness matrix, Cholesky factorisation represents \(A=CC^T\) with a lower triangular \(C\) and positive real diagonal. The first diagonal entry is \(\sqrt{a_{11}}\). Entries below it are fixed by the first column of the product, and subtracting that column’s outer product leaves a smaller positive definite Schur complement. Positivity follows by minimising the full quadratic energy over the eliminated variable for every fixed nonzero remaining vector.

For \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&2\\2&5\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), the first column of \(C\) is \((2,1)^T\). The remaining diagonal is \(\sqrt{5-1^2}=2\). A zero or negative candidate under the square root signals that the positive definite assumption or numerical precision needs inspection. Unrestrained mechanical assemblies can have rigid modes of zero energy. Supplying appropriate physical constraints addresses that singularity directly instead of treating it as a pivot-ordering problem.

Trace element growth during pivoted elimination

Partial pivoting chooses the largest available entry in magnitude within the active column. Each multiplier then has magnitude at most one, an important control on an individual elimination step. It does not bound the size of all updated entries by their original size. The trailing update subtracts a multiplier times the pivot row from another row, and two terms of similar magnitude may add. Repeated reinforcement can build large intermediate entries even when each column choice satisfies the pivot rule.

Measure this effect by the growth factor, the largest magnitude encountered during elimination divided by the largest initial matrix entry. Roundoff in a factorisation is tied to the magnitude of the computed factors, so a large growth factor can weaken a backward-error guarantee. The factor describes a potential loss mechanism. A particular solve can still be exact when every operation happens to be exactly representable, and a large forward error can arise for quite different reasons if the underlying problem is ill-conditioned. These distinctions matter when interpreting numerical experiments designed to stress an algorithm.

Construct a matrix where the final column doubles

Use the four rows \((1,0,0,1)\), \((-1,1,0,1)\), \((-1,-1,1,1)\), and \((-1,-1,-1,1)\). Every initial entry has magnitude at most one. Choose the first available row on tied pivots. Eliminating the first column adds row one to all three lower rows, so their final entries become 2. Eliminating column two adds the second active row to the next two rows, changing their final entries to 4. Eliminating column three adds the third pivot row to the last, producing the final pivot 8. No multiplier exceeded one. The triangular factor has last column \((1,2,4,8)^T\) and the observed growth factor is eight.

The construction extends directly to size \(n\), with an all-ones last column, unit diagonal before that column, and minus ones below the diagonal. After \(j\) stages each remaining row has last entry \(2^j\), as follows inductively by adding two equal entries during the next elimination. The resulting growth factor is \(2^{n-1}\) for this tie rule. Thus even a rule that prevents division by a small pivot relative to its column admits exponential worst-case growth. Selecting a different row on a tie can change the numerical path without changing the physical system.

For a concrete back-substitution test select exact state \({\bf1}\). Multiplication gives load \((2,1,0,-2)^T\). Applying the same row operations transforms it to \((2,3,5,8)^T\). Back substitution gives \(x_4=1\), \(x_3=5-4=1\), \(x_2=3-2=1\) and \(x_1=2-1=1\). Binary arithmetic represents these small integers exactly. A residual check therefore need not reveal anything unusual in this case even while the monitored growth factor exposes an unfavourable factorisation path.

The comparison with no growth is especially clear for a diagonal matrix with entries \((1,1,1,8)\). Its last pivot is also eight, but its largest original entry was already eight and its growth factor is one. A large absolute pivot alone cannot identify element growth; the ratio must refer to the size of the input.

Problem sheet 1 · Measurements and elimination

Use explicit perturbations, row exchanges and reusable factors to diagnose errors in small linear balances.

Two scales in a force measurement

A sensor system has \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,0.02)\) and reports \(b=(2,0.02)^T\). Find the exact unknowns and \(\kappa_2(A)\). Compare two additive disturbances of magnitude 0.002, one entirely in the first measurement and one entirely in the second. Finally, explain which sensor needs the tighter absolute calibration.

Worked solution

Division by each diagonal entry gives \(x=(1,1)^T\). The singular values are 2 and 0.02, so \(\kappa_2=100\). The first disturbance changes the solution by \((0.001,0)^T\), whereas the second gives \((0,0.1)^T\). The second direction therefore produces a hundred times more absolute force error for the same measurement error.

For equal admissible absolute errors in the two forces, the second measurement needs a hundred times smaller absolute uncertainty. Rescaling its output by a factor of 100 makes the two matrix rows comparable but also rescales its uncertainty by 100. It cannot turn the original sensor into a more precise one.

Compare a componentwise and a global test

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1000,1)\), \(b=(1000,1)^T\) and \(\hat x=(1,0.98)^T\). Compute the infinity-norm relative residual and the componentwise relative residual in each equation. If the instrumentation specification allows 0.5% relative error separately in each reading, does the approximation meet it?

Worked solution

The predicted measurements are \((1000,0.98)^T\), giving residual \((0,0.02)^T\). The global infinity-norm ratio is \(0.02/1000=2\times10^{-5}\). In the separate equations the ratios are zero and \(0.02/1=0.02\).

The second equation misses its 0.5% specification by a factor of four. Global normalisation uses the much larger first equation to set the denominator. One remedy is to divide each equation by its nonzero measurement uncertainty and judge the largest resulting scaled residual.

Pivot and reuse

Factor \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&2\\3&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) with a row permutation. Solve for \(b=(4,5)^T\) and for \(c=(2,1)^T\) using that factorisation. Why does the zero leading entry indicate neither singularity nor lack of a unique solution?

Worked solution

Exchange rows with \(P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). The permuted matrix is already upper triangular, so \(L=I\) and \(U=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). For \(Pb=(5,4)^T\), back substitution gives \(x_2=2\), \(x_1=1\). For \(Pc=(1,2)^T\), it gives \(x_2=1\), \(x_1=0\).

The determinant is \(-6\), so the matrix is nonsingular. A zero at one candidate pivot location merely prevents that ordering of elimination. Applying the row permutation to both the matrix and every new load preserves the original problem.

Reuse chain factors after a load change

A three-node chain has diagonal \((4,3,2)\), both off-diagonals equal to \(-1\), and load \((2,4,0)^T\). Compute its pivots and solution. Without refactoring, determine the extra response to a unit load on the last node. Explain which step fails for a nonsingular chain with first row \((0,1)\) and second row \((1,2)\).

Worked solution

The pivots are \(4,\ 3-1/4=11/4,\ 2-4/11=18/11\). The reduced loads are \(2,\ 4+2/4=9/2,\ (4/11)(9/2)=18/11\). Back substitution gives \(x_3=1,\ x_2=(9/2+1)/(11/4)=2,\ x_1=(2+2)/4=1\).

The incremental load transforms to \((0,0,1)\). Back substitution through the same factors gives increments \(11/18,\ 2/9,\ 1/18\) in reverse node order. They are positive throughout the connected chain. In the final matrix the first pivot is zero, although its determinant is −1. A row exchange can solve it. The unpivoted recurrence cannot divide by its first pivot.

Chapter 3 · Least squares and orthogonal factorisation

Fit a straight line to inconsistent observations, derive its residual conditions and explain why an orthogonal factorisation can preserve accuracy better than the normal equations.

From first principles

Derive the orthogonality condition for a fit

With more observations than unknown coefficients, the linear prediction \(Ax\) can miss the observed vector \(b\). Suppose squared residuals carry equal weight and choose coefficients to minimize \(\|b-Ax\|_2^2\). Try any change \(x\mapsto x+t d\), where the direction \(d\) is fixed but the scalar \(t\) can have either sign. Expand with \(r=b-Ax\),

\[\|r-tAd\|_2^2=\|r\|_2^2-2t\,d^TA^Tr+t^2\|Ad\|_2^2 .\]

At a minimum the linear term must vanish in every direction, so \(A^Tr=0\). This says the residual is perpendicular to every vector the model can generate. Rearranging gives the normal equations,

\[A^TAx=A^Tb .\]

If \(A\) has independent columns then the quadratic term is positive for every nonzero direction and the minimizer is unique. A thin QR factorization with orthonormal columns \(Q\) and triangular \(R\) yields the equivalent equation \(Rx=Q^Tb\) and avoids explicitly forming products that can worsen numerical conditioning.

Derivation and applications

Impose one exact calibration condition on a noisy fit

Some data describe noisy readings while another relationship is an exact conservation or calibration requirement. Model noisy data by \(Ax\simeq b\) with independent equal-variance errors, but require \(c^Tx=d\) exactly. Assume \(A\) has full column rank and \(c\ne0\). Write \(H=A^TA\), and let \(x_0\) denote the ordinary least-squares solution. Expanding around \(x_0\) removes the cross term because the residual there is orthogonal to every column of \(A\),

\[\|Ax-b\|^2=\|Ax_0-b\|^2+(x-x_0)^TH(x-x_0).\]

Thus the constrained fit is the closest feasible point in the metric determined by \(H\). Introduce a multiplier \(\lambda\) for the exact condition in half the squared objective. Setting its derivatives to zero and solving the first equation gives

\[H(x-x_0)+c\lambda=0,\]
\[x=x_0-H^{-1}c\lambda .\]

Applying the constraint determines the multiplier and the increase in squared residual,

\[\lambda=\frac{c^Tx_0-d}{c^TH^{-1}c},\]
\[\Delta\|r\|^2=\frac{(c^Tx_0-d)^2}{c^TH^{-1}c}.\]

The denominator is positive. In computation the expression \(H^{-1}c\) denotes a solve, preferably using existing QR factors. An already satisfied constraint leaves the fit unchanged. Directions weakly constrained by the measured data can accommodate an exact calibration with little increase in residual.

Worked example

Reconcile two fitted quantities with an exact total

An ordinary fit gives \(x_0=(2,5)^T\) with \(H=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)\). The correct total is \(x_1+x_2=6\). Now \(H^{-1}c=(0.25,1)^T\), the denominator is \(1.25\), and \(\lambda=0.8\). The adjusted pair is \((1.8,4.2)\). Its extra squared residual is \(4(0.2)^2+(0.8)^2=0.80\), with the less precisely measured second coordinate absorbing most of the correction.

Second application

Enforce a zero offset in a calibration line

Three equally weighted readings at \(t=0,1,2\) are \(1,3,5\). The free fit is \(1+2t\). A separate exact zero calibration demands an intercept of zero. Along that feasible line the slope obeys \((0^2+1^2+2^2)m=0+3+10\), giving \(m=2.6\). The three residuals \(1,0.4,-0.2\) have squared norm \(1.20\), a directly observable cost of the added calibration condition.

Replace exact consistency by an optimisation problem

Three thermometers at different positions need not lie on a common straight temperature profile. Let the rows of \(A\) contain the values of candidate basis functions at those positions and let \(b\) contain the observations. With more independent measurements than unknown coefficients, \(Ax=b\) may have no solution. A least-squares fit chooses \(x\) to minimise \(\|b-Ax\|_2^2\). Equal unweighted terms assume the deviations are comparable in units and importance. If one instrument is much noisier than another, that assumption deserves review before interpreting a fitted slope.

Take any coefficient perturbation \(sd\). Expanding the squared mismatch gives \(\|b-Ax\|^2-2s\,d^TA^T(b-Ax)+s^2\|Ad\|^2\). At a minimum the coefficient of \(s\) must vanish for every \(d\). Hence \(A^TAx=A^Tb\). This is also sufficient: when the linear term vanishes, every perturbation adds a nonnegative square. Geometrically the residual is perpendicular to the column space of \(A\). Full column rank makes the minimiser unique, since no nonzero coefficient perturbation then leaves the fitted values unchanged.

Fit three imperfect readings

Take positions \(t=-1,0,1\) and deviations from a baseline temperature of \(1,2,5\) K. Fit \(b(t)\simeq c+mt\), using a constant and a linear column. Their dot product is zero because the positions sum to zero. Their squared lengths are three and two. Multiplying the observation vector by each column gives 8 and 4, so the normal equations are diagonal and yield \(c=8/3\) K and \(m=2\) K per position unit.

The predicted values are \(2/3,8/3,14/3\) K. The residual \(b-Ax=(1/3,-2/3,1/3)^T\) has zero sum and zero dot product with \((-1,0,1)^T\), as required. Changing the intercept cannot lower the sum of squares because the positive and negative residuals already balance; changing the slope has the analogous weighted balance. The squared residual norm is \(2/3\) K². This measures fit to these readings. It does not tell whether a line will predict temperatures well outside the sampled interval.

Remove redundant precision loss

If \(A\) has full column rank and singular values \(\sigma_i\), then \(A^TA\) has eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\). Consequently \(\kappa_2(A^TA)=\kappa_2(A)^2\). When two model columns are nearly parallel, forming the products explicitly can lose significant information about their small difference. For example, a straight-line fit on positions clustered around a very large offset has nearly proportional constant and position columns. Centering and scaling positions improves the parameterisation and gives an intercept with a local physical interpretation.

Alternatively, factor \(A=QR\) with \(Q\) having orthonormal columns and \(R\) upper triangular. Decompose the residual into its orthogonal parts inside and outside the column space:

\[\|b-Ax\|^2=\|Q^Tb-Rx\|^2+\|(I-QQ^T)b\|^2.\]

The second term is independent of the fit. Solve \(Rx=Q^Tb\) by back substitution. Householder reflectors build this factorisation using length-preserving transformations. In the three-temperature example, dividing the constant column by \(\sqrt3\) and the position column by \(\sqrt2\) already produces \(Q\). The transformed data are \(8/\sqrt3\) and \(4/\sqrt2\). Dividing by the diagonal of \(R\) recovers exactly the same fit. When the columns are nearly dependent, inspect the rank explicitly, using column pivoting or singular values, before giving a physical interpretation to individual coefficients.

Include measurement weights explicitly

If an instrument gives independent errors with standard deviations \(s_i\), divide row \(i\) of both \(A\) and \(b\) by \(s_i\). The sum of squares now measures squared differences in units of each sensor’s own standard deviation. For two readings 0 and 10 with standard deviations 1 and 3, a constant fit minimises \(c^2+(10-c)^2/9\). Differentiation gives \(c=1\), versus the unweighted mean of 5. A reliable low reading exerts more influence on the weighted result.

This weighting still assumes independence and an appropriate error description. If both readings share an uncertain offset, repeated observations can reduce independent fluctuations without removing the offset. Near a rank deficiency, a small weighted residual likewise cannot establish separate physical identifiability of the fitted coefficients. Examine uncertainty or singular directions in the parameter map as well as the final mismatch.

Derive a prediction after withholding one observation

Let \(A\) have full column rank and use equal independent data variances. The fitted vector is \(\hat y=Hy\) with \(H=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\); implement the projection with a stable factorisation. Suppose row \(i\) is withheld and the reduced design stays full rank. Its new prediction at that location, \(\hat y_i^{(-i)}\), can be obtained without refitting the entire data set. Replace \(y_i\) in the full data by this predicted value. The model fitted to the reduced observations now also satisfies the full normal equations because the added row has precisely zero residual at that fit.

Apply the original hat matrix to the modified vector. Its \(i\)-th fitted value is \(\hat y_i+h_{ii}(\hat y_i^{(-i)}-y_i)\), which must equal \(\hat y_i^{(-i)}\). Rearranging gives \(y_i-\hat y_i^{(-i)}=(y_i-\hat y_i)/(1-h_{ii})\). This relation shows how leverage can mask an error inside the training data. If \(h_{ii}=1\), the denominator vanishes because that observation alone identifies some parameter combination. Withholding it then requires a different rank convention or additional modelling information.

Predict the end of a calibration line from its other readings

Fit \(y=a+bt\) to \((t,y)=(0,0),(1,1),(2,4)\). The mean time is one and the mean response is \(5/3\). The slope from centred data is \([(-1)(-5/3)+(1)(7/3)]/[1+1]=2\), giving intercept \(-1/3\). The fitted values are \((-1/3,5/3,11/3)\) and residuals are \((1/3,-2/3,1/3)\). Taken individually, either endpoint looks better fitted than the midpoint. But each endpoint also has disproportionate influence on the slope used to fit itself.

For a straight line the intercept and centred-time columns are orthogonal, so \(h_{ii}=1/3+(t_i-1)^2/2\). The endpoint leverages are \(5/6\), the central leverage is \(1/3\), and their sum is two, the number of fitted parameters. The leave-one-out residuals are consequently \(2,-1,2\). Verify the last directly: the first two readings define the line \(y=t\); it predicts 2 at the final time, while the held-out observation is 4. For the central reading the two endpoints define \(y=2t\), predicting 2 instead of 1.

The scientific question determines whether this test is relevant. Dropping an endpoint forces extrapolation, whereas dropping the midpoint tests interpolation between two retained settings. Averaging these three squared errors mixes two distinct prediction tasks. If future measurements will be taken only inside the calibrated interval, a separate evaluation there is more informative. Serially correlated measurements also invalidate the interpretation of neighbouring held-out readings as independent validation, although the algebraic leave-one-out identity for a fixed design remains valid.

For these three observations the sum of squared training residuals is \(1/9+4/9+1/9=2/3\). The sum after leaving each observation out in turn is \(4+1+4=9\). The discrepancy arises even with an exactly implemented fitting routine. Each training prediction has already used the observation against which it is compared. The difference becomes acute near the boundary because that observation has supplied much of the information about its own fitted value.

Chapter 4 · Stationary iterations and smoothing

Derive two iterative solvers by splitting a matrix, and decide from their error modes whether repeated updates improve an approximation.

From first principles

Turn a balance into a repeatable correction

Suppose a large linear system \(Ax=b\) is difficult to solve, but a nearby matrix \(M\) is easy to invert. At a trial value \(x_k\), compute the imbalance \(r_k=b-Ax_k\). Estimate the correction by solving \(M d_k=r_k\), then add it to the trial value,

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+M^{-1}(b-Ax_k).\]

For the true solution \(x_*\) the residual vanishes. Subtract this fixed solution from the update and define \(e_k=x_k-x_*\). The algorithm propagates its remaining error through one fixed matrix,

\[e_{k+1}=(I-M^{-1}A)e_k,\]
\[e_k=(I-M^{-1}A)^k e_0 .\]

A sufficient test for convergence is an induced norm of the iteration matrix below one. More generally, in finite dimension convergence for every starting error requires all eigenvalues to have magnitude below one. Taking \(M\) as the diagonal of \(A\) gives Jacobi iteration when all diagonal entries are nonzero. Its cheap solves are worthwhile only if the resulting errors decay at a useful rate.

Derivation and applications

Balance the slow and fast modes of a fixed relaxation step

Suppose the linear operator \(A\) is real symmetric positive definite and estimates \(\ell,u\) bound its eigenvalues, with \(0<\ell\le u\). Consider a scalar relaxation \(x_{k+1}=x_k+\omega(b-Ax_k)\). Because \(A\) has an orthonormal eigenbasis, each error component evolves independently. For an eigenvector belonging to \(\lambda\), its scalar error changes according to

\[e_{k+1,\lambda}=(1-\omega\lambda)e_{k,\lambda}.\]

The largest possible amplification over the spectral interval occurs at an endpoint. Indeed the absolute value of an affine function is convex. Thus choosing a single step for every component asks for the smallest possible value of

\[q(\omega)=\max\{|1-\omega\ell|,\ |1-\omega u|\}.\]

Below the optimum the low-frequency endpoint with smaller eigenvalue decays too slowly; above it the high-eigenvalue endpoint overshoots too far. Equalizing their absolute values with opposite signs gives the best fixed step and its worst-case bound,

\[1-\omega\ell=-(1-\omega u),\]
\[\omega_*=\frac2{\ell+u},\]
\[q_*=\frac{u-\ell}{u+\ell}.\]

For coincident endpoints a single step solves the problem. When the ratio \(u/\ell\) is very large, even the optimal scalar choice leaves \(q_*\) near unity. The result bounds the Euclidean norm directly because the eigenvectors are orthogonal. For a nonsymmetric matrix, eigenvalue endpoints alone do not establish this norm bound.

Worked example

Set the step and predict an iteration budget

A model has spectrum in \([2,8]\). Choose \(\omega=0.2\); then the endpoint error factors are \(+0.6\) and \(-0.6\). To guarantee reduction by a factor of 1000 requires \(k\ge\ln(0.001)/\ln(0.6)=13.52\), hence 14 steps. An error entirely in the mode \(\lambda=5\) vanishes after the first update, showing why actual runs can beat the guarantee.

Second application

Diagnose divergence from a missed fast mode

A second solver uses \(\omega=0.25\) on a system initially believed to have largest eigenvalue 6. A newly resolved mode has \(\lambda=9\), giving amplification \(1-0.25(9)=-1.25\). Its error reverses sign and grows by a factor \(1.25^4=2.44\) in four steps. With a verified interval \([1,9]\) the corrected fixed step is \(0.2\), yielding a contraction bound \(0.8\).

Split a difficult solve into easy solves

A direct factorisation may consume substantial memory on a fine mesh. One alternative repeatedly solves with a matrix \(M\) that is easy to invert. Write \(A=M-N\). Starting at \(x^{(0)}\), solve \(Mx^{(k+1)}=Nx^{(k)}+b\). This update can also be written as a correction \(x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}+M^{-1}(b-Ax^{(k)})\). The exact solution is a fixed point. A convenient choice of \(M\) is useful only if these cheap corrections move arbitrary starting guesses toward that point.

Subtract the update from the exact fixed-point equation. The error propagates as \(e^{(k+1)}=Ge^{(k)}\), where \(G=M^{-1}N=I-M^{-1}A\). If \(G\) is diagonalizable, expansion of the initial error in its eigenvectors gives a factor \(\lambda_i^k\) per mode after \(k\) steps. The spectral radius below one gives convergence from every initial vector. A mode with magnitude close to one decays slowly. For a nonnormal matrix the eigenvectors may be nearly parallel, so several early error norms can rise even when the ultimate decay is assured.

Update a two-node network

Let \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(b=(1,1)^T\), with exact solution \((1,1)^T\). Jacobi uses \(M=\operatorname{diag}(A)\). The updates are \(x_1^{new}=(1+x_2^{old})/2\) and \(x_2^{new}=(1+x_1^{old})/2\). Starting at zero gives \((0.5,0.5)\), then \((0.75,0.75)\). Here the initial error lies in the symmetric eigenvector, with factor \(+0.5\). An initial antisymmetric error would change sign each step with factor \(-0.5\). Both shrink at the same magnitude despite different histories.

Gauss–Seidel includes the lower triangular part of \(A\) in \(M\). It uses the just-computed \(x_1^{new}\) in the second equation. From zero, the first sweep gives \((0.5,0.75)\) and the next gives \((0.875,0.9375)\). The error propagation matrix for this ordering is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1/2\\0&1/4\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), whose eigenvalues are zero and one quarter. A sweep uses recent information, but introduces a sequential dependence that plain Jacobi avoids.

Why a good smoother can be a poor solver

On a uniform one-dimensional Dirichlet grid with \(N\) intervals, each Jacobi correction for the Poisson equation replaces the error by its neighbours’ average. Substitute the sine-shaped error \(e_i=\sin(j\pi i/N)\). The addition identity shows that its amplification is \(\cos(j\pi/N)\). Low-frequency modes vary gently between neighbours, so averaging barely changes them. As the grid is refined the slowest factor tends to one. An iteration count measured on a coarse problem therefore gives little indication of fine-grid cost.

Weighted Jacobi mixes one ordinary Jacobi sweep with the old iterate. The factor becomes \(1-\omega+\omega\cos(j\pi/N)\). Choosing \(\omega=2/3\) sends a very oscillatory mode with cosine near minus one to approximately minus one third. The unweighted scheme would retain almost all of its magnitude with alternating sign. For a smooth mode with cosine near one, both updates do little. A multigrid method exploits this distinction: several weighted sweeps remove much of the local oscillation, and a coarser representation corrects the remaining long-scale error. Always evaluate a smoother by its effect on selected frequencies and a standalone solver by the slowest remaining mode.

Estimate the cost before extrapolating a timing

For the slowest sine error on a grid of \(N\) intervals, \(\cos(\pi/N)=1-\pi^2/(2N^2)+O(N^{-4})\). To reduce this component by a factor of ten, solve \(\cos(\pi/N)^k\leq0.1\) using logarithms. For large \(N\) this gives \(k\simeq 2N^2\ln(10)/\pi^2\). Doubling the number of intervals therefore roughly quadruples the sweep count for ordinary Jacobi. The work in a one-dimensional sweep also doubles. A timing extrapolated solely from the cost of one sweep misses this additional factor of four.

This estimate starts with an error containing the first sine mode. If a benchmark uses a carefully chosen high-frequency error, a weighted Jacobi run may look much faster. The two measurements describe different aspects of the iteration. Repeating the comparison with both a smooth and a rapidly alternating initial error makes that dependence observable in a short experiment.

Convergence can begin with a large increase

Write an affine stationary iteration as \(x_{k+1}=Tx_k+c\). Subtracting its fixed-point equation shows that \(e_k=T^ke_0\) for the error in either consistently chosen sign convention. Every initial error decays in finite dimension exactly when the spectral radius of \(T\) is smaller than one. Diagonalising a normal \(T\) then gives \(\|T^k\|_2=\rho(T)^k\). For a nonnormal matrix its eigenvectors need not be orthogonal and this norm identity fails. A shrinking asymptotic factor can coexist with substantial early error growth.

Consider \(T=rI+N\) with \(N^2=0\) and \(0<r<1\). Since \(I\) and \(N\) commute, the binomial expansion leaves \(T^k=r^kI+kr^{k-1}N\). The second term eventually vanishes because exponential decay dominates a polynomial factor. Initially it can be large if the off-diagonal coupling in \(N\) is large. This is a precise way to distinguish eventual convergence from contractivity in a specified physical norm. Monitoring only the eigenvalue magnitude hides the size of short-term excursions experienced by the components.

A coupled recurrence that delivers the wrong early verdict

Take \(r=1/2,\ N=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&8\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), and start from error \(e_0=(0,1)^T\). The exact formula gives \(e_1=(8,1/2)^T\), \(e_2=(8,1/4)^T\), \(e_3=(6,1/8)^T\), and \(e_4=(4,1/16)^T\). An investigator who compares the first error norm with its initial value would conclude that the procedure is unstable. Yet both eigenvalues are one half and the formula assures eventual decay. At ten steps the first component is \(80/512=0.15625\). This slow recovery after a rapid early amplification is a property of the coupling.

A second experiment starts from \(e_0=(1,0)^T\). The nilpotent part annihilates that direction and the first component decays as \(2^{-k}\) from the very beginning. Thus observing a successful trajectory from just one initial condition can also conceal the largest early amplification. In a simulation that uses this solve inside another algorithm, those intermediate excursions may violate physical admissibility even if the mathematical linear system would eventually be solved to high accuracy.

Scaling the first coordinate by a factor of sixteen reduces the numerical off-diagonal entry in the scaled iteration from eight to one half. The infinity norm of that scaled matrix is then one; a slightly stronger scaling makes it strictly contractive. This does not change any eigenvalue or physical trajectory. It changes how coordinate errors are measured. An engineer should specify whether the chosen scaled tolerance actually controls both components to their required physical accuracies before using contractivity in that norm as a practical stopping guarantee.

A related diagnostic is to distinguish the one-step map norm from its spectral radius numerically. Here applying \(T\) to the unit vector \(e_2\) gives length \(\sqrt{64+1/4}>8\), so its two-norm is at least eight. No calculation of eigenvalues can give a comparable lower bound on this one-step gain in a nonnormal problem. Computing a largest singular value would measure exactly that worst initial norm amplification.

The original column dot product is 3000. The centred positions sum to zero and have squared norm two. The new Gram matrix is diagonal with entries 3 and 2, so the fitted mean and trend separate in the normal equations.

Chapter 5 · Krylov subspaces and preconditioning

Derive the first CG steps from a minimum-energy principle and determine what is gained by changing the metric in which a linear system is solved.

From first principles

Derive the first Krylov correction from an energy minimum

For symmetric positive definite \(A\), the unique solution of \(Ax=b\) minimizes a quadratic energy. For any trial state \(x\), moving along direction \(p\) by a scalar \(\alpha\) changes that energy according to

\[E(x)=\tfrac12 x^TAx-b^Tx,\]
\[E(x+\alpha p)-E(x)=-\alpha p^Tr+\tfrac12\alpha^2p^TAp ,\]

where \(r=b-Ax\) is the residual. Every nonzero direction has \(p^TAp>0\), so setting the derivative in \(\alpha\) to zero gives the unique line minimum. The residual at the updated state is then perpendicular to the search direction,

\[\alpha=\frac{p^Tr}{p^TAp},\]
\[p^T(r-\alpha Ap)=0 .\]

Starting with \(p=r\) searches the first Krylov space spanned by the initial residual. Expanding that space to include \(Ar,A^2r,\ldots\) permits a better energy minimum at each step. Conjugate gradients chooses directions mutually orthogonal under the \(A\)-weighted inner product so that later line searches preserve the minima already achieved in earlier directions.

Derivation and applications

Determine the decrease delivered by a preconditioned search direction

A preconditioner changes the scale and orientation of the direction used to correct a linear system. To see how that affects the objective, take symmetric positive definite \(A\) and preconditioner \(M\), with residual \(r=b-Ax\). Solve \(Mz=r\) to choose a direction. For nonzero \(r\), the product \(r^Tz=r^TM^{-1}r\) is positive, so a small positive step along \(z\) reduces the quadratic energy \(E=\tfrac12x^TAx-b^Tx\).

\[E(x+\alpha z)=E(x)-\alpha r^Tz+\frac{\alpha^2}{2}z^TAz .\]

The minimum along this direction is at \(\alpha_*=(r^Tz)/(z^TAz)\). Substitution gives the exact energy decrease. If \(x_*\) solves the original system, the full energy difference to the solution is also a quadratic form,

\[E(x)-E(x+\alpha_*z)=\frac{(r^Tz)^2}{2z^TAz},\]
\[E(x)-E(x_*)=\tfrac12r^TA^{-1}r .\]

Their ratio is a squared cosine. Let \(u=A^{-1/2}r\) and \(v=A^{1/2}z\); their ordinary dot product is \(r^Tz\). The first search removes the fraction

\[\frac{E(x)-E(x+\alpha_*z)}{E(x)-E(x_*)}=\frac{(u^Tv)^2}{(u^Tu)(v^Tv)}\le1 .\]

A direction proportional to the exact correction removes all the error. A positive scalar multiple of \(M\) changes the step length inversely to the direction length, preserving the updated point. These facts concern one search and also apply to the first step of preconditioned conjugate gradients.

Worked example

Compare two directions on the same system

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,9)\), \(r=(1,3)^T\) and initial state zero. The available energy decrease is \((1+9/9)/2=1\). Without preconditioning \(z=r\), so the first step removes \(10^2/[2(1+81)]=0.610\). With \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,3)\), \(z=(1,1)^T\); it removes \(4^2/[2(1+9)]=0.800\). Both updates solve the same physical balances.

Second application

Rescale an exact preconditioner

In a second problem \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,5)\), \(r=(4,10)^T\) and the available solver uses \(M=2A\). Its direction is \((1,1)^T\). The minimizing step is \((4+10)/(2+5)=2\), giving correction \((2,2)^T=A^{-1}r\). Judging a preconditioner by whether the step length is close to unity would reject this exact one-step correction.

A geometrical interpretation of positive definiteness

Assume \(A=A^T\) and \(v^TAv>0\) for all nonzero \(v\). The algebraic solution \(x_\star\) is the unique minimiser of \(J(x)=x^TAx/2-b^Tx\). Indeed, completing the square gives \(J(x)-J(x_\star)=(x-x_\star)^TA(x-x_\star)/2\). For a structural stiffness matrix this has the interpretation of excess elastic potential energy. The negative gradient at an approximation is the residual \(r=b-Ax\). Even in a purely algebraic application this energy geometry explains which approximation CG selects.

In a chosen search direction \(p\), minimise \(J(x+\alpha p)\) by differentiation. The optimal step is \(\alpha=p^Tr/(p^TAp)\). Two successive minimisations should not undo each other. If \(p_i^TAp_j=0\) for distinct directions, minimisation along \(p_j\) preserves minimisation along \(p_i\). Such directions are called conjugate. CG generates them from successive residuals and matrix-vector products. Starting with \(p_0=r_0\), the exact-arithmetic recurrences are \(\alpha_k=(r_k^Tr_k)/(p_k^TAp_k)\), \(r_{k+1}=r_k-\alpha_kAp_k\), \(\beta_k=(r_{k+1}^Tr_{k+1})/(r_k^Tr_k)\), and \(p_{k+1}=r_{k+1}+\beta_kp_k\).

Work through a two-dimensional example

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\), \(b=(1,1)^T\), and \(x_0=0\). Then \(p_0=(1,1)^T\), \(Ap_0=(1,4)^T\) and \(\alpha_0=2/5=0.4\). The first approximation is \((0.4,0.4)^T\); its residual is \((0.6,-0.6)^T\). That residual is perpendicular to the old search direction because the line minimum has already been reached. The update coefficient is \(\beta_0=0.72/2=0.36\), giving \(p_1=(0.96,-0.24)^T\).

Check the conjugacy directly: \(p_0^TAp_1=0.96-0.96=0\). Now \(p_1^TAp_1=1.152\), so \(\alpha_1=0.72/1.152=0.625\). The second approximation is \((1,0.25)^T\), which solves the system. A repeated steepest descent restart would search along the new residual, lose conjugacy, and generally need more than two steps. In floating-point arithmetic, residual orthogonality gradually deteriorates. A fresh residual computed from the final vector is a valuable check.

Improve the geometry of the search

At iteration \(k\), candidates lie in \(x_0+\operatorname{span}\{r_0,Ar_0,\ldots,A^{k-1}r_0\}\). The error can be written \(p_k(A)e_0\) for a polynomial with \(p_k(0)=1\). If the operator has only a few distinct eigenvalues, a low-degree polynomial can vanish on them all. More generally, many eigenvalues in a tight cluster can be reduced together. This explains why the condition number gives only a worst-case convergence estimate.

A positive definite preconditioner \(M\) corresponds to solving with \(M^{-1/2}AM^{-1/2}\). For the diagonal example, \(M=\operatorname{diag}(A)\) makes the transformed operator the identity, so one step suffices. For a full matrix, the same choice corrects scales but may leave strong coupling untouched. Applying an approximate inverse also costs time and memory. Compare total solve time, setup cost and physical error over representative loads before choosing a preconditioner. The heated cube experiment reports how changing the tolerance alters the same temperature observable on a fixed mesh. Compare direct and iterative heat balances.

Read an energy-norm convergence estimate

For symmetric positive definite \(A\), a classical CG estimate is \(\|e_k\|_A/\|e_0\|_A\leq2[(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)]^k\). To see the square root appear, minimise the largest magnitude of an error polynomial over the entire eigenvalue interval \([\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]\), with its value fixed at one at zero. Shifting a Chebyshev polynomial onto that interval produces the stated decay. Treating every point of the interval as a possible eigenvalue makes this a deliberately pessimistic estimate when the actual eigenvalues form tight clusters.

For \(\kappa=100\), the factor is \(9/11\). The bound first guarantees a reduction below \(10^{-3}\) after 38 iterations. If effective preconditioning reduces the condition number to 4, the factor becomes \(1/3\) and seven iterations suffice for the same guarantee. The comparison gives a budget for the search iterations only. An expensive preconditioner can erase that saving when it is set up for a single small load case. For repeated loads, setup can be spread over many solves, changing the economics without changing the convergence analysis.

Relate the stopping norm to energy

For a symmetric positive definite system the energy error squared equals \(r^TA^{-1}r\), since \(r=Ae\). Consequently residuals with the same Euclidean length may have different physical significance. With \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,100)\), residuals \((1,0)^T\) and \((0,1)^T\) both have length one, but their energy errors are 1 and 0.1. A tolerance derived from an energy specification should account for this spectral weighting.

Turn a known spectral interval into a polynomial guarantee

Let \(A\) be symmetric positive definite with spectrum in \([a,b]\), \(0<a<b\). After \(k\) conjugate-gradient steps from \(x_0\), every available state error has the form \(p(A)e_0\), where \(p\) has degree at most \(k\) and \(p(0)=1\). The constraint at zero follows because the correction is an element of the Krylov space generated by the initial residual \(Ae_0\). Expressing \(e_0\) in orthonormal eigenvectors shows that the squared energy error is \(\sum_i\lambda_i |p(\lambda_i)|^2 |e_{0,i}|^2\). Bounding the maximum of the polynomial over the entire spectral interval gives a guarantee independent of the initial error.

Map \([a,b]\) to \([-1,1]\) through \(s=(a+b-2\lambda)/(b-a)\). The degree-\(k\) Chebyshev polynomial \(T_k(s)\) has magnitude at most one there, while at \(\lambda=0\) its argument \(s_0=(a+b)/(b-a)\) exceeds one. Thus \(p(\lambda)=T_k(s)/T_k(s_0)\) is an admissible trial polynomial with worst amplitude \(1/T_k(s_0)\). Since CG minimises the energy norm over all trial corrections, it performs at least as well as this explicit choice. Positive definiteness is used twice: it defines the energy norm and keeps zero outside the proposed spectral interval.

Compare an interval bound with the actual finite spectrum

Suppose the only eigenvalues are 1, 2 and 4. For two steps take \(T_2(s)=2s^2-1\), \(s_0=5/3\). Then \(T_2(s_0)=41/9\), so the worst relative energy error is at most \(9/41\approx0.2195\) for the trial polynomial and hence for CG. At the spectral endpoints the polynomial equals \(9/41\). At \(\lambda=2\), however, \(s=1/3\) and the factor is \(-7/41\). Each mode has acquired a small amplitude even though the signs differ. The actual energy error depends on how much of the initial energy lay in each mode.

For three steps the spectral interval still has infinitely many possible points. But the actual three eigenvalues permit the polynomial \((1-\lambda)(1-\lambda/2)(1-\lambda/4)\), which equals one at zero and vanishes on the entire spectrum. CG can therefore reach the exact solution on this finite example in at most three steps in exact arithmetic. Repeated eigenvalues do not increase this bound. The interval guarantee loses this information by preparing for every point between the smallest and largest eigenvalues, including values absent from the matrix.

A strong preconditioner can produce a narrow spectral cluster plus a few outliers. A helpful interpretation is to spend a small number of polynomial roots suppressing the outliers and use the rest to approximate zero across the cluster. The number of distinct eigendirections and the distribution of initial energy then complement a simple condition-number estimate. Finite precision can spoil exact finite termination, particularly when supposedly eliminated components reappear through roundoff.

If \(A\) is instead indefinite, a polynomial residual argument may still be meaningful for a different Krylov solver, but the quantity \(e^TAe\) can vanish for nonzero error or become negative. The preceding minimisation proof then loses the positive measure of approximation on which it depends. Checking symmetry alone is insufficient to apply it.

Chapter 6 · Eigenvalues, modes and spectral gaps

Estimate a dominant vibration or diffusion mode, explain the role of eigenvalue separation and use a residual to check a computed eigenpair.

From first principles

Watch a power iteration select a mode

Start with a real symmetric matrix \(A\) and express a trial vector \(v_0\) in its orthonormal eigenvectors \(u_j\). Order the eigenvalues so \(|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\ge\cdots\) and suppose the starting component \(c_1\) is nonzero. Applying \(A\) repeatedly multiplies each component by its own eigenvalue,

\[v_0=\sum_jc_j u_j,\]
\[A^k v_0=\lambda_1^k\left[c_1u_1+\sum_{j>1} c_j(\lambda_j/\lambda_1)^k u_j\right].\]

Normalizing after each application keeps vector lengths manageable without changing the relative size of the components. The largest competing component therefore shrinks relative to the first at a geometric rate. A scalar estimate of the eigenvalue comes from projecting the action back onto the trial vector,

\[\rho(v)=\frac{v^TAv}{v^Tv}.\]

For an exact eigenvector the quotient equals its eigenvalue. Equal leading magnitudes can prevent selection of a single direction. A negative leading eigenvalue can make the displayed vector alternate sign even while its one-dimensional direction converges.

Derivation and applications

Derive the eigenvalue shift produced by weak coupling

Two individually measured modes can move apart in frequency or stiffness when they are coupled. Model a real symmetric operator in an orthonormal basis by diagonal values \(\alpha,\beta\) and off-diagonal coupling \(g\). All three numbers must have the same units. A nonzero eigenvector exists only when the determinant of the shifted matrix vanishes,

\[(\alpha-\lambda)(\beta-\lambda)-g^2=0 .\]

Set \(m=(\alpha+\beta)/2\) and \(d=(\alpha-\beta)/2\). The quadratic becomes \((\lambda-m)^2=d^2+g^2\), so the two eigenvalues and their separation are

\[\lambda_\pm=m\pm\sqrt{d^2+g^2},\]
\[\lambda_+-\lambda_-=2\sqrt{d^2+g^2}.\]

Even when the uncoupled values coincide, a nonzero coupling leaves a gap \(2|g|\). For well-separated diagonal entries, assume \(\alpha>\beta\) and expand the square root for \(|g|\ll\alpha-\beta\). The largest eigenvalue has the shift

\[\lambda_+-\alpha=\frac{g^2}{\sqrt{d^2+g^2}+d}\simeq\frac{g^2}{\alpha-\beta}.\]

The rationalized form gives an accurate small shift without subtracting almost equal eigenvalues. The eigenvalue sum remains exactly \(\alpha+\beta\) because the trace is unchanged. A mass-normalized vibration model typically returns squared angular frequencies, so any measured ordinary frequencies must first be converted to the variables of the operator.

Worked example

Predict a shift away from a crossing

With \(\alpha=10\), \(\beta=6\) and \(g=0.5\), the upper shift is \(0.25/(\sqrt{4.25}+2)=0.0616\); the leading approximation is \(0.0625\). The lower eigenvalue is \(5.9384\) and the upper is \(10.0616\). The opposite signs of the shifts preserve the trace of 16.

Second application

Recover coupling and detuning from a measured gap

A second two-mode system has eigenvalues 19 and 25, giving a mean of 22 and half-gap of 3. An independent uncoupled calibration gives \(|\alpha-\beta|=4\), so \(d^2=4\). The inferred coupling magnitude is \(\sqrt{3^2-2^2}=\sqrt5=2.24\). The eigenvalues do not determine the sign of \(g\), which also changes when one basis vector is reversed.

What an eigenproblem measures

In an undamped mechanical model \(M\ddot u+Ku=0\), insert \(u=v\cos(\omega t)\). Nonzero amplitudes require \(Kv=\omega^2Mv\). The normal frequencies are square roots of generalised eigenvalues and the mode shapes are determined only up to scale and sign. For symmetric positive definite \(M\), substituting \(w=M^{1/2}v\) gives the ordinary symmetric operator \(M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}\). This step explains why mass weighting matters when comparing shapes from two different meshes.

For an ordinary symmetric matrix \(A\) with orthonormal eigenvectors, a normalised trial vector \(v=\sum c_i u_i\) has Rayleigh quotient \(\rho=v^TAv=\sum_i\lambda_i c_i^2\). Hence it lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues. Near an isolated mode its value can be much more accurate than the vector direction: shifting a small amount of amplitude into another orthogonal mode changes the quotient by a term proportional to the square of that amplitude.

Select a mode by repeated application

The power iteration repeatedly forms \(Av_k\) and normalises it. Before normalisation, \(A^kv_0=\sum c_i\lambda_i^k u_i\). If one eigenvalue has strictly largest magnitude and the initial vector overlaps its eigenspace, the ratio of any competing component to the leading one gains a factor \((\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k\). Normalisation avoids overflow while preserving those ratios. A negative leading eigenvalue can cause sign changes between successive normalised vectors. Comparing their raw difference then misrepresents convergence.

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(4,2)\) and a starting vector proportional to \((1,1)^T\), the ratio of the second component to the first is \(1/2\) after one multiplication and \(1/4\) after two. A vector proportional to \((4,1)^T\) has Rayleigh quotient \((4\cdot16+2)/17=66/17=3.88235\). After four steps the ratio is \(1/16\), and the quotient is \(1026/257=3.99222\). Check that the quotient error decreases roughly fourfold per additional step, although the directional error decreases only twofold.

Use shifts to find another part of the spectrum

Power iteration selects magnitude. To find a mode near a target \(\sigma\), apply the inverse of \(A-\sigma I\) implicitly by solving a linear system each iteration. The eigenvectors are the same and the eigenvalues are \(1/(\lambda_i-\sigma)\). For eigenvalues 2 and 4 with \(\sigma=2.1\), the transformed magnitudes are 10 and about 0.526. The eigenvalue near 2 dominates for a suitable starter. A fixed shift permits factor reuse, but choosing it exactly equal to an eigenvalue makes the shifted solve singular.

Test a candidate pair by forming \(r=Av-\rho v\) with \(\|v\|_2=1\). In the orthonormal basis, \(\|r\|^2=\sum_i(\lambda_i-\rho)^2 c_i^2\). At least one eigenvalue must lie within \(\|r\|\) of \(\rho\). This is a numerical certificate for a symmetric problem. To certify a particular eigenvector, the gap to neighbouring eigenvalues also matters. At a repeated eigenvalue every normalised linear combination of its modes is valid. Comparing subspaces or projecting one span onto the other is then more meaningful than matching individual component signs.

Follow the whole spectrum by QR iteration

To find several eigenvalues of a small dense operator, factor \(A_k-\mu I=QR\) and form \(A_{k+1}=RQ+\mu I\). Orthogonality gives \(A_{k+1}=Q^TA_kQ\), an exact similarity. Hence every step preserves eigenvalues, trace and determinant while changing the coordinates used to represent the operator. This use of QR is different from solving a triangular least-squares system. Repeated unshifted steps amount to an orthogonalised power iteration on multiple directions. A shift near a separated eigenvalue can make the last coordinate nearly invariant and accelerate its separation.

For a symmetric matrix a preliminary sequence of orthogonal similarities reduces it to tridiagonal form. When the coupling between the last two coordinates is negligible relative to the desired accuracy, setting that coupling and its transpose to zero separates a scalar eigenproblem. This removal is called deflation. If the off-diagonal magnitude is \(e\), setting the pair to zero is a symmetric perturbation of spectral norm \(|e|\), giving a direct measure for that decision. For a general real nonsymmetric matrix, the converged result can also contain real two-by-two blocks representing complex eigenvalue pairs.

A shifted step with a check on the trailing mode

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(\mu=2\). Normalising the first shifted column gives \((2,1)^T/\sqrt5\). Complete the orthogonal basis with \((-1,2)^T/\sqrt5\). Multiplying \(Q^TAQ\) gives diagonal \((22/5,8/5)\) and off-diagonal \(-1/5\). The trace remains six and the determinant remains seven. The last-coordinate Rayleigh estimate, initially two, is now 1.6 compared with the exact lower eigenvalue \(3-\sqrt2\approx1.586\). The residual of this coordinate has fallen from one to 0.2. A subsequent shift can use the improved estimate. Preserving eigenvalues alone would never certify that a coordinate is ready for deflation.

Which boundary modes are physical?

An elastic assembly suspended in free space admits rigid translations, and in three dimensions also rigid rotations when its geometry allows them. In a correctly assembled model these motions cost zero strain energy, so the stiffness matrix has a nullspace and some generalised eigenvalues are zero. Attempting unshifted inverse iteration on that stiffness matrix cannot locate a lowest positive vibration by simply inverting the operator. One can instead constrain rigid motions consistently or project into the mass-orthogonal complement of their span.

For a two-mass system with unit masses and a unit spring connecting them, \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Translation \((1,1)^T\) has zero squared frequency; opposite displacement \((1,-1)^T\) has squared frequency two. Grounding the first mass eliminates translation but changes the second mode to an oscillator with frequency one. Projection leaves the original free-system vibration at \(\sqrt2\). These are physically different comparisons; the treatment of a nullspace determines which frequency an eigenvalue calculation is actually reporting.

Convert an eigenpair residual into an angle bound

Given a real symmetric matrix \(A\), a unit-vector approximation \(v\) and a scalar \(\theta\), write its residual \(r=Av-\theta v\). Expand \(v=\sum_j c_jq_j\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis. The squared residual is \(\sum_j(\lambda_j-\theta)^2|c_j|^2\). This identity always implies that at least one eigenvalue lies within \(\|r\|\) of \(\theta\). It does not by itself show whether the reported vector represents one particular physical mode. Two almost coincident eigenvalues can produce a tiny residual for many different mixtures of their eigenvectors.

To bound leakage out of a target eigenspace, let \(\gamma\) be a lower bound on the distance from \(\theta\) to every eigenvalue outside that space. The squared residual is at least \(\gamma^2\sum_{\rm outside}|c_j|^2\). Consequently the sine of the angle between \(v\) and the target space is at most \(\|r\|/\gamma\). This result assumes a symmetric or Hermitian operator under the chosen inner product. For a mass-weighted mechanical problem, transform to mass-normal coordinates or perform the argument in the proper mass inner product. An ordinary Euclidean angle between unscaled coordinates may have little relevance to vibrational energy.

Resolve a cluster more reliably than either member

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(10,10.02,30)\), and \(v=(1,1,0)^T/\sqrt2\). Its Rayleigh quotient is 10.01. Multiplication gives \(r=(-0.01,0.01,0)^T/\sqrt2\), of length 0.01. Relative to the cluster near ten this residual is small, yet the angle from the first coordinate eigenvector is 45 degrees. Using only the other low eigenvalue as a separation gives \(\gamma=0.01\), so the bound on that individual angle has sine at most one and provides no useful orientation guarantee.

Now take the target as the entire first two-dimensional subspace. Its gap from the reported quotient to the outside eigenvalue is \(30-10.01=19.99\). The same residual implies leakage no greater than \(0.01/19.99\approx0.000500\). In fact the true leakage of this particular vector is exactly zero. If the measured eigenvalues are squared angular frequencies, the two low modes have nearly the same frequency but can mix strongly under a small asymmetric manufacturing perturbation. Their combined subspace may remain very stable and may therefore be the more sensible object to compare with measurements.

A laboratory comparison can project independent excitation patterns onto the computed cluster and compare the total energy in that cluster. Such a result is invariant under rotations of its basis, which a simple table of pairwise eigenvector differences is not. If the gap to the rest of the spectrum closes as well, the subspace estimate becomes weak. Measuring the residual alone cannot repair that missing separation information.

For example the sum of squared projections of the excitation \((1,0,0)^T\) onto the first two computed orthonormal modes is one for any orthonormal basis of the exact low cluster. Individual projections can range between zero and one as the basis rotates. A test based on cluster projection assesses the reproducible quantity without requiring a uniquely labelled first mode.

Chapter 7 · Householder reflections and practical QR

Build one reflection explicitly, use it to triangularise a rectangular system and locate the residual in transformed coordinates.

From first principles

Construct a reflection that zeros a column tail

A reflection reverses the component of a vector perpendicular to a chosen plane and leaves the component in the plane fixed. If a nonzero vector \(v\) is normal to the plane, the perpendicular projection of \(x\) is \(v(v^Tx)/(v^Tv)\). Subtracting twice that projection defines

\[Hx=x-2v\frac{v^Tx}{v^Tv},\]
\[H=I-2\frac{vv^T}{v^Tv}.\]

The reflection preserves lengths because the parallel and perpendicular components remain orthogonal. To reduce a column \(x\), target an axis vector \(y=\alpha e_1\) of equal length, so \(|\alpha|=\|x\|_2\). Set \(v=x-y\). Expanding the dot products gives \(2v^Tx=v^Tv\) because \(x\) and \(y\) have equal norm. Consequently

\[Hx=x-v=y .\]

Choosing \(\alpha\) opposite in sign to \(x_1\) avoids cancellation in \(v_1\). Reflecting only the unprocessed rows of each successive column yields an upper triangular factor while preserving previously generated zeros. An already zero column needs no transformation.

Derivation and applications

Recover the least-squares residual from Householder coordinates

A QR solver can report how much an orthogonal transformation sends into rows below a triangular factor. That part has a precise measurement interpretation. Assume a real matrix \(A\) with \(m>n\) and independent columns. Apply the same sequence of Householder transformations to the augmented matrix \([A\ b]\). Their product \(Q^T\) is orthogonal. Partition its result into an upper block with \(n\) rows and a lower block,

\[Q^T[A\ b]=\begin{pmatrix}R&c\\0&d\end{pmatrix}.\]

Because orthogonal transformations preserve the Euclidean norm, the squared residual of any trial vector decomposes into the sum of two nonnegative terms. Only the first can be changed by the fit,

\[\|b-Ax\|_2^2=\|c-Rx\|_2^2+\|d\|_2^2 .\]

The optimal \(x\) solves \(Rx=c\), leaving residual norm \(\|d\|_2\). In exact arithmetic the original-coordinate residual is obtained by reversing the transforms on a vector whose upper block is zero,

\[r=b-Ax=Q\binom{0}{d},\]
\[A^Tr=R^T[\,I\ \ 0\,]\binom{0}{d}=0.\]

The lower coordinates are combinations of observations. Their individual values depend on the particular reflections, whereas their squared norm is invariant. Retaining that norm directly avoids subtracting two large nearly equal squared norms when the fit is excellent. If some triangular pivots are numerically negligible, ranking the columns and checking the model’s effective rank become necessary before interpreting all \(n\) fitted coefficients.

Worked example

Inspect residuals after fitting a constant

Fit \(A=(1,1)^T\) to readings \(b=(8,10)^T\). Rotate to the orthonormal directions \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((-1,1)/\sqrt2\). The transformed data are \(18/\sqrt2\) and \(2/\sqrt2\), while the transformed model is \((\sqrt2,0)^T x\). The fit gives \(x=9\), residual norm \(\sqrt2\), and original residual \((-1,1)^T\). Its sum is zero as the constant column requires.

Second application

Compare the numerical consequences of subtracting squared norms

Suppose transformed coordinates of another fit have \(\|c\|=10^8\) and \(\|d\|=0.1\). Computing \(\|b\|^2-\|c\|^2\) would subtract two values near \(10^{16}\) to recover \(0.01\). Binary64 spacing near \(10^{16}\) is 2, so the small addition can disappear entirely. Keeping the transformed tail returns residual norm \(0.1\) directly, subject to normal transformation errors.

Eliminate a column without stretching it

An orthogonal transformation applied simultaneously to the data matrix and right-hand side leaves every Euclidean residual length unchanged. A Householder reflector is \(H=I-2vv^T/(v^Tv)\) for a nonzero vector \(v\). Decompose any vector into components parallel and perpendicular to \(v\). The parallel component changes sign and the perpendicular component stays fixed, proving both length preservation and \(H^2=I\). Implementation requires just a dot product and a scaled vector subtraction rather than a dense matrix.

To make the entries below the first element of a column \(x\) vanish, choose \(v=x-\alpha e_1\) where \(|\alpha|=\|x\|_2\). A short calculation gives \(v^Tv=2(\|x\|^2-\alpha x_1)\) and \(v^Tx=\|x\|^2-\alpha x_1\), hence \(Hx=x-v=\alpha e_1\). Choose \(\alpha\) with sign opposite to \(x_1\) to avoid subtracting nearly equal positive numbers when computing \(v_1\). A zero column requires a separate decision about rank.

Follow the data into the triangular system

For \(x=(3,4)^T\), use \(\alpha=-5\) and \(v=(8,4)^T\). The reflection matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-.6&-.8\\-.8&.6\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Take a one-column least-squares problem with measurements \(b=(4,3)^T\). Multiplication sends \(x\) to \((-5,0)^T\) and \(b\) to \((-4.8,-1.4)^T\). The optimum coefficient matches the first transformed measurement, giving \(c=0.96\). The second transformed measurement remains a residual of magnitude 1.4. Back in physical coordinates the predicted vector is \((2.88,3.84)\), with residual \((1.12,-0.84)\) and the same length.

For several columns, apply the first reflector to the entire remaining block. Construct the next reflector only on rows below the first pivot, protecting the already triangular part. Saving the reflector vectors permits application of \(Q\) or \(Q^T\) later without explicitly forming either full matrix. Additional right-hand sides can then use the same orthogonal transformations and triangular solve.

Detect when a coefficient is poorly determined

If a new column is nearly in the span of earlier columns, little of it survives below the preceding pivots. A small trailing diagonal of \(R\) signals potential coefficient sensitivity, but its scale depends on column units and order. An instrument model with one parameter measured in micro-units can naturally have a small column. Scale the columns according to the intended range of the corresponding parameters before interpreting a near-zero diagonal.

Column-pivoted QR selects a remaining column with a large unrepresented component at each stage. It can help reveal effective rank and identify an independent set of measurements or basis functions. A numerical rank decision still needs a tolerance tied to data uncertainty or floating-point arithmetic. Reporting a fitted coefficient to ten digits after discarding an almost invisible direction would conceal the limitation that the factorisation has revealed.

Check a nearly aligned column

Consider \(x=(1,10^{-8})^T\). Its norm differs from one by only about \(5\times10^{-17}\), below the spacing between one and the next larger double precision number. Computing the exact difference \(x_1-\|x\|\) as that subtraction may therefore give zero. Choosing the target with opposite sign makes the first entry of the reflector vector about two and the remaining small entry is retained. This calculation explains the sign convention geometrically: two opposite points on a sphere provide valid reflection targets, and only one directs the subtraction away from severe cancellation.

Update a triangular fit when observations arrive sequentially

A Householder QR factorisation is efficient when the entire design matrix is available. Suppose instead that a new row arrives after the original observations have been reduced to an upper triangular factor \(R\), a transformed data vector \(z\), and an accumulated residual sum of squares \(S\). The original least-squares objective equals \(\|Rx-z\|^2+S\). Appending measurement row \(a^T\) and value \(b\) adds \((a^Tx-b)^2\). Stacking \(a^T\) below \(R\) produces a matrix that is triangular except for one row. Orthogonal plane rotations can restore triangular form by eliminating each incoming entry in turn.

To remove a pair \((r,s)^T\), let \(\rho=\sqrt{r^2+s^2}\), \(c=r/\rho,\ d=s/\rho\). Multiplication by the rotation with rows \((c,d)\) and \((-d,c)\) gives \((\rho,0)^T\). Apply precisely the same two-row operation to all remaining columns and to the transformed data. Its final bottom data entry has no corresponding design column, so its square adds to \(S\). If \(r=s=0\) there is nothing to eliminate. A numerical implementation obtains the length with a scaled hypotenuse evaluation to avoid overflow from squaring a large observation.

Insert a point into an existing two-parameter fit

Suppose the existing transformed problem has \(R=I,\ z=(1,2)^T,\ S=0\). A new sensor reports \(x_1+x_2=5\). The best prior estimate was \((1,2)^T\), which predicts three and therefore disagrees with the incoming reading by two. To reconcile all observations, stack the new row \((1,1\mid5)\) below \((1,0\mid1)\) and \((0,1\mid2)\). Rotate the first and last rows with \(c=d=1/\sqrt2\). The first becomes \((\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2\mid6/\sqrt2)\); the last becomes \((0,1/\sqrt2\mid4/\sqrt2)\).

The second rotation combines leading entries \(1\) and \(1/\sqrt2\), yielding \(\rho=\sqrt{3/2}\), \(c=\sqrt{2/3}\), \(d=1/\sqrt3\). Its surviving right-hand side is \(2\sqrt{2/3}+4/\sqrt6=8/\sqrt6\). Thus the second-coordinate estimate is \((8/\sqrt6)/\sqrt{3/2}=8/3\). Back substitution in the first row gives \(x_1=(6-8/3)/2=5/3\). The final bottom data entry is \(-2/\sqrt3+4/\sqrt3=2/\sqrt3\), contributing \(4/3\) to the residual sum of squares.

Verify directly: the three residual magnitudes are all \(2/3\), so their squared sum is \(4/3\). The new reading moved both coordinates equally because their original transformed information was identical and its row is symmetric. No historical measurement needed to be reread. Conversely deleting an earlier reading can expose rank deficiency and is a different numerical operation; simply running the same update with a negated data value would add another squared term rather than remove information.

When a stream contains different measurement variances, divide each arriving row and its right-hand side by its independently known standard deviation before applying the rotations. The accumulated \(S\) then measures squared standardised discrepancies. If the new reading shares an error with earlier readings, dividing its row alone is insufficient. One must first account for the conditional error and correlated design contribution or refactor a suitable joint whitening transformation. This is a measurement-model limitation, even when the rotations themselves remain perfectly orthogonal.

Repeated readings of a common reference are one practical source of precisely this kind of dependence.

Chapter 8 · Singular values and minimum-norm solutions

Resolve a linear measurement into independent directions, compute its pseudoinverse and predict the effect of truncating a weakly observed component.

From first principles

Count what a rectangular measurement can recover

For a real matrix \(A\), the squared output length in a unit input direction \(v\) is \(v^TA^TAv\). Since \(A^TA\) is symmetric and nonnegative, it has orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_j\) with eigenvalues \(\sigma_j^2\ge0\). Each positive eigenvalue defines a unit output direction \(u_j=Av_j/\sigma_j\). Distinct orthogonal input eigenvectors then have orthogonal output directions because

\[u_i^T u_j=\frac{v_i^TA^TAv_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\delta_{ij}.\]

Resolve an input into these vectors and the null space. A component along \(v_j\) is amplified by \(\sigma_j\), while every null component disappears. For measured data \(b\), matching the observed output components gives

\[x_{\min}=\sum_{\sigma_j>0}\frac{u_j^Tb}{\sigma_j}\,v_j .\]

Adding any null vector produces the same prediction but increases the squared norm by its own squared length. This proves the minimum-norm choice. Data perpendicular to all the \(u_j\) remain as a least-squares residual, and noise along a small positive singular value can be amplified substantially.

Derivation and applications

Find the smallest reconstruction in physically scaled coordinates

The minimum-norm solution of an underdetermined fit depends on which units and physical scale define the norm. Consider exact independent constraints \(Ax=b\) with fewer rows than columns, and let \(W\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix of penalties. Minimize \(\tfrac12 x^TWx\) among all feasible states. A reference value could first be subtracted if zero represents an inappropriate baseline. Factor \(W=L^TL\) and express the optimization in the scaled variable \(y=Lx\),

\[Ax=b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (AL^{-1})y=b,\]
\[x^TWx=\|y\|_2^2 .\]

Thus the usual minimum-norm singular-value solution applies to the scaled matrix. An alternative derivation introduces a multiplier \(\lambda\). Differentiating \(\tfrac12x^TWx-\lambda^T(Ax-b)\) gives \(Wx=A^T\lambda\). Substituting in the constraints yields

\[(AW^{-1}A^T)\lambda=b,\]
\[x=W^{-1}A^T(AW^{-1}A^T)^{-1}b .\]

The reduced matrix is positive definite for independent constraint rows. For any feasible perturbation \(h\) with \(Ah=0\), the cross term in the objective vanishes:

\[(x+h)^TW(x+h)-x^TWx=2\lambda^TAh+h^TWh=h^TWh .\]

This establishes uniqueness and explains the role of the weighting. Directions that data leave unresolved are assigned by a stated metric. Changing units while transforming both \(A\) and the metric consistently preserves the physical result.

Worked example

Distribute a measured total with unequal motion costs

A balance gives \(x_1+x_2=10\) and the squared cost is \(x_1^2+4x_2^2\). Stationarity requires \(x_1=4x_2\), yielding \((8,2)\) at cost \(80\). The unweighted minimum-norm pair \((5,5)\) satisfies the balance but has weighted cost \(125\). There is no change in agreement with the measurement.

Second application

Check invariance under a change of coordinate units

Write a second balance \(u+v=6\) and equal physical cost \(u^2+v^2\). If \(w=100v\), the new row is \((1,0.01)\) and the cost is \(u^2+0.0001w^2\). The weighted solution is \(u=3,w=300\), recovering \(v=3\). Applying an unweighted norm to the rescaled coordinates instead gives \(u=6/(1.0001)\simeq5.9994\), exposing a unit-dependent extra assumption.

Find directions that a rectangular map measures separately

For real \(A\), the matrix \(A^TA\) is symmetric positive semidefinite since \(v^TA^TAv=\|Av\|^2\). Choose orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_i\) with positive eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\) and define \(u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i\). Dotting two such vectors shows that the \(u_i\) are orthonormal. Completing both bases gives the singular value decomposition \(A=U\Sigma V^T\). Each \(\sigma_i\) is a gain from one parameter direction into a corresponding output direction. Zero singular values indicate directions that no observation in this model can distinguish.

To minimise \(\|Ax-b\|_2\), express both vectors in these orthogonal bases. For each positive singular value the residual component vanishes by taking \(v_i^Tx=u_i^Tb/\sigma_i\). Output components outside the range remain unavoidable, while input components in the nullspace can take any value without changing the fit. Setting those free components to zero gives the minimum-norm least-squares answer. This derivation identifies the pseudoinverse without assuming either a square matrix or a unique exact solution.

Distinguish an unobserved coordinate from an error

Suppose two instruments read \(b_1=x_1+x_2\) and \(b_2=0.02x_3\), giving readings 4 and 0.06. One singular value is \(\sqrt2\), corresponding to the mean direction of \(x_1,x_2\); another is 0.02 along \(x_3\). The difference \(x_1-x_2\) is unobserved. The minimum-norm exact fit is \((2,2,3)^T\), and every \((2+s,2-s,3)^T\) has identical data. More precision on the mean measurement cannot determine the hidden difference.

A noise change of +0.01 in the second reading raises the reconstructed third parameter by 0.5. If the admissible parameter scale is one, this is a substantial perturbation. Truncating the 0.02 singular direction instead sets \(x_3=0\). Then the residual grows from zero to the entire second reading, but its noise no longer controls the fitted third coordinate. The decision trades bias against uncertainty; it does not prove that the true third parameter is zero.

Assess a reduced measurement model

Keeping the largest \(k\) singular components produces \(A_k=\sum_{i\leq k}\sigma_i u_iv_i^T\). In the spectral norm its error is \(\sigma_{k+1}\): expansion in the right singular basis gives this upper bound, and applying the difference to \(v_{k+1}\) attains it. Any rank-\(k\) alternative must annihilate some unit vector in the span of the first \(k+1\) right singular vectors, on which the original map has norm at least \(\sigma_{k+1}\). Hence no lower-rank replacement improves the worst-case absolute output error.

That best-approximation result concerns forward predictions in a specified norm. Inverting small singular values magnifies noise, so a good low-rank approximation for forward use does not automatically determine the appropriate inverse for parameter recovery. Work with physically scaled parameters and noise-weighted observations before deciding which singular components are meaningful.

Choose an output before discarding a direction

Suppose an instrument observes \(x_1\) accurately and \(0.001x_2\) with uncertainty 0.01. The second variable alone has uncertainty of order ten. The output \(x_1\), however, has no projection onto the poorly observed direction. Its uncertainty receives no contribution from that amplification. Conversely the output \(x_1+x_2\) inherits the full uncertainty. This distinction can change whether a rank decision is acceptable. The spectrum describes the data acquisition matrix, while the projection of the chosen observable onto right singular vectors determines which losses of information affect the reported result.

Prove that truncation is optimal in two matrix norms

When compressing a dense linear map \(A\), decide which discrepancy is being minimised over all approximations of rank at most \(k\). Its spectral norm measures the largest output error over unit inputs. For any rank-\(k\) map \(B\), consider the span of the first \(k+1\) right singular vectors of \(A\). Its dimension exceeds the rank of the restriction of \(B\), so there exists a unit vector \(w\) in this span satisfying \(Bw=0\). Therefore \(\|(A-B)w\|=\|Aw\|\ge\sigma_{k+1}\). Retaining the first \(k\) singular pairs attains this lower bound, as its discarded part has largest singular value exactly \(\sigma_{k+1}\).

For the Frobenius norm, project each column of \(A\) into the column space of a candidate \(B\). Orthogonal projection is the closest approximation with columns in that space. If its projector is \(P\), the captured squared norm is \(\sum_j\sigma_j^2\|Pu_j\|^2\). The weights \(\|Pu_j\|^2\) lie between zero and one and sum to at most \(k\). The largest possible weighted sum assigns weight one to the \(k\) largest squared singular values. Hence the optimal discarded Frobenius norm is \((\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2)^{1/2}\). Both arguments require the norm and coordinate scaling just stated. Entrywise maximum error or application-specific weighted errors generally have different optimisers.

Decide how many responses a reduced map must reproduce

Suppose the four singular values of a unit-scaled instrument model are 8, 3, 2 and 1. A rank-two approximation has best possible worst-case gain error two and Frobenius error \(\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5\). Its relative squared Frobenius loss is \(5/(64+9+4+1)=5/78\), approximately 6.41 percent. Describing this as a loss of just 6.41 percent can be misleading if the experiment deliberately excites the third singular direction. For that unit input, the exact output has length two and the compressed output is zero, giving complete loss of the signal of interest.

Suppose instead inputs have equal independent unit variance in all four right singular coordinates. Then the expected squared error in the output is the sum of the discarded squared gains, exactly five. If the third coordinate is excited with variance one hundred while the others retain variance one, the error from truncating that coordinate alone becomes 400. Under that different usage distribution the meaningful objective is \(\|(A-B)C^{1/2}\|_F\), where \(C\) is the input covariance.

In these diagonal coordinates the weighted gains become \(8,3,20,1\). The best rank-two design for expected output error now retains directions three and one, and the discarded expected squared error becomes \(3^2+1^2=10\). The ranking changed because the physical question changed. It did not change the optimality theorem for the unweighted matrix; it changed which matrix correctly represents the intended error measure.

When an input covariance is singular, some directions are never excited under the stated design. The weighted objective can be indifferent to large errors in just those directions. A later change of operating regime may activate them, so a reduced model qualified for one input ensemble should retain the conditions under which its ranking was derived.

Decide whether a low-rank approximation is worthwhile

The best rank-\(k\) approximation discards the tail of the singular spectrum. Its relative Frobenius error is the square root of discarded squared singular values divided by total squared singular values, with the square root applied to the complete ratio.

Chapter 9 · Graph structure and fill-in

Predict which entries appear when an unknown is eliminated and use graph ordering to compare storage for two equivalent factorisations.

From first principles

Derive fill-in from one sparse elimination step

Represent a symmetric matrix as a graph with one vertex per unknown and edges at its nonzero off-diagonal entries. Reorder the first pivot to the top and write its diagonal entry as \(a\ne0\), with couplings \(c\) to the remaining vertices and a trailing block \(B\). Solving the pivot equation for its unknown gives

\[a x+c^Ty=f,\]
\[x=(f-c^Ty)/a .\]

Insert this expression into the remaining equations. The matrix update at a pair of remaining vertices is

\[B_{\rm new}=B-\frac{cc^T}{a},\]
\[(B_{\rm new})_{ij}=B_{ij}-\frac{c_i c_j}{a}.\]

If both \(i\) and \(j\) touch the pivot, a new entry can appear even when \(B_{ij}=0\). This is fill-in. A symbolic elimination graph therefore joins all remaining neighbours before removing the pivot. Exact numerical cancellation can remove a predicted entry, so the graph describes the general pattern. Choosing vertices with fewer or better-connected neighbours can reduce fill and factorization work.

Derivation and applications

Reduce a conducting star and preserve boundary currents

In a resistor network, symbolic fill has an observable magnitude. Consider one interior node of potential \(u\) connected to boundary nodes with prescribed potentials \(v_i\) through positive conductances \(g_i\). Assume ideal linear resistors and no independent current injected at the interior node. Charge conservation there requires the sum of currents into it to vanish. With \(G=\sum_i g_i\), the interior voltage is the conductance-weighted average

\[\sum_i g_i(u-v_i)=0,\]
\[u=\frac{\sum_i g_i v_i}{G}.\]

The boundary current entering the star at terminal \(i\) is \(I_i=g_i(v_i-u)\). Substitute the reconstructed \(u\), then collect potential differences relative to the other terminals. The reduced graph contains a physical branch between every pair of formerly separate neighbours,

\[I_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{g_i g_j}{G}(v_i-v_j),\]
\[g_{ij}^{\rm eff}=\frac{g_i g_j}{G}.\]

Summing the currents gives zero by pairwise cancellation. Multiplying the currents by the corresponding terminal voltages and summing gives the dissipated power, which must agree before and after elimination,

\[\sum_i v_i I_i=\sum_i g_i(v_i-u)^2=\sum_{i<j}g_{ij}^{\rm eff}(v_i-v_j)^2 .\]

The two graphs can have very different numbers of branches while producing identical boundary measurements. An interior current source would additionally transfer a load vector to the terminals and cannot be dropped in a pure graph reduction.

Worked example

Reduce a three-terminal network

With conductances \(1,2,3\,\mathrm S\), the new pairwise conductances are \(1/3,1/2,1\,\mathrm S\) for pairs \(12,13,23\). For terminal voltages \(1,0,0\,\mathrm V\), the original centre sits at \(1/6\,\mathrm V\). Current from the first terminal is \(5/6\,\mathrm A\), equal to \((1/3+1/2)\times1\,\mathrm A\) in the reduced graph. Both dissipate \(5/6\,\mathrm W\).

Second application

Price the fill of a high-degree node

A centre joined to ten isolated boundary nodes by identical \(5\,\mathrm S\) branches produces \(10\times9/2=45\) reduced branches of \(25/50=0.5\,\mathrm S\). Driving one terminal to \(2\,\mathrm V\) with the other nine grounded gives current \(9(0.5)(2)=9\,\mathrm A\). The unreduced centre is at \(0.2\,\mathrm V\), giving \(5(2-0.2)=9\,\mathrm A\) by an independent balance.

Interpret matrix entries as connections

For a symmetric sparse stiffness matrix, put one graph vertex at each unknown and an edge between distinct vertices whose corresponding entry is nonzero. A spring, diffusion face or shared element typically couples nearby unknowns, so the assembled matrix has far fewer entries than a dense array. Gaussian elimination changes this graph. Partition a pivot \(a\), its column below the pivot \(v\) and the trailing block \(B\). Eliminating the pivot replaces \(B\) by the Schur complement \(B-vv^T/a\). For every pair of nonzero entries of \(v\), the outer product can create a coupling between their vertices.

Thus the remaining neighbours of the eliminated vertex can become a clique. These added entries are called fill. Some may cancel for particular coefficients, but symbolic analysis usually retains every position that can be nonzero. It predicts a storage pattern before numerical values are known and can often be reused when material properties or loads change on the same mesh.

Compare two orders on a star

Take four unknowns arranged as a centre joined to three leaves, with no direct leaf-to-leaf connections. Give each diagonal value 4 and each centre-to-leaf coupling −1. Eliminating the centre first subtracts \(1/4\) from every leaf diagonal and creates three off-diagonals of \(-1/4\) connecting the three pairs of leaves. Although those leaves were initially independent when the centre was specified, eliminating it lets a disturbance at one leaf affect every other.

Instead eliminate one leaf at a time. Each has only one remaining neighbour, so its elimination modifies only the central diagonal and creates no off-diagonal fill. Three eliminations lower the central diagonal from 4 to \(4-3/4=3.25\). The two factorizations give the same physical answer in exact arithmetic, but their intermediate matrices require different storage and work. Relabelling the equations and unknowns together preserves symmetry and changes which order the eliminator encounters.

Use separators on a mesh

A one-dimensional chain eliminated from an end retains its narrow bandwidth. Eliminating interior vertices in an inconvenient order can create distant couplings relative to the original numbering. In a two-dimensional mesh even a careful sweep can leave a long front of mutually coupled vertices. Nested dissection separates the graph into smaller disconnected regions by setting aside a separator, factors the interiors independently and processes that separator later.

There is a practical trade between the cost of constructing an ordering and the savings in repeated factorisations. For a fixed matrix shape with many load cases this overhead can be amortised. For unsymmetric or indefinite systems numerical pivoting may alter the predicted pattern to maintain accuracy. Record both the number of nonzeros in the original operator and the number stored in the factors. Quoting the former alone can severely underestimate memory requirements, particularly for a three-dimensional mesh whose separators contain many more nodes than a one-dimensional chain.

Count the work created by a mesh separator

Ordering influences more than the final number of nonzeros. In a two-dimensional \(m\)-by-\(m\) grid with nearest-neighbour couplings, remove a cross of order \(m\) unknowns and factor the resulting quadrants before the cross. Eliminating each interior communicates among the adjacent separator variables. In a generic factorisation their remaining block is dense: eliminating a separator of size proportional to \(m\) then costs order \(m^3\) arithmetic operations and stores order \(m^2\) factor entries. The cost of finding the ordering is separate from this symbolic estimate.

Repeat the argument recursively on the four quadrants of side approximately \(m/2\). At level \(j\) there are about \(4^j\) independent subdomains with separator side \(m/2^j\). Total dense-block storage for this level is proportional to \(4^j(m/2^j)^2=m^2\). There are order \(\log m\) levels, yielding storage \(O(m^2\log m)\). Arithmetic at that level is proportional to \(4^j(m/2^j)^3=m^3/2^j\); summing a geometric series gives \(O(m^3)\). In terms of the total unknown count \(N\simeq m^2\), these are \(O(N\log N)\) storage and \(O(N^{3/2})\) arithmetic.

Compare memory growth on a sequence of grids

To see why the two resource estimates differ, use an idealised separator count with top separator length 64 and seven levels through length one. Ignore constants and store the square of each separator length. The root contributes \(64^2=4096\) units; the four children of length 32 contribute \(4\cdot32^2=4096\) as well. Seven levels therefore contribute 28,672 units. For work counted as the cube of each length, the root contributes 262,144 units and its children 131,072. Summing all seven contributions gives \(262144(1+1/2+\cdots+1/64)=520192\).

Refining each spatial direction by a factor of two produces top length 128 and eight levels. Storage becomes \(8\cdot128^2=131072\), about 4.57 times as much. Work becomes \(128^3(2-1/128)=4177920\), about 8.03 times as much. The unknown count grew by four. A budget estimate assuming linear growth in both memory and factorisation time would miss the dominant cost early in this idealised sequence. The constants are structural units, not predictions in bytes or seconds.

These estimates assume balanced geometric partitions, bounded local connectivity and a factorisation that does not introduce unexpected pivot exchanges. Strong global couplings can destroy small separators; in three dimensions the separator of an \(m^3\) grid is a surface of order \(m^2\) unknowns. Substituting surface size into the same argument immediately predicts much heavier top-level work. Before extrapolating timings, inspect actual separator sizes and factor nonzeros for the physical mesh family at hand.

The same symbolic factorisation can often serve many load cases on an unchanged mesh. After numerical factors have been computed, each new pair of triangular substitutions has work proportional to the stored factor entries rather than the cubic separator work. A memory increase can therefore affect every repeated solve even when the one-time factorisation cost is amortised over many sources.

Predict the cost of moving from a sheet to a solid

For a three-dimensional \(m\)-by-\(m\)-by-\(m\) grid, divide a cube into eight smaller cubes separated by coordinate planes. An idealised top separator has order \(m^2\) unknowns rather than order \(m\). At level \(j\) the separator storage is proportional to \(8^j(m/2^j)^4=m^4/2^j\), and separator arithmetic to \(8^j(m/2^j)^6=m^6/8^j\). Both level sums are geometrically dominated by the largest separators. In terms of \(N=m^3\) unknowns the resulting estimates are \(O(N^{4/3})\) storage and \(O(N^2)\) factorisation work. The logarithmic storage factor of the two-dimensional sheet has given way to a different power of system size.

Doubling resolution along each axis multiplies \(N\) by eight, asymptotic factor storage by roughly sixteen, and factor arithmetic by roughly sixty-four. Suppose the factors of a current three-dimensional problem occupy 3 GB and take 90 seconds to compute. Applying these scalings gives about 48 GB and 96 minutes after such refinement, subject to ordering and machine efficiency. By contrast, applying the existing factors to each extra load scales primarily with factor entries. If memory is limited, the predicted top separator may rule out the planned direct factorisation before the nominal total number of input matrix entries does. An iterative alternative should then be compared at the actual required tolerance and with the cost of its preconditioner included.

Chapter 10 · Arnoldi iteration and GMRES

Construct an orthonormal basis for a nonsymmetric operator and use a small least-squares problem to choose an approximation.

From first principles

Project a large operator onto successive Krylov directions

For \(Ax=b\), start with residual \(r_0=b-Ax_0\ne0\). Choose the first basis vector as \(v_1=r_0/\beta\), where \(\beta=\|r_0\|_2\). Repeated matrix products generate a search space, but their columns can become nearly parallel. At step \(j\), remove previously resolved components of \(Av_j\) before normalizing the remainder,

\[h_{ij}=v_i^TAv_j,\]
\[w=Av_j-\sum_{i=1}^j h_{ij}v_i,\]
\[h_{j+1,j}=\|w\|_2 .\]

If \(h_{j+1,j}>0\), take \(v_{j+1}=w/h_{j+1,j}\). The resulting Arnoldi identity \(AV_k=V_{k+1}\bar H_k\) expresses each large product using a small upper-Hessenberg matrix. A correction \(V_ky\) gives the residual norm

\[\|r_0-AV_ky\|_2=\|\beta e_1-\bar H_k y\|_2 .\]

Orthonormality justifies the equality. GMRES solves the smaller least-squares problem to minimize the actual residual over this search space. When the new norm is zero the subspace is already invariant, and no further independent direction is available through another ordinary Arnoldi step.

Derivation and applications

Calculate exactly when the first GMRES step stalls

For one Krylov step the only available correction is a multiple of the initial residual \(r=b-Ax\). The resulting change in the residual is parallel to \(Ar\). Assume a real nonsingular \(A\) and \(r\ne0\), which guarantees \(Ar\ne0\). To choose the coefficient \(\alpha\), expand the squared residual norm,

\[\|r-\alpha Ar\|_2^2=r^Tr-2\alpha(Ar)^Tr+\alpha^2\|Ar\|_2^2 .\]

The derivative vanishes at the coefficient of the orthogonal projection of \(r\) onto \(Ar\). Hence the new residual is perpendicular to the direction in which it was allowed to change,

\[\alpha_*=\frac{(Ar)^Tr}{\|Ar\|_2^2},\]
\[(Ar)^T(r-\alpha_*Ar)=0 .\]

Let \(\theta\) denote the angle between \(r\) and \(Ar\). At the minimum the squared length removed is exactly the squared length of that projection. The remaining fraction of residual is

\[\frac{\|r_{\rm new}\|}{\|r\|}=\sqrt{1-\frac{[(Ar)^Tr]^2}{\|Ar\|^2\|r\|^2}}=|\sin\theta|.\]

Thus equal or opposite directions allow an exact one-step solve, while perpendicular directions deliver no change. This is purely geometric and applies without symmetry. It also clarifies why restarting after one step can be costly: later basis vectors could expose a direction inaccessible on the first line.

Worked example

Compute the residual reduction for a shear

Use \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(r=(0,1)^T\). Then \(Ar=(2,1)^T\), \(\alpha_*=1/5\), and \(r_{\rm new}=(-0.4,0.8)^T\). Its norm is \(\sqrt{0.8}=0.894\). The dot product with \(Ar\) is \(-0.8+0.8=0\), an independent check that the selected coefficient minimizes the residual along this line.

Second application

Detect stagnation despite a nonsingular operator

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,-1)\) and \(r=(1,\sqrt2)^T\). The dot product \(r^TAr=2-2=0\) forces the first coefficient to zero. A second direction completes a two-dimensional space because \(Ar=(2,-\sqrt2)^T\) is independent of \(r\). In fact \(A^{-1}r=(0.5,-\sqrt2)^T=-0.5r+0.5Ar\). The two-direction correction solves the problem exactly although a one-step restart would repeat the same state.

A residual as a polynomial

Consider a square nonsingular matrix and an initial residual \(r_0=b-Ax_0\). A correction in the span of \(r_0,Ar_0,\ldots,A^{k-1}r_0\) is a polynomial in \(A\) applied to \(r_0\). After subtracting the action of \(A\), the new residual is \(p_k(A)r_0\) with \(p_k(0)=1\). This constraint matters because the zero polynomial would certify convergence before the calculation began. Generalised minimal residual iteration, or GMRES, minimises the Euclidean norm over all such residuals. The spaces are nested, so the exact-arithmetic residual norms cannot increase during a cycle.

Replacing large products by small coordinates

Start with \(v_1=r_0/\beta\), where \(\beta=\|r_0\|_2\). For each new direction form \(w=Av_j\), subtract its projections onto the existing orthonormal columns, and normalise what remains. Each projection coefficient occupies one row of column \(j\) of a small matrix. The final normalising length occupies its first subdiagonal. This Arnoldi construction gives \(AV_k=V_{k+1}\bar H_k\). If the length vanishes, the current subspace is invariant and no further normalisation is possible.

\[\|b-A(x_0+V_ky)\|_2=\|V_{k+1}(\beta e_1-\bar H_ky)\|_2=\|\beta e_1-\bar H_ky\|_2.\]

The second equality uses orthonormality. Solve this small rectangular least-squares problem, for example by updating triangular factors using plane rotations. The resulting residual is orthogonal to the range of \(AV_k\). Repeated projection may be necessary when rounding spoils orthogonality. A freshly calculated physical residual can test the small model's estimate before reporting convergence.

One eigenvalue and two directions

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&4\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(x_0=0\), \(b=e_2\). The first correction is \(\alpha e_2\), so the squared residual is \(16\alpha^2+(1-\alpha)^2\). Differentiation gives \(\alpha=1/17\) and length \(4/\sqrt{17}\), about 0.9701. Almost the entire initial residual survives despite every eigenvalue being one. The second direction comes from the component of \(Ae_2\) perpendicular to \(e_2\), namely \(4e_1\). With both directions available the exact solution \((-4,1)^T\) is accessible, and its residual vanishes.

For a diagonal matrix equal to the identity, a single step would suffice for every right-hand side. Here a nonzero off-diagonal entry couples two coordinates and the minimal polynomial is \((z-1)^2\). Counting distinct eigenvalues alone misses that coupling.

Paying for a long memory

Each new direction needs storage and projections against earlier directions. Restarting after a fixed number releases memory, but discards those directions. A new cycle still starts from the best current iterate. It lacks the previous cycle's simultaneous freedom to alter its old coefficients. Record matrix-vector products and the residual after each restart when comparing costs. On the example, two directions retained together already achieve the exact answer, providing a direct check on a proposed implementation.

Read a preconditioned residual

Left preconditioning with \(M^{-1}\) makes GMRES minimise \(\|M^{-1}(b-Ax)\|\) instead. If \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,1000)\), a physical residual \((0,1)^T\) has preconditioned norm 0.001. A small number in the stopping display could therefore leave the second physical balance comparatively inaccurate. Right preconditioning solves \(AM^{-1}y=b\), then forms \(x=M^{-1}y\); for exact preconditioner actions its minimisation still uses the original residual. Compare work and physically meaningful residuals when assessing either choice, especially if the equations use different units.

How restarting can erase a necessary direction

GMRES minimises the full residual norm over its current correction space. A one-step restart retains only the first correction direction generated from the new residual. If that direction makes no angle with the gradient of the residual objective in the appropriate sense, progress can cease even for an invertible, perfectly conditioned matrix. To see the geometry, suppose the current residual is \(r\). The one-dimensional trial residual is \(r-\alpha Ar\). Minimising its squared norm for real arithmetic yields \(\alpha=(Ar)^Tr/\|Ar\|^2\). Thus no reduction occurs when \(Ar\) is perpendicular to \(r\). Restarting immediately recreates precisely the same subproblem.

Unrestarted GMRES can still add \(Ar\) and further powers to its Krylov space. In exact arithmetic it can therefore escape one-step stagnation. This distinction matters for storage allocation: reducing the basis length can change the convergence mechanism itself. Merely multiplying the allowed number of restart cycles cannot recover directions if every cycle begins and ends with the same residual. Conversely increasing the restart length helps only if the additional space contains directions that produce a lower residual.

Compare restart lengths for a quarter-turn operator

Set \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(b=(1,0)^T\), beginning at zero. Its transpose times itself is the identity, so its two-norm condition number is one. Yet \(Ab=(0,1)^T\) is perpendicular to the initial residual \(b\). The residual after trial step \(\alpha b\) is \((1,-\alpha)^T\), whose squared length is \(1+\alpha^2\). The best step is exactly zero. GMRES restarted every step thus leaves the residual norm at one forever.

With a two-vector basis use corrections \(x=\alpha b+\beta Ab=(\alpha,\beta)^T\). The residual is \((1+\beta,-\alpha)^T\), which vanishes for \(\alpha=0,\beta=-1\). The exact solution is reached. In polynomial language no degree-one real polynomial satisfying \(p(0)=1\) can vanish at both eigenvalues \(+i,-i\). The degree-two choice \(p(z)=1+z^2\) does so because \(A^2=-I\). Both formulations explain the same measurement without attributing the failure to singularity or loss of precision.

For a nonsingular real skew-symmetric matrix the one-step numerator vanishes for every real \(r\), since a scalar \(r^TAr\) equals its own negative after transposition. This family therefore offers a repeatable stress test for short-restart implementations. A preconditioner or a shift that alters the search geometry can change the result, but any claimed gain should be checked using the residual of the original equations and a comparable count of matrix-vector products.

There is also a simple residual recomputation check at convergence: for the two-step state \((0,-1)^T\), multiplying by the original matrix gives \((1,0)^T\) exactly. If a code reports the same state after one iteration, it has either used a different search direction or mislabelled its iteration count. Exposing the current basis length alongside the total matrix-vector count makes a restart experiment easier to reproduce.

For complex arithmetic, use the real or complex least-squares minimisation appropriate to the actual correction space; the perpendicularity argument above assumes real data.

Problem sheet 5 · Graphs and search spaces

Predict elimination fill and compute minimum-residual updates for two systems with identical eigenvalues.

Choose an elimination order

A four-vertex star has centre 1, joined only to leaves 2, 3 and 4. Its symmetric positive definite matrix has generic nonzero edge weights. Count newly introduced edges if the centre goes first. Give an ordering avoiding fill and explain why it works.

Worked solution

Eliminating 1 connects each pair of its surviving neighbours. The three unordered pairs are (2,3), (2,4) and (3,4), none previously present. They occupy three new positions in one triangular factor's remaining graph.

In order (2,3,4,1) each eliminated leaf has a single surviving neighbour. There is no pair to connect, so no fill occurs. Positive definiteness ensures diagonal pivots exist without changing this structural ordering.

Two minimum-residual steps

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(b=e_2\) and \(x_0=0\). Compute the first GMRES iterate and its residual norm. Show that the second unrestarted step can give zero residual.

Worked solution

Along \(x=\alpha e_2\) the squared residual is \(4\alpha^2+(1-\alpha)^2\). Its derivative is \(10\alpha-2\), so \(x_1=e_2/5\) and \(r_1=(-2/5,4/5)^T\). Its norm is \(2/\sqrt5\).

The directions \(e_2,Ae_2=(2,1)^T\) span the plane. Their combination can realise \(x_\star=(-2,1)^T\); multiplication gives \(Ax_\star=(0,1)^T\). Thus the second minimum is exactly zero.

Does the load matter?

Reuse the preceding matrix with \(b=e_1\). Determine the Krylov dimension and first residual. Generalise your result to loads \((c,0)^T\), and explain the discrepancy with the previous problem.

Worked solution

Now \(Ae_1=e_1\), so every power of \(A\) produces the same direction. Choosing \(x=e_1\) solves the system in one step. For \(c\ne0\), the dimension is one and \(x=ce_1\) already solves it. A zero load has zero initial residual.

The earlier load occupied the generalised eigenvector direction, so \(Ae_2\) added an independent component. Matrix size and eigenvalues agree in both tests, but the minimal polynomial relative to the starting residual changes.

Chapter 11 · Iterative refinement and backward error

Recover accuracy from an approximate factorisation and determine when a small residual genuinely certifies a linear solve.

From first principles

Derive a correction using the observed imbalance

For a computed solution \(\widehat x\) of \(Ax=b\), evaluate \(r=b-A\widehat x\). Assume \(A\) is nonsingular. Subtract the equation for \(\widehat x\) from the exact equation to identify the missing correction,

\[A(x_*-\widehat x)=r .\]

If the matrix factors are already available, a correction solve uses those factors at much lower cost than a fresh factorization. In floating point suppose the solve acts like an exact solve with a matrix \(M\) close to \(A\). Ignoring rounding in the residual and final addition for the moment, the update gives

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+M^{-1}r_k,\]
\[e_{k+1}=(I-M^{-1}A)e_k ,\]

where \(e_k=x_k-x_*\). A norm of the error propagation matrix below one gives a contraction. Accurate residual evaluation matters because its own rounding error becomes new correction data. A tiny change in the iterate can mean either that the equations are satisfied or that small corrections are being rounded away. The scaled residual distinguishes these cases.

Derivation and applications

Show why a componentwise backward error survives a change of units

Equations and unknowns often have very different numerical scales. A componentwise backward error measures how much each coefficient and measured load must change relative to its own magnitude to make a computed state exact. For fixed \(\widehat x\), ask for perturbations satisfying \((A+\Delta A)\widehat x=b+\Delta b\) with \(|\Delta A|\le\eta|A|\) and \(|\Delta b|\le\eta|b|\), entrywise. The residual then obeys

\[r=b-A\widehat x=\Delta A\,\widehat x-\Delta b,\]
\[|r_i|\le\eta\bigl(|A||\widehat x|+|b|\bigr)_i .\]

Each row can attain any imbalance between these two extremes by choosing signs for its coefficient and load perturbations and scaling them together. Therefore the smallest admissible common relative tolerance is

\[\eta_*=\max_i\frac{|r_i|}{(|A||\widehat x|+|b|)_i}.\]

A zero denominator means that row has zero imbalance for the specified state; assign its ratio zero. Now change equation units with a positive diagonal \(D\) and coordinate scales with another positive diagonal \(S\). Use \(A'=DAS,\ b'=Db,\ \widehat x'=S^{-1}\widehat x\). Direct substitution gives

\[r'=Dr,\]
\[|A'||\widehat x'|+|b'|=D\bigl(|A||\widehat x|+|b|\bigr).\]

Every row ratio is unchanged. The plain norm of a residual can instead change by orders of magnitude under exactly the same re-expression. A small componentwise error means a nearby model fits; the forward uncertainty also depends on the conditioning of that model.

Worked example

Compare two differently scaled balances

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,1000)\), \(b=(2,1000)^T\), and \(\widehat x=(1.01,0.999)^T\), the residual is \((-0.020,1)^T\). Row ratios are \(0.020/4.020=0.00498\) and \(1/1999=0.000500\). The first equation sets the backward error despite the larger absolute residual in the second.

Second application

Change coordinate and equation units simultaneously

For a scalar balance \(3x=12\), test \(\widehat x=3.9\). Its backward error is \(0.3/(11.7+12)=0.01266\). Express \(x=100x'\) and multiply the equation by \(0.01\). The transformed equation is \(3x'=0.12\), with \(\widehat x'=0.039\), residual \(0.003\), and denominator \(0.237\). Their ratio again gives \(0.01266\), verifying invariance directly.

A correction equation

Suppose a large matrix has already been factored at modest precision. Let \(x\) be the resulting approximation and recompute \(r=b-Ax\) using the original coefficients. The exact missing correction solves \(Ad=r\). Reusing the approximate factors is cheap because the expensive elimination has finished. If their effective matrix is \(M\), one refinement step solves \(Md=r\) and updates \(x\leftarrow x+d\). Forming the residual from the factors themselves could erase the very discrepancy being measured.

With \(e=x_\star-x\), the residual equals \(Ae\) in exact arithmetic. Substitution yields \(e_{\mathrm{new}}=(I-M^{-1}A)e\). Thus a sufficient convergence condition in a chosen induced norm is \(\|I-M^{-1}A\|<1\). The size of the initial error affects how many corrections are needed, while the quality of the approximate inverse controls the contraction. The availability of more accurate residual arithmetic matters once cancellation in \(b-Ax\) competes with the true defect.

Diagonal model for imperfect factors

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(3,7)\), \(b=(3,7)^T\), and use \(M=\operatorname{diag}(3,8)\) throughout. The first approximate solve gives \(x_0=(1,0.875)^T\). Its residual is \((0,0.875)^T\), and the inexpensive correction is \((0,0.109375)^T\). Hence the second component becomes 0.984375. The corresponding errors in that coordinate decrease from \(1/8\) to \(1/64\). Each further update divides the error by eight because \(1-7/8=1/8\). The exact first component stays fixed.

If the incorrect factor were 3 instead of 8 in the second equation, its error multiplier would be \(1-7/3=-4/3\). Iterating would alternate across the solution with increasing amplitude. Extra residual accuracy could reveal that failure promptly but could never turn this correction matrix into a contraction.

Asking which equation was solved

A backward error asks for the smallest perturbation of the input for which the computed vector is exact. For relative, componentwise changes to both coefficients and load, \(|\Delta A|\leq\eta|A|\) and \(|\Delta b|\leq\eta|b|\), the rowwise correction must absorb \(r_i\). The triangle inequality bounds its capacity by \(\eta(|A||x|+|b|)_i\). Choosing perturbation signs independently within each row attains this capacity, giving

\[\eta=\max_i\frac{|r_i|}{(|A||x|+|b|)_i}.\]

A row with zero denominator and zero residual contributes zero. In the example's second row the initial backward error is \(0.875/(7\cdot0.875+7)=1/15\), even though its forward error is one eighth of the true component. After one correction the denominator is 13.890625 and the residual is 0.109375. The resulting backward error is \(1/127\). Uniformly changing that row's units leaves both ratios unchanged.

A stopping decision

Record the residual obtained using the original data and monitor the size of each accepted correction. Once the residual is comparable to accumulated rounding in its evaluation, more corrections may stagnate. A small backward error then certifies compatibility with nearly identical equations. Converting it to a forward accuracy statement also needs sensitivity information about those equations and the particular output of interest.

Separate data accuracy from solve accuracy

Suppose the second load in the diagonal example was measured as \(7.00\pm0.07\). Even an exact linear solve then gives a second coordinate known only to about 0.01. The initial solve error of 0.125 matters relative to that scale. Two corrections already reduce this error to about 0.00195. Further corrections improve the numerical solution of the nominal input but cannot resolve variation in the measured load. Stating both uncertainties clarifies the practical value of the last digits.

Track residual error through the correction equation

Let the approximate correction solve use a nonsingular matrix \(M\). Suppose residual evaluation returns \(\hat r=Ae+\delta r\), where \(\delta r\) includes accumulated rounding, and the state update has an additional absolute error \(\delta x\). One correction yields \(e_{\rm new}=(I-M^{-1}A)e-M^{-1}\delta r-\delta x\) if \(e=x_\star-x\). A contraction \(\|I-M^{-1}A\|\le q<1\) shrinks the first term, but every iteration injects new errors. If their combined effect is bounded by \(d\), induction gives \(\|e_k\|\le q^k\|e_0\|+d(1-q^k)/(1-q)\).

This inequality separates an initial geometric improvement from a possible long-run floor. A residual-evaluation bound must use scales of the terms being subtracted, such as \(|b|+|A||x|\), rather than the size of the tiny mathematical residual alone. Also distinguish errors in evaluating the residual from the final rounding of \(x+d\): very small corrections may be accurate but cannot change a stored state if they fall below its local spacing. Computing residuals in higher precision helps only when that residual error is a limiting contribution.

Resolve a correction lost in a cancellation

Consider the decimal thought experiment \(1000x_1+x_2=1001,\ x_1+x_2=2\), whose exact state is \((1,1)^T\). Store the candidate \((1.00,0.990)^T\) and evaluate terms using three significant decimal digits with rounding to nearest. Even if the original input coefficients and load are kept exactly, evaluating the first row naively makes \(1000+0.990\) round to \(1.00\times10^3\); the rounded representation of the right-hand side 1001 is also \(1.00\times10^3\). A residual assembled entirely in that low precision therefore sees a first component of zero. The second component, \(2-1.99=0.01\), remains visible.

Computing the residual using the original data with adequate precision gives \((0.01,0.01)^T\). Subtracting these two correction equations yields \(999d_1=0\), hence \(d_1=0,\ d_2=0.01\), recovering the exact state in one exact correction solve. If instead the low-precision residual \((0,0.01)^T\) is used with the same exact correction matrix, subtraction yields \(999d_1=-0.01\), giving \(d_1\approx-0.00001001,\ d_2\approx0.01001001\). Part of the estimated correction is now allocated to a coordinate that was already exact.

The example uses an exact correction solve to isolate the residual effect. Low-precision factors introduce a separate perturbation. If 1001 had first been irreversibly stored as 1000 in the only surviving copy of the problem, later high-precision arithmetic could not restore the intended equation. Preserving the original coefficients and loads is therefore part of the method whenever refinement is expected to recover accuracy relative to higher-precision input data.

One can estimate which digit was lost without rerunning the solve. The absolute first-row summands have total magnitude about one thousand, while their desired residual is one hundredth. The computation is extracting a quantity roughly one hundred thousand times smaller than its large terms. By comparison the corresponding factor in the second row is only a few hundred. Precision can therefore be inadequate in one equation long before it limits the others.

Chapter 12 · Eliminating constraints with Schur complements

Eliminate auxiliary variables, recover constraint forces and account for the sign and conditioning of a reduced system.

From first principles

Eliminate one block of unknowns before solving the rest

A coupled model can have different types of unknowns, such as internal and boundary displacements. Write its linear system in block form with a nonsingular first block \(K\). The first row expresses one group in terms of the other,

\[Ku+Cv=f,\]
\[u=K^{-1}(f-Cv).\]

Substitute into the remaining balance \(Bu+Dv=g\). Collect all terms that still depend on \(v\) to obtain a smaller system and a recovery step,

\[(D-BK^{-1}C)v=g-BK^{-1}f,\]
\[Ku=f-Cv .\]

The new coefficient matrix is the Schur complement of \(K\). The term in the right-hand side is needed even when no external load acts directly on the retained variables. In practice, notation such as \(K^{-1}C\) denotes solves with columns of \(C\) or an operator action; explicit inversion is unnecessary. If the full matrix and \(K\) are both nonsingular, so is the Schur complement. Very small reduced eigenvalues can signal a weakly constrained remaining quantity.

Derivation and applications

Update a solved system after adding one spring or penalty

Suppose factors of a symmetric positive definite matrix \(A\) already exist and a small model change adds one positive stiffness \(k\) acting on the relative coordinate \(u^Tx\). The added energy is \(\tfrac12k(u^Tx)^2\), whose gradient adds \(ku(u^Tx)\) to the original balance. Introduce its scalar force \(t=ku^Tx\). Solving the old matrix with the new internal force gives

\[(A+kuu^T)x=b,\]
\[x=x_0-zt,\]
\[Ax_0=b,\]
\[Az=u .\]

Use the constitutive equation for \(t\) to eliminate the full state and solve a one-dimensional Schur complement. The scalar \(u^Tz=u^TA^{-1}u\) is positive for nonzero \(u\). Hence the denominator is safely nonzero for a positive added stiffness,

\[(k^{-1}+u^Tz)t=u^Tx_0,\]
\[x=x_0-z\frac{k\,u^Tx_0}{1+k\,u^Tz}.\]

The new relative displacement follows by applying \(u^T\) again,

\[u^Tx=\frac{u^Tx_0}{1+k\,u^TA^{-1}u}.\]

This explicitly shows how the added stiffness reduces extension. If the old extension is zero, adding the spring changes neither the old force balance nor the solution. As \(k\) becomes arbitrarily large the extension tends to zero while the internal force approaches a finite constraint reaction. The original factors can serve many choices of stiffness when the location \(u\) stays fixed.

Worked example

Retrofit a link between two supported nodes

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,4)\,\mathrm{N/m}\), load \(b=(2,0)^T\,\mathrm N\), and \(u=(1,-1)^T\). Initially \(x_0=(1,0)^T\,\mathrm m\); the unit response \(z=(0.5,-0.25)^T\,\mathrm{m/N}\) gives \(u^Tz=0.75\,\mathrm{m/N}\). Adding \(k=4\,\mathrm{N/m}\) gives \(t=1\,\mathrm N\), \(x=(0.5,0.25)^T\,\mathrm m\) and relative extension \(0.25\,\mathrm m\).

Two coupled sets of equations

A constrained equilibrium may contain displacements \(u\) and reaction coefficients \(\lambda\). Suppose the energy Hessian \(K\) is symmetric positive definite and the constraint matrix \(B\) has independent rows. With applied force \(f\) and prescribed values \(g\), stationarity of \(u^TKu/2-f^Tu+\lambda^T(Bu-g)\) gives \(Ku+B^T\lambda=f\) and \(Bu=g\). The full symmetric matrix is indefinite. Minimising the quadratic energy under the constraints still gives a unique displacement because the energy curves upward along every permissible direction.

Deriving a reduced operator

Solve the first equation formally for \(u=K^{-1}(f-B^T\lambda)\). Inserting this expression into the constraint gives a smaller positive system,

\[S\lambda=BK^{-1}f-g,\qquad S=BK^{-1}B^T.\]

The literal lower diagonal block left by elimination is \(-S\). Multiplying both sides by minus one produces the positive form written above. For any nonzero vector \(z\), full row rank implies \(B^Tz\ne0\), hence \(z^TSz=(B^Tz)^TK^{-1}(B^Tz)>0\). This proof explains why redundant constraints create a singular reduced matrix. Even in that case the physical displacement can remain unique while reactions assigned to duplicate equations become ambiguous.

Two springs sharing a displacement

Let \(K=\operatorname{diag}(2,8)\) in N/m and apply \(f=(10,0)^T\) N. A rigid link demands \(u_1-u_2=0\), so \(B=(1,-1)\) and \(g=0\). The uncoupled displacement is \((5,0)^T\) m. The reduced compliance is \(1/2+1/8=5/8\) m/N, giving \(\lambda=5/(5/8)=8\) N. Recovering the displacement gives \(u=(1,1)^T\) m. The first spring carries 2 N and the other carries 8 N. Independently, the common displacement under a total stiffness of 10 N/m is exactly one metre.

Prescribing a gap \(g=0.5\) m changes the reduced right-hand side to 4.5 m. The reaction drops to 7.2 N and the displacements become 1.4 and 0.9 m. Their difference verifies the new constraint. This calculation also identifies the multiplier sensitivity to a gap error as \(-1/S\), here \(-1.6\) N/m.

Solving without an explicit inverse

Applying \(S\) to a trial vector takes a multiplication by \(B^T\), a solve with \(K\), and a multiplication by \(B\). The reduced matrix may be dense even with local springs because the stiffness solve communicates through the structure. Reuse a factorisation when the load changes. With approximate inner solves, control their errors relative to the requested outer accuracy. Check force balance and compatibility independently after reconstructing \(u\). A tiny reduced residual obtained with inaccurate inner solves need not imply that the reconstructed state satisfies the original load equations.

A duplicate constraint as a diagnostic

Append a second row \(2B\) to the rigid-link constraint and prescribe a compatible right-hand side \(2g\). The new reduced matrix is \(S\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), which has determinant zero. Only the combination \(\lambda_1+2\lambda_2\) affects the applied reaction in the mechanical equations. In the zero-gap example every pair with that combination equal to 8 N gives the same displacement and resultant link force. A solver's complaint about a singular constraint block can thus expose a duplicated physical statement, even when a force measurement or displacement plot looks perfectly reasonable.

Condense internal degrees of freedom while preserving energy

Partition the degrees of freedom of a symmetric positive definite stiffness matrix into interior coordinates \(i\) and retained boundary coordinates \(b\). For a specified boundary displacement, the interior balance is \(K_{ii}u_i=f_i-K_{ib}u_b\). Eliminating it produces the boundary operator \(S=K_{bb}-K_{bi}K_{ii}^{-1}K_{ib}\) and the effective boundary load \(f_b-K_{bi}K_{ii}^{-1}f_i\). This elimination differs physically from setting the interior displacement to zero: the interior has been allowed to respond to the prescribed boundary value and applied load.

With zero load, insert \(u_i=-K_{ii}^{-1}K_{ib}u_b\) into the full strain energy. Completing the square in \(u_i\) shows that the minimum energy at fixed boundary coordinates is \(u_b^TSu_b/2\). Since the full stiffness is positive definite, any nonzero boundary vector yields positive minimum energy. This gives a physical proof that the condensed operator is positive definite. A free-floating component may instead have rigid-body modes; then the same proof establishes only nonnegativity, and some imposed displacement patterns cause no stress.

Reduce a spring chain and preserve the internal load

Two springs connect a fixed left support to an observable endpoint through an internal joint. Their stiffnesses are 6 and 3 N/mm. Let the internal and boundary displacements be \(u_i,u_b\), and apply forces 3 and 6 N at those nodes. The strain energy is \([6u_i^2+3(u_b-u_i)^2]/2\). Differentiating gives \(K_{ii}=9,\ K_{ib}=K_{bi}=-3,\ K_{bb}=3\). Condensing yields \(S=3-9/9=2\) N/mm and effective boundary load \(6-(-3)(3/9)=7\) N. Thus \(u_b=3.5\) mm. Recovering the internal state gives \(u_i=(3+3u_b)/9=1.5\) mm.

The first spring therefore carries \(6(1.5)=9\) N, and the second carries \(3(3.5-1.5)=6\) N. Their difference is the internal force of 3 N and the support reaction balances the total applied load of 9 N. Simply using the well-known series stiffness \(2\) N/mm with the endpoint force 6 N would yield 3 mm, missing the transmitted effect of the interior load. Both condensed stiffness and condensed right-hand side are necessary in a reduced model.

A local finite-element assembly can repeat this calculation for many independent element interiors, retaining only interface coordinates for the global solve. To compute internal stress afterward, keep enough local factor information to reconstruct each eliminated state. The boundary displacement alone does not directly encode the internal load distribution; two differently loaded elements can have the same endpoint displacement and different internal stresses. This distinction matters whenever reduction is justified using one observable but results are later requested for another.

Under a change in the prescribed end condition, the same reduced law can be used in the opposite direction. Hold the endpoint at \(u_b=0\) with the internal 3 N force still present and remove the applied endpoint load. The recovered internal displacement is \(1/3\) mm. The computed endpoint force required to hold it fixed is \(-3u_i=-1\) N. A boundary law containing only the series stiffness would predict zero force for this imposed zero displacement.

Reuse an inaccurate scalar factor

Solve \(5x=10\) using the approximate factor 6 for the initial solution and every correction. Derive the error after \(k\) corrections and count how many bring absolute error below 0.002.

Worked solution

The starting value \(10/6\) leaves \(e_0=1/3\). The correction adds \(5e_k/6\), so \(e_{k+1}=e_k/6\) and \(e_k=1/(3\cdot6^k)\).

Two corrections give \(1/108\approx0.00926\). Three give \(1/648\approx0.00154\), below the threshold. Residuals must use the original factor 5 because the initial value solves the approximate equation exactly.

Scale the units

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,1000)\), \(b=(1,1000)^T\), \(x=(0.99,1)^T\), compute the infinity norm of the residual and the maximum componentwise backward error. Repeat when the first row and its load are multiplied by 100.

Worked solution

Initially \(r=(0.01,0)^T\), hence its infinity norm is 0.01. The maximum row ratio is \(0.01/(0.99+1)=1/199\).

Rescaling changes the first residual to 1 and its denominator to 199. The maximum absolute residual increases hundredfold, whereas the backward error remains \(1/199\). The two systems describe exactly the same solution.

Prescribe a weighted displacement

Minimise \((u_1^2+4u_2^2)/2\) subject to \(u_1+2u_2=3\). Using \(Ku+B^T\lambda=0\), determine the reduced stiffness equation, the multiplier and the stored energy. Compare that energy with the admissible state \((3,0)\).

Worked solution

With \(K=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\), \(B=(1,2)\), the scalar compliance is \(1+4/4=2\). The reduced system is \(2\lambda=-3\). Back-substitution gives \(u=(1.5,0.75)^T\), satisfying the prescribed value.

The energy is \((2.25+4\cdot0.5625)/2=2.25\). The competing state has energy 4.5. Positive definiteness on the constraint line guarantees that the stationary state is its unique minimum.

Reconstruct a displacement measured almost twice in the same direction

Two gauges measure b = Ax with A having rows (1, 0) and (1, 0.01). With b = (1.000, 1.010) find x. Each reading has independent standard deviation 0.001. Estimate the standard deviation of each displacement and their covariance. Evaluate a proposed third gauge measuring only the second displacement.

Worked solution

The first equation gives x₁ = b₁ and differencing gives x₂ = 100(b₂−b₁) = 1. The solution is (1, 1). A residual of zero follows automatically for an invertible square system, even though the second displacement is obtained by subtracting two close readings.

The covariance follows by propagating the independent reading errors. Var(x₁) = 10⁻⁶ and Var(x₂) = 10⁴(2 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.020, so the standard deviations are 0.001 and 0.1414. The covariance is −100 × 10⁻⁶ = −0.0001. An upward perturbation of the first gauge increases x₁ but decreases the reconstructed x₂.

A third independent reading of x₂ with the same standard deviation carries much more precision along the weak direction. Fuse the old x₂ estimate with this reading using inverse variance: the new variance is 1/(1/0.020 + 1/10⁻⁶) ≈ 9.9995 × 10⁻⁷. In the joint fit solve the overdetermined system by QR. Repeating the original two directions could improve repeatability, but would retain the poor geometry.

Applied study

Separate residual, solution error and conditioning when solving linear systems.

Can a small residual hide a large error?

After discretising steady heat conduction, the unknown nodal temperatures form a vector \(x\). Heat balance at the nodes gives \(Ax=b\). Substitute an approximate solution \(\hat x\) back into this equation. The mismatch \(r=b-A\hat x\) is the residual. The temperature error \(e=x-\hat x\) satisfies \(Ae=r\). Even an exactly evaluated residual still has to pass through \(A^{-1}\) to tell us about temperature.

For nonsingular \(A\), the Euclidean norm gives \(\|e\|_2\leq\|A^{-1}\|_2\|r\|_2\). Since \(\|b\|_2\leq\|A\|_2\|x\|_2\), a nonzero right-hand side gives

\[\frac{\|e\|_2}{\|x\|_2}\leq \kappa_2(A)\frac{\|r\|_2}{\|b\|_2},\qquad \kappa_2(A)=\|A\|_2\|A^{-1}\|_2.\]

The condition number measures how strongly a problem can amplify a relative perturbation. The bound covers the worst direction. An error in a different direction may have a much smaller amplification.

Work through a poorly resolved direction

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,10^{-4})\), \(b=(1,10^{-4})^T\). The exact solution is \((1,1)^T\). The trial vector \((1,0)^T\) gives a residual of magnitude \(10^{-4}\), nearly \(10^{-4}\) relative to \(b\). Yet one component is missing altogether and the relative solution error is \(1/\sqrt{2}=0.707\). Along the second coordinate, multiplying by \(A\) suppresses an error by a factor of ten thousand.

Scale the second equation by \(10^4\). The exact solution stays the same and the scaled matrix is the identity. The new residual of the same trial vector is \((0,1)^T\). Always say which system and norm a reported stopping test uses. Temperature differences from a direct solve on a small mesh provide an independent check in physical units.

Choose an iteration from the matrix structure

For a symmetric positive definite matrix, solving \(Ax=b\) minimises the strictly convex quadratic \(J(x)=x^TAx/2-b^Tx\). Its negative gradient at \(\hat x\) is \(r\). Conjugate gradients (CG) searches in the span of \(r_0,Ar_0,\ldots\), using search directions orthogonal in the \(A\)-inner product. It minimises the energy error \(\|e\|_A=\sqrt{e^TAe}\) over the growing trial space.

In exact arithmetic CG terminates in at most as many iterations as there are distinct eigenvalues. A positive definite preconditioner \(M\) seeks a more favourable spectrum for \(M^{-1/2}AM^{-1/2}\). For a moderate problem a direct factorisation gives a convenient reference. Both methods solve a discrete system, whose mesh error persists even when the algebraic equations are solved to roundoff.
