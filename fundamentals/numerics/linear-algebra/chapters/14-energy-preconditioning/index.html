Coordinates in the energy

Suppose one displacement is measured in millimetres and the other in metres. A steepest-descent direction depends on the Euclidean lengths of the coordinates. The physical minimum does not. Let \(A\) be symmetric positive definite, \(D=\operatorname{diag}(A)\), and introduce \(y=D^{1/2}x\). Substituting \(x=D^{-1/2}y\) in the energy produces

\[\widetilde A=D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2},\]
\[\widetilde b=D^{-1/2}b.\]

The diagonal of the new matrix is one. Its off-diagonal entry is the original coupling divided by the geometric mean of the two direct stiffnesses. This is a dimensionless measure of interaction. The transformed gradient step maps back to \(x_{j+1}=x_j+\alpha D^{-1}(b-Ax_j)\).

Choosing a common step

For any eigenvector of the transformed matrix the iteration multiplies the error by \(1-\alpha\lambda\). With eigenvalues in \([m,M]\), equalise the two extreme absolute factors by taking \(1-\alpha m=-(1-\alpha M)\). This gives \(\alpha=2/(M+m)\) and worst-case contraction \((M-m)/(M+m)\). If \(m\) approaches zero the direction barely moves, however accurately the linear algebra is performed.

Two quite different causes of a slow solve

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(100,1)\), an optimal unscaled step shrinks the error by only \(99/101\). Diagonal scaling gives the identity. One step of length one then solves both components exactly. This is the effect of unlike units or stiffness scales.

For a unit-diagonal matrix with coupling 0.95, scaling leaves the matrix unchanged. Its eigenvalues are 1.95 and 0.05. Each optimal step can retain 95% of the error. The nearly free opposing motion is part of the problem itself. A preconditioner that represents the coupling, or a different physical constraint, is needed for faster convergence in that direction.

Tutorial on interpreting convergence

If a bound decreases from 1 to \(10^{-3}\) in \(k\) steps, how do you determine \(k\) from a known factor 0.8?

Worked solution

Take logarithms in \(0.8^k\le10^{-3}\). Because \(\log(0.8)\) is negative, division reverses the inequality. The smallest integer is 31.

Why should a conjugate-gradient implementation use a positive definite preconditioner?

Worked solution

In transformed variables CG minimises an energy for a symmetric positive definite operator. A positive definite preconditioner admits an invertible square root and transfers this property by congruence. An indefinite matrix does not define the corresponding norm.
