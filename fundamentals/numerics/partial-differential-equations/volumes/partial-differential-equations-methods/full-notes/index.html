Spatial discretisation and field equations · 1. Choose the physically admissible wave in a conservative update

A discontinuous scalar flow can satisfy integral conservation in more than one way. Derive the shock speed, use a convex entropy to distinguish a compression from an expansion and build a finite-volume flux from the resulting local solution.

Integrate across a moving discontinuity

Consider inviscid Burgers' equation \(u_t+(u^2/2)_x=0\). Where \(u\) is smooth its characteristics move at speed \(u\). For left state \(u_L>u_R\), faster left characteristics catch slower right ones and the profile steepens. If discontinuity position is \(x=st\), integrate the conservation law across a narrow moving control volume. The storage change from the moving boundary gives \(s(u_R-u_L)=f(u_R)-f(u_L)\), so for unequal states \(s=(u_L+u_R)/2\). The value is a conservation-law jump speed; applying the pointwise product \(u\,u_x\) across a discontinuity without defining it could predict another speed.

The same jump calculation formally allows \(u_L<u_R\), although a smooth monotone connection then spreads. To identify the physical weak solution, add a small positive viscosity \(\nu u_{xx}\) and consider its limiting dissipation. For convex entropy \(\eta=u^2/2\) with entropy flux \(q=u^3/3\), multiplication of the viscous equation by \(u\) gives \(\eta_t+q_x=\nu\eta_{xx}-\nu u_x^2\). As viscosity vanishes the entropy production is nonpositive in distributions. Across a sharp jump the coefficient is \([q]-s[\eta]=(u_R-u_L)^3/12\), where brackets mean right minus left. It is negative for a compression shock and positive for an expansion shock. Thus the latter satisfies mass conservation but violates this entropy criterion.

Construct the rarefaction from its self-similar coordinate

For a left-to-right increase seek \(u(x,t)=U(\xi)\) with \(\xi=x/t\). Substituting into the smooth equation gives \((U-\xi)U'=0\), so each region is constant or \(U=\xi\). Matching both states yields \(u_L\) for \(\xi<u_L\), \(\xi\) for \(u_L\le\xi\le u_R\), and \(u_R\) beyond. This continuous fan is the physically selected expansion. With \(u_L=-1,u_R=1\), the origin lies inside the fan and \(u(0,t)=0\). A stationary expansion jump would misleadingly carry flux \(1/2\) across that interface rather than the physical zero.

In a finite-volume scheme cell averages are updated by shared interface fluxes: \(\bar u_i^{n+1}=\bar u_i^n-(\Delta t/\Delta x)(F_{i+1/2}-F_{i-1/2})\). Solve the local Riemann problem between the piecewise-constant left and right cell states and take \(F=f(U(0))\). For a compression use the left state if \(s>0\) and the right if \(s<0\); either flux agrees for a stationary shock. For an expansion reaching across zero take zero; if the expansion moves wholly right use its left state, and if wholly left use its right. These cases give the scalar Godunov flux. The update telescopes internally because both neighbouring cells use exactly the same physical interface flux.

Distinguish shock location from smoothness and numerical thickness

A first-order Godunov discretisation under its CFL restriction is monotone and avoids spurious oscillations, but a moving discontinuity generally spreads over several cells. A centred pointwise difference may look sharper for smooth data while producing oscillations or the wrong propagation under discontinuous data. Test shock speeds from integrated cell mass or a consistent location reconstruction, compare fan areas and measure \(L^1\) error under refinement. The pointwise maximum error right at a jump can remain large even as the location and integral error converge. A visually thin front is not by itself an accuracy certificate.

For data \(u_L=1,u_R=-0.5\) the shock moves right at 0.25. At \(t=0.25\) it lies at \(x=0.0625\). In the interval \([-1,1]\) with unchanged boundary states, the total mass begins at 0.5 and grows by \([f(1)-f(-0.5)]t=(0.5-0.125)0.25=0.09375\), so the exact integral is 0.59375. A conservative numerical calculation can achieve this integral essentially to roundoff while retaining a visibly smeared shock. Conservation, entropy selection and resolved spatial profile are three independent questions.

Switch between a shock and a transonic fan

The experiment solves the finite-volume update on \([-1,1]\) with constant exterior states, a time step chosen for maximum CFL 0.8 and output time 0.25. Compare the conservative cell averages with the exact self-similar profile. Reverse both initial states to turn a compression into an expansion, then put the fan across zero. Increasing cell count changes numerical thickness, while the mass should follow the boundary flux difference at every resolution. Equal left and right states are a separate constant-preservation test.

Infer shock position from an integral measurement

A blurred profile does not require an arbitrary definition of its centre. Suppose the plateau values on \([-L,L]\) are measured reliably and a single discontinuity lies at \(X\). Its integrated state is \(M=(L+X)u_L+(L-X)u_R\). The equivalent sharp-interface position is therefore \(X=[M-L(u_L+u_R)]/(u_L-u_R)\). With \(L=1,\ u_L=1,\ u_R=-1/2\), a cell-sum reading \(M=0.59375\) gives \(X=0.0625\). A mass uncertainty of \(0.003\) gives a position uncertainty of \(0.002\) in the same coordinates. The inference becomes ill-conditioned as the plateau contrast tends to zero.

This provides a mesh-independent diagnostic when numerical viscosity smears the jump. Track both an area-based position and a measure of thickness, for example the distance between the two intermediate levels one-quarter and three-quarters across the jump. A conservative solver may retain a highly accurate area-based position while thickness changes strongly under refinement. In a rarefaction the same scalar area exists, but two real moving edges delimit a resolved fan. Replacing the fan by one jump would throw away precisely the information the entropy condition supplied.

Spatial discretisation and field equations · 2. Bound diffusion error by balancing a reconstructed flux

A temperature field from a finite-element calculation looks smooth. Reconstruct a heat flux which exactly balances the source, then derive an upper bound for the true energy error without knowing the exact temperature.

Identify the natural error measure in a diffusion problem

Consider \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla u)=f\) on a bounded domain, with homogeneous Dirichlet temperature for clarity and strictly positive scalar conductivity \(k(\mathbf x)\) bounded above and below. The weak form is \(\int k\nabla u\cdot\nabla w=\int f w\) for every test \(w\) vanishing on the boundary. A conforming approximate temperature \(v\) satisfies the prescribed boundary trace but may not solve the equation. Its energy error \(\|\nabla(u-v)\|_k^2=\int k|\nabla(u-v)|^2\) weights local gradient errors according to their physical conductivity. This differs from pointwise temperature error, total dissipation error or the absolute error of one designated sensor.

Define true heat flux \(q=-k\nabla u\). It obeys \(\nabla\cdot q=f\). Suppose a reconstructed flux \(\sigma\) obeys the same divergence exactly and has the prescribed normal flux on any Neumann boundary, with normal component continuous across interior mesh faces. Such fluxes belong naturally to a space often written \(H(\mathrm{div})\). Conforming temperature and equilibrated flux are separate requirements: a continuous piecewise-linear temperature gives generally discontinuous normal gradient flux across faces, even if all global nodal equations were solved accurately.

Prove an upper bound by cancelling a cross term

Take the weighted constitutive defect \(\sigma+k\nabla v=(\sigma-q)+k\nabla(v-u)\). Expand its squared \(k^{-1}\) norm. The cross term is \(2\int(\sigma-q)\cdot\nabla(v-u)\). Integrating by parts gives a volume term proportional to \(\nabla\cdot(\sigma-q)=0\) and a boundary term zero where \(v-u=0\) or the normal flux difference is prescribed zero. Interior normal continuity prevents an extra jump term. Hence

\[\|\sigma+k\nabla v\|_{k^{-1}}^2=\|\sigma-q\|_{k^{-1}}^2+\|\nabla(v-u)\|_k^2 .\]

The left side can be calculated from the approximation and reconstruction. It bounds the unknown energy error from above, with equality precisely when the reconstructed flux equals the true flux. A divergence-compatible but inaccurate \(\sigma\) still certifies an upper bound, albeit possibly a loose one. This identity is an instance of the Prager–Synge hypercircle principle. With approximated data or inexact equilibration, extra data oscillation and residual terms must be bounded before calling an indicator a guaranteed bound.

Carry out the entire construction on two elements

On \([0,1]\) let \(k=1,\ f=2,\ u(0)=u(1)=0\). The exact \(u=x(1-x)\) is available for checking although unnecessary for the estimator. The conforming piecewise-linear interpolant on two half-elements has slopes \(+0.5,-0.5\). Any flux \(\sigma(x)=2x+C\) exactly balances the source. Choose \(C\) to minimise \(\int(\sigma+v')^2dx\). Its derivative is \(2\int(2x+C+v')dx=2(1+C)\), since \(v(1)-v(0)=0\). Thus \(C=-1\), giving \(\sigma=2x-1=q\). The estimator is \(\sqrt{1/12}=0.288675\): on each half interval the defect is a linear ramp from \(-0.5\) to \(+0.5\) or its translated equivalent. Direct integration of \((u'-v')^2\) confirms equality.

If instead choose \(C=-0.8\), source equilibrium is still exact but the flux carries a uniform 0.2 offset. Its squared error contribution is 0.04, giving bound \(\sqrt{1/12+0.04}=0.3512\). Choosing an equilibrated flux means satisfying local balance; optimising its closeness to the constitutive flux sharpens the estimate. The true temperature error is unchanged in both comparisons. In more dimensions mixed finite elements or local face-flux reconstructions enforce shared normal flux degrees of freedom; local least-squares choices can improve sharpness without requiring a global exact solution.

Use element contributions without confusing local and global bounds

The squared estimator splits as \(\sum_K\int_K k^{-1}|\sigma+k\nabla v|^2\). Large element contributions can guide refinement and give a reproducible stopping rule for global energy error. The exact orthogonality proof, however, integrated over the full domain. Boundary terms between elements may fail to vanish on one element individually, so one local contribution need not be a strict independent upper bound on that element's actual error. For a particular output of interest, e.g. a mean temperature in a small sensor region, use the dual problem or a proven continuous inequality to translate energy uncertainty into output uncertainty.

Vary an equilibrated flux while holding the solution fixed

The source and exact quadratic temperature are those of the worked one-dimensional example. The plot compares the exact flux, the raw elementwise flux from the continuous linear temperature and a reconstructed flux with the correct divergence and a freely chosen constant offset. Changing this constant is permitted because the original problem specifies boundary temperatures. The mean energy-error contribution from an offset appears in quadrature with the true temperature-gradient error, exactly as in the derived identity. For two elements and offset 0.20 the bound is about 0.3512 while the actual energy error is 0.2887.

Increase the number of cells with the offset held fixed. The raw staircase becomes more accurate, but the reconstruction bound eventually plateaus near the absolute offset because the reconstructed flux still contains its deliberate uniform error. Set the offset to zero to recover the sharp certificate on each mesh. Notice that a constant flux offset preserves the source balance in this Dirichlet test; it would generally violate a specified Neumann flux at a boundary. A valid estimator depends as much on the boundary conditions as on the differential equation.

Spatial discretisation and field equations · 3. A Fourier Poisson solver and the trouble caused by unresolved products

In a periodic medium the constant part of a source decides whether a Poisson problem has any solution. Derive the Fourier solver, separate spectral and finite-difference symbols, and see how a nonlinear product can inject a false low-frequency source.

Find compatibility and gauge before dividing by a wave number

On a periodic interval of length \(2\pi\), solve \(-u''=f\). Integrating over one period gives \(0=\int f\) because periodic derivative values agree at the ends. This is an existence condition. If a solution exists, adding a constant changes neither field gradient nor source, so specify a mean \(u_0\) separately to fix uniqueness. These two roles sometimes get conflated in numerical code that simply sets the Fourier coefficient at zero to zero. Setting \(u_0=0\) fixes a gauge; deleting nonzero \(f_0\) removes a physical mean source and changes an incompatible model. Check the measured source mean before applying a projected solve.

For \(f(x)=\sum_k f_ke^{ikx}\), take \(u_k=f_k/k^2\) for \(k\ne0\). For a length \(L\) in physical units replace \(k\) by \(2\pi j/L\). A compatible source \(f=\cos3x+0.2\sin x\) gives mean-zero \(u=\cos3x/9+0.2\sin x\). Higher spatial frequencies are smoothed by the inverse Laplacian; data noise at low but nonzero wavenumber can affect the inferred field more strongly than visually rapid fluctuations of equal source amplitude. A compatible constant reference level of potential cannot be inferred from this equation even with noiseless source data.

Compare an exact spectral solve with a discrete operator

On \(N\) equispaced nodes, an FFT diagonalises any periodic translation-invariant discrete convolution, but the diagonal symbol depends on the operator being solved. A spectral second derivative uses \(k^2\) for \(-u''\), while a second-order periodic difference stencil uses \(\lambda_h(k)=4\sin^2(kh/2)/h^2\), \(h=2\pi/N\). The same FFT can invert either symbol. Verify a solver by applying the matching discrete operator back to its result. A Fourier result with tiny residual for the finite-difference stencil is a precise solution of that stencil; it does not thereby reproduce the continuum Fourier amplitude exactly.

At \(N=16,k=4\), \(kh/2=\pi/4\), hence \(\lambda_h=2/h^2=12.97\), compared with continuum \(k^2=16\). A unit cosine source has spectral potential amplitude 0.0625; the finite-difference inverse gives approximately 0.0771. Both can pass a discrete residual at machine precision in their appropriate systems. At fixed physical mode the symbols approach one another as \(N\) increases; at mode number constantly near the Nyquist limit their relative discrepancy need not vanish. A convergence test keeping \(k/N\) fixed follows different physical sources at each refinement.

Identify aliased modes before applying the inverse

For \(N\) samples, Fourier modes with indices differing by an integer multiple of \(N\) coincide at all nodes. Suppose a source includes \(v^2\), where \(v=\cos(5x)\) on a 12-point grid. Squaring exactly yields \([1+\cos(10x)]/2\). But sampled \(\cos10x=\cos2x\), so the grid reports an apparent low \(k=2\) component. Subtracting the known mean gives a compatible source, but the resulting potential amplitude becomes \(0.5/2^2=0.125\) at the wrong spatial frequency rather than \(0.5/10^2=0.005\) at the true one. The inverse Laplacian magnifies the false low-mode contribution, transforming a sampling error into a large field error.

To obtain the product projected on a retained Fourier band, zero-pad the input Fourier coefficients on a sufficiently enlarged grid, transform to space, multiply there, transform back and discard unretained output modes. For modes \(|k|\le K\), a quadratic product extends to \(2K\). To ensure none of its high modes folds back into the retained range, choose multiplication grid size strictly greater than \(3K\), or handle endpoint/Nyquist modes explicitly. This is the usual three-halves padding rule relative to a base grid around \(2K\). Different FFT normalisations require scaling when padding and truncating. Simply padding already multiplied samples cannot recover frequency information that has already aliased.

For a deliberately filtered small problem one can instead drop the top third of the spectrum on a fixed grid before nonlinear multiplication, retaining only \(|k|<N/3\). This avoids erroneous feedback into the retained modes but also discards legitimate high-frequency information. Choosing a finer physical grid can be preferable when the physical quantity of interest depends on those modes. Dealiasing corrects a numerical representation of the chosen projected equations; it does not supply a physical closure for unresolved turbulence, interfaces or material fluctuations.

Follow one unresolved harmonic through the Poisson inverse

The simulation multiplies a cosine of mode m by itself, removes its exact mean and inverts the periodic Poisson operator. The continuum curve retains the true product mode 2m. The unpadded curve uses whichever harmonic the coarse point samples represent, while the third curve shows the correctly projected solution restricted to the original resolvable Fourier band. At 12 samples and m equal to 5, coarse multiplication creates a false mode two whose potential amplitude is 25 times the desired continuum amplitude. The resolved-band projection correctly eliminates that harmonic rather than inventing a low mode.

Increase N until the true product is representable. The three answers then agree, including the phase of the spatial variation. The control limits the actual input frequency to a representable non-Nyquist input and reports the mode used, so changing the grid cannot silently inject an impossible input. A projected curve equal to zero means none of this particular product survives the retained band. It does not mean the continuum product or the underlying fine-scale field vanishes.

Spatial discretisation and field equations · 4. How much of an outgoing wave reflects from the numerical boundary?

A finite grid truncates an effectively open medium. Derive an exact one-dimensional radiation condition, predict the angular reflection of its two-dimensional extension and design a pulse experiment that separates boundary reflection from interior dispersion.

Factor the one-dimensional wave equation

For a constant wave speed \(c>0\), \(u_{tt}-c^2u_{xx}=(\partial_t-c\partial_x)(\partial_t+c\partial_x)u=0\). A right-moving waveform \(F(x-ct)\) satisfies \(u_t+c u_x=0\) and a left-moving waveform satisfies \(u_t-c u_x=0\). At the right boundary impose \(u_t+c u_x=0\) to transmit a purely outgoing right-moving wave; at the left boundary reverse the normal sign. This exact radiation condition requires no knowledge of \(F\) for the constant-coefficient 1D medium. A Dirichlet end instead forces a reflected amplitude minus the incident one, while a Neumann end reflects with the same sign. Both have unit reflected energy for an ideal undamped wave.

A separate energetic argument tests the sign. The interior energy \(E=\tfrac12\int(u_t^2+c^2u_x^2)dx\) has derivative \(dE/dt=c^2[u_tu_x]_{\rm left}^{\rm right}\). At a transmitting right end \(u_x=-u_t/c\), giving outward energy loss \(-c u_t^2\). Imposing the opposite sign there would permit energy injection. A dissipative boundary need not absorb perfectly: it can still reflect some of the incoming wave. The energy identity tests passivity, while a pulse-amplitude experiment tests reflection separately. An initially localised pulse that has not yet reached any boundary is a useful independent check of the interior solver.

Predict reflection as the incidence direction changes

For a flat boundary normal to \(+x\) in two dimensions, the local condition \(u_t+c u_x=0\) is exact only for normal incidence. Consider incident wave \(e^{i(k_xx+k_yy-\omega t)}\) and reflected wave of amplitude \(R\) with \(k_x\) reversed. Use \(\omega=c\sqrt{k_x^2+k_y^2}\) and place the boundary at \(x=0\). Substitution into the condition gives \((-\omega+ck_x)+R(-\omega-ck_x)=0\), hence

\[R=\frac{ck_x-\omega}{ck_x+\omega}=\frac{\cos\theta-1}{\cos\theta+1},\]

where \(\theta\) is measured from the outward normal. The reflected normal-energy fraction is \(|R|^2\) because incident and reflected magnitudes and normal speed factors match. Normal incidence gives zero. At \(60^\circ\), \(R=-1/3\) and power reflection \(1/9\); at grazing incidence its magnitude approaches one. An unspecified claim of a “nonreflecting boundary” has no definite quantitative meaning without frequency, angle, medium and discretisation.

An ideal exterior transparent condition can be expressed after transforming in tangential direction and time: \(\partial_x\hat u=ik_x\hat u\) with \(k_x=\sqrt{(\omega/c)^2-k_y^2}\) on the outgoing or decaying branch. The square root shows why a single local first derivative cannot cover all tangential frequencies exactly. Expanding the square root near normal incidence motivates higher-order absorbing boundary conditions; other methods use a carefully matched absorbing layer. The present first-order formula provides a baseline against which such alternatives can be tested. An evanescent component with imaginary normal wavenumber requires the decaying branch; interpreting it as a propagating beam with a real incidence angle would be incorrect.

Measure boundary error independently of grid dispersion

An interior finite-difference solver usually has a numerical dispersion relation \(\omega_h(k_x,k_y)\). A discrete version of the continuum boundary condition need not be exactly matched even for a strongly resolved normally incident wave, particularly if boundary and interior stencils have different dispersion errors. To isolate the two effects, first propagate the same pulse on a much longer or periodic domain until it passes the observation probe and before any distant return. Record that reference. Then repeat with the open boundary placed closer and compare at the same probe over the predicted return window. Project signals onto spatial directions or separate incident and reflected time windows to estimate amplitude and energy.

For a probe distance \(D\) from a flat boundary and a narrow incident beam at angle \(\theta\), normal group speed is \(c\cos\theta\). The time between incident and reflected peaks measured on a normal coordinate is roughly \(2D/(c\cos\theta)\), provided the probe or array also samples the reflected beam's tangential position. A point probe on an oblique pencil beam need not see both pulses at all; an extended plane-wave excitation or a probe array is more suitable for estimating the stated reflection coefficient. Run a sequence of grid refinements at fixed physical angle and wavelength. Reflection approaching the analytical \(|R|\) indicates continuum boundary-model error; reflection shrinking toward zero at normal incidence indicates discretisation mismatch.

Measure a boundary reflection across incidence angles

The plotted energy fractions follow directly by matching an incoming and a reflected plane wave at a flat boundary. One curve uses the physically correct wave speed in the first-order condition; the other lets the specified boundary speed differ from the interior. Use the angle slider to inspect the signed amplitude as well as its squared magnitude. At matched speed and sixty degrees the amplitude is minus one third and the reflected energy is about 11.11 percent. Choose a boundary speed larger than the interior speed. There is then an oblique angle at which the mismatch exactly compensates the projected normal phase in this local equation. It obeys cosine of the angle equal to the reciprocal speed ratio. For a ratio 1.25 this is about 36.87 degrees. Matching this one direction sacrifices perfect normal incidence; it is not an omnidirectional improvement. The graph concerns the continuous condition. An actual spatial discretisation can add reflection through numerical dispersion, which the longer-domain pulse comparison in this unit is designed to detect.

Work through the solution

Spatial discretisation and field equations · 5. Can a stable finite-element heat step turn a cold node negative?

Start a conducting rod with one positive temperature pulse. Assemble the heat capacity from first principles and determine when an explicit step respects both decay and the sign of every temperature.

Integrate the heat equation against a hat function

Take a uniform rod of length one with the two endpoint temperatures held at zero. With constant diffusivity \(\kappa>0\) the evolution is \(u_t=\kappa u_{xx}\). Approximate \(u(x,t)\) as a linear combination of continuous piecewise-linear hat functions. Multiply by an interior hat and integrate. Integration by parts transfers one spatial derivative onto each hat. The boundary term vanishes. The nodal temperature vector satisfies \(M\dot u=-Ku\).

On an element of length \(h\) the two hats are \(1-x/h\) and \(x/h\) in local coordinates. Integrating their products gives diagonal entries \(h/3\) and off-diagonal entries \(h/6\) in the element mass matrix. Their constant derivatives give diagonal stiffness \(\kappa/h\) and off-diagonal stiffness \(-\kappa/h\). After assembly and imposing Dirichlet values, the interior mass has diagonal \(4h/6\) and neighbouring entries \(h/6\). The stiffness has diagonal \(2\kappa/h\) and neighbours \(-\kappa/h\).

Forward Euler advances with \(G=I-\Delta t M^{-1}K\). On an eigenvector of \(K\phi=\lambda M\phi\) it multiplies the amplitude by \(1-\Delta t\lambda\). A bound \(\Delta t\lambda_{\max}\leq2\) therefore ensures no growth in the mass norm. Preserving nonnegative temperatures for every nonnegative initial vector requires every entry of \(G\) to be nonnegative. Each column is the response to a single positive nodal impulse, so the latter condition can be tested directly.

One alternative assigns half of each element's total heat capacity to each endpoint, before removing boundary degrees of freedom. Each interior mass then equals \(h\) and the explicit stencil has weights \(r,1-2r,r\), with \(r=\kappa\Delta t/h^2\). These coefficients are all nonnegative for \(0\leq r\leq 1/2\). The first and last interior equations also couple to the fixed zero boundary temperatures.

Compute the remote temperature after one update

With three interior nodes, \(h=1/4\), initially set the left interior node to 1 K above the boundary reference and both others to zero. Inverting the consistent mass in this three-by-three system gives the first column of \((h^2/\kappa)M^{-1}K\) as \((51,-36,9)/14\). At \(r=0.1\) the interior temperatures after one step become \((0.63571,0.25714,-0.06429)\) K. The cold node two edges away immediately drops below the lower reference.

The largest dimensionless eigenvalue is about 7.9223. The chosen step gives \(r\lambda_{\max}h^2/\kappa\approx0.7922\), comfortably inside the linear stability interval. Lumping instead gives \((0.8,0.1,0)\) K. The negative consistent-mass entry shrinks linearly when the step shrinks. For this impulse its sign persists for every positive first step. With OneSimulation installed locally, the notebook also constructs a physical line mesh and obtains the mass and stiffness by integrating its hat functions at mapped quadrature points. Compare the assembled result against the explicit tridiagonal entries. The experiment shows why reducing the time step can satisfy a stability test without restoring a discrete maximum principle.

Exercise · derive the largest mode on successively finer meshes

An interior sine wave has neighbouring sum \(2\cos(\theta)\) times its own nodal value. Use this fact to find the limiting stability condition for forward Euler with consistent mass as the number of uniform elements grows.

Work through the solution

The mass eigenvalue is \(h(2+\cos\theta)/3\) and the stiffness eigenvalue is \(2\kappa(1-\cos\theta)/h\). Their ratio, multiplied by \(h^2/\kappa\), is \(6(1-\cos\theta)/(2+\cos\theta)\).
