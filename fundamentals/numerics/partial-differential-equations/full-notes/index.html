Chapter 1 · Flux differences for an elliptic balance

Assemble a conservative boundary-value problem from face fluxes, trace the effect of boundary conditions and establish a discrete energy identity.

From first principles

Derive a cell equation by integrating the physical balance

Consider steady one-dimensional conduction in a rod with area \(A(x)\), conductivity \(k(x)>0\) and heating per unit length \(s(x)\). Define heat current \(F\) positive to the right. Conservation in any finite interval requires that the current leaving equals the current arriving plus generation. Integrating over cell \(i\) fixes a discrete equation without yet approximating a derivative:

\[F_{i+1/2}-F_{i-1/2}=\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}s(x)\,dx .\]

Each shared face must use the same current in its two adjacent cells. To close a face between constant-material regions with no intervening source, integrate Fourier’s law \(T'=-F/(kA)\) from one cell centre to the other. This gives

\[F_{i+1/2}=\frac{T_i-T_{i+1}}{\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}[k(x)A(x)]^{-1}dx}.\]

On a uniform homogeneous mesh this reduces to \(kA(T_i-T_{i+1})/h\). Across a material change the half-cell resistances must instead be added. Substituting face currents into the cell balances produces the matrix system. Boundary temperatures, prescribed currents or film laws determine which additional boundary equations close it.

Derivation and applications

Why a flux-only boundary requires a compatibility condition

An insulated or flux-controlled steady diffusion calculation can fail even when each individual face law is correct. Consider a one-dimensional bar of unit area, positive conductivity \(k\), and cell sources \(s_i\) per unit length. Define \(F_{i+1/2}\) as heat current positive to the right everywhere, including both boundaries. At steady state the current leaving the right face minus the current entering the left face equals the total source in that cell:

\[F_{i+1/2}-F_{i-1/2}=s_i\Delta x_i .\]

Summing over cells cancels every shared interior current. No detail of the face-gradient approximation can change the remaining global condition:

\[F_R-F_L=\sum_i s_i\Delta x_i .\]

With only prescribed fluxes, the discrete gradient also cannot determine a temperature offset. For a connected linear system with positive face transmissibilities, its assembled quadratic form is a sum of squared temperature differences:

\[\mathbf T^\mathsf{T}K\mathbf T=\sum_{\text{interior faces}}g_{ij}(T_i-T_j)^2,\]
\[K\mathbf 1=0 .\]

The nullspace consists precisely of constant temperatures. A compatible right-hand side gives a family of solutions; prescribe one reference temperature or a weighted mean to select a member. An incompatible right-hand side gives no steady solution. Physically, the imbalance would accumulate energy. Removing one algebraic equation or pinning a temperature without checking global balance can inadvertently create an unreported heat sink.

Worked example

Reconstruct temperatures from compatible flux data

Take three equal cells with source totals \(1,2,1\) W, left incoming current \(F_L=2\) W and right outgoing current \(F_R=6\) W. Internal face currents are successively \(3\) and \(5\) W. With both conductances equal to \(2\) W/K, the temperature drops are \(1.5\) and \(2.5\) K from left to right. For a prescribed arithmetic mean of \(300\) K the temperatures are \(301.833\), \(300.333\) and \(297.833\) K. Adding a common constant would leave every face measurement unchanged.

Second application

Diagnose an unbalanced insulated test

Seal both ends so \(F_L=F_R=0\) but retain the same sources. Their total is \(4\) W. If the combined heat capacity is \(200\) J/K, the heat-capacity-weighted mean temperature must rise at \(4/200=0.0200\) K/s. A steady solver reporting fixed temperatures under those conditions should be checked for a hidden imposed temperature or a dropped residual. The missing four watts have a direct transient interpretation.

Place fluxes where conservation uses them

Consider steady conduction on \(0<x<L\) with heat flux \(q=-k(x)T'\) and volumetric generation \(f\). Taking unit cross-sectional area, steady energy balance gives \(q'=f\). On uniform nodes separated by \(h\), integrate the balance over a control segment centred on \(x_i\): \(q_{i+1/2}-q_{i-1/2}\simeq h f_i\). A shared face must have the same numerical flux in both adjacent equations, otherwise summing local equations can create an artificial internal source or sink.

Use \(q_{i+1/2}=-k_{i+1/2}(T_{i+1}-T_i)/h\). Then row \(i\) of the discrete operator has diagonal \((k_{i-1/2}+k_{i+1/2})/h^2\) and off-diagonals \(-k_{i\pm1/2}/h^2\). For positive \(k\) the off-diagonals are negative and the diagonal balances them. With zero source, an unconstrained interior value is a weighted average of its neighbours. This relation explains why a source-free discrete conductor with fixed end temperatures cannot develop an interior temperature larger than both ends.

Compute the face law across an interface

For smooth \(k\) an arithmetic average of neighbouring nodal coefficients provides a second-order face approximation on a uniform grid. A sharp material interface calls for a different physical calculation. Consider two half-segments in series with constants \(k_L,k_R\) and no interface storage or contact resistance. The same flux crosses both halves. Adding their temperature drops gives \(T_L-T_R=q[h/(2k_L)+h/(2k_R)]\), hence \(k_{\rm eff}=2/(1/k_L+1/k_R)\). This is a harmonic average for equal half-widths.

With \(k_L=1,\ k_R=9\) W/(m·K) and a 10 K drop over a 0.1 m pair, the effective conductivity is 1.8 W/(m·K) and the flux is 180 W/m². Using the arithmetic mean would give 500 W/m², severely underestimating the resistance of the poorer conductor. In the limit \(k_L\to0^+\), the physical flux tends to zero while that arithmetic estimate remains finite. For unequal layer thicknesses use the actual layer resistances rather than either unweighted average.

Examine uniqueness and the role of the boundary

For homogeneous fixed temperatures and an interior vector \(u\) extended by zero at both ends, grouping adjacent row contributions gives

\[h\sum_i u_i(A_hu)_i=\sum_{\rm faces}\frac{k_{i+1/2}}h(u_{i+1}-u_i)^2.\]

Each term is nonnegative. Vanishing energy requires a constant vector, and the fixed ends force that constant to be zero. Thus the interior matrix is symmetric positive definite. This reasoning justifies CG and gives a uniqueness argument that does not depend on inspection of a particular numerical matrix. With only prescribed heat fluxes a spatial constant instead remains undetermined. A solution then requires overall heat compatibility and one temperature reference or a mean constraint.

As a check, take constant \(k=1\), \(L=1\), zero end temperatures and generation 2. The exact profile \(T=x(1-x)\) has central value 0.25. Central differences recover its second derivative exactly, so all uniform grid nodes satisfy the balance without truncation error. This successful check verifies signs, boundary handling and the quadratic patch. To measure a convergence order use a smooth field with a nonzero leading truncation term, such as a sine profile, and compare at common physical positions.

Why fixing one boundary value controls the solution

Suppose the left end of a conducting rod is held at zero temperature and the right end is insulated. A finite system of flux balances may be invertible on one mesh. We would also like a bound which survives refinement. In the continuous problem, use the space \(V=\{v\in H^1(0,L):v(0)=0\}\). Its members have square-integrable first derivatives and a well-defined endpoint value. Every admissible difference between two temperature fields belongs to this same space. The fundamental theorem of calculus and Cauchy–Schwarz give

\[|v(x)|^2=\left|\int_0^x v'(s)\,ds\right|^2\le x\int_0^x|v'(s)|^2\,ds,\qquad \|v\|_2\le\frac{L}{\sqrt{2}}\|v'\|_2 .\]

The second inequality follows by integrating the first and bounding its inner integral by the full derivative norm squared. Thus a small temperature gradient forces the temperature itself to be small in this particular space. Take a possibly discontinuous conductivity with \(0<k_0\le k(x)\le k_1\) almost everywhere and a square-integrable heat source \(f\). Testing the balance and integrating once gives \(a(u,v)=\ell(v)\) for every \(v\in V\), with

\[a(u,v)=\int_0^L ku'v'\,dx,\qquad \ell(v)=\int_0^L fv\,dx .\]

The upper bound on conductivity gives \(|a(u,v)|\le k_1\|u'\|_2\|v'\|_2\). The lower bound gives \(a(v,v)\ge k_0\|v'\|_2^2\), called coercivity. The endpoint inequality bounds \(|\ell(v)|\) by \(L\|f\|_2\|v'\|_2/\sqrt2\). These are the hypotheses of the Lax–Milgram theorem on the Hilbert space with norm \(\|v'\|_2\). Consequently the weak solution exists and is unique. For two loads, subtract the weak equations and test with their solution difference. Cancelling one derivative norm gives \(\|(u-\tilde u)'\|_2\le L\|f-\tilde f\|_2/(\sqrt2\,k_0)\). Every conforming discrete subspace inherits the same estimate.

How large a change can a load error produce?

In scaled units let \(L=1,\ k=2,\ f=3\). Integration of \(-2u''=3\), first from the insulated end and then from the fixed end, gives \(u'=1.5(1-x)\) and \(u=1.5x-0.75x^2\). Its derivative norm is \(1.5/\sqrt3=0.8660\). The theorem guarantees at most \(3/(2\sqrt2)=1.0607\). Raising the source everywhere by \(0.12\) changes that norm of the difference by \(0.06/\sqrt3=0.03464\), within the guaranteed \(0.04243\). The estimate also applies to nonuniform perturbations of the same load norm. If both ends are insulated, constants have zero derivative and can be added freely. A net heat input then rules out steady balance altogether.

Eliminate boundary values with the correct sign

With four intervals on a unit rod, fixed left temperature 10, fixed right temperature 20 and zero source, multiply each row by \(h^2/k\). The three unknowns then satisfy a tridiagonal system with diagonal two, off-diagonal minus one and load \((10,0,20)^T\). Moving a fixed value to the right changes its sign, producing positive boundary contributions here. Solving gives the linearly spaced interior temperatures 12.5, 15 and 17.5. Every discrete face reports the same flux for constant \(k\).

This deliberately simple case isolates boundary elimination and sign conventions. Reversing only one load sign can generate a plausible-looking smooth curve with entirely wrong end-to-end transport. After checking the values, also sum all interior balances and verify cancellation of the internal face contributions. The resulting global statement includes only boundary fluxes and any source integrated over the interior control volumes.

Prove a discrete maximum principle from the face balances

Let positive conductances \(G_{i-1/2}\) and \(G_{i+1/2}\) connect each internal node to its neighbours, with both end temperatures specified. In the absence of internal sources its balance reads \(G_{i-1/2}(u_i-u_{i-1})+G_{i+1/2}(u_i-u_{i+1})=0\). Dividing by the sum of the conductances makes \(u_i\) a convex combination of its two neighbours. If a strict global maximum occurred inside the domain above both prescribed boundary values, its neighbours would each have to equal this maximum. Repeating along the connected grid would carry the same value to a boundary, contradicting the assumption.

The same reasoning applied to the negative field bounds the minimum. With a nonnegative internal heat source \(s_i\) on the right, an interior minimum below both boundaries would have both outgoing temperature differences nonpositive. Their positive weighted sum cannot equal a positive source, and if the sum is zero, propagation again carries the minimum to the boundary. A positive source may raise an interior maximum while preserving the lower comparison bound. Negative face conductances or a disconnected component invalidate the argument; symmetry and a small algebraic residual do not by themselves restore it.

Compare two thermal loads without inverting their matrices

Connect internal nodes 1 and 2 to end temperatures zero and ten, using consecutive conductances 1, 2 and 4 W/K. With no source the equations are \(3u_1-2u_2=0\) and \(-2u_1+6u_2=40\). The first gives \(u_1=2u_2/3\), and the second gives \(u_2=60/7,\ u_1=40/7\). Both values lie between the boundary temperatures. The common leftward heat flow has magnitude \(40/7\) W, checked independently from each of the three temperature drops times its conductance.

Add a heat source of 7 W at node one. The difference \(v\) between new and old solutions has zero boundary values and satisfies \(3v_1-2v_2=7,\ -2v_1+6v_2=0\). The second relation gives \(v_1=3v_2\), so \(v_2=1,\ v_1=3\). Both internal states warmed, consistent with the comparison principle applied to the difference. The additional outflow at the left boundary is \(1\cdot3=3\) W and at the right is \(4\cdot1=4\) W, accounting for the full applied source.

This provides two independent implementation tests. Conservation checks that the boundary power change equals the injected power. Monotonicity checks the signs of all changes in the field. A sign error in a face contribution can violate one of these checks even while the computed algebraic residual for the incorrectly assembled matrix is tiny. Both tests use physically accessible quantities and remain meaningful when no exact closed-form solution of a complex geometry is available.

The argument extends to a connected graph, since a weighted average at a maximising node requires equality along every positive-conductance edge. This observation is useful for testing an unstructured implementation. Deliberately disconnecting an entirely insulated component produces a free constant there, so boundary comparisons cannot control that component until a temperature reference or independent constraint is supplied.

Chapter 2 · Weak forms and finite elements

Derive a variational statement by integration by parts, assemble element matrices for a one-dimensional rod and explain what the Galerkin solution minimises.

From first principles

Obtain the weak diffusion problem by moving one derivative

For a stationary diffusion problem \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla u)=f\), suppose temperature is prescribed on part of the boundary and the outward flux \(q_n=-k\nabla u\cdot\mathbf n\) on the remainder. A classical second derivative may not exist across a material interface. Instead multiply the conservative equation by a smooth test function \(v\) that vanishes where \(u\) is prescribed. The divergence theorem moves one derivative from the flux onto \(v\):

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega=\int_\Omega fv\,d\Omega-\int_{\Gamma_N}vq_n\,d\Gamma .\]

Approximate \(u\) with continuous element shape functions \(N_j\) that are differentiable inside each element. Choose each allowable shape function as the test function. Linearity then yields element contributions

\[K_{ij}^{(e)}=\int_e k\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j\,d\Omega,\]
\[f_i^{(e)}=\int_e fN_i\,d\Omega .\]

Neighbouring elements contribute to the same global unknown at a shared node. Adding their contributions enforces weak equilibrium. The flux sign in the boundary term follows from an outward-positive definition; switching to prescribed inward heat supply reverses that sign in the load vector.

Derivation and applications

How a Robin boundary enters both the stiffness matrix and the load

A convecting boundary couples an unknown surface temperature to an ambient temperature. Unlike a prescribed heat source it contributes to both sides of the assembled equations. Consider steady diffusion \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)=s\) in a two-dimensional region of unit thickness, with \(k>0\). On a straight boundary segment impose the empirical film law \(-k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n=h(T-T_\infty)\) using the outward normal. Multiply the field equation by a test function \(v\) vanishing on prescribed-temperature boundaries, integrate, and apply the divergence theorem:

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla v\cdot\nabla T\,dA-\int_{\partial\Omega}v k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=\int_\Omega vs\,dA .\]

Substitution on the film boundary \(\Gamma\) places the unknown surface term on the left and the ambient contribution on the right:

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla v\cdot\nabla T\,dA+\int_\Gamma hvT\,ds=\int_\Omega vs\,dA+\int_\Gamma hvT_\infty\,ds\]

with any other natural boundary terms added separately. To assemble one linear edge of length \(\ell\), use edge functions \(N_1=1-\xi\), \(N_2=\xi\), \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Insert the nodal interpolation for \(T\) and choose each \(N_i\) as test function. Constant \(h,T_\infty\) give

\[K_\Gamma=\frac{h\ell}{6}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\]
\[f_\Gamma=\frac{h\ell T_\infty}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The off-diagonal term is present because both nodes influence temperature across the same edge. In the limit \(h=0\) the boundary is insulated. A very large film coefficient constrains the temperature toward ambient but can introduce a large stiffness contrast.

Worked example

Assemble a convecting edge by hand

Use a one-metre out-of-plane thickness, \(\ell=0.300\) m, \(h=20.0\) W/(m² K) and \(T_\infty=290\) K. Then \(K_\Gamma\) has diagonals \(2.00\) W/K and off-diagonals \(1.00\) W/K. The load is \((870,870)\) W. At nodal temperatures \(300\) and \(310\) K the film residual \(K_\Gamma T-f_\Gamma\) is \((40,50)\) W. The sum \(90\) W agrees with \(h\ell[(300+310)/2-290]\).

Second application

Test a constant solution before using the boundary routine

Set both temperatures to \(290\) K, the ambient value. Each row has sum \(3.00\) W/K, so every residual is \(3(290)-870=0\). If the code includes the ambient load but forgets the matrix, it predicts \(1740\) W of heating at thermal equilibrium. This constant-field patch test detects sign errors and omission of the surface matrix independently of the volume mesh.

Reduce the differentiability required by equilibrium

A rod is fixed at \(x=0\), pushed by a distributed load \(f\) and by an end force \(t\) at \(x=L\). Axial displacement \(u\) satisfies \(-(EAu')'=f\), \(u(0)=0\) and \(EAu'(L)=t\). The product \(EA\) is positive. A trial profile assembled from linear pieces has jumps in its derivative, so differentiating it twice pointwise is unsuitable. Instead multiply the strong equation by a test displacement \(v\) vanishing at the fixed end and integrate by parts once.

The resulting equation is \(\int_0^L EA\,u'v'\,dx=\int_0^L fv\,dx+t v(L)\). Only first derivatives are required. The fixed displacement is imposed directly on trial and test spaces; the end traction appears in the load functional. This clarifies why prescribing both force and displacement independently at the same end can overconstrain a second-order problem. The test functions represent allowable virtual displacements, and the integral identity expresses equality of internal and external virtual work.

Assemble one element and connect it to its neighbour

On an element from \(x_i\) to \(x_{i+1}\) of length \(h\), use shape functions \(N_1=1-s,\ N_2=s\) with \(s=(x-x_i)/h\). Their physical derivatives are \(-1/h,\ 1/h\). For constant \(EA\), integrating products \(EA N'_aN'_b\) gives the matrix \((EA/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A constant distributed load contributes \(fh(1,1)^T/2\). Summing element contributions for a shared global node enforces balance there without prescribing the internal traction manually.

Take \(L=1,\ EA=1,\ f=2,\ t=0\) in consistent units and use two equal elements. After eliminating the fixed displacement, the stiffness matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&-2\\-2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and the load is \((1,0.5)^T\). Solving gives \(u(0.5)=0.75,\ u(1)=1\). The exact solution is \(2x-x^2\) and agrees at both nodes. Inside the first element at \(x=0.25\), the linear interpolant gives 0.375, whereas the exact value is 0.4375. Nodal agreement can coexist with a substantial interior error.

Assemble a physical triangle from a reference element

A line element has one length scale. A triangle can stretch, rotate and shear, so the element area alone cannot determine its diffusion matrix. Start with the reference vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\) and the linear shape functions \(1-\xi-\eta,\xi,\eta\). Send the reference triangle to a physical cell with vertices \(P_0,P_1,P_2\) by \(x=P_0+J(\xi,\eta)^T\), where the columns of \(J\) are \(P_1-P_0\) and \(P_2-P_0\). For a nondegenerate triangle, the chain rule and area transformation give

\[\nabla_x N_i=J^{-T}\nabla_{\xi,\eta}\widehat N_i,\qquad dx\,dy=|\det J|\,d\xi\,d\eta .\]

To see why the inverse transpose occurs, take an arbitrary displacement \(d\widehat x\). Both gradients must describe the same change in a shape function. Thus \((\nabla_xN_i)^TJ\,d\widehat x=(\nabla\widehat N_i)^Td\widehat x\) for every such displacement. For unit isotropic conductivity, the transformed gradients are constant and the physical stiffness entries reduce to \(K_{ij}=|T|\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j\), with \(|T|=|\det J|/2\). A constant cell load \(f\) contributes \(f|T|/3\) at each vertex because each linear shape function has mean \(1/3\).

A sheared cell and an exact affine-field check

Take \(P_0=(0,0), P_1=(2,0), P_2=(1,1)\). The columns of \(J\) are \((2,0)\) and \((1,1)\), so the cell area is \(1\). Applying \(J^{-T}\) to the three reference gradients gives \((-1/2,-1/2),(1/2,-1/2),(0,1)\). Their dot products and the load \(f=3\) yield

\[K=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0&-1/2\\0&1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&-1/2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad b=(1,1,1)^T.\]

Every row sums to zero because a constant field has zero gradient. For a more discriminating test use \(u=x+2y\), with vertex values \(U=(0,2,3)^T\). Weighting the three gradients by these values gives \((1,2)\). The physical integral of its squared gradient equals \(1(1^2+2^2)=5\), and direct multiplication gives \(U^TKU=5\). A transposed mapping error can pass the constant test and fail this second one.

Identify the energy that is best approximated

Let \(a(u,v)=\int EA\,u'v'\) and denote the external work by \(\ell(v)\). The discrete solution \(u_h\) satisfies \(a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)\) for every test function from its finite-dimensional space. The continuous solution satisfies the same equation for every such test, so \(a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\). This Galerkin orthogonality means the error is perpendicular to the trial space in the energy inner product. For any other admissible discrete \(w_h\), write \(u-w_h=(u-u_h)+(u_h-w_h)\) and expand its squared energy norm. The cross term vanishes and \(\|u-w_h\|_a^2=\|u-u_h\|_a^2+\|u_h-w_h\|_a^2\).

Thus the conforming Galerkin solution is the best energy approximation in its space for this symmetric linear problem and exact integration. The statement concerns a specific norm and model. It does not guarantee pointwise optimal stresses or carry unchanged into nonsymmetric transport problems. In the rod example the computed strain is constant on each element, replacing a linearly varying exact strain. Refinement improves this derivative field even when a table of nodal displacements already looks exact.

Recover a support reaction

The reaction at a prescribed-displacement node is hidden if one inspects only the reduced interior system. Assemble all element internal forces \(Ku_h\) before discarding constrained rows, then subtract the full external load vector. The entries at free degrees of freedom should be near zero; the entry at a prescribed degree of freedom gives the reaction in the chosen sign convention.

In the uniformly loaded rod, the first element strain is \((0.75-0)/0.5=1.5\). Its internal nodal force at the left is \(-1.5\), while the first node receives an external distributed-load share of \(+0.5\). Their difference \(-2\) is the support reaction. It balances the total applied load \(2\cdot1=2\). Looking only at the derivative in the first element would have reported magnitude 1.5, missing that load share. A reaction from assembled balance and a stress recovered by pointwise differentiation are related but distinct quantities on a finite mesh.

Obtain actual norm errors from the Galerkin property

For \(-u''=f\) on the unit interval with homogeneous Dirichlet values, the elementary energy inner product is \(a(u,v)=\int u'v'\). Let \(I_hu\) be the continuous piecewise-linear interpolant of a sufficiently smooth exact solution at mesh nodes. On each element its slope is the average of \(u'\). Every linear finite-element test function \(v_h\) has constant derivative on that element. Consequently \(\int_E(u-I_hu)'v_h'=v_h'[u-I_hu]_{\partial E}=0\). Summing proves global energy orthogonality.

The Galerkin solution satisfies the same orthogonality, and positive definiteness gives uniqueness. Thus \(u_h=I_hu\) in this one-dimensional, constant-coefficient problem with exactly integrated load and matching boundary values. Nodal errors vanish, although the field and derivative between nodes can still be inaccurate. Variable coefficients inside an element generally destroy the simple telescoping argument because the factor multiplying \(v_h'\) is no longer constant. The result also should not be transferred to multidimensional triangulations without proof.

Compare nodal, field and flux accuracy for one solution

Choose \(u=x(1-x)\), so \(f=2\). On an element starting at \(a\) write \(s=x-a\in[0,h]\). Subtract the straight interpolant from the parabola to get \(u-u_h=s(h-s)\), vanishing at both nodes and attaining \(h^2/4\) at the element centre. Its derivative is \(h-2s\); the exact and numerical slopes agree at the midpoint but differ by magnitude \(h\) at each endpoint taken from that element. Testing only the midpoint slope or the nodal values would entirely conceal these other discrepancies.

Compute the squared energy error on this element: \(\int_0^h(h-2s)^2ds=h^3/3\). With \(1/h\) equal elements, the global energy error is \(h/\sqrt3\). Similarly the element squared field error is \(\int_0^hs^2(h-s)^2ds=h^5/30\), giving global \(L^2\) error \(h^2/\sqrt{30}\). For \(h=1/4\), the two errors are approximately 0.14434 and 0.01141; the maximum field error is 0.015625. Halving the mesh width halves the energy error but divides the field error by four. All these measurements concern the very same numerical solution whose nodal error is exactly zero.

For a physical diffusion flux with constant conductivity one, flux error equals minus derivative error. A cell-averaged flux test is also special here: the numerical slope equals the exact average slope on each element for the same reason used in the proof. To verify local flux resolution, compare at more than a midpoint or a cell average and retain the measure used for convergence. A reported order is a statement about an observable and a norm as well as a finite-element space.

These formulas also identify a misleading manufactured test: if an automated comparison stores only nodal differences, the estimated order is undefined because both coarse and fine errors vanish. Adding independently evaluated quadrature points inside elements exposes the field error immediately. To check a flux implementation, use a quadrature rule capable of resolving the squared linear derivative error; a one-point midpoint rule would again report zero despite the nonzero exact energy norm.

Chapter 3 · Finite volumes and moving fronts

Derive an upwind transport update by conservation, interpret the Courant number as a fraction of cell contents and determine what conservation says about front speed.

From first principles

Derive a conservative update from storage in a cell

Consider the one-dimensional conservation law \(u_t+\partial_x f(u)=0\) with no distributed source. Let \(\bar u_i\) denote the average over cell \(i\) of width \(\Delta x\). The integral of \(u\) over the cell is what the discrete variable stores. Integrate first over the cell and then over one time step. Only fluxes crossing the two faces can change its inventory:

\[\Delta x(\bar u_i^{n+1}-\bar u_i^n)=-\int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}}[f_{i+1/2}(t)-f_{i-1/2}(t)]\,dt .\]

Replace each time-averaged face flux by one numerical function \(\hat f\) of neighbouring states, using the same value from both sides. For constant positive advection speed \(a\), the state reaching a face comes from upstream. With piecewise-constant cell data and \(C=a\Delta t/\Delta x\le1\) this gives

\[\hat f_{i+1/2}=a\bar u_i^n,\]
\[\bar u_i^{n+1}=(1-C)\bar u_i^n+C\bar u_{i-1}^n .\]

The update conserves total mass on a periodic grid because every internal face cancels. Nonlinear fluxes also need a rule selecting the physically admissible interface state, especially when a jump separates two different initial cell values.

Derivation and applications

Quantify the quadratic quantity lost by an upwind update

A conservative finite-volume method for advection preserves the sum of cell contents under periodic boundary conditions. It need not preserve their contrast. Consider the constant-speed transport equation \(u_t+a u_x=0\) with \(a>0\), uniform cells of width \(\Delta x\) and periodic indexing. Exact translation preserves \(\int u^2\,dx\). The first-order upwind scheme transports a fraction \(C=a\Delta t/\Delta x\) of a cell's content across its right boundary per step:

\[u_i^{n+1}=(1-C)u_i^n+C u_{i-1}^n .\]

For \(0\le C\le1\) this is a convex combination and cannot create a value outside its two inputs. To see precisely how much variation is removed, square one update and complete a difference square:

\[[(1-C)x+Cy]^2=(1-C)x^2+Cy^2-C(1-C)(x-y)^2 .\]

Sum over all cells. Periodicity allows the shifted sum of old squared values to be relabelled. The exact discrete identity, with \(E^n=(\Delta x/2)\sum_i(u_i^n)^2\), becomes

\[E^{n+1}-E^n=-\frac{\Delta x}{2}C(1-C)\sum_i(u_i^n-u_{i-1}^n)^2 .\]

This loss vanishes for spatially constant data and for \(C=1\), when the update shifts by one complete cell. Strong cell-to-cell jumps dissipate much more of the quadratic quantity than smooth profiles sampled with the same grid. Total mass still telescopes exactly; the two balance laws diagnose different aspects of accuracy.

Worked example

Follow one step on four periodic cells

Take \(\Delta x=1\), \(C=1/2\) and initial averages \((1,1,0,0)\). The updated values are \((0.5,1,0.5,0)\). Both arrays sum to two. The quadratic energy falls from \(1.00\) to \(0.750\). There are two unit jumps at the start, and the loss formula gives \((1/2)(1/2)(1/2)(2)=0.250\), accounting for the full change.

Second application

Use an alternating pattern as an instability detector

For \((1,-1,1,-1)\) each neighbour has opposite sign, so one step multiplies the amplitude by \(1-2C\). At \(C=1/2\) all variation disappears in one step although the exact wave would keep its amplitude. At \(C=1.10\) the factor is \(-1.20\) and energy grows by \(1.44\). The sign reversal in \(C(1-C)\) therefore predicts growth at this resolved wavelength. Conservation of the zero mean still holds in both experiments and alone cannot detect the instability.

OneSimulation laboratory · Measure spatial convergence on a known field

Store quantities over cells

Consider a passive concentration satisfying \(u_t+(au)_x=0\) with positive constant speed \(a\) on a periodic line. Let \(\bar u_i=h^{-1}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}u\,dx\) be its cell mean. Integrating the conservation law in space and time shows that every change in cell inventory comes from fluxes through its two faces. If neighbouring cells use the same face flux, summing all updates cancels every internal transfer exactly. On a periodic line the total stays fixed, even for a discontinuous concentration.

For positive speed, material reaching the right face during a short step comes from the cell to its left. Approximate the outgoing state by that cell’s mean, giving \(F_{i+1/2}=a\bar u_i\). Forward Euler in time yields \(\bar u_i^{n+1}=(1-C)\bar u_i^n+C\bar u_{i-1}^n\), where \(C=a\Delta t/h\). For \(0\leq C\leq1\) this is a convex average, preserving the range of neighbouring values. At \(C=1\) the entire array shifts exactly by one cell per step. If \(a<0\), information comes from the other side of the face and the upwind direction must reverse.

Identify the source of artificial spreading

Start with one cell mean equal to one and zeros everywhere else. With \(C=1/2\), one step gives adjacent means \(1/2,1/2\). A second step gives \(1/4,1/2,1/4\), while the total remains one. Exact advection over two such steps shifts the original pulse by one cell with no change in shape. The scheme has conserved mass yet broadened the pulse. Peak height or variance therefore tests information not supplied by a global balance.

For smooth \(u\), Taylor-expand the upwind update in space and time. Replace the leading \(u_{tt}\) using the exact transport equation, \(u_{tt}=a^2u_{xx}\). The leading modified equation is \(u_t+a u_x=[ah(1-C)/2]u_{xx}+\cdots\). The bracket acts as numerical diffusivity. It is positive for the monotone range and vanishes at \(C=1\). This local smooth expansion helps interpret observed spreading, but the Taylor expansion itself does not hold across a jump.

Choose a flux when speed depends on the state

For a nonlinear conservation law \(u_t+f(u)_x=0\), characteristics can intersect and create a discontinuity. Enclose a moving jump in a thin control volume. The net flux imbalance fixes its speed \(s=(f(u_R)-f(u_L))/(u_R-u_L)\). For Burgers flux \(f=u^2/2\) with left state 2 and right state 0, this gives \(s=1\). The faster characteristics on the left overtake the slower ones to form a shock. Reversing these states produces a spreading fan under the entropy condition even though the algebraic jump formula alone would still supply a speed.

A numerical face flux must be consistent when the two cells agree and must incorporate information from both sides when a nonlinear wave can travel either way. A conservative method that converges can reproduce the weak jump balance, while selection of a physically admissible solution also requires an entropy criterion. For practical assessment, monitor total inventory, motion of the front, overshoot and the number of cells over which it is smeared. The four measurements address distinct properties, and reducing one defect need not reduce all of them.

Move the control surface instead of the grid

Across a front moving with speed \(s\), flux in the front’s own frame is \(f(u)-su\). The jump relation enforces equality of this quantity on both sides. For the Burgers jump from 2 to 0 with speed one, the left flux in the moving frame is \(2-1\cdot2=0\), and the right flux is also zero. The laboratory flux differs across the front because a moving interface is sweeping conserved material through stationary space.

This distinction helps when checking a simulation by counting material in a region that tracks a front. Differentiating an integral over moving endpoints adds boundary-motion terms. In a periodic fixed window the update should conserve total amount without those extra terms. In a moving measurement window, physical transport through the window and motion of the window both influence the recorded inventory, even under a perfectly conservative scheme.

Distinguish a conservative jump from the physical weak solution

For inviscid Burgers flow \(u_t+(u^2/2)_x=0\), integrating conservation across a moving jump gives the Rankine–Hugoniot speed \(s=[f]/[u]=(u_L+u_R)/2\). It uses only balance, so a discontinuity with that speed satisfies the weak conservation equation for either orientation of its left and right states. To select the physically relevant solution one needs an entropy condition. For a compressive shock characteristic speeds enter it from both sides: \(u_L>s>u_R\). If \(u_L<u_R\), the characteristics separate and a continuous rarefaction fan fills the gap.

For example the entropy \(\eta(u)=u^2/2\) has entropy flux \(q(u)=u^3/3\), because \(q'=\eta'f'\). A jump produces distributional entropy \([q]-s[\eta]\), which must be nonpositive for a dissipative solution. At equal-magnitude opposite states the entropy jump \([\eta]\) is zero, so orientation alone changes the sign of the entropy production. A conservative scheme can preserve every cell total correctly yet converge to an inadmissible expansion shock if it fails this additional condition.

Choose the correct interface flux for two opposite Riemann data

Take \(u_L=-1,\ u_R=1\). Both endpoint fluxes equal one half, and the jump-speed formula gives zero. Holding this discontinuity fixed would conserve the cell totals. Its entropy production, however, is \(1/3-(-1/3)=2/3>0\). The admissible Riemann solution is the self-similar fan: at coordinate \(\xi=x/t\), \(u=-1\) for \(\xi\le-1\), \(u=\xi\) between minus one and one, and \(u=1\) for \(\xi\ge1\). At the interface \(\xi=0\), the flux is therefore zero. Using an arithmetic average of the two endpoint fluxes would instead maintain the inadmissible jump.

For a finite-volume update with \(\Delta t/\Delta x=0.5\) and initially constant states on either side, the neighbouring left cell changes from \(-1\) to \(-1-0.5(0-0.5)=-0.75\). The right cell changes from one to \(1-0.5(0.5-0)=0.75\). Their sum remains zero. A direct integration of the fan gives the same cell averages: the disturbance occupies half of each cell and linearly brings that half from its far-state value toward zero. Thus both conservation and the local wave pattern can be verified independently.

Reverse the initial states to \(u_L=1,\ u_R=-1\). The zero-speed jump now has entropy production \(-2/3\), and both families of characteristics enter it. Its correct interface flux is one half. The same numerical update then leaves both neighbouring cell averages unchanged, exactly as required for a stationary shock. Testing both orientations is more revealing than testing a single conserved jump: they have identical endpoint flux magnitudes and the same Rankine–Hugoniot speed but different physically admissible interface states.

A further integration test shifts the interface by precisely one cell and reruns the update. On a uniform periodic grid with unchanged physical states, the new solution should be a one-cell translation of the old one. A difference between the two calculations could reveal an indexing error in the face flux even when a global conservation diagnostic remains identically zero.

Chapter 4 · Time-dependent diffusion

Derive the diffusion time-step restriction from two independent arguments and compare explicit and implicit decay of spatial modes.

From first principles

Derive the explicit heat step and its grid-dependent time scale

Take the heat equation \(T_t=\alpha T_{xx}\) with constant positive diffusivity on a uniform one-dimensional grid. Fourier conduction through each side of a cell produces a temperature update proportional to the difference of the two neighbour gradients. With forward Euler timing, collect those fluxes to obtain

\[T_i^{n+1}=T_i^n+r(T_{i-1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i+1}^n),\]
\[r=\alpha\Delta t/h^2 .\]

When \(0\le r\le1/2\) the new temperature is a convex average of three old ones, preserving bounds on a source-free grid. To check whether a higher step could still be stable for all periodic grid disturbances, substitute \(T_j^n=g^n e^{ij\theta}\), where \(j\) is the node index. The amplification simplifies to

\[g=1+r(e^{-i\theta}-2+e^{i\theta})=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2).\]

The alternating mode at \(\theta=\pi\) gives \(g=1-4r\), so \(|g|\le1\) again requires \(r\le1/2\). Halving spatial spacing therefore quarters the largest explicit time step for this discretization. Implicit stepping changes this restriction but still has to resolve any transient relevant to the measurement.

Derivation and applications

Can an energy-stable heat step create an alternating transient?

A diffusion mode should decay rather than oscillate. To distinguish decay of a norm from preservation of sign, examine the finite-element or finite-volume semidiscrete system \(M\dot u+Ku=0\), with symmetric positive definite mass matrix \(M\) and thermal stiffness \(K\) positive semidefinite. An eigenvector satisfying \(Kv=\lambda Mv\) has scalar amplitude \(y(t)\) obeying \(y'=-\lambda y\). A generalized \(\theta\) step applied to this mode gives

\[[1+\theta z]y^{n+1}=[1-(1-\theta)z]y^n,\]
\[z=\Delta t\,\lambda .\]

For Crank–Nicolson take \(\theta=1/2\). The factor \(G=(1-z/2)/(1+z/2)\) has \(|G|\le1\) for all \(z\ge0\) but changes sign when \(z>2\). An initial grid-scale component can therefore alternate in time. To test a remedy, replace the first full step by two half backward-Euler steps. Their combined factor is

\[G_{\rm start}=(1+z/2)^{-2}.\]

Thereafter return to the second-order Crank–Nicolson update. After \(n\) further full steps the mode amplitudes, with and without the initial damping, are

\[y_{\rm damped}=G_{\rm start}G^ny^0,\]
\[y_{\rm plain}=G^{n+1}y^0 .\]

For small \(z\), both startup strategies agree through first order, while for large \(z\) the two half steps strongly suppress the unresolved mode. This argument explains a startup treatment for nonsmooth initial data; sign preservation of every physical grid value is a stronger matrix property than sign preservation of one mode.

Worked example

Check damping of a near-grid-scale eigenmode

Take \(\lambda=2000\) s⁻¹ and \(\Delta t=0.0100\) s. Then \(z=20\). A single Crank–Nicolson step leaves \(-9/11=-0.818\) of the amplitude; two backward-Euler half steps leave \(1/121=0.00826\). The exact amplitude factor is \(e^{-20}=2.06\times10^{-9}\). Both discrete results are bounded, but the startup treatment removes almost all of a mode that the time grid cannot resolve.

Second application

Check the cost for a physically slow component

For the same mesh and step, a slow mode with \(\lambda=2.00\) s⁻¹ has \(z=0.0200\). Crank–Nicolson gives \(0.99/1.01=0.980198\), the startup gives \(1/1.01^2=0.980296\) and the exact factor is \(0.980199\). The slow component changes by less than \(10^{-4}\) in this comparison. A timestep must still resolve a transient that is itself part of the desired output.

Discretise space and time separately

A heat pulse in a thin insulated rod smooths over time according to \(u_t=D u_{xx}\) for constant positive diffusivity \(D\). Use a uniform grid of spacing \(h\), and initially examine interior points or periodic boundaries so the stencil is unchanged everywhere. Replacing the second derivative by a centred difference gives a system of ordinary differential equations for the nodal temperatures. The time method still has to resolve the fastest rate that this spatial operator can generate.

An explicit Euler step yields \(u_i^{n+1}=r u_{i-1}^n+(1-2r)u_i^n+r u_{i+1}^n\) with \(r=D\Delta t/h^2\). If \(0\leq r\leq1/2\), the update is a convex combination of three temperatures. The maximum cannot rise and the minimum cannot fall in a source-free interior step. Starting with one hot node at 1 and neighbours at zero, \(r=0.4\) gives a centre value 0.2 and neighbours 0.4 after a single step. At \(r=0.6\) the centre would become negative although the continuum heat equation preserves nonnegativity.

Identify the spatial modes that set the limit

Insert \(u_j^n=g^n e^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}\), where \(\mathrm{i}\) is the imaginary unit and \(j\) indexes the grid. The two neighbouring values contribute factors \(e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\theta}\), yielding \(g=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Long waves have \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\simeq\theta^2/4\), so their decay approaches the continuum rate as the mesh is refined. The alternating grid mode \(\theta=\pi\) has the most negative amplification and enforces \(r\leq1/2\) for bounded magnitude.

At the limiting value \(r=1/2\) the alternating mode flips sign without losing amplitude. A strict margin damps it. For \(D=10^{-4}\) m²/s and \(h=2\) mm, the maximum explicit step is \(h^2/(2D)=0.020\) s. Refining to 1 mm reduces that limit to 0.005 s. The mesh gives a sharper temperature profile yet can cost four times more steps over the same physical interval, besides the increased number of spatial degrees of freedom.

Compare backward Euler and Crank–Nicolson

Writing the semidiscrete system as \(u'=-A_hu\), backward Euler solves \((I+\Delta t A_h)u^{n+1}=u^n\). Crank–Nicolson averages the old and new diffusive fluxes, giving \((I+\Delta t A_h/2)u^{n+1}=(I-\Delta t A_h/2)u^n\). For a Fourier mode put \(s=4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Their factors are \(1/(1+s)\) and \((1-s/2)/(1+s/2)\), respectively. Both remain bounded at arbitrarily large \(s\), but Crank–Nicolson may alternate for under-resolved sharp features.

With \(s=20\), backward Euler retains only \(1/21\) per step. Crank–Nicolson yields \(-9/11\), retaining much more of the fast component and reversing its sign. For a smooth low-frequency mode Crank–Nicolson generally gives more accurate temporal decay because it is second order. Decide whether early sharp transients or later smooth behaviour is the relevant physical quantity. To identify spatial and temporal error separately, refine \(\Delta t\) first on a fixed mesh, then refine space with time error kept well below spatial error. Merely halving both at once cannot reveal which term dominated the original discrepancy.

Apply a prescribed heat input without confusing it with temperature

An insulated periodic rod with uniform heating rate \(s\) measured in K/s has an average temperature that must rise by \(s\Delta t\) each step. Sum the centred second differences over the periodic indices: each nodal value appears with coefficients \(1,-2,1\), which cancel. The source alone changes the mean. For \(s=3\) K/s and a step of 0.02 s the numerical average must increase by 0.06 K, independently of the diffusivity and initial profile.

A code can pass this global test while still mishandling short-wave damping, since an alternating error has zero spatial mean. Pair a mean-temperature test with a nonconstant mode-decay test. Use a homogeneous periodic run for isolates of diffusion, and a uniform temperature with prescribed source for the storage term. A failure in one test then identifies a different part of the mathematical balance than a failure in the other.

Prove decay of a discrete thermal energy

Let a closed semidiscrete diffusion model be \(M\dot u+Ku=0\), with \(M\) symmetric positive definite and \(K\) symmetric positive semidefinite. Assume \(K{\bf1}=0\), expressing zero flux for a uniform temperature. Backward Euler satisfies \(M(u^{n+1}-u^n)+\Delta t Ku^{n+1}=0\). Multiplying by the constant vector and using symmetry gives \({\bf1}^TMu^{n+1}={\bf1}^TMu^n\). Thus the capacity-weighted sum, proportional to stored sensible heat for constant capacities and a fixed reference, is conserved for any step size.

Now multiply by \(u^{n+1}\). The identity \(a^TM(a-b)=[\|a\|_M^2-\|b\|_M^2+\|a-b\|_M^2]/2\) yields the exact balance

\[\tfrac12\|u^n\|_M^2-\tfrac12\|u^{n+1}\|_M^2=\Delta t\,(u^{n+1})^TKu^{n+1}+\tfrac12\|u^{n+1}-u^n\|_M^2 .\]

Both terms are nonnegative. The quadratic quantity therefore decays although total sensible heat is conserved. Subtracting the conserved mean first yields the same decay statement for temperature variation around equilibrium. The first term represents spatial smoothing measured at the new time, and the second reflects the numerical time update. This derivation gives a stability certificate on nonuniform grids without assuming Fourier modes.

Audit two cells with different heat capacities

Choose capacities 1 and 3 J/K, conductance 2 W/K, and initial temperature deviations \((4,0)^T\) K. The conserved sensible heat is 4 J, so the final equilibrium in a closed system must be one kelvin above reference. At \(\Delta t=0.5\) s the matrix \(M+\Delta tK\) has rows \((2,-1)\) and \((-1,4)\), and the right-hand side is \((4,0)^T\). The new temperatures are \((16/7,4/7)^T\). Checking their weighted sum gives \(16/7+3(4/7)=4\), as required. The unweighted average changes, which is physically appropriate for unequal capacities.

The quadratic quantity at the start is \(4^2/2=8\). After the step it is \([(16/7)^2+3(4/7)^2]/2=152/49\). Its drop is \(240/49\). The conductive contribution is \((0.5)(2)(12/7)^2=144/49\); the time-update contribution is \([(-12/7)^2+3(4/7)^2]/2=96/49\). Their sum recovers the measured drop exactly. Neither contribution equals a loss of the 4 J of sensible heat. The quadratic measure depends on squared temperatures and has different physical units.

A positive heat supply or a prescribed exterior temperature introduces a boundary or source work term into these identities. Applying the closed-system decay test to an externally heated experiment would therefore diagnose the wrong physical model. Verify which conserved or dissipated quantity is actually implied by the capacity matrix, boundary conditions and source terms before comparing numerical integrators.

The conserved mean suggests another check after many large steps. The steady state must have zero conductance dissipation, which for two connected cells requires equal temperatures. Together with the weighted heat total this fixes both temperatures at one kelvin above reference. A scheme that instead approaches the unweighted initial average of two would be converging to the wrong conserved quantity despite producing a visually smooth, uniform endpoint. The measured quadratic variation about the correct mean tends to zero; the uncentred squared quantity tends to a positive constant because the remaining uniform state still has temperature above the chosen reference.

Advect a marked volume

Worked solution

After an exact shift of half a cell, the two overlapping cell averages are \(1/2,1/2\) and the other two vanish, although the pointwise pulse height is still one. A peak comparison must specify whether it uses point values or cell means.

Identify a shock and a fan

For \(f(u)=u^2/2\), consider left and right states 3 and 1. Compute the discontinuity speed, compare it with the two characteristic speeds and locate its position after 0.4 s from the origin. Reverse the states and identify the edges of the entropy-admissible solution.

Worked solution

Fluxes are 4.5 and 0.5, giving \(s=(0.5-4.5)/(1-3)=2\). Since the characteristic speeds \(f'(u)\) are 3 on the left and 1 on the right, both enter the discontinuity in its moving frame. Its position is \(0.4(2)=0.8\).

With left 1 and right 3 the characteristics move apart and an expansion fan fills the region between \(x=0.4\) and \(x=1.2\). A single jump satisfying the algebraic jump balance would have the wrong entropy behaviour for these data.

Allocate a time budget on a refined grid

On a periodic rod \(D=2\times10^{-5}\) m²/s and \(h=1\) mm. Find the explicit diffusion step limit. To reach 1 s with \(r=0.4\), how many steps are needed? Repeat after halving spatial spacing and compare the total number of nodal updates on a fixed length.

Worked solution

The stability limit is \(h^2/(2D)=0.025\) s. For \(r=0.4\), use \(0.4h^2/D=0.020\) s, or 50 steps. Halving \(h\) quarters the step to 0.005 s, giving 200 steps.

Chapter 5 · Wave speeds and numerical dispersion

Derive the leapfrog dispersion relation, use the Courant condition to distinguish boundedness from accurate travel time and initialise a standing-wave calculation consistently.

From first principles

Derive the numerical wave dispersion relation by advancing a single mode

Consider a one-dimensional homogeneous wave with speed \(c\), uniform spatial grid \(h\) and time increment \(\Delta t\). Centred second differences approximate both accelerations and spatial curvatures using the values on either side of their evaluation point. The governing equation \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\) becomes

\[u_j^{n+1}-2u_j^n+u_j^{n-1}=C^2(u_{j+1}^n-2u_j^n+u_{j-1}^n),\]
\[C=c\Delta t/h .\]

To determine what the grid does to one travelling component, insert \(u_j^n=e^{i(kjh-\omega n\Delta t)}\) and divide by the value at \((j,n)\). In both directions the pair of opposite phases combines to a cosine. Using \(2-2\cos z=4\sin^2(z/2)\) then gives

\[\sin^2(\omega\Delta t/2)=C^2\sin^2(kh/2).\]

Long wavelengths recover \(\omega\simeq c|k|\). Short waves can propagate at a different phase speed even in a perfectly homogeneous physical medium. Requiring a real \(\omega\) for every resolved \(k\) gives \(C\le1\). This derivation concerns interior grid modes; domain boundaries can introduce reflected waves as a separate numerical effect.

Derivation and applications

Derive a conserved energy for the centred wave update

A time-stepping method can support a numerical travelling wave while transporting its phase at a slightly incorrect speed. For a second diagnostic, ask whether total discrete vibration energy remains bounded. Consider a uniform one-dimensional periodic grid for \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\), unit mass density and no forcing. Denote the forward spatial difference by \(D\) and the half-step velocity by \(v^{n+1/2}=(u^{n+1}-u^n)/\Delta t\). Summation by parts on this grid gives \(\langle v,D^\mathsf TD u\rangle=\langle Dv,Du\rangle\) using the grid inner product. The centred step can be written

\[v^{n+1/2}-v^{n-1/2}=-c^2\Delta t D^\mathsf TD u^n .\]

Take the inner product with \((v^{n+1/2}+v^{n-1/2})/2\). The left side becomes a difference of two kinetic energies. On the right use \(\Delta t(v^{n+1/2}+v^{n-1/2})=u^{n+1}-u^{n-1}\) and move \(D\) to the other factor:

\[E^{n+1/2}=E^{n-1/2},\]
\[E^{n+1/2}=\tfrac12\|v^{n+1/2}\|^2+\tfrac12c^2\langle Du^{n+1},Du^n\rangle.\]

The potential term couples consecutive time levels. Writing it in terms of the midpoint displacement \(\bar u=(u^{n+1}+u^n)/2\) identifies when the invariant bounds the solution:

\[E^{n+1/2}=\tfrac12\|v\|^2-\tfrac18 c^2\Delta t^2\|Dv\|^2+\tfrac12c^2\|D\bar u\|^2 .\]

Since \(\|Dv\|\le2\|v\|/\Delta x\), the kinetic part is bounded below by \((1-C^2)\|v\|^2/2\), with \(C=c\Delta t/\Delta x\). The invariant controls nonconstant waves for \(C<1\). An exact but indefinite conservation law alone would not guarantee a bounded numerical solution.

Worked example

Read a timestep from a material and grid

Take \(c=100\) m/s, \(\Delta x=0.0500\) m and \(\Delta t=0.400\) ms. Then \(C=0.800\) and the bound preserves at least \(1-C^2=0.360\) of the usual half-step kinetic term even in the worst spatial mode. The customary limiting step from this argument is \(0.500\) ms.

Second application

Show why equality of successive energies can mislead

Take the alternating grid mode for which \(\|Dv\|=2\|v\|/\Delta x\), and choose adjacent displacements whose midpoint is zero. At \(C=1.20\) the invariant for this state is \(E=(1-1.44)\|v\|^2/2=-0.220\|v\|^2\). Negative energy here is a property of the numerical quadratic form. A solver can preserve it exactly and still exhibit amplitude growth.

Distinguish transport of phase from transport of energy

A taut string with small transverse displacements and fixed tension obeys \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\). The physical speed \(c\) is independent of wavelength in this ideal one-dimensional model. A discrete solver can transport long waves at nearly the correct speed while short waves arrive late. In a pulse comprising many wavelengths, this artificial dependence on frequency distorts its shape even in a stable computation. A phase comparison at a probe provides information beyond monitoring maximum amplitude.

Use centred second differences in both time and space, with \(C=c\Delta t/h\). Solving explicitly for the new level gives \(u_i^{n+1}=2u_i^n-u_i^{n-1}+C^2(u_{i-1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i+1}^n)\). To initialise, expand \(u(\Delta t)=u(0)+\Delta t\,v(0)+(\Delta t)^2 c^2u_{xx}(0)/2+\cdots\). Replacing \(u_{xx}(0)\) by the same spatial difference gives a first level consistent with the initial velocity and acceleration. Simply copying the starting displacement to level one can introduce a spurious launch velocity.

Derive the discrete dispersion relation

Take a travelling mode \(u_i^n=\exp[\mathrm{i}(i\theta-n\omega_h\Delta t)]\) with spatial phase \(\theta=kh\). Inserting it makes the temporal second difference proportional to \(-4\sin^2(\omega_h\Delta t/2)\) and the spatial second difference proportional to \(-4\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Thus on the positive-frequency branch

\[\sin(\omega_h\Delta t/2)=C\sin(kh/2).\]

A real \(\omega_h\) exists for all resolvable real spatial phases only when \(C\leq1\). If \(C>1\), the shortest wave makes the right-hand side exceed one; a temporal amplification root then lies outside the unit circle. Even when stable, the ratio of numerical to physical phase speed is \(2\arcsin[C\sin(\theta/2)]/(C\theta)\). A fine spatial sample makes \(\theta\) small and the ratio tends to one. At \(C=1\) the ideal uniform one-dimensional interior stencil reproduces the wave phase on the resolved branch. Boundaries, material transitions and multiple dimensions require separate analysis.

Estimate a travel-time error

Take \(C=0.5\) and four points per wavelength, so \(\theta=\pi/2\). Then \(\omega_h\Delta t=2\arcsin[0.5\sin(\pi/4)]=0.72273\) rad. The physical advance is \(C\theta=\pi/4=0.78540\) rad. The numerical phase speed is about 0.920 of the true speed. After ten physical periods, its accumulated phase is about 9.20 cycles. A stable solution can therefore be nearly one oscillation behind while retaining a visually plausible amplitude.

Group velocity is \(d\omega_h/dk\), obtained by differentiating the dispersion relation. On this branch \(v_g/c=\cos(\theta/2)/\sqrt{1-C^2\sin^2(\theta/2)}\), about 0.756 in the four-point example. A narrow-band packet’s envelope and an individual crest then propagate at different numerical speeds. When interpreting a probe trace, say whether the reported arrival corresponds to a crest, an envelope or a threshold. Repeat the calculation at additional points per wavelength using the same Courant number to isolate spatial-phase improvement from changes in the time integration.

Interpret the grid’s travelling domain of dependence

In one explicit wave step, a node uses only itself and its two nearest neighbours. After \(n\) steps its numerical domain of dependence extends at most \(n\) grid cells to either side. The continuous wave at the same time can depend on initial data a distance \(cn\Delta t\) away. For convergence that physical domain must fit inside the numerical one, giving \(c\Delta t\leq h\). This reproduces the Courant condition from geometry without inserting a Fourier mode. It is a necessary information-speed test; by itself it does not establish stability or accuracy of an arbitrary scheme.

As an example, with \(c=340\) m/s and \(h=0.01\) m, the step bound is about 29.4 microseconds. A 20 microsecond step satisfies it but need not give an accurate answer for a mode represented by only three or four points per wavelength. Time-of-flight information and wavelength resolution are related to different discretisation choices. Document both when comparing two wave calculations.

Derive a reflected wave from a discrete outgoing condition

For a one-dimensional wave travelling right at speed \(c\), the outgoing continuum condition is \(u_t+cu_x=0\) at the right boundary. It annihilates an exact right-travelling waveform. A numerical boundary update using forward time and backward space gives \(u_N^{n+1}=(1-C)u_N^n+C u_{N-1}^n\), where \(C=c\Delta t/h\). A wave satisfying the interior discrete dispersion relation need not satisfy this one-sided relation. Even when the continuum boundary is perfectly absorbing, a mismatch of discrete phase relations can send energy back into the computational domain.

Let \(z=e^{-i\omega\Delta t}\), with the incident and reflected grid waves represented by \(e^{i\theta(j-N)}z^n\) and \(R e^{-i\theta(j-N)}z^n\). At the boundary their sum is \(1+R\) times \(z^n\). Substitution in the outgoing update and grouping incident and reflected terms gives

\[R=-\frac{z-1+C-Ce^{-i\theta}}{z-1+C-Ce^{i\theta}}.\]

This amplitude ratio assumes a homogeneous grid, a propagating frequency and distinct incident and reflected branches. It compares each wave at the boundary itself. Signals measured farther inward acquire additional phase and travel-time factors and can interfere if both arrive in the same observation window.

Calculate a reflection generated by numerical mismatch

Use the centred wave scheme with \(C=1/2\) and grid angle \(\theta=\pi/2\), four cells per wavelength. The interior relation gives \(\sin(\omega\Delta t/2)=(1/2)\sin(\pi/4)=1/(2\sqrt2)\), hence \(z=0.75-i\sqrt7/4\). The incident mismatch in the numerator is \(0.25-i(\sqrt7/4-0.5)\); the reflected denominator is \(0.25-i(\sqrt7/4+0.5)\). Squaring the magnitudes gives \((3-\sqrt7)/4\) and \((3+\sqrt7)/4\), respectively. Therefore \(|R|=\sqrt{(3-\sqrt7)/(3+\sqrt7)}\approx0.2505\). Roughly one quarter of the incident amplitude is reflected solely by this discrete boundary treatment.

For incident and reflected branches on the same symmetric grid, their group-speed magnitudes match. The corresponding reflected energy fraction is \(|R|^2\approx0.0627\), about six percent. That is small enough for a short experiment to appear satisfactory until the pulse returns to a sensor. Testing only the incident travel time would miss this boundary error. Compare a time window before reflection with the expected window for the return journey.

At \(C=1\) and \(0<\theta<\pi\), the right-moving interior root is \(z=e^{-i\theta}\). The numerator vanishes exactly and the boundary update transmits that branch without reflection. This exact compatibility is special to this one-dimensional grid, this step size and this outgoing condition. Varying the propagation angle in two dimensions, changing an interface spacing or using a higher-order interior stencil changes the phase relation that the boundary operator needs to match.

There is a practical way to isolate this reflection in a pulse computation. Record incident and outgoing velocity or flux at the same interior sensor, and choose the duration so that the reflected signal arrives after the incident pulse has passed. Normalise amplitudes in the same physical variable, and avoid dividing a pressure peak by a displacement peak. A broadband pulse contains many values of the grid angle, so its returned waveform can change shape even when each monochromatic component obeys the calculated reflection coefficient.

Chapter 6 · Verification, refinement and local error

Use a manufactured solution to verify an implementation, estimate observed convergence and identify when adaptivity is needed for the physical quantity of interest.

From first principles

Derive the observed order and an extrapolated continuum output

Verification compares the discretized problem against a known solution or controlled refinement. Choose one scalar output \(Q\), the same geometry and the same physical observation point on each mesh. Assume a smooth leading error \(Q_h=Q+C h^p+o(h^p)\) on geometrically similar meshes, with refinement ratio \(r>1\). Subtract successive estimates to remove the unknown continuum output:

\[Q_h-Q_{h/r}\simeq Ch^p(1-r^{-p}),\]
\[Q_{h/r}-Q_{h/r^2}\simeq Ch^pr^{-p}(1-r^{-p}).\]

Their ratio approaches \(r^p\), provided the leading coefficient is nonzero. Solve for the order, then use the finest pair to remove the leading error term:

\[p_{\rm obs}=\frac{\ln|(Q_h-Q_{h/r})/(Q_{h/r}-Q_{h/r^2})|}{\ln r},\]
\[Q_{\rm ext}=Q_{h/r^2}+\frac{Q_{h/r^2}-Q_{h/r}}{r^{p_{\rm obs}}-1}.\]

The ratio is meaningful only when a consistent leading error dominates. Differential changes of solver tolerance, geometry or timestep across the meshes can contaminate it. A manufactured solution independently tests discretization and boundary terms by inserting a chosen exact field into the differential operator and supplying the resulting source.

Derivation and applications

How the measured norm changes an observed convergence rate

A verification study may report very different rates for the same discrete field. Choose a fixed one-dimensional domain of length one, divided into cells of width \(h\). Suppose the solution is smooth away from an interface, where a nearby discontinuity, boundary approximation or reconstruction produces a localized error. Let the error magnitude be \(A h^\alpha\) on a fixed number \(m\) of cells near the interface, and at most \(B h^p\) elsewhere. The count \(m\) stays fixed under refinement. For a finite \(q\ge1\) use the physically weighted norm instead of an unweighted vector norm:

\[\|e_h\|_q^q=\sum_i h|e_i|^q \ \lesssim\ m A^q h^{q\alpha+1}+B^q h^{qp}.\]

As \(h\) decreases the term with the smaller exponent decays more slowly. If the indicated leading magnitudes are actually attained, the corresponding rate of the norm is

\[\|e_h\|_q=O(h^{\min(\alpha+1/q,p)}),\]
\[\|e_h\|_\infty=O(h^{\min(\alpha,p)}).\]

The extra \(1/q\) comes from the shrinking physical measure of the affected region. It disappears in a maximum norm. A single signed integral of error can give still another result because opposite local errors cancel:

\[\left|\sum_i h e_i\right|\le\sum_i h|e_i|=\|e_h\|_1 .\]

A convergence statement therefore requires the reported norm, physical location, and mesh sequence. If an interface stays at a changing subcell position as the grid is refined, the leading coefficient can vary; observed slopes from just two grids can then be misleading.

Worked example

Calculate three norms for a displaced step

Let one cell of a piecewise-constant reconstruction differ from the exact field by \(1.00\), with zero error elsewhere. At \(h=1/64\), the \(L^1\) error is \(1/64=0.015625\), the \(L^2\) error is \(\sqrt{1/64}=0.125\) and the maximum error is \(1.00\). Refining to \(h=1/256\) gives \(0.003906\), \(0.0625\) and \(1.00\). The measured rates are one, one half and zero for precisely the same displacement.

Second application

Compare cancellation with local accuracy

Suppose two adjacent cells at \(h=1/100\) have errors \(+0.500\) and \(-0.500\). The signed integral discrepancy is zero; the \(L^1\) error is \(0.0100\) and the \(L^2\) error is \(\sqrt{0.005}=0.0707\). Checking only total inventory would certify the output but miss the incorrect distribution. A local measurement or absolute-error norm verifies a different and often more demanding quantity.

Build a test with a known answer

Manufacturing a source tests whether the code solves the stated model and boundary conditions. Experimental validation asks a separate question about fidelity to physical observations. A polished plot resembling the exact field does not substitute for a numerical error measure with defined sample positions and units.

Read a three-grid study

On successively halved mesh spacings suppose the error in one norm follows \(E_h=C h^p+o(h^p)\). Taking ratios gives \(p\simeq\log_2(E_h/E_{h/2})\). Decide where further resolution matters

A manufactured sine is smooth everywhere. Actual domains may contain a re-entrant corner, a material jump or a narrow source. Derivative bounds used in theoretical convergence estimates can fail locally there. Uniformly halving a two-dimensional mesh roughly quadruples the cell count while spending most new cells away from a localised feature. Local refinement attempts to concentrate new degrees of freedom where they matter to a chosen output. Which cells merit refinement depends on the equation, boundary data and the output being measured.

For a conforming finite-element approximation, one class of error indicators combines an element residual with jumps in normal flux across interior faces. The residual tests whether each element satisfies the differential balance, while the jumps measure disagreements between neighbouring reconstructed fluxes. In a constant-coefficient Poisson example, a piecewise linear solution can have zero elementwise Laplacian even when it bends globally; the slope changes then appear at interelement faces. An indicator using only the interior derivative would miss this information. Mesh-length factors and coefficient weights are necessary to combine the terms in a specific norm.

Finish a refinement study by tracking the physical observable itself. If the objective is integrated heat flow, a changing maximum pointwise gradient near one corner may have little influence on that integral. Conversely, apparently stable domain averages can hide an unresolved peak. Report observed change with resolution and an independent balance or analytic check, specifying exactly which quantity has been verified.

Choose cells to refine from residuals and flux jumps

A mesh-convergence table needs several complete solutions. To spend the next refinement locally, examine the equation that the present field still fails to satisfy. For \(-u''=f\) with zero Dirichlet endpoints, let \(u_h\) be its conforming piecewise-linear Galerkin solution. Integrating the error equation by parts on each cell \(K\) gives

\[\int_0^1(u-u_h)'v'\,dx=\sum_K\int_K(f+u_h'')v\,dx-\sum_z J_z v(z),\qquad J_z=u_h'(z^-)-u_h'(z^+) .\]

The sum over \(z\) includes interior nodes only. Even when the cell derivative is constant and \(u_h''=0\) in each open cell, its jumps measure failures of flux continuity. Dropping these terms would miss part of the weak residual. Galerkin orthogonality lets us substitute \(v-I_hv\) for \(v\), where \(I_hv\) is a local conforming approximation. Local interpolation and trace estimates supply the mesh-length factors in an indicator. One conventional allocation on a uniform mesh is

\[\eta_K^2=h_K^2\int_K(f+u_h'')^2\,dx+\frac{h_K}{2}\sum_{z\in\partial K\ {\rm interior}}J_z^2 .\]

The half shares a node between its two neighbors. An upper bound on the energy error involves the square root of the summed indicators and a mesh-independent constant. Efficiency estimates can also involve unresolved variations in the load. The indicator is a refinement criterion, not a measured cell error.

Chapter 7 · Interfaces, contact resistance and Robin data

Derive a face flux that transports the same heat through two different materials and through a convective boundary.

From first principles

Derive an interface flux from two half-cell temperature drops

Two neighbouring cell centres can lie in materials with sharply different conductivities. Consider a planar interface between them, normal to a one-dimensional steady heat flow. Let \(d_L,d_R\) be the distances from the centres to the interface, and suppose there is no interface source, storage or contact resistance. Steady conservation requires one heat flux \(q\), positive to the right, and perfect contact requires one interface temperature \(T_I\). Applying Fourier’s law to each half gives

\[q=k_L(T_L-T_I)/d_L=k_R(T_I-T_R)/d_R .\]

Solve each expression for its temperature drop, add the two drops and eliminate \(T_I\). The conductance follows from the actual material distances:

\[q=\frac{T_L-T_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R},\]
\[k_{\rm eff}=\frac{d_L+d_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R}.\]

For equal distances this is the harmonic mean; only equal conductivities recover the same result as the arithmetic mean. If one material approaches zero conductivity with positive thickness, the flux approaches zero. A finite thermal contact resistance adds another temperature drop \(qR_c\), while a heat-generating interface alters the flux-continuity condition itself.

Derivation and applications

Enforce the flux jump at a heat-generating interface

Two materials in series carry the same conductive current only if their common interface stores and generates no energy. Consider two homogeneous slabs with an idealized thin heater between them. Let \(T_L,T_R\) be prescribed outer surface temperatures, \(R_L,R_R\) the positive thermal resistances per unit area in m² K/W, and \(s\) the heater release in W/m². Assume perfect thermal contact, steady conduction and negligible thickness of the heater. Define the interface temperature as \(T_I\) and flux \(q\) positive to the right.

Fourier’s law integrated through each slab relates its own constant flux to its temperature drop. The pillbox balance across the heater requires the right flux to exceed the left one by the generated heat:

\[q_L=(T_L-T_I)/R_L,\]
\[q_R=(T_I-T_R)/R_R,\]
\[q_R-q_L=s .\]

Substitute the two face laws into the balance. With \(g_j=1/R_j\) as the two conductances per area, the interface temperature and individual fluxes are

\[T_I=\frac{g_LT_L+g_RT_R+s}{g_L+g_R},\]
\[q_R=g_R(T_I-T_R),\]
\[q_L=g_L(T_L-T_I).\]

Alternatively eliminate \(T_I\) to express the fluxes as through-flow plus a split source. The resistances determine the fraction of added heat directed to each boundary:

\[q_L=\frac{T_L-T_R-sR_R}{R_L+R_R},\]
\[q_R=\frac{T_L-T_R+sR_L}{R_L+R_R}.\]

Setting \(s=0\) restores a single common flux. Equal outside temperatures send more generated heat toward the lower resistance. For an interface at a mesh face the two cells therefore need distinct physical face fluxes linked by the stated jump; assigning exactly the same flux while adding no source anywhere violates energy balance.

Worked example

Determine both heat flows through an instrumented wall

Let \(T_L=320\) K, \(T_R=300\) K, \(R_L=0.100\), \(R_R=0.200\) m² K/W and \(s=150\) W/m². The interface sees conductances \(10.0\) and \(5.00\) W/(m² K) and reaches \(323.33\) K. The left flux is \(-33.3\) W/m² and the right flux \(116.7\) W/m². Heat leaves on both sides, including toward the hotter external surface. Their oriented difference equals \(150\) W/m².

Second application

Select a heater setting that makes one sensor read zero

Keeping the same temperatures and layers, impose \(q_L=0\). Then \(T_I=T_L=320\) K and \(s=(T_L-T_R)/R_R=100\) W/m². All generated heat exits on the right. Reducing \(s\) to zero instead gives \(66.7\) W/m² from left to right through both layers. A zero left-side flux in the heated experiment therefore does not imply an insulating left layer.

One flux through resistances in series

Let \(q=-kT'\) be heat flux in the positive coordinate direction, with unit cross-sectional area and no internal source. Between two cell centres separated by an interface, let the left and right centre-to-interface distances be \(d_L,d_R\). Piecewise constant conductivities \(k_L,k_R>0\) make the equilibrium temperature linear in each segment. If the interface has perfect contact, its temperature \(T_f\) is continuous. Integrating Fourier's law on each side gives \(T_L-T_f=q\,d_L/k_L\) and \(T_f-T_R=q\,d_R/k_R\). Adding eliminates the unknown interface temperature.

\[q=\frac{T_L-T_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R}.\]

For equal distances this amounts to using the harmonic mean conductivity over the full centre spacing. Inserting the same signed \(q\) as the outflow of the left cell and the inflow of the right cell produces cancellation when their balances are added. If a material interface stores energy or supplies heat independently, a flux jump belongs in the balance and this steady series calculation must be modified.

Measure the temperature drop

Take \(d_L=d_R=0.01\) m, \(k_L=2,\ k_R=10\) W/(m K) and \(T_L=30,\ T_R=18\) °C. The series resistance per unit area is \(0.005+0.001=0.006\) m²K/W. The flux is \(2000\) W/m² and the interface temperature is \(30-2000(0.005)=20\) °C. The lower conductivity carries the larger temperature drop despite carrying the same flux. Using the arithmetic mean 6 with the centre difference over 0.02 m instead predicts \(3600\) W/m².

Introduce a contact resistance \(R_c=0.002\) m²K/W. Its temperature jump is \(qR_c\); the total resistance rises to 0.008, reducing the heat flux to 1500 W/m². The two sides of the contact now have temperatures 22.5 and 19.5 °C. Imposing a single interface temperature on this model would remove the physically measured 3 K jump.

Apply a convective boundary

At an exterior face the outward heat flux obeys \(q=h_c(T_s-T_\infty)\), a Robin condition. Eliminate the unknown surface temperature as above to obtain \(q=(T_P-T_\infty)/(d/k+1/h_c)\). If \(h_c\) tends to zero, the boundary becomes insulating. With very large \(h_c\), the surface approaches ambient temperature while the finite centre-to-face resistance remains. For \(d=0.01\) m, \(k=2\), \(h_c=100\) W/(m² K), \(T_P-T_\infty=15\) K, the predicted flux is 1000 W/m² and the surface is 10 K above ambient. Multiplying by face area converts this flux to the power used in a cell energy balance. Mixing up these two quantities makes the result depend incorrectly on mesh area.

A jump located inside a cell

If the materials change at a known position inside a segment, the fractions of that segment matter. Moving the interface so that 75 percent of the length has conductivity 2 and 25 percent has conductivity 10 produces effective conductivity \(1/(0.75/2+0.25/10)=2.5\) W/(m K). Giving each material half the segment instead would give \(10/3\) W/(m K). The harmonic rule depends on actual layer thicknesses. A mesh-refinement study that snaps the interface to different positions at every refinement can mix geometric representation error with flux-discretisation error.

Derive effective transport parallel and normal to layers

A fine laminate of isotropic materials can have a direction-dependent macroscopic conductivity. Consider two perfectly bonded planar layers occupying thickness fractions \(\theta\) and \(1-\theta\), with conductivities \(k_1,k_2\). Assume steady conduction, no internal sources, and boundary conditions that permit a one-dimensional field for the loading orientation being tested. For transport normal to the layers, continuity forces equal flux. Adding their temperature drops across a total thickness \(L\) gives \(k_\perp=[\theta/k_1+(1-\theta)/k_2]^{-1}\). For transport parallel to the layers the imposed longitudinal gradient is common and the area-weighted fluxes add, giving \(k_\parallel=\theta k_1+(1-\theta)k_2\).

The arithmetic and harmonic expressions are thus both correct, but apply to different geometries of imposed transport. Their ordering follows without a conductivity table: multiplying the two positive weighted sums \(\theta k_1+(1-\theta)k_2\) and \(\theta/k_1+(1-\theta)/k_2\) gives \(1+\theta(1-\theta)(k_1-k_2)^2/(k_1k_2)\ge1\). Equality requires identical conductivities or a missing layer. Spatial averaging performed before identifying the flux direction would erase precisely the distinction that determines the coarse physics.

Predict two experiments on the same material sample

Suppose a repeated laminate consists of 40 percent material with conductivity 1 and 60 percent with conductivity 10 W/(m K). Then \(k_\parallel=0.4+6=6.4\) W/(m K) and \(k_\perp=1/(0.4+0.06)=50/23\approx2.174\) W/(m K). A temperature drop of 4 K over 0.02 m imposes gradient magnitude 200 K/m. Perfectly normal loading produces flux magnitude about 434.8 W/m², whereas the same gradient parallel to the layers yields 1280 W/m². The larger flux is consistent with the fast material providing a continuous path in the latter orientation.

For the normal experiment the low-conductivity phase occupies 0.008 m and accounts for a drop \(434.8(0.008)/1\approx3.478\) K. The high-conductivity phase occupies 0.012 m and drops only \(434.8(0.012)/10\approx0.522\) K. Adding recovers the imposed four kelvin. For the parallel experiment individual flux densities are 200 and 2000 W/m². Averaging by their area fractions gives \(0.4(200)+0.6(2000)=1280\) W/m². These local checks use the same two material properties and explain the difference in the observed coarse responses.

Using the effective tensor assumes the observation scale spans representative layers. A sensor within one layer may respond to its local temperature slope instead. Finite contact resistance adds a temperature jump in the normal direction; if the number of interfaces per macroscopic length changes, the effective normal resistance changes even at identical material fractions. Measuring both orientations can distinguish geometric or contact effects that a single scalar fitted conductivity would conceal.

Rotating a laminate specimen through ninety degrees interchanges the roles of these two effective values without changing its volume fractions. This makes an orientation experiment particularly discriminating: a fit that happened to reproduce the first orientation by adjusting a scalar conductivity predicts the same flux for the rotated specimen, whereas the fine-layer balance predicts a factor of about \(6.4/(50/23)=2.94\) between them.

Repeating the test over multiple layer spacings also probes the assumption of negligible contact resistance.

Chapter 8 · Anisotropic diffusion and mesh alignment

Relate a diffusion tensor to principal directions and test whether a face-flux approximation can represent a rotated material.

From first principles

Derive the anisotropic diffusion operator from a rotated material law

Choose the principal axes of a homogeneous medium. Along them the measured flux response is diagonal, so heat conduction in direction 1 has coefficient \(k_1>0\) and in direction 2 has \(k_2>0\). Let orthogonal matrix \(R\) map principal-axis components into laboratory coordinates. Gradients rotate back with \(R^\mathsf T\), while the calculated flux rotates forward with \(R\). The material law in laboratory axes is therefore

\[\mathbf q=-K\nabla T,\]
\[K=R\begin{pmatrix}k_1&0\\0&k_2\end{pmatrix}R^\mathsf T .\]

Insert this vector law into local steady conservation \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=s\), with \(s\) a positive volumetric source. Expanding for constant \(K\) gives the mixed derivative explicitly:

\[-K_{xx}T_{xx}-2K_{xy}T_{xy}-K_{yy}T_{yy}=s .\]

Positive principal conductivities make \(\nabla T^\mathsf T K\nabla T\) nonnegative, the basic energy property that a spatial discretization should respect. In a rotated material, the flux need not be perpendicular to an isotherm. A face formula using only the difference of two temperatures separated along the face normal may therefore omit a real transverse-gradient contribution.

Derivation and applications

When a two-point face flux is exact for anisotropic diffusion

A discretization can preserve conservation and still send heat in the wrong direction. Consider a planar face with area \(A\), outward unit normal \(\mathbf n\) from cell \(P\) toward cell \(N\), and centre displacement \(\mathbf d=\mathbf x_N-\mathbf x_P\). Let the constant material tensor \(K\) be symmetric positive definite. For a linear temperature field \(T=T_0+\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf x\), the continuum face rate and measured neighbour difference are

\[F=-A\mathbf n^\mathsf{T}K\mathbf g=-A(K\mathbf n)\cdot\mathbf g,\]
\[T_N-T_P=\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf g .\]

A candidate two-point law is \(F_2=-\tau(T_N-T_P)\). For it to reproduce the exact flux for every possible gradient, the two vectors multiplying \(\mathbf g\) must be identical. This gives a joint mesh-material condition:

\[\tau\mathbf d=AK\mathbf n .\]

On a grid aligned with a principal axis and with cell centres aligned along the normal, this relation gives the familiar transmissibility. Otherwise split \(AK\mathbf n\) into a component parallel to \(\mathbf d\) and a remainder \(\mathbf c\) perpendicular to it. Taking a scalar product with \(\mathbf d\) yields

\[\tau=A(K\mathbf n)\cdot\mathbf d/|\mathbf d|^2,\]
\[\mathbf c=AK\mathbf n-\tau\mathbf d,\]
\[F=-\tau(T_N-T_P)-\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf g .\]

The correction requires a second gradient component, usually reconstructed from more cells. Using one corrected flux with opposite signs for the two neighbours still preserves conservation. The derivation checks consistency for an exactly linear field; monotonicity and stability require separate tests.

Worked example

Test a Cartesian face in a tilted material

Let \(A=1.00\) m², \(\mathbf d=(0.200,0)\) m, \(\mathbf n=(1,0)\) and \(K\) have entries \((K_{xx},K_{xy},K_{yy})=(4,1,2)\) W/(m K). For \(\mathbf g=(0,3.00)\) K/m the neighbour difference vanishes, yet the exact outward flux is \(-3.00\) W. The decomposition gives \(\tau=20.0\) W/K and \(\mathbf c=(0,1)\) in compatible units. The entire flux therefore comes from the reconstructed transverse gradient.

Second application

Choose a skew cell arrangement that needs no correction

Keep the same tensor and normal but set \(\mathbf d=(0.200,0.0500)\) m. It lies along \((4,1)=K\mathbf n\), so \(\tau=20.0\) W/K and \(\mathbf c=0\). For a gradient \((2.00,-1.00)\) K/m the neighbour temperature rise is \(0.350\) K, giving \(-7.00\) W. Direct evaluation \(-[4(2)+1(-1)]\) agrees. Geometric orthogonality to the physical face is not required when the tensor itself tilts the relevant direction.

Begin with an energy and a constitutive law

In a conducting sheet with directional properties, heat flux is \(\boldsymbol q=-K\nabla T\), where \(K\) is symmetric positive definite. With constant volumetric capacity, conservation gives a diffusion operator proportional to \(\nabla\cdot(K\nabla T)\). For insulating boundaries and no sources, multiplying by \(T\) and integrating by parts gives a decay term \(-\int\nabla T^TK\nabla T\). It is nonpositive because the eigenvalues of \(K\) are positive. This energy test checks the sign of a discrete spatial operator even when flux and gradient do not point along the same line.

Let the principal conductivities be 4 and 1. Rotating the principal axes through 45 degrees gives \(K=R\operatorname{diag}(4,1)R^T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2.5&1.5\\1.5&2.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Along the diagonal direction \((1,1)/\sqrt2\), multiplying by \(K\) scales the gradient fourfold; along the other diagonal the scale is one. Along the Cartesian \(x\) direction a transverse flux component appears. The off-diagonal entries express a real constitutive response and cannot be dropped by refining the same incorrect stencil.

A patch test for a face flux

Use the exact affine temperature \(T(x,y)=y\), whose gradient is \((0,1)^T\). The flux is \((-1.5,-2.5)^T\). A vertical face with outward normal \(e_x\) therefore carries \(-1.5\) units of flux per unit length. A two-point formula based only on temperatures at the left and right cell centres sees identical temperatures and returns zero. Both fields have zero divergence in the interior because each flux is constant, so checking interior residuals alone would miss the incorrect boundary transport. Compare individual face fluxes as well as cell residuals in a linear patch test.

To recover the normal flux one can reconstruct the full gradient using neighbouring data, or choose a weak form integrating \((\nabla v)^TK\nabla T\) over each element. On a triangle with affine basis functions the gradients are constant, so constant \(K\) yields an element matrix \(A_{ij}=|E|\nabla\phi_i^TK\nabla\phi_j\). Symmetry is immediate and the row sums vanish because the basis functions sum to one.

Distinguish length scales from transport axes

For constant \(K\), a Fourier mode \(e^{i\xi\cdot x}\) decays with factor \(e^{-t\xi^TK\xi}\) when capacity is one. At unit wave number, a mode along the fast principal axis falls to \(e^{-4}\approx0.0183\) after one unit of time; a perpendicular one retains \(e^{-1}\approx0.368\). This test excites the two conductivities separately. On an elongated mesh the mesh dimensions also affect the fastest discrete time scale, so the same physical tensor can require different explicit step sizes on two grids.

Rotate the temperature, keeping the material fixed

The directional heat transport along a unit vector \(n\) is controlled by \(n^TKn\) when the imposed temperature gradient is parallel to \(n\). For the worked tensor and \(n=(1,0)^T\), this effective conductivity is 2.5 even though the full flux has a transverse component. If the specimen instead has insulated long sides, then its transverse gradient can adjust until transverse flux vanishes. Solving \(1.5T_x+2.5T_y=0\) gives \(T_y=-0.6T_x\) and effective longitudinal flux \(-1.6T_x\). The same material therefore gives different one-dimensional reductions under different boundary constraints.

Construct a positive directional stencil for a rotated tensor

For constant \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), the diffusion operator is \(a u_{xx}+2b u_{xy}+c u_{yy}\). On a square grid, the centred second difference along the lattice diagonal \((1,1)\), divided by \(h^2\), approximates \((\partial_x+\partial_y)^2u\), including the required mixed derivative with positive sign. For \(b\ge0\) one can therefore combine the three directional second differences with coefficients \(a-b,c-b,b\) along the horizontal, vertical and positive diagonal directions. Expanding these terms recovers the desired operator for every quadratic field.

If all three coefficients are nonnegative, a local maximum has a nonpositive spatial diffusion update. This sign property supports a discrete maximum principle and, with a sufficiently small explicit time step, a positive averaging update. For negative \(b\), the opposite diagonal changes the mixed derivative sign and the analogous coefficients involve \(|b|\). Positive definiteness of the continuum tensor only requires \(ac>b^2\), which is weaker than both \(a\ge|b|\) and \(c\ge|b|\). A physically valid material can therefore defeat this particular compact positive stencil.

Reach a strongly tilted direction using a wider stencil

Set \(a=1,\ b=1.5,\ c=4\). The determinant \(4-2.25=1.75\) is positive and both eigenvalues are positive. Yet the compact three-direction construction would assign horizontal coefficient \(-0.5\). To see the numerical problem, give the centre value zero, both horizontal neighbours value one and all diagonal and vertical neighbours zero. The centre is a minimum, but the compact operator returns \(-1/h^2\). An explicit positive time step would drive that minimum below zero, contradicting the smoothing behaviour expected from a monotone diffusion discretisation.

Consider instead a second difference along the longer lattice vector \((1,2)\). Dividing by \(h^2\) with coordinate increments \(h,2h\) approximates \(u_{xx}+4u_{xy}+4u_{yy}\). Multiplying it by \(0.75\) produces the desired mixed coefficient three. The remaining horizontal and vertical coefficients are \(1-0.75=0.25\) and \(4-3=1\), both positive. At an interior node this wider stencil has all positive neighbour couplings. On the quadratic patch \(u=xy\) the exact operator value is three; the long diagonal evaluates the centred difference as four, and its weighted contribution is exactly three while both axial differences vanish.

The longer reach introduces a boundary question: a point one row from the edge may lack a neighbour two rows away. Replacing it ad hoc with a nearest available value can destroy consistency or positivity. A boundary treatment, a mesh aligned to the material axes or a compatible finite-element formulation must address that issue separately. Selecting a positive interior stencil alone does not prove a complete domain discretisation monotone.

For the wider stencil an explicit update at an interior node has centre coefficient \(1-2(0.25+1+0.75)\Delta t/h^2\) for unit heat capacity. Preserving nonnegative weights therefore requires \(\Delta t\le h^2/4\) in the units of this example. Positive face couplings are necessary for this argument, while a compatible time step supplies the remaining condition.

Chapter 9 · Cell Péclet number and monotonicity

Derive the sign restriction for centred transport, identify a numerical boundary layer and quantify the diffusion introduced by upwinding.

From first principles

Derive the Péclet restriction for a centred steady stencil

Take steady one-dimensional transport with constant speed \(a>0\), diffusivity \(D>0\) and no interior source. Conservation requires constant total flux \(J=au-Du'\). On a uniform mesh of spacing \(h\), approximate the advective face value by the arithmetic mean and the face gradient by a difference. The right face flux becomes

\[J_{i+1/2}=a(u_i+u_{i+1})/2-D(u_{i+1}-u_i)/h .\]

Equating the two neighbouring face fluxes and collecting the centre coefficient gives a relation among the three consecutive values:

\[2u_i=(1+\mathrm{Pe}_h/2)u_{i-1}+(1-\mathrm{Pe}_h/2)u_{i+1},\]
\[\mathrm{Pe}_h=ah/D .\]

For \(|\mathrm{Pe}_h|\le2\) the centre value is a positive weighted average of its neighbours. Above that threshold one weight becomes negative and the discrete solution can overshoot even when the continuum solution is monotone. This restriction is on the local cell Péclet number, not on \(aL/D\) for the whole physical domain. Smaller cells, an upwind flux or an exponentially fitted flux can change the discrete behaviour.

Derivation and applications

Distinguish an imposed inlet flux from an imposed inlet concentration

With diffusion, a concentration measurement alone does not specify the total material entering a channel. Consider steady one-dimensional advection at constant speed \(a>0\) and diffusion coefficient \(D>0\), constant area, no reaction, and coordinate \(0<x<L\). Concentration is \(u(x)\). The net species flux is the sum of advection and diffusive backflow. Conservation makes it constant throughout the interval:

\[J=a u-Du',\]
\[J'=0 .\]

Suppose the upstream supply delivers a specified total flux \(J=a u_f\), where \(u_f\) is the incoming feed concentration used to normalize the supply rate. Prescribe an outlet concentration \(u(L)=u_L\) maintained by a reservoir. Subtract the uniform particular solution \(u_f\). The resulting first-order homogeneous equation for the excess has a convenient outlet-referenced solution:

\[(u-u_f)'=(a/D)(u-u_f),\]
\[u(x)=u_f+(u_L-u_f)e^{-a(L-x)/D}.\]

At the inlet the interior concentration can differ from the feed because diffusion also contributes to the prescribed mass rate. Define \(\mathrm{Pe}=aL/D\) and evaluate both terms at \(x=0\):

\[u(0)=u_f+(u_L-u_f)e^{-\mathrm{Pe}},\]
\[-D u'(0)=-a[u(0)-u_f] .\]

For large Péclet number the outlet affects only a layer of thickness \(D/a\). For small Péclet number the outlet reservoir can affect the whole channel. Imposing both \(u(0)=u_f\) and the specified total flux at the inlet would impose more conditions than this problem needs. A numerical method should record which physical boundary condition it approximates.

Worked example

Compute a moderate-Péclet-number channel profile

Take \(L=1.00\) m, \(a=0.0200\) m/s, \(D=0.0100\) m²/s, \(u_f=1.00\) and \(u_L=0\) kg/m³. The inlet concentration is \(1-e^{-2}=0.8647\) kg/m³ and the midpoint is \(1-e^{-1}=0.6321\) kg/m³. Inlet advection carries \(0.01729\) kg/(m² s) and diffusion supplies \(0.00271\) kg/(m² s), giving the prescribed total \(0.0200\).

Second application

Quantify a boundary-condition error

If a code instead fixes \(u(0)=1.00\) and \(u(L)=0\), its exact constant flux for those Dirichlet data satisfies \(u(0)=(J/a)(1-e^{-\mathrm{Pe}})\). Hence \(J=0.0200/(1-e^{-2})=0.0231\) kg/(m² s), about \(15.7\%\) too large for the original feed problem. Grid refinement can converge cleanly to that incorrect-for-purpose value because the error lies in the boundary model.

Which length sets the balance?

Model a steady scalar on \([0,L]\) with \(a u'-\kappa u''=0\), constant speed \(a>0\) and diffusivity \(\kappa>0\). Advection and diffusion balance over length \(\ell=\kappa/a\). On a mesh with spacing \(h\), the cell Péclet number \(P=ah/\kappa\) measures how many layer lengths fit into one cell. This definition uses the full cell spacing; some conventions divide it by two. Stating the convention matters when quoting a threshold.

Write the centred interior equation in flux form. Its coefficients multiplying \(u_{j-1},u_j,u_{j+1}\) are \(-\kappa/h^2-a/(2h),2\kappa/h^2,-\kappa/h^2+a/(2h)\). For \(P\le2\), the neighbour coefficients are nonpositive and the row sum is zero, so each interior value is a weighted average of its neighbours. For \(P>2\), one weight changes sign. The discrete maximum principle can fail even though the centred derivative is formally second-order accurate on smooth resolved fields.

Watch a node cross below the boundary range

Take \(L=1,a=1,\kappa=0.1\), \(u(0)=0,u(1)=1\) and two equal cells. Then \(h=0.5,P=5\). At the only interior node the coefficients are \(-1.4,0.8,0.6\). The numerical value is \(-0.75\), outside the prescribed boundary range. The exact solution is \((e^{ax/\kappa}-1)/(e^{aL/\kappa}-1)\), increasing and close to zero except near the outlet. At \(x=0.5\) it is \(1/(e^5+1)\approx0.00669\). The coarse grid does not resolve a physical layer of width 0.1.

Using the upstream difference \((u_j-u_{j-1})/h\) changes the coefficients to \(-2.4,2.8,-0.4\); the computed midpoint is \(1/7\). The value lies in range but the layer is excessively broad. A monotone method can replace an oscillation with a large smoothing error. Plot the magnitude of the profile at fixed physical locations while refining to distinguish those outcomes.

Explain the extra diffusion

Expand \(u_{j-1}=u_j-hu'_j+h^2u''_j/2+\cdots\). The upstream derivative is \(u'_j-hu''_j/2+O(h^2)\). The leading modified equation therefore contains effective diffusivity \(\kappa+ah/2\). In the example the artificial addition is 0.25, larger than the physical diffusivity 0.1. Reducing \(h\) below 0.2 restores the coefficient-sign requirement for the centred formula, while resolving the layer shape accurately generally needs several cells across \(\ell\). A convergence study should report both bounds compliance and the actual layer width or flux.

Fit the face flux to a constant-coefficient cell

For smooth constant coefficients the total flux \(J=au-\kappa u'\) is constant in a source-free cell. Solve this first-order equation between endpoints separated by \(h\). The exact relation is \(u_R=e^P u_L+(J/a)(1-e^P)\), hence \(J=a(e^P u_L-u_R)/(e^P-1)\). At large positive \(P\), the flux approaches \(a u_L\), the value carried from upstream. As \(P\) tends to zero, expansion gives a diffusive difference \(-\kappa(u_R-u_L)/h\) plus the centred advective contribution \(a(u_L+u_R)/2\). This exponential fitting uses additional local knowledge of the differential equation. It reproduces the constant-coefficient cell relation exactly, but variable material properties and sources require new local approximations.

Allocate grid points to a singularly perturbed outflow layer

For steady positive advection and small diffusivity the outflow layer shape is approximately \(e^{-(L-x)/\ell}\), with physical thickness scale \(\ell=\kappa/a\). A uniform grid that resolves that scale at a fixed number of points may waste most of its cells where the solution is almost flat. A simple layer-adapted strategy divides the domain into a coarse interval and a fine interval ending at the outflow boundary. Choose the fine-region width \(\tau\) by specifying how small the layer should already be at their interface.

If \(e^{-\tau/\ell}=N^{-2}\) for a grid with \(N\) total cells, then \(\tau=2\ell\log N\). Capping this width at \(L/2\) prevents a separate-layer construction when the physical layer is no longer thin. Place half the cells on each subinterval. This produces a piecewise-uniform grid, often called a Shishkin mesh for this boundary-layer setting. The amplitude threshold motivates its construction; it is not itself a proof of a particular numerical convergence rate. Such a proof must also control truncation, stability and matching across the grid transition.

Compare the information captured by sixty-four cells

Consider \(L=1\) m, \(a=1\) m/s and \(\kappa=0.002\) m²/s. Then \(\ell=0.002\) m. A uniform mesh of 64 cells has spacing 0.015625 m, almost eight physical layer lengths per cell. The first interior point back from the outflow has layer amplitude about \(e^{-7.8125}\approx0.000405\). Most of the transition from nearly zero to one takes place in a single cell. A method may fit an acceptable boundary value while giving a poor near-wall shape or slope.

The two-region design instead places its transition \(\tau=0.004\log64\approx0.01664\) m before the end. With 32 fine cells, their width is approximately 0.000520 m, so the fine cell Péclet number is about 0.260. At the last interior point the layer amplitude is now \(e^{-0.260}\approx0.771\). Several distinct intermediate values are available near the wall. At the coarse-to-fine transition the amplitude is \(64^{-2}\approx0.000244\), just as designed.

The coarse cells on the remaining 0.98336 m have width about 0.03073 m and cell Péclet number about 15.4. Their small expected layer amplitude does not license an unstable or nonmonotone coarse discretisation. Use a spatial scheme whose behaviour is controlled there, and check flux continuity through the changing cell sizes. If advection reverses, the thin layer moves to the opposite boundary; retaining the old mesh would concentrate cells in the wrong region. In time-dependent transport, moving layers may similarly call for adaptive tracking instead of a fixed partition.

The wall derivative supplies a more demanding check than a prescribed boundary value. For the exponential layer its exact slope at outflow has magnitude \(1/\ell=500\) per metre. A backward difference across the final fine cell gives approximately \((1-0.771)/0.000520=440\) per metre. Even this substantially improved mesh still underestimates the wall slope by about twelve percent, which matters if wall flux is the reported observable.

Expose a monotonicity failure on ten cells

For constant positive advection and diffusion solve \(u''-\mathrm{Pe}\,u'=0\) with end values zero and one. At cell Péclet number three, the centred recurrence has ratio \((1+\mathrm{Pe}_h/2)/(1-\mathrm{Pe}_h/2)=-5\); the upwind ratio is four.

Chapter 10 · Incompressibility and discrete pressure modes

Derive the velocity–pressure block system and use a periodic mesh to detect a pressure pattern that exerts no discrete force.

From first principles

Eliminate velocity to expose the pressure equation

In a mixed incompressible discretization, velocity responds to applied force and pressure while pressure enforces zero discrete divergence. Let \(A\) be a positive definite velocity block after applying boundary conditions, \(B\) the discrete divergence, and use \(B^\mathsf T\) with the corresponding weak pressure sign convention. With forcing \(f\) and prescribed divergence \(g\) the block equations are

\[Au+B^\mathsf T p=f,\]
\[Bu=g .\]

Eliminate velocity by applying the inverse action of \(A\) to the first equation and substituting into the second. The resulting pressure Schur system is

\[S p=BA^{-1}f-g,\]
\[S=BA^{-1}B^\mathsf T .\]

Since \(p^\mathsf TSp=(B^\mathsf Tp)^\mathsf T A^{-1}(B^\mathsf Tp)\), a pressure vector is invisible precisely when its discrete gradient vanishes. A constant pressure often has this property by physics; additional oscillatory pressure null modes can indicate incompatible spaces. Fixing a pressure reference removes the arbitrary constant but does not cure those extra modes. Once pressure is obtained, substitute it back to recover a velocity satisfying the original continuity equation.

Derivation and applications

Follow a pressure Fourier mode through two discrete gradients

The divergence and pressure-gradient operators must recognize every nonconstant pressure that is meant to constrain the velocity. Consider an even number of uniformly spaced periodic cells along a line, with pressure \(p_j\) at cell centres. A one-dimensional Fourier mode \(p_j=e^{ij\theta}\) is a convenient algebraic test; the constant mode is the expected arbitrary pressure reference. Suppose a colocated method uses a centred pressure gradient at the same locations:

\[(G_cp)_j=(p_{j+1}-p_{j-1})/(2h)=i[\sin\theta/h]e^{ij\theta}.\]

The pressure Schur operator in a simplified projection with identity velocity mass is \(G_c^\ast G_c\). Its eigenvalue is the squared magnitude of that gradient multiplier. It vanishes both at \(\theta=0\) and at the checkerboard \(\theta=\pi\). To compare, place normal velocity at faces and difference its adjacent cell pressures:

\[(G_sp)_{j+1/2}=(p_{j+1}-p_j)/h=\frac{2i\sin(\theta/2)}h e^{i(j+1/2)\theta}.\]

Combining the face gradient with its adjoint yields two different pressure operators on the same grid:

\[\lambda_c(\theta)=\sin^2\theta/h^2,\]
\[\lambda_s(\theta)=4\sin^2(\theta/2)/h^2 .\]

At long wavelengths both agree with \(k^2\) for physical wavenumber \(k=\theta/h\). At the alternating mode, staggered differences detect adjacent pressure differences even though a centred two-cell difference cancels them. This calculation isolates one spatial compatibility issue; other geometries and velocity boundary conditions require the full divergence-gradient pairing to be checked.

Worked example

Compare a resolved oscillation with a checkerboard

On a grid with \(h=0.100\) m, choose \(\theta=\pi/2\). The two Schur eigenvalues are \(100\) and \(200\) m⁻². At \(\theta=\pi\) they become zero and \(400\) m⁻² respectively. A unit-amplitude alternating pressure consequently produces zero centred forces, although its differences across neighbouring faces have magnitude \(20.0\) per metre.

Second application

Test the effect of setting the pressure mean to zero

An even periodic grid with pressure values \(+3,-3,+3,-3\) Pa has zero arithmetic mean. Fixing that mean does nothing to its spurious mode in the centred scheme. With face velocities the pressure gradients alternate between \(-60\) and \(+60\) Pa/m for \(h=0.100\) m. A compatible projection sees these forces and excludes this pattern from the nullspace. Removing an arbitrary constant addresses a different degeneracy.

Pressure enforces a constraint

For a steady incompressible viscous fluid, one simplified model is \(-\mu\Delta u+\nabla p=f\), \(\nabla\cdot u=0\), with prescribed wall velocity. Multiplying momentum by a velocity test function vanishing at the wall and integrating by parts gives \(\mu\int\nabla v:\nabla u-\int p\,\nabla\cdot v=\int f\cdot v\). Testing incompressibility with a pressure test function completes a coupled weak formulation. Adding a constant to pressure leaves its gradient unchanged, so use a reference pressure or impose zero mean in a closed domain.

Choose discrete velocity and pressure spaces, and define \(B\) as the negative weak divergence. After eliminating prescribed velocities, the block equations are \(Au+B^Tp=f,\ Bu=0\), with \(A\) positive definite under these boundary conditions. Eliminating velocity yields the pressure operator \(BA^{-1}B^T\). Its quadratic form is \((B^Tp)^TA^{-1}(B^Tp)\). A nonconstant pressure whose discrete gradient vanishes therefore survives as a spurious null mode, even if the velocity block is exceptionally well conditioned.

Test a checkerboard

On an even periodic one-dimensional grid use the collocated centred gradient \((p_{j+1}-p_{j-1})/(2h)\). Choose \(p_j=(-1)^j\). The two neighbouring pressures match, so the gradient is zero at every velocity point. The pressure has zero mean and amplitude one, making it independent of the constant pressure ambiguity. For values \(1,-1,1,-1\) and \(h=0.25\), the result is still the identically zero vector. A pressure solver applied to such a discretisation can retain arbitrary amounts of this alternating pattern without changing the velocity residual.

Placing velocities on the faces instead leads to a face gradient \((p_{j+1}-p_j)/h\). On the same four-cell grid its magnitudes are all 8 with alternating signs. This layout couples the previously invisible pressure variation to the velocity unknowns. The one-dimensional example diagnoses an algebraic pairing issue. In higher dimensions the geometry, boundaries and full selection of discrete spaces also affect stability.

A uniform coupling requirement

A family of stable mixed spaces requires a lower bound, independent of mesh size, on the largest discrete divergence pairing a normalised pressure can obtain with a normalised velocity test. This is the discrete inf-sup condition, with pressures measured after removing their constant ambiguity. It excludes both exact checkerboards and nearly invisible modes that become worse as the mesh is refined. A standard finite-element pairing on suitable triangulations combines continuous quadratic velocities with continuous linear pressures. Other pairings need a separate stability argument or stabilising terms.

Check discrete divergence and momentum, then inspect pressure on grid-scale patterns. A smooth velocity, or even a small residual in both block equations, provides very little information about the amplitude of a pressure component on which those equations have no sensitivity.

Give pressure a reference without changing forces

Replacing a four-cell periodic pressure vector \((2,4,6,8)^T\) by its zero-mean representative \((-3,-1,1,3)^T\) leaves every face difference unchanged. The mean removal fixes one arbitrary pressure level. Adding the checkerboard \((1,-1,1,-1)^T\) also preserves its mean, yet changes the individual face differences. This distinction makes a practical diagnostic: verify a constant shift has no physical effect, then test whether a nonconstant alternating shift produces a physically nonzero force in the chosen velocity space. Passing the former test alone cannot reveal uncoupled pressure coordinates.

Project a tentative velocity onto discrete incompressibility

After an explicit force update a tentative velocity \(v^\ast\) may have nonzero discrete divergence. Suppose \(M\) is a symmetric positive definite velocity mass matrix and the desired constraint is \(Bv=0\). Find the divergence-free velocity closest to \(v^\ast\) in the kinetic metric by minimising \((v-v^\ast)^TM(v-v^\ast)/2\). Introducing a multiplier \(p\) gives \(M(v-v^\ast)+B^Tp=0\). Applying \(B\) to the resulting corrected velocity yields \(BM^{-1}B^Tp=Bv^\ast\). Here \(p\) acts as a pressure impulse; conversion to a physical pressure depends on the time-step and scaling conventions.

The correction \(M^{-1}B^Tp\) is mass-orthogonal to every admissible velocity \(w\), since \(w^TB^Tp=(Bw)^Tp=0\). Hence the norm of the tentative state splits into squared norms of the corrected velocity and removed component. This projection never increases kinetic energy in homogeneous constraints. A null pressure mode with \(B^Tp=0\) does not affect the corrected velocity; fixing a gauge can select a unique multiplier. If the discrete gradient and divergence are assembled inconsistently, the orthogonality and corresponding energy argument may fail even if a pressure equation is solved accurately.

Follow an impulse through three velocity unknowns

Choose \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,1)\), and let \(Bv=(v_1-v_2,v_2-v_3)^T\). Constraint-compatible velocities have all three components equal. The tentative velocity is \((0,0,2)^T\), with weighted momentum two and kinetic energy two. Its divergence vector is \((0,-2)^T\). Multiplying \(BM^{-1}B^T\) gives diagonal entries 1.5 and off-diagonal entries −0.5. Solving this two-by-two system yields \(p=(-0.5,-1.5)^T\).

Recover \(B^Tp=(-0.5,-1,1.5)^T\), then \(M^{-1}B^Tp=(-0.5,-0.5,1.5)^T\). Subtracting gives \(v=(0.5,0.5,0.5)^T\), now satisfying both constraints. Its weighted momentum is still two because the constant velocity vector is admissible and therefore mass-orthogonal to the correction. The final kinetic energy is 0.5. The removed component has energy \([0.25+2(0.25)+2.25]/2=1.5\), exactly the energy difference. Both conservation of uniform momentum and removal of the incompatible component have been checked numerically.

With prescribed boundary fluxes the target constraint is generally inhomogeneous. The closest admissible velocity then lies in an affine space; the preceding zero-centred energy decrease need no longer hold because boundaries can do work. Transforming to fluctuations about a boundary-compatible lifting can restore an orthogonality statement for the correction. Before interpreting a pressure projection's apparent dissipation or energy injection, specify the boundary conditions and the metric used in its derivation.

For the same tentative state an ordinary unweighted Euclidean projection would set all velocities to their arithmetic mean \(2/3\). That also makes both discrete differences zero, but the weighted momentum becomes \(8/3\), exceeding its initial value two. A check on incompressibility alone would accept this physically different state. Choosing a mass matrix in the pressure correction is therefore consequential whenever cell masses, staggered volumes or basis-function weights differ. Evaluating both the discrete divergence and the physically weighted momentum provides information that neither result supplies on its own.

The impulse components give an additional equation-level diagnostic because applying their discrete gradient must reproduce the actual velocity correction.

Problem sheet 5 · Transport weights and pressure spaces

Locate the onset of a sign error and test which discrete pressure differences actually drive a velocity.

Resolve a layer

Let \(a=3\) m/s, \(\kappa=0.06\) m²/s on a 1 m interval. Find the minimum number of equal cells for the centred operator's nonpositive neighbour coefficients, and the number giving five cells per physical layer length. Estimate the upwind artificial diffusivity at each resolution.

Worked solution

The layer length is \(\ell=\kappa/a=0.02\) m. The sign test requires \(h\le2\ell=0.04\) m, hence at least 25 cells. Five cells across \(\ell\) require \(h\le0.004\) m or 250 cells.

The upwind addition \(ah/2\) is 0.06 m²/s at 25 cells and 0.006 at 250. The monotonicity threshold and satisfactory physical resolution describe quite different demands.

Change transport direction

On an equally spaced grid take \(a<0\). Derive the upstream discrete derivative, its leading artificial diffusivity in \(a u'-\kappa u''\), and the boundary near which a thin outflow layer forms for fixed unequal Dirichlet values.

Worked solution

With flow toward decreasing \(x\), upstream information lies to the right, giving \((u_{j+1}-u_j)/h=u'_j+hu''_j/2+O(h^2)\).

Multiplying by a negative speed gives the modified coefficient \(-(\kappa+|a|h/2)u''\). On \([0,L]\) inflow is at \(L\) and outflow at zero. The term \(e^{ax/\kappa}\) decays away from zero, locating a possible outflow boundary layer there.

Find the pressure nullspace

For an even periodic grid with six pressure nodes, solve \(p_{j+1}-p_{j-1}=0\) for all \(j\). Remove the constant ambiguity and give the dimension of the remaining nullspace. Repeat the argument for five nodes.

Worked solution

The relation is \(p_{j+2}=p_j\). Six nodes split into independent even and odd cycles, giving two arbitrary amplitudes. Removing the constant leaves one alternating mode.

With five nodes, increments of two cycle through every index, so all entries agree. Only the constant remains and zero mean removes it. This finite odd-grid observation by itself does not establish a mesh-independent mixed stability bound.

Chapter 11 · Multigrid and coarse-grid correction

Combine smoothing with a coarse residual equation and compute which components a two-grid correction can remove exactly.

From first principles

Derive the coarse-grid correction as a residual equation

Let a linear discretization satisfy \(A_h u_h=b_h\). For an iterate \(\tilde u_h\) define error \(e_h=u_h-\tilde u_h\) and residual \(r_h=b_h-A_h\tilde u_h\). Subtracting the equations gives an exact identity:

\[A_he_h=r_h .\]

A coarse mesh cannot represent arbitrary fine error. Suppose the error remaining after smoothing is approximated by \(Pe_H\), with prolongation \(P\) from coarse to fine. Choose coarse coefficients so the remaining residual is orthogonal to the coarse test directions; with restriction \(R=P^\mathsf T\) this condition becomes

\[R(r_h-A_hPe_H)=0,\]
\[A_H e_H=Rr_h,\]
\[A_H=RA_hP .\]

Update the fine iterate by \(\tilde u_h\leftarrow\tilde u_h+Pe_H\). If the coarse solve is exact, this removes any error initially in the range of \(P\). Components outside that range must be reduced by smoothing or another correction. This separation explains why simply smoothing a fine mesh for longer can be inefficient when long-wavelength error survives each relaxation sweep.

Derivation and applications

Choose a Jacobi weight for the errors a coarse grid cannot resolve

A multigrid smoother should damp oscillations left invisible or ambiguous on the coarse mesh. Consider the uniform one-dimensional discretization of \(-u''\) with stencil \((-1,2,-1)/h^2\), away from boundaries or on a periodic grid. Let the error be a Fourier mode \(e_j=e^{ij\theta}\). The Jacobi diagonal is \(D=(2/h^2)I\). Inserting the Fourier mode into the discrete operator and dividing by \(D\) gives

\[(D^{-1}Ae)_j=\tfrac12(2-e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta})e_j=(1-\cos\theta)e_j.\]

One weighted Jacobi step \(\tilde u\leftarrow\tilde u+\omega D^{-1}(b-A\tilde u)\) therefore multiplies the error by a real factor. With standard factor-two coarsening, call the interval \(\pi/2\le|\theta|\le\pi\) high frequency:

\[g_\omega(\theta)=1-\omega(1-\cos\theta),\]
\[g_\omega\in[1-2\omega,1-\omega]\quad(\omega>0).\]

The maximum absolute value on this interval lies at an endpoint. To minimize that worst factor, set the two endpoint values equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. The solution is

\[1-\omega=-(1-2\omega),\]
\[\omega=2/3,\]
\[\max_{\rm high}|g_\omega|=1/3 .\]

An ordinary undamped Jacobi sweep (\(\omega=1\)) leaves the checkerboard oscillating with unchanged magnitude. The optimal weight here depends on the operator, dimensionality and coarsening convention. It should not automatically be transferred to anisotropic grids or different stencils.

Worked example

Calculate how many smoothing sweeps are needed

A pure high-frequency error initially has amplitude \(0.900\) K. With \(\omega=2/3\), three sweeps reduce it to at most \(0.900/27=0.0333\) K. For the checkerboard the factor is \(-1/3\), so after the third sweep the error has reversed sign. Four sweeps bound the amplitude by \(0.0111\) K. A residual norm insensitive to sign still sees the decay.

Second application

Observe why smoothing alone is slow

At \(\theta=\pi/16\), the same smoother gives \(g=1-(2/3)(1-\cos(\pi/16))=0.9872\). After ten sweeps a unit component still has amplitude \(0.9872^{10}=0.879\). A high-frequency component over the same ten sweeps is at most \(3^{-10}=1.69\times10^{-5}\). Coarse-grid correction targets the slowly decaying component that these sweeps leave behind.

Two kinds of persistent error

For a uniform Dirichlet grid let \(A_h\) be the positive three-point approximation to \(-u''\). A smooth algebraic error changes slowly between adjacent nodes. Its local residual can be modest even when its amplitude is appreciable. High-frequency errors have large second differences and respond rapidly to a suitable relaxation sweep. Weighted Jacobi uses \(u\leftarrow u+\omega D^{-1}(f-A_hu)\), with diagonal \(D=2I/h^2\). On a sine mode of grid angle \(\theta\) its error multiplier is \(1-\omega(1-\cos\theta)\).

For \(\omega=2/3\), this multiplier lies between \(-1/3\) and \(1/3\) for angles between \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\). Low angles have multipliers near one, so repeated sweeps alone cost increasingly many iterations as the grid is refined. Those surviving smooth errors can often be represented on a coarser mesh. The purpose of restricting a residual is to solve an error equation for that representation.

Derive the correction

Let \(P\) interpolate coarse values to the fine grid and \(R\) restrict a fine vector. Choose the Galerkin coarse operator \(A_H=RA_hP\). Given current residual \(r=f-A_hu\), solve \(A_He_H=Rr\) and add \(Pe_H\). If the true error is precisely \(Pv\), the right-hand side equals \(A_Hv\). An exact coarse solve recovers \(v\) and removes the entire error in one correction. For general errors the new residual satisfies \(Rr_{\rm new}=0\); high-frequency components may remain on the fine grid.

Compute one correction by hand

Take \(h=1/4\) with three interior fine nodes and one interior coarse node at the midpoint. Linear interpolation is \(P=(1/2,1,1/2)^T\), and full weighting gives \(R=P^T/2\). The fine operator is 16 times the tridiagonal matrix with diagonal 2 and off-diagonal −1. Multiplication gives \(A_hP=(0,16,0)^T\) and \(A_H=8\). Suppose \(u=0\) and the exact fine solution is \(P\), so the load is \((0,16,0)^T\). The restricted residual is 8. The coarse correction returns the coefficient one and removes the error exactly.

For error \(e=(1,0,-1)^T\), the fine residual is \(32e\), whose restriction is zero. The coarse grid cannot correct this component. Its grid angle is \(\pi/2\), so one weighted Jacobi sweep leaves \(e/3\). These two vectors demonstrate why combining the two operations matters. Repeating the idea recursively gives a V-cycle. With a fixed number of smoothing steps per level, geometrically shrinking grids have total work proportional to the finest number of unknowns, provided coarse solvers and transfers also have linear cost. Good convergence rates still depend on smoothing and approximation properties for the actual operator.

Coarse linear solves do not have to be exact at every level

In the three-node example with fine error \(P\), suppose an approximate coarse solve returns 0.9 instead of one. The correction leaves \(0.1P\), so the restricted residual falls from 8 to 0.8. A recursive V-cycle effectively uses one approximate correction of this kind on intermediate levels; only the smallest system is usually solved directly. Measure the residual reduction per cycle across a family of grids. A roughly constant work per unknown is valuable only if the number of cycles required for a fixed reduction also remains controlled.

Predict a smoothing failure in anisotropic diffusion

For the operator \(-\alpha u_{xx}-\beta u_{yy}\) on an equal-spacing Cartesian grid, weighted point Jacobi has error multiplier

\[g(\theta_x,\theta_y)=1-\omega\frac{\alpha(1-\cos\theta_x)+\beta(1-\cos\theta_y)}{\alpha+\beta}.\]

This follows by substituting a Fourier error mode into the tridiagonal second differences in each direction and dividing by the diagonal \(2(\alpha+\beta)/h^2\). Algebraic smoothness depends on the coefficients, while representability on a coarse mesh depends on geometric sampling. Under strong anisotropy a mode highly oscillatory in the weak direction can produce little residual relative to the diagonal and therefore survive point relaxation. A standard coarse interpolation space may also fail to represent it.

A line smoother solves all strongly coupled unknowns along a row together. Its block diagonal retains the horizontal second differences and the diagonal part of the vertical coupling. Modes constant along that row see no horizontal stiffness after applying the block, so the scale of their correction is set by the weaker vertical coupling rather than the large point diagonal. Alternatively, semicoarsening retains fine resolution in a direction in which troublesome oscillations must remain representable. The correct choice depends on the orientation of the anisotropy relative to the actual mesh.

Evaluate an error mode ignored by the ordinary two-grid cycle

Take a periodic grid with an even number of rows, \(\alpha=100,\ \beta=1,\ \omega=2/3\), and error \(e_{ij}=(-1)^j\), constant along each row. Its discrete horizontal curvature is zero and its vertical curvature has eigenvalue \(4/h^2\). Point Jacobi divides by \(202/h^2\), giving \(g=1-4/(303)=299/303\approx0.9868\). Ten sweeps still leave about \(0.876\) of the error amplitude. Geometrically the mode is as oscillatory as the grid permits, yet pointwise smoothing barely changes it.

Full weighting in the vertical direction uses weights \(1/4,1/2,1/4\). Applied to the alternating signs it gives zero, so the restricted residual also vanishes and an ideal coarse solve receives no information about this error. This calculation identifies a concrete failure of the usual assumption that all components missed by coarsening have already been strongly damped. A high-quality coarse linear solve cannot repair a zero coarse right-hand side.

For a horizontal line Jacobi block the horizontal part contributes zero on a row-constant vector, leaving block eigenvalue \(2/h^2\). The preconditioned error-mode eigenvalue is now \((4/h^2)/(2/h^2)=2\), and a damped line sweep has multiplier \(1-(2/3)2=-1/3\). Ten such sweeps reduce the amplitude to \(1/59049\). Each line sweep costs more than a point sweep, so the practical comparison also depends on implementation and geometry. The dramatic contrast in this mode calculation explains which coupling the extra solve is buying and why iteration counts alone are insufficient for benchmarking.

For diagonal anisotropy the identity of a strongly coupled row can change as the material rotates across the grid. A line relaxation designed only for horizontal coupling may then lose its advantage in another subdomain. Compare mode or patch tests aligned with several local principal directions before generalising the success of the horizontal example.

Chapter 12 · Adjoints and errors in a chosen output

Weight equation residuals by the response of a chosen observation and distinguish algebraic error from mesh error.

From first principles

Derive an output error from an adjoint solve

Consider a nonsingular discrete linear problem \(Au=b\) with approximate solution \(\tilde u\). A user may care about one output \(J(u)=c^\mathsf Tu\) rather than every entry of \(u\). The unknown error \(e=u-\tilde u\) satisfies \(Ae=r\) with residual \(r=b-A\tilde u\). Directly computing \(e\) would require another solve for a complete field. To weight the known residual for this output, select a vector \(z\) whose transposed action reproduces the output coefficients:

\[A^\mathsf Tz=c,\]
\[c^\mathsf T e=z^\mathsf T A e .\]

Substitution yields an exact algebraic output identity and a norm bound:

\[J(u)-J(\tilde u)=z^\mathsf T r,\]
\[|J(u)-J(\tilde u)|\le\|z\|_2\|r\|_2 .\]

Large entries of \(z\) identify rows where a residual matters strongly to this particular observable, including possible sign cancellation between rows. A different output requires a different adjoint. The identity concerns error in solving the stated algebraic system. To estimate error relative to a continuum solution, discretization residuals and a suitable continuous or enriched adjoint must also enter.

Derivation and applications

Weight a distributed residual for one point-temperature measurement

A local output need not be equally sensitive to residuals everywhere on the domain. Consider \(-u''=f\) on \(0<x<L\) with homogeneous Dirichlet boundaries and constant unit diffusivity. Suppose a smooth reconstructed numerical field \(\tilde u\) satisfies both boundary values. Define the residual \(r=f+\tilde u''\) so that the error \(e=u-\tilde u\) obeys \(-e''=r\). Seek the error in one output \(J(u)=u(\xi)\) at an interior location \(\xi\).

Introduce a weight \(z\) that solves \(-z''=\delta(x-\xi)\) with zero end values. Away from \(\xi\) the weight is linear, and integrating the equation through the observation point determines its slope jump:

\[z(0)=z(L)=0,\]
\[z'(\xi^-)-z'(\xi^+)=1.\]

Continuity at \(\xi\) and the endpoint conditions determine both linear pieces. They give an explicit tent-shaped influence function

\[z(x)=\begin{cases}x(L-\xi)/L,&x\le\xi,\\ \xi(L-x)/L,&x\ge\xi.\end{cases}\]

Multiply the error equation by \(z\) and integrate by parts on the two intervals. The exterior terms vanish and the unit slope jump isolates the requested point value:

\[J(u)-J(\tilde u)=e(\xi)=\int_0^L z(x)\,r(x)\,dx .\]

A residual near the chosen output can have a larger effect than an equal residual near a prescribed boundary. Residuals of opposite signs can cancel for this observation without producing a globally accurate field. For piecewise linear finite-element fields, the distributional second derivative also contains jumps of the first derivative; omitting those terms would invalidate the identity.

Worked example

Calculate a local temperature error

Take \(L=1\) m and \(\xi=0.250\) m. Suppose \(r=8.00\) K/m² on \(0<x<0.250\) m and zero elsewhere. The relevant weight is \(0.750x\), so the point error is \(8(0.750)(0.250)^2/2=0.1875\) K. The positive residual means the exact temperature at the observation exceeds the reconstructed value.

Second application

Move the same residual nearer the far boundary

Put the same \(8.00\) K/m² on \(0.750<x<1.00\) m instead. Now the weight is \(0.250(1-x)\), yielding \(8(0.250)(0.250)^2/2=0.0625\) K. Both residuals have identical \(L^1\) and \(L^2\) magnitude; the first affects this particular sensor three times as strongly. Moving the sensor would change the ranking without changing either residual field.

A residual has a direction

Let \(Au=b\) be an invertible linear discrete model and let \(Q(u)=c^Tu\) report a scalar output, for example a weighted mean temperature. For a computed approximation \(\tilde u\), set \(r=b-A\tilde u\). The error is \(A^{-1}r\), and hence \(Q(u)-Q(\tilde u)=c^TA^{-1}r\). Define the adjoint or dual solution by \(A^Tz=c\). Then the exact output identity is

\[Q(u)-Q(\tilde u)=z^Tr.\]

A single adjoint gives the output effect of many possible residuals. A large residual where the observation has low sensitivity can contribute less than a small residual elsewhere. Opposite contributions can cancel in this particular output, even though they represent substantial state errors.

Compute the weight of each row

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(b=(1,0)^T\), and observe the second unknown with \(c=(0,1)^T\). Then \(u=(2/3,1/3)^T\) and \(z=(1/3,2/3)^T\). For \(\tilde u=(0.6,0.3)^T\), the residual is \((0.1,0)^T\). Its weighted sum is \(1/30\), exactly the missing part \(1/3-0.3\). If a solve produced residual \((0,0.1)^T\) instead, the same residual norm would double the output error. This output cares twice as much about the second row as about the first.

For residual \((0.2,-0.1)^T\), the signed estimate is exactly zero. The sum of absolute contributions is \(2/15\), an upper bound insensitive to cancellation. An analyst interested only in the selected output can accept a calculation whose state vector another observer would reject. Specify the observation before choosing stopping and refinement criteria.

Move from an algebraic error to a mesh error

An accurately solved finite-element system has almost zero algebraic residual in its own discrete test space. It can still differ from the continuum solution. Let \(a(u,v)=\ell(v)\) be the continuum weak equation and define \(R(v)=\ell(v)-a(u_h,v)\). For a linear observation the continuum dual satisfies \(a(v,z)=Q(v)\), giving \(Q(u)-Q(u_h)=R(z)\). Galerkin orthogonality means \(R(z_h)=0\) for every test function on the same mesh, hence \(R(z)=R(z-z_h)\).

Estimating this term requires a dual approximation richer than the test space used for the primal solve, or an independent reconstruction. Integrating element residuals by parts can expose both cell defects and jumps in normal flux. Weight these local contributions by the unresolved dual variation to discover which regions can affect the output strongly. Check an estimated correction against actual output changes on a refined mesh; a poor dual approximation can misidentify important regions even when the primal residuals have been evaluated exactly.

Compare a point observation with an average

In the two-unknown example, replace the second-node observation by the mean \(c=(1/2,1/2)^T\). The constant vector is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue one, so the adjoint becomes \(z=c\). A purely antisymmetric residual \((0.1,-0.1)^T\) makes the mean output error zero. For the earlier second-node output the same residual gives \(-1/30\). A mesh designed to resolve an average can therefore allocate effort differently from one designed for a single sensor. Whenever a reported error changes markedly between observables, inspect the dual weights before attributing the change to a flaw in the physical simulation.

Turn primal and dual approximation errors into an output bound

For a symmetric coercive bilinear form \(a\), let the exact state \(u\) satisfy \(a(u,v)=\ell(v)\), and the exact dual \(z\) satisfy \(a(v,z)=Q(v)\) for a linear output \(Q\). With conforming Galerkin approximations on the same trial space, \(a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\) for every discrete test. Consequently \(Q(u)-Q(u_h)=a(u-u_h,z-z_h)\). Cauchy–Schwarz in the energy inner product gives an absolute bound by \(\|u-u_h\|_a\|z-z_h\|_a\). Accuracy of either the state or the observation sensitivity can separately make the output error small.

When the primal and dual energy errors both decrease proportionally to \(h\), their product guarantees an \(O(h^2)\) output error under these assumptions. It does not guarantee second order for every possible observation. A point evaluation in a higher-dimensional continuum problem may be unbounded on the energy space or yield a dual solution with a strong singularity. Then the dual error may converge much more slowly. Likewise nonconforming approximation, inexact integration or inconsistent boundary terms introduce additional residual terms that are absent from the simple Galerkin cancellation.

A case where the product bound is exact

Let \(a(v,w)=v^TAw\) with \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,8)\), exact state \(u=(1,2)^T\), and reduced approximation space spanned by \(s=(1,1)^T\). The Galerkin coefficient is \((s^TAu)/(s^TAs)=(2+16)/10=1.8\). Choose output \(Q(v)=(6,8)v\); its exact dual is \(z=(3,1)^T\) since \(Az=(6,8)^T\). Projecting that dual onto the same space gives coefficient \((6+8)/10=1.4\). The primal and dual errors are therefore \((-0.8,0.2)^T\) and \((1.6,-0.4)^T\).

Their energy inner product is \(2(-0.8)(1.6)+8(0.2)(-0.4)=-3.2\). Direct evaluation gives \(Q(u)=22\) and \(Q(u_h)=25.2\), confirming the signed error exactly. Squared energy errors are \(2(0.8)^2+8(0.2)^2=1.6\) and \(2(1.6)^2+8(0.4)^2=6.4\). Their square-root product is 3.2, attaining the absolute bound because the two error vectors are antiparallel. The bound cannot determine the sign; the signed dual residual can.

Replace the observation by \(Q_2(v)=(2,8)v\). Its exact dual is \(s\), already representable, so \(z-z_h=0\) and the output error vanishes even though the state error is unchanged. Indeed \(Q_2(u)=18=Q_2(u_h)\). A mesh judged solely by that particular observable could look exact. A different sensor weighing the same simulation states produces a demonstrably nonzero output error, which is why adaptive refinement objectives should name their actual observations.

If the approximate dual used the same one-dimensional space and one simply evaluated the primal residual on it, the reported correction would be exactly zero for either output. The first observation shows that such a number can be badly misleading. An enriched dual must include a direction outside the primal test space; in this two-coordinate example, adding either coordinate direction gives the full space and recovers the signed error exactly. On a fine physical mesh full enrichment may be too expensive, making local higher-order reconstruction or an independently refined dual mesh attractive. The product bound then clarifies which additional error in the dual approximation needs to be controlled.

It also identifies the observation for which refining the primal mesh alone is unnecessary.

Bound the uncertainty of an approximate dual correction

A practical enriched dual \(\tilde z\) may itself have numerical error. In the symmetric positive definite algebraic setting the difference between true output error and its estimated correction is \((z-\tilde z)^Tr\), where \(r=b-Au_h\). With dual residual \(r_z=c-A\tilde z\), this equals \(r_z^TA^{-1}r\). Cauchy–Schwarz bounds its magnitude by \(\|r_z\|_{A^{-1}}\|r\|_{A^{-1}}\). This identity explains why a small ordinary residual norm can be insufficient for either the primal or dual solve: directions associated with small eigenvalues receive greater weight in the inverse-energy norm.

In the preceding example choose \(\tilde z=(2.5,1)^T\). The primal residual is \((-1.6,1.6)^T\); the approximate correction is therefore \(-4+1.6=-2.4\), moving the coarse output 25.2 to 22.8. The actual output is 22, leaving error −0.8 after correction. The dual residual is \((1,0)^T\), with squared inverse-energy norm \(1/2\). The squared primal residual norm in the same metric is 1.6. Their product of norms is \(\sqrt{0.8}\approx0.8944\), containing the observed remaining discrepancy. For larger problems, estimating these norms can itself require a separate bound or preconditioner. Reporting only the corrected value without assessing the accuracy of the dual can make a visually improved output appear more certain than the calculation warrants.

Chapter 13 · Pure flux boundaries, compatibility and the missing constant

If a steady diffusion equation prescribes only fluxes, a solver can return a singular-matrix warning for two different physical reasons. Derive the mass balance and the nullspace separately, then decide when choosing a reference value resolves the problem.

Test the continuum equation with a constant

On \(0<x<1\) take a unit conductivity, positive rightward flux \(q=-u'\), and a steady source f with \(q'=f\). Prescribe the rightward flux at both endpoints as \(q_L,q_R\). Integrating the divergence equation gives \(q_R-q_L=\int_0^1 f\,dx\). The outward unit normal is negative at the left endpoint, so the net outward flow is \(q_R-q_L\), even when both recorded sensor values are positive. In several dimensions the same requirement follows by integrating the divergence and applying the divergence theorem.

\[\int_0^1 u'v'\,dx=\int_0^1 fv\,dx+q_L v(0)-q_R v(1).\]

Testing the weak equation with \(v=1\) makes its left side identically zero. This recovers the flux balance without differentiating an approximate field. For every compatible right-hand side, adding any constant to u leaves its gradient and both prescribed fluxes unchanged. The magnitude of a potential is undetermined, while potential differences and flux can be well defined. Each disconnected region with pure-flux boundaries has its own compatibility equation and arbitrary constant.

Separate the left nullspace test from the gauge condition

Take cell-centred finite volumes with equal width h and face flux \(q_{i+1/2}=-(u_{i+1}-u_i)/h\). The balance of each cell is \(q_{i+1/2}-q_{i-1/2}=h f_i\), with prescribed values replacing the two exterior faces. Add all rows. Interior face contributions cancel in equal and opposite pairs. The resulting symmetric matrix A has a constant right null vector and its range is orthogonal to that vector. Let b be the integrated loads including boundary terms and \(w_i=h\) the integration weights. A mean value \(\bar u\) on this unit interval can be imposed by the augmented system

\[\begin{bmatrix}A&w\\ w^T&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\ \lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}b\\\bar u\end{bmatrix},\]
\[\lambda={\mathbf 1^T b\over \mathbf 1^Tw}.\]

For compatible data \(\lambda=0\). For incompatible data the first block solves for a deliberately modified source \(f_i-\lambda\). Choosing a mean makes the augmented system solvable either way; checking the multiplier identifies what it solved. Pinning a node and silently dropping one conservation equation can conceal the same mismatch as an artificial reaction at the pin.

Predict four cell values and audit an inconsistent measurement

Set \(f=2,\ q_L=0,\ q_R=2\). Exact integration gives \(q=2x,\ u=-x^2+C\). For a continuous mean of zero \(C=1/3\). With four equal cells the centre coordinates are 1/8, 3/8, 5/8 and 7/8. The centroid samples of \(-x^2\) have mean −0.328125, so the discrete mean-zero solution is \(0.3125,0.1875,-0.0625,-0.4375\). Adjacent differences give interior face fluxes 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5. The difference between the discrete centre values and the continuous mean-zero samples is the constant \(-h^2/12\); every interior face gradient is exact.

Now change only the recorded right flux to 2.4. The original source supplies 2 units per unit time and the boundaries demand 2.4. Thus \(\lambda=-0.40\) and the augmented system supplies an effective uniform source of 2.4. Alternatively, keeping the original source and the left measurement forces a right flux of 2. The plot shows both reconstructions and tells which data were modified. Adjusting only the mean translates either field without changing its slope. In a transient storage model, imbalance would change the mean in time instead of permitting a steady solution.

Set a nonzero flux mismatch, then vary the mean and the cell count independently. Observe which differences can be removed by a vertical translation. Check that the integrated imbalance remains the same under refinement.

Chapter 14 · Building a local residual estimator and refining where it matters

A finite-element solution can satisfy the assembled equations to machine precision while still missing curvature between nodes. Integrate the residual of the computed field by parts on each element and use the resulting cell and interface terms to decide where to spend the next degrees of freedom.

Ask what the discrete solution does to an unresolved test function

Consider \(-u''=f\) on a unit interval with zero Dirichlet data. Use continuous piecewise-linear finite elements with exact integration on a possibly nonuniform mesh. The weak solution satisfies \(a(u,v)=\int u'v'=\int fv\) for every test function vanishing at the endpoints. For \(e=u-u_h\), subtract the computed field to obtain \(a(e,v)=R(v)=\int fv-\int u_h'v'\). The algebraic system makes \(R(v_h)=0\) for all functions in the chosen finite-element space. Tests with more local structure expose what is missing.

\[R(v)=\sum_K\int_K (f+u_h'')v\,dx+\sum_{i\ {\rm interior}} J_i v(x_i),\]
\[J_i=u_h'(x_i^+)-u_h'(x_i^-).\]

The sign follows by adding the right boundary term of the left cell and the left boundary term of the right cell. On a linear cell \(u_h''=0\) classically. Its piecewise constant gradient jumps at interior nodes. In diffusion with varying coefficient k the corresponding jump is in \(k u_h'\), since continuity of normal flux is the conservation condition. A discontinuous material coefficient can cause an exact gradient jump without any flux imbalance.

Scale the cell and node terms before marking

Subtract the finite-element interpolant or a local quasi-interpolant from v inside the residual. Galerkin orthogonality permits this subtraction and bounds the remaining test magnitude by local length times its gradient. This leads to a computable residual indicator. One common one-dimensional choice with a symmetric edge length \(h_i\) equal here to the smaller neighbouring cell is

\[\eta_K^2=h_K^2\int_K f^2\,dx+{1\over2}\sum_{i\in\partial K\ {\rm interior}}h_i J_i^2,\]
\[\eta^2=\sum_K\eta_K^2.\]

Sharing the node contribution prevents double counting. Under the usual mesh and coefficient assumptions such estimates bound the energy error up to constants; their absolute numbers need not equal the measured error. Data oscillation can matter if the source is approximated or oscillates within a cell. To mark by bulk contribution, sort \(\eta_K^2\) in descending order and take cells until their sum exceeds a chosen fraction of the global squared estimator. Bisect marked cells, transfer the coefficient data, assemble and solve again. Reusing the old gradient jumps after refinement would skip the effect of the new trial space.

Work through the first two-element decision

Let \(f=1\) on the left half and \(f=9\) on the right. With two equal cells and zero end values there is a single interior hat unknown. Diagonal stiffness is 4 and the integrated hat load is \(1(0.25)+9(0.25)=2.5\). The middle value is therefore 0.625. The slopes are 1.25 and −1.25, giving \(J=-2.5\). Since \(h=0.5\), the two cell terms are 0.125 and 10.125. Each side receives \((1/2)(0.5)(2.5)^2=1.5625\) from the node. The right cell contributes \(11.6875/(1.6875+11.6875)=87.38\%\) of the squared indicator. A 50% bulk mark selects only that cell.

For this particular problem the finite-element solution equals the piecewise-linear interpolant of the true nodal values. This follows by testing the derivative of the interpolation error against a constant basis gradient on each element. For constant f on each element the derivative error is therefore a line of slope −f with zero mean. Its exact squared energy norm is \(\sum f_K^2 h_K^3/12\). Splitting the right half into two equal cells reduces that half's contribution by a factor of four. Total energy error falls from 0.924 to 0.470 with one extra degree of freedom. Splitting only the left cell leaves about 0.920. Run the loop and compare the indicator's ranking with this independent verification quantity.

Chapter 15 · What a periodic grid does to a decaying Fourier mode

A periodic diffusion solver predicts the shape of a sampled wave while its amplitude decays too slowly. Fourier analysis separates spatial differentiation error, an unresolved physical mode and errors caused by the time integrator.

Diagonalise the differential operator on the periodic interval

Use a dimensionless coordinate \(0\le x<2\pi\) and solve \(u_t=\kappa u_{xx}\) with periodic values and periodic derivatives. The complex exponentials \(e^{imx}\), with integer m, satisfy both conditions and are eigenfunctions of the second derivative. Put \(u(x,t)=A_m(t)e^{imx}\) and cancel the common exponential. The scalar amplitude equation is \(A'_m=-\kappa m^2 A_m\). Thus every cosine or sine mode decays by \(\exp(-\kappa m^2t)\), with no phase shift, while the m=0 mean is conserved.

On a uniform grid \(x_j=2\pi j/N\), the samples of \(m\) and \(m+\ell N\) agree for every integer \(\ell\). A discrete Fourier transform returns coefficients in one representative band. For the real cosine test with even N we can fold a positive physical mode into \(k=\min(r,N-r)\), \(r=m\bmod N\). A Fourier spectral Laplacian multiplies that coefficient by \(-k^2\). For a resolved mode it differentiates the trigonometric interpolant exactly, up to arithmetic. For unresolved data it differentiates the aliased representative; no operation using only those samples can identify the original m.

Apply the centred difference stencil to a single wave

The standard second difference is \((u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1})/h^2\), \(h=2\pi/N\). Put the grid wave \(u_j=e^{ikjh}\) into the stencil. Factor out the common phase and simplify with \(e^{i\xi}+e^{-i\xi}=2\cos\xi\). This gives the symbol and its small-h expansion:

\[{e^{ikh}-2+e^{-ikh}\over h^2}=-{4\over h^2}\sin^2(kh/2)=-k^2+{k^4h^2\over12}+O(k^6h^4).\]

The effective squared wavenumber is smaller than \(k^2\) for every nonconstant mode in the resolved band, so this spatial approximation decays too slowly if the time equation is integrated exactly. The error is modest when \(kh\ll1\), but at the Nyquist mode \(kh=\pi\) the ratio is \(4/\pi^2=0.4053\). A stable time integrator cannot repair this spatial attenuation error without additional modeling. The experiment exponentiates both discretizations' symbols exactly, isolating space and sampling here.

Measure separate errors with the same sampled initial data

Choose \(\kappa=0.5,\ m=3,\ N=16\). The true squared wavenumber is 9 and the difference stencil gives 8.006. Starting at unit amplitude, at \(t=0.20\) exact and spectral amplitudes are 0.40657; finite differences give 0.44906. Doubling N to 32 changes the difference result to 0.41716. The absolute amplitude error falls from 0.04249 to 0.01059, a ratio of 4.01 consistent with the second-order expansion. This is a spatial-convergence study because time evolution introduces no truncation error.

Now take \(m=12,\ N=16\). Every sampled cosine equals the low mode \(k=4\) at \(t=0\). Chapter 16 · Why two finite-element wave solvers propagate the same mode at different speeds

Build the element mass matrix by integrating shape functions and track the effect of diagonal lumping on the wave spectrum.

From a continuum wave to nodal masses

For \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\), multiply by a test function and integrate by parts. On a periodic line the boundary contributions cancel. A piecewise-linear element of width \(h\) uses shape functions \(1-\xi,\xi\), with \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Integrating products of shape functions gives \(M_e=(h/6)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Integrating derivative products gives \(K_e=(c^2/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Row-sum lumping replaces each mass row by its sum on the corresponding diagonal, \(h/2\). This preserves the mass associated with a uniform velocity but changes the response to a velocity that varies within an element.

Diagonalise the assembled periodic problem

A periodic nodal mode has \(u_j=e^{ij\theta}\). Each neighbour contributes \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) relative to node \(j\). Thus \(K\) multiplies by \(2c^2(1-\cos\theta)/h\), consistent \(M\) by \(h(2+\cos\theta)/3\), and lumped \(M\) by \(h\). Dividing stiffness by mass gives the discrete squared frequency. Relative to the exact frequency \(c\theta/h\),

\[\frac{\omega_L}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta},\]
\[\frac{\omega_C}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2+\cos\theta}}.\]

Both tend to one for long waves; the leading quadratic errors have opposite signs. This is a spatial effect, present even with exact time integration.

Two wavelengths in one mesh

At four cells per wavelength \(\theta=\pi/2\). The lumped ratio is \(2\sqrt2/\pi\approx0.9003\); the consistent ratio is about 1.1027. One wave solver lags by roughly ten per cent and the other leads by roughly ten per cent. At eight cells per wavelength the ratios move much closer to one, but repeated propagation over many wavelengths can still accumulate visible phase error.

At the shortest grid mode \(\theta=\pi\), the consistent frequency is \(\sqrt{12}c/h\) and the lumped frequency \(2c/h\). A central-difference time integrator stable for \(h_t\omega\le2\) therefore requires a smaller step for the consistent mass. Accuracy at one wavelength and stability at the grid scale are different design criteria.

Chapter 17 · Moving a cell average without inventing or losing material

Derive a conservative transport step from cell overlap and measure how a monotone scheme spreads a pulse despite conserving its total mass exactly.

Transport is a translation

For constant \(a>0\), the solution to \(u_t+au_x=0\) is \(u(x,t)=u_0(x-at)\). Consider periodic cells of width \(h\). Over one step \(\Delta t\), a cell translated back through distance \(a\Delta t\) overlaps part of its original cell and part of its left neighbour. If the initial reconstruction is constant in each cell and \(C=a\Delta t/h\) lies between zero and one, the new average is

\[u_j^{n+1}=(1-C)u_j^n+C u_{j-1}^n.\]

Summing all cells telescopes on a periodic grid, preserving total material \(h\sum_j u_j\). Each new value is a convex combination, so the maximum cannot increase and the minimum cannot fall. For \(C>1\) this particular two-cell overlap ceases to describe the translated interval.

Derive the spreading instead of reading it off a plot

Start from one unit of material in one cell and follow its distribution on an effectively unbounded grid. After one step its index increment behaves like a Bernoulli choice: zero with weight \(1-C\), one with weight \(C\). Repeated convolution gives a binomial profile. Its mean index after \(n\) steps is \(nC\) and variance \(nC(1-C)\). Multiplying by \(h^2\) and using \(n=t/\Delta t\) gives a physical variance \(a h(1-C)t\). Equating this with \(2D_\mathrm{num}t\) gives \(D_\mathrm{num}=ah(1-C)/2\). This growth describes numerical spreading of a translated shape even though the scheme conserves mass.

Two ways to reach the same physical time

A pulse should move 12 cells. Taking 12 steps with \(C=1\) shifts data by exactly 12 indices. Taking 24 steps with \(C=0.5\) has the same mean displacement but an added variance of six cell widths squared. In a source-free transport test, peak height and width reveal an error hidden from the total-mass budget.

Applied study

Discretise a boundary-value problem and separate mesh error from solver and sampling errors.

Replace a continuum balance by nodal equations

Suppose a scalar diffuses along a unit interval, with a fixed value at each end. The steady balance is \(-u''=f\) for constant unit diffusivity. At equally spaced interior nodes \(x_i=ih\), approximate the derivative at each face by the difference between its neighbouring values divided by \(h\). Subtracting the outgoing and incoming diffusive fluxes and dividing by cell width gives

\[\frac{2u_i-u_{i-1}-u_{i+1}}{h^2}=f(x_i).\]

The endpoints are known, leaving \(N-1\) interior unknowns for \(N\) intervals. Each interior flux appears once with each sign in neighbouring balances. Summing balances therefore leaves the net boundary flux and the total source. With variable diffusivity \(D\), multiply each face gradient by its face value of \(D\) before taking the difference.

Find the error made by a single stencil

Insert the exact values \(u(x_i-h)\) and \(u(x_i+h)\), expand both in Taylor series and add them. Odd terms cancel. The central second difference satisfies \(\delta_{xx}u=u''+h^2u^{(4)}/12+O(h^4)\) for sufficiently smooth \(u\). This local consistency calculation predicts the scale of the defect when the exact solution is inserted in the discrete equations.

Write the discrete problem \(A_hu_h=f_h\) and define \(\tau_h=A_hu_\star-f_h\) using exact nodal values \(u_\star\). Then \(A_h(u_\star-u_h)=\tau_h\). A stability bound on \(A_h^{-1}\), uniform in mesh size in the chosen norm, turns second-order consistency into second-order solution error. A tight iterative solve reduces the residual of \(A_hu_h=f_h\), but leaves \(\tau_h\) in place.

Use an exact sine mode to predict the discrete amplitude

Prescribe \(u(0)=u(1)=0\) and \(f(x)=\pi^2\sin(\pi x)\). The exact solution is \(u_\star=\sin(\pi x)\). For its grid samples \(s_i=\sin(\pi ih)\), the sine addition formula yields \(A_hs=\lambda_hs\), with \(\lambda_h=4\sin^2(\pi h/2)/h^2\). The right-hand side is \(\pi^2s\), so the discrete solution has the same nodal shape and amplitude \(\pi^2/\lambda_h\).

With four intervals, \(\lambda_h=64\sin^2(\pi/8)=9.3726\) and the numerical midspan value is 1.0530. The exact value is 1. The sign makes sense: the discrete stiffness of this mode is below \(\pi^2\), so the same load produces a larger amplitude. Expansion gives \(\pi^2/\lambda_h=1+\pi^2h^2/12+O(h^4)\), predicting a fourfold error reduction on refinement.
