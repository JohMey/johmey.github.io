Inferring parameters and fields from observations · 1. Eliminate linear nuisance parameters before fitting a decay

A measured relaxation has an unknown rate, amplitude and background. Derive a one-dimensional profile fit without fixing the nuisance parameters, explain why short observation windows can hide the rate and test an actual numerical dataset.

Separate the unknowns by how they enter the observations

Suppose observations at known times \(t_i\) have mean \(y_i=b+a e^{-k t_i}\) with \(k>0\). Both \(a\) and \(b\) enter linearly once \(k\) is specified. For independent equal-variance measurement noise, minimising squared residuals is a natural fit. More general known covariance can be incorporated by whitening the data and model columns together. Fitting all three unknowns by nonlinear iteration is possible, but every candidate rate should be judged against the best available amplitude and background at that rate. Otherwise an apparent improvement or failure of the rate may merely reflect a poor nuisance estimate.

Let \(s_i=e^{-kt_i}\), bars denote sample means, and regress \(y_i\) on \(1,s_i\). Differentiating the sum of squared residuals with respect to \(b\) gives \(b=\bar y-a\bar s\). Substitution and differentiation with respect to \(a\) yield

\[a(k)=\frac{\sum_i(s_i-\bar s)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(s_i-\bar s)^2},\]
\[b(k)=\bar y-a(k)\bar s .\]

The denominator vanishes when all times coincide or \(k=0\), at which point the two columns are identical. A QR or SVD of the whitened design reveals this rank loss. The simple centred formulas are transparent for analysis; for large poorly scaled datasets a stable factorisation remains preferable to unmonitored normal equations. Define profile objective \(\Phi(k)=\tfrac12\sum_i r_i^2\), \(r_i=y_i-b(k)-a(k)s_i\). A one-dimensional search can now explore its shape, bounds or competing minima.

Differentiate the profiled objective without differentiating the whole fit

Write \(X=[\mathbf1,s]\), \(c=(b,a)^T\) and \(r=y-Xc\). At the least-squares solution \(X^Tr=0\). Taking the total derivative of \(\Phi=r^Tr/2\) gives \(-r^T(X'c+Xc')\). The term with \(c'\) vanishes by orthogonality, leaving \(\Phi'=-r^TX'c=a\sum_i r_i t_i e^{-kt_i}\). This simplification applies to the scalar objective gradient, not necessarily to the full residual Jacobian or second derivative. The projected component \((I-P_X)X'c\) is the local model change with rate that cannot be absorbed by a linear-nuisance adjustment. Its whitened squared norm characterises rate information near a well-fitting model.

At short times, \(b+a e^{-kt}\simeq(a+b)-ak t+ak^2t^2/2+\cdots\). Data resolving only a constant and a slope constrain \(a+b\) and \(ak\), not \(a,b,k\) individually. Making the inner least-squares solve arbitrarily accurate cannot create curvature information absent from the acquisition. Include times approaching the decay time and a late observation near the baseline if feasible. Alternatively supply an independently characterised background with uncertainty rather than fixing it silently. A flat profile exposes this identifiability issue more clearly than a single successful three-parameter optimiser status.

Recover a rate exactly before introducing noise

At \(t=0,1,2,3\) s consider the noiseless data \(5,3,2,1.5\) in sensor units. Successive differences are \(-2,-1,-0.5\), a geometric sequence with ratio \(q=e^{-k}=1/2\). The rate is \(k=\ln2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), and substituting the first two points gives \(a=4,b=1\). At the correct rate the profile residual vanishes. An independent numerical implementation should reproduce both the rate and these coefficients and show \(X^Tr\) near zero for every rate, including incorrect candidate rates. These checks distinguish errors in profiling from failures of the outer search.

The difference ratio gives a poor general estimator when the later differences approach the measurement noise. Its numerator and denominator share observed samples, so their errors are correlated. Profiling the original observations uses all data without creating these ratio singularities and permits weighting by known variances. A late-time outlier can nonetheless bias a nonlinear fit with only a handful of samples. Examine the four individual residuals and compare predictions at a held-out time before interpreting a narrow numerical minimum as an identified physical mechanism.

Inspect the rate profile after refitting amplitude and background

The calculation uses the four times above and adds a controlled perturbation to one late observation. It solves the two linear nuisance parameters afresh at each trial rate; a second curve evaluates the same rate with amplitude and background fixed to their original noiseless values. Comparing them makes visible how allowing nuisance parameters to adapt changes both the best rate and the apparent uncertainty. At zero perturbation both curves share \(k=\ln2\), but only the profiled curve describes the stipulated joint fit.

What precision would the four measurements support?

Let the four observations have independent, equal standard deviation \(\sigma\). A small change in rate has derivative vector \(v_i=-a t_i e^{-kt_i}\). Refitting amplitude and background can mimic any change in the span of \(s_i=e^{-kt_i}\) and the constant vector. Only the orthogonal component \(v_\perp=(I-P_X)v\) distinguishes the rate. At the baseline \(a=4,\ k=\log 2\), the unprojected derivative is \((0,-2,-2,-1.5)\). Regressing it on \(s\) and a constant gives slope \(1.98261\) and intercept \(-2.30435\). Subtract that fit and add the squared remainders to obtain \(\|v_\perp\|^2=0.92174\).

The local least-squares change in rate due to a noise vector is \(v_\perp^T\epsilon/\|v_\perp\|^2\). Its standard deviation is therefore \(\sigma/\|v_\perp\|\). For \(\sigma=0.10\) this is approximately \(0.104\ {\rm s}^{-1}\). Tiny tolerances in the scalar optimiser cannot supply additional information. The estimate assumes small perturbations and an adequate decay law. A visibly asymmetric profile requires inspecting the actual objective on both sides before attaching symmetric error bars.

Inferring parameters and fields from observations · 2. Choose a regularisation scale by predicting omitted data

Regularised least squares can fit each observation partly with itself. Derive leave-one-out residuals from a single factorisation, approximate them by a trace and identify when a generalised cross-validation score misses a highly influential sensor.

Express a regularised fit as a map on measured data

Let whitened observations \(y\in\mathbb R^n\) be modelled by \(Ax\), and for \(\lambda>0\) minimise \(\|Ax-y\|^2+\lambda\|x\|^2\). The minimiser satisfies \((A^TA+\lambda I)x=A^Ty\). Predicted data are \(\hat y=H_\lambda y\), where \(H_\lambda=A(A^TA+\lambda I)^{-1}A^T\). This “hat” matrix maps the observations to their own fitted values. If \(A=U\Sigma V^T\), its nonzero eigenvalues are \(h_j=\sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)\). Their sum \(d(\lambda)=\operatorname{tr}H_\lambda\) is an effective degrees-of-freedom count. A weakly determined direction with small singular value can contribute a fraction of an independently fitted parameter.

The norm of the training residual decreases or stays constant as the penalty weakens. For a square full-rank model it tends to zero as the penalty tends to zero, even with noisy data. Individual residuals can change in either direction. It does not measure out-of-sample error. Instead ask how well the fitted model predicts each observation when it was left out. Repeating the full solve \(n\) times conceptually works but wastes structure. An exact deletion identity gives each left-out residual \(r_i^{(-i)}=(y_i-\hat y_i)/(1-H_{ii})\), where \(H_{ii}\) is the observation's leverage. To derive the formula, remove row \(a_i^T\), subtracting \(a_i a_i^T\) from the penalised normal matrix. Multiplying its inverse update by the original residual along \(a_i\) gives the factor \(1/(1-a_i^TG^{-1}a_i)\), with \(G=A^TA+\lambda I\). This derivation assumes the same penalty strength in the objective for the held-out and full fits.

Replace individual influences by their average only once

The leave-one-out score is the mean of \([r_i/(1-H_{ii})]^2\). Generalised cross-validation replaces the individual diagonal elements by the common mean \(d/n\), giving

\[{\rm GCV}(\lambda)=\frac{\|y-\hat y\|^2/n}{(1-d(\lambda)/n)^2}.\]

When every leverage is equal, this approximation reproduces leave-one-out exactly. Its expression is invariant to orthogonal transformations of the whitened data space because both trace and residual norm are invariant. Exact leave-one-observation-out instead depends on which physical observations are removed, which matters for a particular instrument arrangement. GCV is useful when a rotationally averaged prediction criterion is appropriate; it is not a universal interpretation of removing an actual sensor or predicting an unmeasured parameter direction.

Consider \(A=(1,1,1)^T\) fitting a scalar common signal, \(y=(1.1,0.9,1.0)\), and \(\lambda=1\). The fit is \(\hat x=3/4=0.75\), so residuals are \(0.35,0.15,0.25\). All leverages are \(1/4\). Exact prediction errors on deleting each datum are residuals divided by 0.75. For example removing 1.1 leaves \((0.9+1.0)/(2+1)=0.6333\), giving 0.4667 error, equal to \(0.35/0.75\). GCV equals exact leave-one-out here. Clear agreement in this symmetric scalar case should be a first numerical verification before interpreting a higher-dimensional score whose influences vary.

See how one sensitive observation can dominate a fit

For another scalar design \(A=(1,1,4)^T\) with \(\lambda=2\), \(G=20\) and \(H_{ii}=(0.05,0.05,0.80)\). A residual on the third sensor is multiplied by five in a true deletion while the first two are amplified only slightly. GCV uses the same factor \(1/(1-0.9/3)=1.429\) for all. If the model discrepancy is concentrated in that high-leverage reading, trace averaging can underestimate the consequence of losing it. Conversely if errors fall only on low-leverage sensors it can overstate their delete-one loss. Inspecting the leverage distribution is inexpensive compared with attributing precision to a single scalar tuning criterion.

Whitening rotates and rescales observations when errors are correlated. Leaving out one row after a dense whitening transformation is generally not the same experiment as removing one physical sensor before whitening. A time-correlated series may demand holding out an entire time block so training data cannot predict a held-out noise realisation through short-range correlation. Predictive selection should specify whether the intended prediction concerns a new repeat at known conditions, a missing detector or a new range of the physical parameters. A penalty that works well for a data prediction need not minimise reconstruction error in a weakly observable component of \(x\).

When does using the average leverage change the answer?

Four sensors have sensitivity one. Their readings are 1.10, 0.90, 1.05 and 0.95. The fifth reading equals its sensitivity plus an adjustable offset. First give all five equal sensitivity. The two scores coincide even with a nonzero offset. Now set the fifth sensitivity to 5 and its offset to 0.8. At λ = 1, compare the fifth leverage with the mean leverage. Predict the fifth reading from the other four, then use this single omitted-sensor error to check which score can reach 0.66.

Inferring parameters and fields from observations · 3. Recovering sparse coefficients without hiding amplitude bias

A sensor records a linear mixture of a few active components. Derive the soft-threshold map, use it in an iterative reconstruction and decide when correlated response columns make an apparently sharp sparse answer ambiguous.

Derive a nonsmooth optimum before choosing a solver

Let a whitened linear model be \(y=Ax+\eta\) and consider minimising \(F(x)=\|Ax-y\|^2/2+\lambda\sum_j|x_j|\). The parameter \(\lambda\ge0\) balances fit with sparsity in this chosen coordinate system. Rescaling one column of \(A\) while leaving its penalty unchanged changes how expensive the corresponding physical component is; normalise columns or use documented coefficient-specific weights before comparing support selection across different quantities. Sparsity of a signal in one basis is a modelling assumption, not evidence generated by the penalty itself.

For an orthonormal design \(A^TA=I\), expanding the square shows that minimisation separates into scalar terms \((x_j-z_j)^2/2+\lambda|x_j|\), with \(z=A^Ty\). For a positive solution the derivative is \(x-z+\lambda=0\); for a negative one it is \(x-z-\lambda=0\). At zero the absolute-value subgradient is the interval \([-1,1]\), so zero is optimal when \(|z|\le\lambda\). These cases give \(S_\lambda(z)=\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0)\). Unlike simply removing small coefficients, soft thresholding also shrinks every retained amplitude by \(\lambda\). This predictable bias can matter even if the selected support is physically correct.

For \(z=(2.4,-0.7,0.18)\) and \(\lambda=0.5\), the solution is \((1.9,-0.2,0)\). The residual correlations \(A^T(y-Ax)\) must equal \(\lambda\) times the sign on active coordinates and stay in \([-\lambda,\lambda]\) on inactive coordinates. Here they are \((0.5,-0.5,0.18)\). These conditions independently certify optimality for the convex objective, without proving the true sample actually contains only two active mechanisms.

Majorise the smooth loss and solve its local model exactly

For a general \(A\), write \(f(x)=\|Ax-y\|^2/2\) and \(\nabla f=A^T(Ax-y)\). Taylor expansion is exact with second-order term \(\|A(z-x)\|^2/2\le L\|z-x\|^2/2\), \(L=\|A\|_2^2\). Replace the true quadratic term by this upper bound, keep the penalty exact and minimise over \(z\). Completing the square produces

\[x^{k+1}=S_{\lambda/L}\left(x^k-\frac1L A^T(Ax^k-y)\right)\]

componentwise. This proximal-gradient or ISTA step decreases the objective for this step bound. An under-estimated \(L\) can break the majorisation. A backtracking check evaluates the true smooth loss at the candidate instead of requiring an exact power-method estimate of the largest singular value. Acceleration can reduce the number of iterations, while a numerical termination test based on the subgradient or objective gap remains necessary.

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right), y=(1,0)^T,\lambda=0.2\). Its spectral \(L=(3+\sqrt5)/2=2.618\). Starting at zero, \(A^Ty=(1,1)\), so the first iterate is \((0.8/L,0.8/L)\simeq(0.3056,0.3056)\). Both components initially enter equally even though the first column alone fits the pattern exactly. The second measurement penalises a nonzero second coefficient. At the optimum \((0.8,0)\), residual correlations are \((0.2,0.2)\), so the inactive second coefficient sits exactly on its subgradient boundary. A small data perturbation can change its selected status, despite a well-defined convex optimum for this full-rank matrix. Stability of objective value and stability of support are different questions.

Interpret support and debias only after validating it

If two columns of \(A\) are identical, two nonnegative amplitudes with the same sum give the same predictions and the same \(\ell^1\) penalty. A solver may split an identifiable total between the columns or report one arbitrary active component. Lowering the residual tolerance will not resolve this ambiguity. Acquire a measurement where the columns respond differently, merge them if only the total is scientifically meaningful or report a range of admissible allocations. Near-collinear columns give an approximate version of this effect that can be revealed by perturbing the data within their measured noise or holding out a sensor condition.

A common second step is to refit by ordinary or weighted least squares on the selected support to remove shrinkage bias. For the orthonormal example it would restore the retained amplitudes to \(2.4,-0.7\). This is a conditional estimate after choosing that support; it does not undo selection effects and can amplify noise strongly for a nearly singular active submatrix. Test the selected support and refitted prediction on held-out observations. An uncertainty interval that assumes the support known in advance can be too optimistic when small perturbations switch the active set.

Can support and amplitudes both be correct?

The six orthogonal channels have three true nonzero coefficients. Set the perturbation multiplier to one and start with zero threshold. Increase the threshold just far enough to remove the three noise-only channels. Does the weak third signal survive? Now set the perturbation to zero while keeping that threshold fixed. Compare the third estimate with its true value to measure the shrinkage introduced by the penalty itself.

Prove nonidentifiability with two equal columns

Take \(A=(1,1)\), \(y=3\) and \(\lambda=1\). Characterise an entire family of optimal nonnegative solutions and the unique total that data and penalty identify.

Work through the solution

On nonnegative inputs the objective depends only on \(s=x_1+x_2\) as \((s-3)^2/2+s\). Its minimum is at \(s=2\). The triangle inequality shows no signed decomposition of that same sum can lower its penalty. Every pair \((q,2-q)\) for \(0\le q\le2\) is therefore globally optimal. An algorithm returning \((2,0)\) has not established that the first physical species is present and the second absent. Only their aggregate is determined without a distinguishing observation or another justified prior.

Inferring parameters and fields from observations · 4. What each new sensor reading changes in a Gaussian inverse problem

Start with an uncertain vector, condition on one noisy linear observation, and reconstruct the posterior mean and covariance without repeating a batch inversion. Check shared calibration errors before treating repeated measurements as independent evidence.

Condition a joint Gaussian by eliminating a correlated component

Let \(x\) have a Gaussian prior with mean \(m\) and positive definite covariance \(P\). A scalar instrument returns \(y=h^Tx+\epsilon\), with zero-mean Gaussian noise independent of \(x\) of variance \(r>0\). The predicted observation mean is \(h^Tm\); the innovation \(d=y-h^Tm\) has variance \(S=h^TPh+r\), and cross covariance of \(x\) with this innovation is \(Ph\). Seek a corrected error \((x-m)-Kd\) uncorrelated with \(d\). Its covariance with \(d\) vanishes when \(K=Ph/S\). Because the joint distribution is Gaussian, uncorrelated then implies independent, so conditioning on the observed \(d\) gives

\[m^+=m+K d,\]
\[P^+=P-\frac{Phh^TP}{S}.\]

The covariance correction is rank one and positive semidefinite when subtracted. It depends on geometry and declared noise, not the particular measurement value. The new mean can move strongly when a large innovation occurs even if the predicted covariance reduction was modest. A very unlikely innovation is reason to check the physical model and reported uncertainty before proceeding as though the calibration were known. Recomputing an ad hoc smaller measurement variance after seeing the data would change the probabilistic assumptions.

An equivalent route minimises \((x-m)^TP^{-1}(x-m)+(y-h^Tx)^2/r\), yielding posterior precision \(P^{-1}+hh^T/r\). Multiplying by the proposed covariance above verifies its inverse directly, a one-row Woodbury identity. For several independent readings, applying rank-one updates sequentially produces the same posterior as one batch solve in exact arithmetic. If the instrument errors are correlated, either whiten the full observation model or explicitly include shared error components in the state before applying independent updates. The matrix identity is exact only for the covariance actually specified in the statistical model.

A correlated prior transfers information to an unmeasured coordinate

Set \(m=(0,0)^T\), \(P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Measure only the first coordinate with \(h=(1,0)^T,\ r=1,\ y=3\). The innovation variance is 5 and the gain is \((0.8,0.2)^T\). Hence \(m^+=(2.4,0.6)\) and \(P^+=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.2&0.8\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). The second coordinate moved even though the instrument does not directly sense it. That inference uses prior correlation. If prior off-diagonal covariance were zero, its posterior mean and variance would remain unchanged. A conclusion about an unobserved material parameter should therefore report whether sensitivity comes from data or from that imposed correlation structure.

The innovation itself is \(3/\sqrt5=1.34\) predicted standard deviations, plausible under the original model. Conditioned residual at the same reading becomes \(3-2.4=0.6\), much smaller because that observation helped fit the estimate. A model check should use the pre-update innovation or an independent held-out observation. In a sequence of independent conditionally generated data, correctly normalised innovations provide a useful diagnostic for error scale and neglected correlation. An extremely small training residual after every assimilation can simply reflect a model that overweights its own observations.

Maintain a valid covariance over many updates

In exact arithmetic \(P^+=P-Kh^TP\). In finite precision subtracting two nearly equal matrices can erode symmetry or produce a small negative eigenvalue in a direction known very precisely. The equivalent Joseph form \(P^+=(I-Kh^T)P(I-Kh^T)^T+KrK^T\) expresses the result as a sum of positive semidefinite pieces. It also derives directly from the prior state error and independent measurement noise entering the update. A square-root covariance or information factor can improve numerical robustness over repeated strongly informative observations. Simply setting negative diagonal entries to zero after a bad update may still leave an indefinite correlated matrix.

If the state evolves between observations with \(x_{\rm next}=Fx+w\), independent process noise covariance \(Q\) gives predicted mean \(Fm^+\) and covariance \(FP^+F^T+Q\). Choosing \(Q=0\) asserts exact dynamics; it is not a harmless way to remove an implementation parameter. Repeated observations can then drive covariance toward zero in a falsely rigid physical model. Conversely arbitrarily increasing process noise to make any data appear plausible sacrifices the predictive test. Estimate or validate process variability independently when deciding whether a sequential inverse problem represents one stationary parameter or an evolving hidden state.

Quantify a shared calibration floor

A scalar prior has variance 4. Two or more readings each have independent noise variance 1 and share the same unknown additive calibration bias of variance 0.25. Find posterior variance of the scalar from \(n\) readings and its limit.

Work through the solution

The sample mean measures the scalar plus shared bias with variance \(0.25+1/n\); independent deviations of readings from their mean contain no information about the scalar in this Gaussian model. Inferring parameters and fields from observations · 5. What can unfinished endurance tests reveal about lifetime?

When a test stops, a surviving bearing has supplied information. Derive the likelihood from first principles and examine how changing the observation deadline moves the rate inferred from a small batch.

Count failures and add the time spent on test

Assume identical bearings fail independently at a constant rate \(\lambda\) per hour. Over a short interval \(dt\), the conditional probability of failure for an operating bearing is \(\lambda\,dt+o(dt)\). This gives the differential equation \(S'(t)=-\lambda S(t)\) with \(S(0)=1\) for the fraction surviving beyond \(t\). Its solution is \(S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\) and the failure-time density is \(f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\).

Choose a fixed administrative deadline \(c\) in advance. Each observed duration is the earlier of the failure time and \(c\). Write \(\delta_i=1\) for a recorded failure, zero for an unfinished run. A failure contributes its density. An unfinished run contributes the probability of lasting at least its recorded duration. Multiplying these quantities over the bearings gives

\[L(\lambda)=\prod_i f(t_i)^{\delta_i}S(t_i)^{1-\delta_i} =\lambda^d e^{-\lambda T},\]
\[d=\sum_i\delta_i,\]
\[T=\sum_i t_i.\]

For positive \(d\), differentiating \(d\log\lambda-\lambda T\) gives a score \(d/\lambda-T\). It vanishes at \(\hat\lambda=d/T\). The curvature \(-d/\lambda^2\) is negative. With zero failures the likelihood falls immediately to the right of zero, making zero the boundary maximum. A survivor contributes no failure to \(d\) and still adds positive exposure to \(T\). Removal triggered by a bearing's imminent failure would require a model for that mechanism. A predeclared deadline avoids this selection issue.

Audit a batch stopped after seven hours

Suppose the simulated complete lifetimes are 2, 4, 6, 9 and 14 hours. At seven hours the actual records are three failures and two unfinished tests. Their total exposure is \(2+4+6+7+7=26\) bearing-hours. The rate estimate is \(3/26=0.11538\) per hour and the fitted mean lifetime is \(26/3=8.667\) hours. Discarding the two survivors leaves 12 bearing-hours and inflates the apparent rate to \(3/12=0.25\) per hour.

For a candidate rate of 0.20 per hour the log likelihood relative to the full-data maximum is \(3\log(0.20/(3/26))-26(0.20-3/26)\approx-0.550\). This is a relative likelihood near 0.58. The absolute density changes when time units change, while this likelihood ratio is invariant. In the notebook change the deadline to five hours. Each failure that now occurs after the deadline becomes an unfinished observation. Inspect the time lines before interpreting the new estimate.

A generally increasing or decreasing trend with a moving deadline is therefore not guaranteed.

Exercise · infer an upper rate after zero failures

Five fresh bearings all survive ten hours. Find the rate for which observing no failures would have probability 0.05 under the model.

Work through the solution

All five must survive ten hours. Independence gives probability \(e^{-50\lambda}\). Setting this equal to 0.05 gives \(\lambda=-\log(0.05)/50\approx0.05991\) per hour. Inverting this zero-failure event gives a one-sided 95% upper confidence bound for the rate in this experiment. The likelihood maximum itself remains at zero.
