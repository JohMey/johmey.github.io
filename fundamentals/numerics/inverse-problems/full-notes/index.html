Chapter 1 · Forward maps, information and identifiability

Decide what a measurement can tell you before attempting to fit it. Separate nonuniqueness, noise sensitivity and model error.

From first principles

Decide what a measurement uniquely determines

A forward model predicts readings from a state. In a linear discretization write \(y=Ax\); row \(i\) lists the sensitivity of the \(i\)th reading to each state component. If two states give the same data, subtract their equations. Their difference must be invisible to every measurement,

\[Ax_1=Ax_2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A(x_1-x_2)=0 .\]

Thus, if \(x_0\) is one solution, all exact solutions are \(x_0+h\) for \(h\) in the null space of \(A\). A reported quantity \(q=c^Tx\) can still be unique even if the full state is ambiguous: its value must be unchanged by every invisible displacement. A constructive test is to express its weight vector in terms of the measurement rows,

\[A^Tw=c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q=w^Ty .\]

This equality identifies the quantity directly from the measurements. Small but nonzero sensitivities create a different difficulty: the state can be unique in exact arithmetic while realistic measurement errors leave its numerical value uncertain.

Derivation and applications

Separate amplitude and decay rate using two observation times

A single positive reading of \(y(t)=A e^{-kt}\) admits many pairs of amplitude \(A\) and rate \(k\). Two measurements at different times can remove that ambiguity, but the spacing matters when readings are imperfect. Assume a single exponential, constant positive amplitude and positive noiseless signal, with independently calibrated times \(t_1<t_2\). Taking the ratio of the two exact data eliminates the unknown amplitude,

\[\frac{y_1}{y_2}=e^{k(t_2-t_1)},\]
\[k=\frac{\ln y_1-\ln y_2}{t_2-t_1},\]
\[A=y_1 e^{kt_1}.\]

This inversion is unique even though an individual observation cannot distinguish the two parameters. The local sensitivity of the inferred rate is obtained by differentiating both logarithms. For small errors and independently known times,

\[\delta k\simeq\frac{\delta y_1/y_1-\delta y_2/y_2}{t_2-t_1}.\]

If the measurements have independent relative standard deviations \(s_1,s_2\), squaring the coefficients and adding their variances gives

\[\operatorname{sd}(\widehat k)\simeq\frac{\sqrt{s_1^2+s_2^2}}{t_2-t_1}.\]

A shared multiplicative calibration factor cancels from the ratio exactly. A shared additive background generally does not cancel, particularly once the signal becomes small. Choosing measurements far apart helps at fixed fractional precision; under fixed absolute noise the later signal may lose sufficient precision to reverse that benefit. Two data can fit two parameters exactly and consequently do not check whether a single exponential describes intermediate times.

Worked example

Recover a decay and test an unused observation

At \(t=1\) and \(4\,\mathrm s\), a sensor reads 12 and 3 in the same units. The inferred rate is \(\ln4/3=0.4621\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(A=12e^{0.4621}=19.05\). At \(2.5\,\mathrm s\), halfway between the observations, the model predicts the geometric mean \(\sqrt{12\times3}=6\). A third reading of 7 reveals a deviation that the exact two-point fit could not show.

Second application

Specify the timing separation required for precision

For a second instrument the fractional standard deviation is 0.030 in both channels. To achieve rate standard deviation \(0.010\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), the separation must be at least \(\sqrt2(0.030)/0.010=4.24\,\mathrm s\) under these noise assumptions. Observations separated by only \(0.50\,\mathrm s\) give \(0.0849\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), even though they still determine a unique mathematical solution.

Turn a measurement protocol into an operator

Imagine reconstructing the temperatures of two adjoining cells, \(x_1,x_2\), from sensor readings. A sensor averaging both cells gives \(y=(x_1+x_2)/2\). The vector \(x\) describes physical properties; the forward map predicts what this particular instrument will report. A second instrument may measure the same properties through a different map. Include spatial averaging, response time and calibration in that map if they are significant. Treating a finite-width sensor as an exact point sample can make an accurate experiment look inconsistent.

For a linear experiment write \(y=Ax+\epsilon\), separating the ideal response from data error. With the averaging sensor, \(x=(20,24)\) and \((21,23)\) both give 22. The difference is a multiple of \((1,-1)\), which \(A\) maps to zero. The data specify an affine family \(x=x_0+z\), \(z\in\ker A\). More decimal places for the very same measurement cannot select one member. A unique reported answer may therefore reflect a chosen convention such as minimum norm or smoothness instead of new physical information.

Build a ray matrix from attenuation measurements

How does a detector reading become a row of a matrix? In a simple transmission scanner, assume a narrow monochromatic beam, negligible scattering into the detector and a known incident intensity \(I_0\). Over a short path segment \(ds\), the fractional intensity loss is \(\mu(s)\,ds\) to first order. Dividing the resulting differential equation \(dI/ds=-\mu I\) by \(I\), then integrating along the ray \(\gamma_i\), gives

\[y_i=-\log(I_i/I_0)=\int_{\gamma_i}\mu(s)\,ds=\sum_j\ell_{ij}\mu_j .\]

The last equality assumes the attenuation is constant within each model cell. The coefficient \(\ell_{ij}\) is the length of the path through cell \(j\). An unvisited cell contributes zero. Model refinement changes these lengths and the number of columns. With lengths in centimetres and attenuation in inverse centimetres, each log reading is dimensionless. This construction models individual rays. A finite detector that pools their intensities requires an additional averaging step in the forward model.

Which extra direction resolves a four-cell ambiguity?

Arrange four square cells of side \(1\) cm in two rows and list them in row-major order. Two horizontal and two vertical centre rays have log readings \(0.4,0.6,0.5,0.5\). Each traversed cell contributes one centimetre. The sum of the two horizontal readings equals the sum of the vertical readings, leaving three independent equations. If the top-left attenuation is \(t\), successive substitutions give the family

\[(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3,\mu_4)=(t,\,0.4-t,\,0.5-t,\,0.1+t)\quad{\rm cm}^{-1}.\]

Even positivity admits \(0\le t\le0.4\). The vector \((1,-1,-1,1)\) describes a checkerboard change invisible to the original views. Add a diagonal ray joining the outer corners through cells 1 and 4. Each diagonal chord is \(\sqrt2\) cm. If the reading is \(0.3\sqrt2\), division by \(\sqrt2\) gives \(2t+0.1=0.3\), so \(t=0.1\). The field is \((0.1,0.3,0.4,0.2)\) in inverse centimetres. The extra row acts on the previous null vector by \(2\sqrt2\). Its orientation supplies the missing information. Repeating any original ray could improve its measurement precision but would preserve the checkerboard ambiguity.

Find the observable combinations

Every row of \(A\) is a measured linear combination. Every vector in its null space is orthogonal to all those rows. Thus a derived linear quantity \(c^\mathsf{T}x\) is uniquely determined by ideal data exactly when \(c\) lies in the row space. If \(c=A^\mathsf{T}w\), compute it as \(w^\mathsf{T}y\). Conversely, any component of \(c\) along a null direction changes the quantity while leaving the data unchanged. This test can rescue a useful question from an underdetermined experiment. Both temperatures may be ambiguous while their mean is measured precisely.

Take a second reading \((x_1+x_2)/2+\eta(x_1-x_2)/2\). Subtract the first reading to isolate the contrast, \(x_1-x_2=2(y_2-y_1)/\eta\). With \(\eta\ne0\) both temperatures are identifiable in exact arithmetic. At \(\eta=0.01\), a difference error of just 0.02 K changes the recovered contrast by 4 K. Algebraic rank has improved, but the measurement has barely separated the two states. Changing the second sensor's spatial weighting can be much more effective than repeating its reading.

Translate a nonlinear ambiguity into an experiment

For a decay \(y(t)=A e^{-kt}\), an observation at \(t=0\) identifies \(A\) and says nothing about \(k\). A single later observation allows the continuum \(A=y(t)e^{kt}\). Two exact positive observations at distinct times yield \(k=\ln[y(t_1)/y(t_2)]/(t_2-t_1)\). This identifies both parameters under the assumed model. When the times are almost equal the denominator is small, which makes the slope sensitive to measurement noise. Late measurements can also be difficult when responses approach the instrument's noise floor.

Always state the admissible parameter set. A power reading \(y=p^2\) determines \(|p|\); if physics requires \(p\ge0\), the sign ambiguity disappears, though sensitivity near zero still needs attention. In later chapters we will assess local sensitivity with derivatives. A full-rank local derivative distinguishes sufficiently close parameters under suitable regularity; widely separated alternatives can remain.

Establish a baseline for a reconstruction

Write down the unknowns with units, the controlled inputs, the measurements and their precision. Predict what changes when one unknown increases with all others fixed. Look for symmetries and parameter combinations that move predictions together. Finally name a second measurement able to separate them. This sequence gives a reason for the later numerical choices: least squares reconciles inconsistent data, while regularisation controls amplification or deliberately selects among weakly distinguished solutions.

Characterise an identifiable output without reconstructing the state

Suppose an experiment provides exact linear observations \(Ax=y\) and a scientist wants a scalar \(Q=c^Tx\). Any two compatible states differ by a vector \(v\) satisfying \(Av=0\). Their outputs differ by \(c^Tv\). Consequently the output is identifiable for every compatible data set precisely when \(c\) is orthogonal to the nullspace. This condition can be checked entirely from the design, before taking a measurement. If it fails, arbitrarily precise measurements with the same operator cannot answer the scientific question unless other assumptions restrict the states.

There is a constructive version. Every row of \(A\) is orthogonal to every null vector. The row space has dimension equal to the rank, whereas the nullspace dimension is the number of columns minus the rank. These dimension counts show that all vectors orthogonal to the nullspace lie in the row space. Hence the test is equivalent to solving \(A^Tw=c\) for observation weights \(w\). When the test succeeds, \(Q=w^TAx=w^Ty\). The weights specify exactly which combination of instrument readings answers the question without requiring a particular choice of unobserved coordinates.

Recover one observable from overlapping measurements

Three masses sit on a support. One instrument reports \(x_1+x_2=7\) kg and another reports \(x_2+x_3=9\) kg. Subtracting gives \(x_3-x_1=2\) kg. In matrix language the target \(c=(-1,0,1)^T\) equals the second measurement row minus the first, so \(w=(-1,1)^T\). Independently parameterise all solutions by \(x=(7-t,t,9-t)^T\). Every admissible value of \(t\) produces the same difference. In contrast the total mass is \(16-t\), so even requiring all masses to be nonnegative leaves totals ranging from 9 to 16 kg. A convenient equal-mass assumption would select a value by adding external information.

Now give the two readings independent measurement errors of standard deviation 0.20 and 0.30 kg. The same weighted difference has variance \(0.20^2+0.30^2=0.13\) kg² and standard deviation about 0.361 kg. It remains an unbiased estimate of the mass difference. A shared additive zero-offset error cancels in this difference, whereas it would affect an output using the sum. This is a second design question: identifiability determines whether suitable weights exist, while the error covariance determines whether applying those weights yields useful precision.

A third instrument measuring \(x_2\) alone would remove the remaining ambiguity. Measuring the first overlapping pair again would tighten one known sum but retain the null direction \((-1,1,-1)^T\). When choosing the next measurement, evaluate its dot product with that unresolved direction. A nonzero product measures some of the missing information; a very small product makes the newly identifiable quantity fragile under noise.

For example a reading of \(x_1+2x_2+x_3\) merely adds the two original readings. It has nonzero sensitivity to every individual mass yet zero dot product with the missing direction. The instrument can improve precision on a known combination while leaving exactly the same continuum of compatible total masses.

How much information does a second sensor actually add?

The first measurement is the mean of two cells. The second is the same mean plus \(a(x_1-x_2)/2\). Differencing cancels the common mean and allows the contrast to be recovered. Assume independent, identically noisy readings.

Chapter 2 · Weighted least squares and residual diagnostics

Derive the fitting criterion from measurement error and separate the uncertainty of an estimated mean from the scatter of a new observation.

From first principles

Derive the least-squares distance from the instrument noise

Suppose \(y=Ax+\epsilon\), with Gaussian measurement noise of zero mean and known positive definite covariance \(\Sigma\). Independent errors of unequal sizes should not count equally in raw units, and correlated errors should not be counted twice. Factor \(\Sigma=LL^T\) and multiply the model by \(L^{-1}\). The transformed noise has unit covariance,

\[b=L^{-1}y,\]
\[B=L^{-1}A,\]
\[\operatorname{Cov}(L^{-1}\epsilon)=I .\]

For a candidate state, the negative log likelihood differs by a constant from half the squared norm of the transformed residual. This follows from the Gaussian density,

\[\tfrac12(y-Ax)^T\Sigma^{-1}(y-Ax)=\tfrac12\|b-Bx\|_2^2 .\]

The likeliest state therefore minimizes ordinary squared residuals in these whitened coordinates. Solves with the triangular factor \(L\) preserve this interpretation without forming its inverse. If the covariance is estimated or state dependent, the stated probability model and resulting uncertainty estimates need to include that extra information.

Derivation and applications

Construct a model check that requires no fitted parameters

A residual can be compared with noise most cleanly when the particular combination of readings was forced to vanish by the model itself. Consider \(y=Ax+\epsilon\) with zero-mean noise of known positive definite covariance \(\Sigma\). Choose a nonzero contrast \(c\) satisfying \(A^Tc=0\). No value of the unknown \(x\) can change the predicted contrast because its mean is

\[\operatorname{E}(c^Ty)=c^TAx=0,\]
\[\operatorname{var}(c^Ty)=c^T\Sigma c .\]

Under a Gaussian noise model its standardized value is a unit normal random variable. Squaring it gives a one-degree-of-freedom discrepancy,

\[Z=\frac{c^Ty}{\sqrt{c^T\Sigma c}},\]
\[Z^2\sim\chi^2_1 .\]

The corresponding fit interpretation follows by whitening \(b=L^{-1}y,\ B=L^{-1}A\). The vector \(u=L^Tc/\sqrt{c^T\Sigma c}\) has unit norm and is perpendicular to every column of \(B\). Thus every fitted residual has projection \(Z\) along the same direction,

\[u^T(b-B\widehat x)=u^Tb=Z,\]
\[\|b-B\widehat x\|^2\ge Z^2 .\]

When there is exactly one independent residual direction, equality holds for the least-squares fit. The contrast is a model check only to the extent that the covariance and zero-mean error assumption are justified. It identifies a combination of inconsistent readings, without uniquely locating a faulty sensor.

Worked example

Check an affine trend from three measurements

At times \(0,1,2\) a linear trend predicts zero second difference. Observations \(2,3,6\), each with independent standard deviation \(0.20\), give contrast \(2-2(3)+6=2\). Its standard deviation is \(0.20\sqrt6=0.490\), hence \(Z=4.08\) and \(Z^2=16.7\). Fitting the intercept and slope cannot remove that discrepancy.

Second application

Include a shared calibration mode in a conservation test

Three independent channels should satisfy a balance with contrast \(c=(1,1,-1)^T\). Suppose their independent errors each have deviation \(0.10\) and a common additive offset has independent deviation \(0.20\). Since \(c^T(1,1,1)^T=1\), the offset contributes \(0.20^2\) to contrast variance. For readings \(1.0,2.0,2.6\) the discrepancy is \(0.4/\sqrt{0.03+0.04}=1.51\) standard deviations. Dropping the shared offset would inflate it to \(2.31\).

A distance in data space depends on the instrument

Let \(y=Ax+\epsilon\) with zero-mean error of known positive-definite covariance \(\Sigma\). If the errors are jointly Gaussian, the negative log likelihood differs from \((y-Ax)^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}(y-Ax)/2\) only by constants independent of \(x\). This expression still defines a weighted least-squares criterion without normality. Correlated readings can vary together substantially while their difference is very precise; individually dividing them by standard deviations fails to capture that information.

Factor \(\Sigma=LL^\mathsf{T}\) and solve \(L b=y,\ L B=A\) by triangular substitution. Then \(b=Bx+\eta\) with \(\operatorname{Cov}\eta=I\); \(\|b-Bx\|^2\) is the original weighted distance. This is whitening. It also cures a units problem: residuals measured in different units are compared after division by their uncertainty scales. For truly independent readings of standard deviations \(s_i\), the transformation simply divides each row and response by \(s_i\).

Derive the projection and compute it without losing precision

Differentiating the squared norm gives \(B^\mathsf{T}(b-B\hat x)=0\). Thus \(B\hat x\) is the orthogonal projection of \(b\) onto the column space. For full column rank write a thin QR factorisation \(B=QR\) with \(Q^\mathsf{T}Q=I\). The minimiser solves \(R\hat x=Q^\mathsf{T}b\). Forming the normal matrix \(B^\mathsf{T}B\) instead squares its two-norm condition number. QR avoids this extra source of precision loss, though it cannot create information absent from the measurements.

Suppose two instruments measure one constant as 10 and 14 with independent standard deviations 1 and 2. The weighted mean is \((10+14/4)/(1+1/4)=10.8\), with standard deviation \((1+1/4)^{-1/2}=0.894\). The less precise instrument still contributes, but with one quarter of the weight. Giving it half the weight would confuse standard deviation with variance. If both instruments share an unknown calibration bias, the stated independence model overstates what averaging can achieve.

Work with residuals after fitting

The fitted whitened response is \(Hb\) with \(H=QQ^\mathsf{T}\), and residual \(r=(I-H)b\) has covariance \(I-H\). Consequently raw whitened residuals after fitting do not have equal independent unit variance. Observation \(i\) has leverage \(H_{ii}\); a high-leverage point can pull the fit toward itself and show a deceptively small residual. Under the stated model, \(\mathbb E\|r\|^2=m-p\) for \(m\) observations and \(p\) independent fitted coefficients. With Gaussian errors the sum has a chi-square distribution with those degrees of freedom. Plot signed residuals against input, time and sensor identity to look for omitted curvature, drift or serial correlation.

Uncertainty in a fitted trend and a future measurement

In the full-rank linear model \(\operatorname{Cov}\hat x=(B^\mathsf{T}B)^{-1}\). For the model mean \(c^\mathsf{T}\hat x\) the variance is \(c^\mathsf{T}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat x)c\); solve \(R^\mathsf{T}v=c\) to obtain it as \(\|v\|^2\). Predicting a future individual reading requires adding its independent measurement variance. This distinction matters when validating fitted response curves. A single point can legitimately lie far outside a narrow interval for the mean. When only relative weights are known, estimate a common variance scale from the residual degrees of freedom and state the additional assumption.

Separate common and independent errors before estimating a mean

Measurements made after one calibration often share a random offset. Write \(y_i=\mu+b+e_i\), where the common offset has mean zero and variance \(\tau^2\), and the individual errors are independent of one another and of \(b\), with variances \(s_i^2\). The covariance matrix is the diagonal matrix of individual variances plus the rank-one matrix \(\tau^2{\bf1}{\bf1}^T\). Consider any linear estimate \(w^Ty\) that reproduces an arbitrary constant, so that \(\sum_iw_i=1\). Its variance separates into \(\tau^2+\sum_iw_i^2s_i^2\). The first term survives every admissible choice of weights because the shared error is indistinguishable from the unknown mean within this set of readings.

Minimising the second term with a multiplier for the sum constraint gives \(2s_i^2w_i=\lambda\). Normalisation yields weights proportional to the individual precisions, with variance \(\tau^2+1/\sum_i s_i^{-2}\). In particular, estimating the weights from the marginal variances \(s_i^2+\tau^2\) as if every reading were independent gives both an incorrect covariance and generally incorrect relative weights. This derivation uses only second moments. Gaussian assumptions enter if the standard deviation is subsequently turned into a normal-theory probability interval.

Decide whether to repeat a reading or recalibrate

A balance has a random calibration uncertainty of 0.30 g. The first reading is 12.40 g with individual standard deviation 0.10 g. The second, acquired during a shorter averaging window, is 11.90 g with individual standard deviation 0.20 g. The individual precisions are 100 and 25 per g², so the relative weights are 0.8 and 0.2. The estimate is 12.30 g. Its variance is \(0.09+1/125=0.098\) g², giving standard deviation 0.313 g. A long sequence of additional readings using this same calibration can only push that standard deviation toward 0.30 g.

The difference between these two readings removes both \(\mu\) and the shared offset. Its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.10^2+0.20^2}=0.224\) g. The observed difference of 0.50 g is about 2.24 such standard deviations. That discrepancy is relevant to the model for individual readings and averaging windows. It supplies no direct evidence for or against a common calibration offset of a particular size, because that offset has cancelled algebraically.

What if the entire two-reading procedure is repeated after an independent calibration? Averaging the two independently calibrated group estimates gives variance \(0.098/2=0.049\) g² and standard deviation 0.221 g. Taking the same number of readings with one calibration instead gives variance \(0.09+1/250=0.094\) g². The choice of sampling protocol changes the uncertainty even when the balance, total reading count and individual variances are identical. If calibrations share an uncertain reference standard, independence between groups must itself be revised.

A separate reference mass weighed during each calibration provides another way to estimate a shared offset. Subtracting that reference reading removes the offset but also introduces the reference measurement error into every corrected value. The corrected readings are therefore correlated again, now through the shared reference. Their covariance should be recomputed from this modified measurement protocol.

Find a measurement floor that averaging cannot remove

Each reading has independent noise of variance one and the same additional zero-mean calibration offset. Repeating the measurement divides the independent variance by the number of readings. The calibration error is shared by every term in their average.

Problem sheet 1 · Which combinations do the data determine?

Use a null-space argument, a covariance model and a residual calculation to assess three proposed measurements.

1. Two instruments on three cells

Let \(y_1=x_1+x_2=5,\ y_2=x_2+x_3=7\). (a) Write all exact solutions. (b) Determine whether \(x_3-x_1\) and \(x_1+x_2+x_3\) are identifiable. (c) Name one additional reading that makes the full vector identifiable and prove it.

Test each quantity along the null direction

With \(x_2=t\) all solutions are \((5-t,t,7-t)\). Their directional change is \((-1,1,-1)\). The contrast \(x_3-x_1=2=y_2-y_1\) stays fixed, while the sum \(12-t\) varies. A reading of the total gives a unique \(t\), since its dot product with the null direction is \(-1\). Repeating either original reading cannot remove this null direction.

2. A shared calibration error

Two sensors read the same constant plus independent random errors of variance 1 and a shared random offset of variance 4. All three errors have zero mean and are mutually independent before the shared offset is added to both readings. (a) Write their covariance matrix. (b) Find the variance of their mean and difference. (c) Extend the mean variance to \(N\) independent sensor-specific errors with the same shared offset.

Track the common component once

Each reading has variance 5 and their covariance is 4. Thus the mean variance is \((5+2\cdot4+5)/4=4.5\), while difference variance is \(5-2\cdot4+5=2\). For \(N\) readings it is \(4+1/N\) for the mean. Treating all readings as independent would instead predict \(5/N\) and would hide the calibration floor. An external calibration can address that floor.

3. A line with room to test its fit

At \(x=-1,0,1\) observe \(y=0,2,1\), with independent unit-variance errors. Fit \(y=b+kx\). (a) Find the coefficients and signed residuals (observed minus fitted). (b) Compute the residual sum of squares and its degrees of freedom. (c) Compare variance of the fitted mean and variance of a future observation at \(x=2\).

Use the orthogonal design columns

The columns have squared lengths 3 and 2 and zero inner product. The intercept is the mean, 1, and the slope is \(1/2\). The fitted responses are 0.5, 1 and 1.5, leaving residuals \((-0.5,1,-0.5)\) of squared norm 1.5 and one residual degree of freedom. The coefficient covariance is diagonal with entries \(1/3,1/2\). The fitted mean at \(x=2\) has variance \(1/3+4/2=7/3\); an independent future reading there has variance \(10/3\).

A detector that pools two unresolved rays

A detector averages the transmitted intensities of two equally illuminated rays before reporting one reading. Each traverses 1 cm of material, with attenuation \(0.2\) and \(0.8\) cm\(^{-1}\), respectively. Compute the apparent homogeneous attenuation. Compare it with the equal-weight arithmetic mean and explain whether one pooled reading identifies the two attenuations.

Worked solution

The normalized transmitted intensities are \(e^{-0.2}=0.818731\) and \(e^{-0.8}=0.449329\). Their mean is \(0.634030\), so the apparent homogeneous attenuation is \(-\log(0.634030)/(1\ {\rm cm})=0.455659\ {\rm cm}^{-1}\). Averaging the two separately measured log readings would have given \(0.5\ {\rm cm}^{-1}\).

The pooled result lies \(0.044341\ {\rm cm}^{-1}\) below the arithmetic mean. Convexity of \(e^{-x}\) explains the sign: the more transparent ray contributes disproportionately to the transmitted intensity. An infinite family of attenuation pairs has this same summed transmission. Two equal attenuations of \(0.455659\ {\rm cm}^{-1}\) provide another example.

Chapter 3 · Singular systems and the amplification of noise

Identify which patterns survive a forward measurement and which become dominated by uncertainty when inverted.

From first principles

Identify the measured pattern hidden in each singular direction

A forward measurement \(y=Ax+\epsilon\) is easiest to interpret in orthonormal directions where \(A\) acts by simple scaling. For a singular triplet with positive \(\sigma_j\), \(Av_j=\sigma_ju_j\) and \(A^Tu_j=\sigma_jv_j\). Project the complete data equation onto its output direction,

\[u_j^Ty=\sigma_j v_j^Tx+u_j^T\epsilon .\]

Recovering the coefficient in the state divides the measured projection by \(\sigma_j\). Thus the reconstruction error in that direction is

\[v_j^T(\widehat x-x)=\frac{u_j^T\epsilon}{\sigma_j}.\]

When the data are whitened with unit noise covariance, the projected noise has standard deviation one and the recovered coefficient has noise standard deviation \(1/\sigma_j\). A zero singular value hides the corresponding state component completely. The numerical rank of a matrix and the set of coefficients that can be inferred at useful precision can therefore differ even for an exactly known forward model.

Derivation and applications

Derive the singular spectrum of a local averaging instrument

An instrument that averages neighbouring locations transmits broad patterns and attenuates rapid changes. Consider a periodic one-dimensional array of \(N\) equally spaced cells. At every cell the detector returns a symmetric three-point average, with response coefficient \(0\le a\le1/2\). With wraparound indices the noiseless map is

\[(Ax)_j=a x_{j-1}+(1-2a)x_j+a x_{j+1}.\]

Translation symmetry suggests using normalized Fourier patterns \(v_k(j)=e^{2\pi ikj/N}/\sqrt N\). Neighbouring samples of one mode differ only by a phase. Substitution yields its eigenvalue,

\[A v_k=\lambda_k v_k,\]
\[\lambda_k=1-2a+2a\cos(2\pi k/N)=1-4a\sin^2(\pi k/N).\]

The averaging matrix is symmetric and the Fourier modes are orthonormal, so the singular value of each mode is \(|\lambda_k|\). A negative eigenvalue reverses the measured phase of a real sinusoidal pattern but has the same noise amplification as a positive one of equal magnitude. A zero destroys the mode. For independent equal-variance data errors of variance \(s^2\), an inverse on a nonzero mode gives

\[\operatorname{E}|\delta x_k|^2=\frac{s^2}{|\lambda_k|^2}.\]

The periodic boundary assumption supplies the Fourier eigenvectors; a finite array with distinct edge rules generally has a different spectrum. Preservation of the constant mode, \(\lambda_0=1\), checks that the normalized averaging kernel conserves total signal.

Worked example

For \(N=8\) and \(a=1/4\), the alternating vector corresponds to \(k=4\) and has \(\lambda_4=0\). No improvement in readout precision can infer its coefficient from these data. The quarter-cycle mode \(k=2\) instead has \(\lambda_2=1/2\); noise of standard deviation \(0.020\) in its measured coefficient becomes \(0.040\) in the inferred amplitude.

At coefficient noise deviation \(0.030\) that is only a two-standard-deviation response. To infer the same state coefficient with deviation \(0.050\), the measurement deviation must fall to \(0.20(0.050)=0.010\).

Rotate the coordinates to expose the experiment

For a whitened matrix \(B=U\Sigma V^\mathsf{T}\), the unit vector \(v_j\) is mapped to \(\sigma_j u_j\). Parameter patterns \(v_j\) and measurement patterns \(u_j\) are orthonormal in their respective coordinates. A small singular value means a full unit change in a particular parameter combination makes only a small change in the data. This conclusion depends on scaling: metres or millimetres for one coordinate change the geometry. Scale each parameter by a physically meaningful range before interpreting a singular vector as a weak process.

For nonzero singular values the least-squares minimum-norm inverse takes the form

\[\hat x=\sum_{\sigma_j>0}\frac{u_j^\mathsf{T}b}{\sigma_j}v_j .\]

Left-singular directions orthogonal to the range contribute to the residual and cannot be fitted. Right-null directions contribute nothing to the data and are assigned zero by the minimum-norm convention. For a full-column-rank matrix the largest response of the inverse to a unit data perturbation has norm \(1/\sigma_{\min}\). A relative worst-case bound involves \(\kappa_2=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\); the actual perturbation direction can be far less damaging.

A blur that almost removes the contrast

Let a sensor mix two unknown levels with \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.505&0.495\\0.495&0.505\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). It passes the average direction unchanged and attenuates the difference direction by 0.01. For \(x=(2,0)\) it predicts \(y=(1.01,0.99)\). Add the modest perturbation \((0.01,-0.01)\). Inverting the perturbed data exactly gives \((3,-1)\): a 0.01 perturbation per sensor produced a unit change in each reconstructed level. In contrast, perturbation \((0.01,0.01)\) just raises both fitted levels by 0.01. Both inversions have zero residual. Compare the recovered patterns with independently known precision to decide which interpretation is supportable.

Why finer discretisation can make an inverse problem harder

An integrating or blurring instrument smooths rapidly oscillating parameter fields. Discretising more finely introduces additional oscillatory patterns, each with very small image under the forward map. For a convolution on a periodic grid the Fourier modes themselves diagonalise the operation: the response to each frequency is the Fourier transform of the kernel there. In a Gaussian blur this multiplier falls rapidly with frequency. Formally dividing by it at every resolved frequency amplifies high-frequency noise. Finer output sampling cannot automatically recover what the original acquisition smoothed away.

Plot \(\sigma_j,\ |u_j^\mathsf{T}b|\), and their ratio against index. For recoverable modes the projected signal can be distinguished from the noise floor. When the singular values keep decreasing but the data coefficients fluctuate at roughly constant noise magnitude, division begins to generate structure from noise. The transition can be gradual and projections are random, especially in a short record. Use noise estimates or replicated observations before choosing a cutoff.

Distinguish numerical rank from usable resolution

A singular value large enough to represent in floating point may still give a parameter uncertainty too large for the scientific question. Conversely several poorly measured parameters may admit a well-measured sum. In whitened coordinates the uncertainty of a linear functional follows from how it projects onto \(v_j\). A functional orthogonal to small-singular-value directions can remain stable without the full reconstruction being stable. The next chapter makes the decision to suppress these directions explicit and quantifies the bias it creates.

Ask whether a continuum solution has finite energy

A finite matrix has only finitely many singular values, so a formally inverted vector can always be printed after zero modes have been removed. For a compact smoothing operator on a space of square-integrable functions, there can be infinitely many positive singular values tending to zero. Write the data coefficients as \(b_j\) in the left singular basis, with no data component orthogonal to the closure of the range. Solving mode by mode forces the state coefficients to be \(x_j=b_j/\sigma_j\). Orthogonality gives \(\|x\|^2=\sum_j |b_j/\sigma_j|^2\). A square-integrable solution exists exactly when that last series converges; an arbitrary additional nullspace component can then be added if the operator is not injective.

This range condition explains why diminishing data amplitudes are insufficient evidence of a reconstructible state. They must diminish sufficiently fast relative to the strength with which the instrument transmits each mode. The converse is constructive: if the squared quotient series converges then the partial reconstructed states converge in norm, and continuity of the forward operator takes their limit to the original compatible data. A finite truncation conceals failure of this condition because every partial sum is finite.

Follow two spectra as the reconstruction grid grows

Take singular values \(\sigma_j=1/j\), and compare data sets with coefficients \(j^{-2}\) and \(j^{-3/2}\), respectively. Both data sets have finite squared norm. In the first, the reconstructed coefficients are \(1/j\) and the squared state norm is \(\sum j^{-2}\), which converges. Beyond a cutoff \(N\), its tail is bounded by \(\int_N^\infty x^{-2}dx=1/N\). Keeping 100 modes therefore leaves state error at most 0.10 in this particular normalisation. A corresponding forward residual can be much smaller because applying the operator divides each tail term by an additional \(j\).

In the second data set, the state coefficients are \(1/\sqrt j\). Their squared norm through mode \(N\) is the harmonic sum. From 100 to 200 retained modes it increases by about \(\log 2\), and the same approximate increase recurs on every doubling. Meanwhile the squared data discrepancy after truncation is \(\sum_{j>N}j^{-3}\), bounded by \(1/(2N^2)\). At 100 modes the data residual is at most 0.0071. The residual can become arbitrarily small although no finite-energy exact state exists for this idealised data set.

In a measured spectrum, only finitely many coefficients are observed and their far tail includes instrument noise. Exact membership in an infinite-dimensional range cannot be proven by drawing a straight line through a short log plot. The continuum calculation instead suggests two independent checks during refinement: inspect whether reconstruction norms stabilise, and compare data coefficients to an independently estimated noise floor before dividing them by small singular values.

For the first sequence even an excellent forward residual is a relatively optimistic diagnostic: the squared forward tail behaves like \(N^{-3}\), while the squared state tail behaves only like \(N^{-1}\). Comparing norms without specifying whether the operator has already smoothed the discrepancy makes the two convergence rates appear contradictory.

Chapter 4 · Regularisation, resolution and the price of stability

Choose which features to recover, derive the associated filter and quantify the bias introduced by that choice.

From first principles

Derive how a quadratic penalty filters small singular values

Suppose whitened data \(b\) are modeled by \(Ax\), and large reconstructed coefficients are implausible. Penalize their squared norm with strength \(\lambda^2>0\). The minimizer balances the measured residual and penalty,

\[\min_x\,\|Ax-b\|^2+\lambda^2\|x\|^2 .\]

In singular-vector coordinates let \(z_j=v_j^Tx\) and \(\beta_j=u_j^Tb\), where \(Av_j=\sigma_j u_j\). Orthogonality makes the objective a sum of scalar terms \((\sigma_jz_j-\beta_j)^2+\lambda^2z_j^2\), plus a constant from unmodeled data directions. Setting the derivative of each term to zero gives

\[z_j=\frac{\sigma_j}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda^2}\beta_j .\]

At \(\sigma_j\gg\lambda\) this is close to the ordinary inverse. At a tiny singular value it avoids dividing the noise by that tiny number, at the price of attenuating the true coefficient. A null component is set to zero by this penalty. A different physical reference state or a derivative penalty imposes different information and changes what the reconstruction preserves.

Derivation and applications

Predict a withheld observation without repeating every regularized fit

Suppose a linear model has independent equal-variance readings \(y_i\), design rows \(a_i^T\), and a fixed positive quadratic penalty \(\lambda^2\|x\|^2\). One way to compare penalty strengths is prediction on withheld readings. Define \(H=A^TA+\lambda^2I\), fit all rows to obtain \(\widehat x\), and let \(\widehat x_{-i}\) be the fit with row \(i\) removed while retaining the same penalty. Adding and subtracting the omitted row in the smaller normal equations yields

\[H\widehat x=A^Ty,\]
\[H\widehat x_{-i}=A^Ty-a_i(y_i-a_i^T\widehat x_{-i}).\]

Subtract the second identity from the first. Multiplying by \(a_i^TH^{-1}\) shows that the shift in the fitted prediction is proportional to the withheld residual. The constant of proportionality \(h_i\) is its regularized leverage,

\[a_i^T(\widehat x-\widehat x_{-i})=h_i(y_i-a_i^T\widehat x_{-i}),\]
\[h_i=a_i^TH^{-1}a_i .\]

Writing \(r_i=y_i-a_i^T\widehat x\) and rearranging gives the leave-one-out residual and corresponding validation score,

\[y_i-a_i^T\widehat x_{-i}=\frac{r_i}{1-h_i},\]
\[{\rm CV}(\lambda)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\left(\frac{r_i}{1-h_i}\right)^2 .\]

A point with large leverage helps set its own fitted value, so its ordinary residual can be misleadingly small. The formula assumes rowwise omission makes sense for the data. If nearby observations share noise or the intended prediction is a whole new experiment, withholding complete independent groups answers a different question.

Worked example

Check the shortcut against two explicit refits

Fit a constant to \(y=(1,3)^T\) with \(\lambda^2=1\). The all-data estimate is \(4/3\), and both leverages are \(1/3\). Dividing the ordinary residuals by \(2/3\) gives withheld errors \(-0.5,2.5\), hence \({\rm CV}=3.25\). Fitting only 3 yields \(3/2\); fitting only 1 yields \(1/2\), confirming both predictions.

Second application

Detect a high-leverage reading

For another scalar fit take sensitivities \(1,3\), data \(2,4\) and \(\lambda^2=1\). The estimate is \(14/11\), and the second reading has ordinary residual \(2/11\) and leverage \(9/11\). Its withheld error is \((2/11)/(2/11)=1\). Directly using only the first reading gives \(x=1\), which predicts 3 at the second station rather than the observed 4.

Two ways to control the unstable inverse

A truncated SVD keeps only the first \(k\) singular vectors ordered by decreasing singular value. All retained coefficients are inverted exactly; the others are set to zero. Its noiseless reconstruction is a projection onto the retained right-singular subspace. The choice is transparent but a hard cutoff can place two comparably informative directions on opposite sides of the threshold. In some spatial reconstructions sharp spectral cutoffs also introduce oscillations.

A smoother choice is zero-order Tikhonov regularisation. In scaled parameters and whitened measurements minimise \(\|Bx-b\|^2+\lambda^2\|x\|^2\) for \(\lambda>0\). Differentiation gives \((B^\mathsf{T}B+\lambda^2 I)x_\lambda=B^\mathsf{T}b\). Solve with an augmented QR or with the SVD. Each coefficient now carries a filter:

\[x_\lambda=\sum_j\frac{\sigma_j}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda^2}(u_j^\mathsf{T}b)v_j,\qquad f_j=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda^2}.\]

If \(b=Bx\) exactly, the estimate of \(v_j^\mathsf{T}x\) is the true coefficient times \(f_j\). Where \(\sigma_j=\lambda\) the noiseless amplitude is halved. For a very small singular value, the noise gain now approaches zero; the price is that a real fine-scale feature in the same direction is also suppressed.

Calculate the bias and variance for one component

Suppose a scalar instrument reads \(b=0.1x+\epsilon\), true \(x=2\), with noise standard deviation 0.1. Direct inversion gives an unbiased estimate with standard deviation 1. At \(\lambda=0.1\) the filter is \(1/2\); the estimated mean is 1 and the standard deviation is 0.5. Mean squared error is bias squared plus variance: 1.25 here, compared with 1 for direct inversion. For true \(x=0\) the same regularisation would reduce mean squared error from 1 to 0.25. Stabilising an estimate does not guarantee a smaller error for every possible signal. The importance assigned to plausible signals is part of the modelling decision.

Turn a source condition into a noise-dependent accuracy bound

The bias–variance calculation describes a particular signal and a noise distribution. Suppose instead we know only a deterministic data-error bound \(\|b^\delta-Bx^\dagger\|_2\le\delta\). To derive a uniform reconstruction guarantee we must also specify which signals qualify. One useful assumption is the source condition \(x^\dagger=B^Tw,\ \|w\|_2\le R\), in scaled coordinates. In singular coordinates a coefficient of \(x^\dagger\) is then \(\sigma_j\) times a coefficient of \(w\). Thus arbitrarily large features cannot hide in arbitrarily weak directions for the given \(R\). This assumption also excludes a component in the null space.

Write \(\alpha=\lambda^2\) for the penalty coefficient. Split the reconstruction error into the data perturbation passed through the regularised inverse and the loss produced by regularising exact data. In a mode of singular value \(\sigma\), the noise multiplier is \(\sigma/(\sigma^2+\alpha)\). The noiseless bias is \(-\alpha/(\sigma^2+\alpha)\) times the true coefficient, or \(-\alpha\sigma/(\sigma^2+\alpha)\) times the source coefficient. Because \((\sigma-\sqrt\alpha)^2\ge0\), the first multiplier is bounded by \(1/(2\sqrt\alpha)\). The same inequality bounds the magnitude of the second by \(\sqrt\alpha/2\). Orthogonality and the triangle inequality now give

\[\|x_\alpha^\delta-x^\dagger\|_2\le\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt\alpha}+\frac{R\sqrt\alpha}{2}.\]

If \(R,\delta>0\), the two terms balance at \(\alpha=\delta/R\), giving the bound \(\sqrt{R\delta}\). This is an a priori parameter choice based on stated uncertainty. For a family of increasingly resolved operators, keeping \(R\) controlled matters. If the model class admits large coefficients in directions of vanishing sensitivity, the same claim has no uniform basis.

When bias and data error reinforce one another

Take \(B=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.1)\) and \(w=(0,1)^T\), so \(R=1\), the true signal is \((0,0.1)^T\), and exact data are \((0,0.01)^T\). Suppose the second measurement is perturbed downwards by \(0.01\). Then \(\delta=0.01,\ \alpha=0.01\). With exact data, reconstruction would retain half the weak coefficient, producing \(0.05\) and bias \(-0.05\). The data perturbation is multiplied by \(0.1/(0.01+0.01)=5\), adding another \(-0.05\). The reported signal is zero, and the error \(0.1\) attains the theoretical bound. Here the vulnerable direction has precisely \(\sigma=\sqrt\alpha\). On another spectrum the achievable error may be much smaller.

For an expected background \(x_0\) and a derivative operator \(D\), a suitable criterion may use \(\|D(x-x_0)\|^2\). If a bound \(\delta\) for the whitened data error is known, the discrepancy principle seeks a fit whose residual norm is comparable to \(\delta\). When a specified discrepancy determines a unique strength

Consider the zero-background quadratic penalty and express the data in left singular coordinates \(b_j\). Substituting the filtered solution back into the forward model gives residual coefficient \(\lambda^2 b_j/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda^2)\), with the sign chosen as data minus prediction. The data component \(b_\perp\) orthogonal to the range of the matrix survives unchanged for every strength. Orthogonality therefore expresses the squared discrepancy as \(\|b_\perp\|^2+\sum_j |b_j|^2[\lambda^2/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda^2)]^2\), with the sum taken over positive singular values. This equation allows a statement more definite than the heuristic that smoothing worsens the fit.

For each positive singular value the quantity in brackets increases strictly from zero to one as \(\lambda\) grows from zero to infinity. Thus the residual norm increases continuously from the irreducible distance \(\|b_\perp\|\) to the full data norm. There is a unique positive finite strength for every target strictly between these endpoints provided at least one measured singular component is nonzero. A target below the first endpoint is infeasible. A target near the upper endpoint can permit a nearly zero reconstruction. The exact same reasoning for general penalties needs care when some physical modes receive no penalty.

Allocate a discrepancy after accounting for an unmodelled reading

A scaled instrument produces \(b=(3,1)^T\) for a single unknown \(x\), with predictions \(Bx=(2x,0)^T\). The second channel, which should be zero according to the stated model, already consumes one unit of residual norm. Set a total target of \(\sqrt{1.36}\) and use the zero-background criterion. Squaring the norm leaves \(1.36-1=0.36\) for the squared discrepancy in channel one. The required nonnegative first-coordinate discrepancy is therefore 0.6. Its prediction is \(3-0.6=2.4\), so the reconstructed coefficient must be 1.2.

Check by solving the actual penalised problem. The normal equation is \((4+\lambda^2)x=6\). Substituting \(x=1.2\) gives \(4+\lambda^2=5\), hence \(\lambda=1\). The noiseless resolution factor for that coefficient is \(4/(4+1)=0.8\). This calculation identifies the amplitude tradeoff associated with the selected discrepancy. It also shows why setting the target to 0.9 could never work: the second channel alone contributes one unit for every value of \(x\).

Suppose a second independent reading of the missing channel also repeatedly lies near one, despite a nominal uncertainty of only 0.05 units. The persistent signed defect would be evidence to inspect the forward model or reference subtraction. Treating that behaviour as an excuse to enlarge the noise budget can produce an attainable discrepancy while systematically oversmoothing the channel that does measure \(x\). Inspect residuals by physical observation as well as their aggregate norm before attributing a discrepancy-selected strength to the uncertainty of the instrument.

In this example the formula for arbitrary strength is \(x=6/(4+\lambda^2)\). Differentiating it shows that near \(\lambda=1\) a strength increase of 0.1 initially lowers the coefficient by about 0.048. This local sensitivity is another interpretable number to publish if the noise target is itself estimated from a short calibration series.

Chapter 5 · Nonlinear fitting and local models

Derive a Gauss–Newton step, recognise misleading local curvature and use step acceptance to protect a nonlinear fit.

From first principles

Build a local inverse from sensitivities

Let a differentiable forward model \(f(p)\) predict several measurements from parameters \(p\). Whiten its prediction errors by the measured noise covariance, and denote the resulting residual by \(r(p)\). To infer a parameter change \(s\) from the current mismatch, expand about the current state,

\[r(p+s)=r(p)+J(p)s+O(\|s\|^2).\]

Here column \(j\) of \(J\) records the response to changing parameter \(j\), with the other parameters fixed at the current values. Minimizing the squared norm of the linearized prediction supplies the Gauss–Newton step,

\[s=\mathop{\rm argmin}_d\|r+Jd\|_2^2,\]
\[J^TJs=-J^Tr .\]

Independent columns make the local step unique. Nearly parallel columns indicate parameter combinations that can compensate for each other in the data. Because \(J\) describes only the present neighbourhood, compare the actual residual at the proposed new state with the linear prediction. A new trial point must also respect any physical parameter bounds assumed by the forward model.

Derivation and applications

Compare finite updates in a parameter and its logarithm

A positive coefficient such as a diffusivity or decay rate can be fitted directly or via a logarithm that enforces positivity automatically. Consider \(p>0\) and use a reference \(p_0\) of the same units so that \(q=\ln(p/p_0)\) is dimensionless. Let \(r(p)\) be a whitened residual vector and \(J_p\) its derivative column at the current parameter. The chain rule and a linearized solve give

\[J_q=pJ_p,\]
\[\delta q=-\frac{J_q^Tr}{J_q^TJ_q}=\frac{\delta p}{p},\]
\[\delta p=-\frac{J_p^Tr}{J_p^TJ_p}.\]

Thus direct and logarithmic coordinates describe the same first-order change when the column is nonzero. Their undamped finite proposals differ. The direct step adds the change; the logarithmic step exponentiates it. Expanding the exponential gives the difference explicitly,

\[p_{\rm direct}=p+\delta p,\]
\[p_{\log}=p\,e^{\delta p/p}=p+\delta p+\frac{\delta p^2}{2p}+O(\delta p^3/p^2).\]

The local variance from the same linear model transforms consistently, assuming unit whitened noise and no added prior:

\[\operatorname{var}(\widehat q)\simeq\frac1{p^2J_p^TJ_p},\]
\[p^2\operatorname{var}(\widehat q)\simeq\operatorname{var}(\widehat p).\]

The positivity of a proposal does not ensure it reduces the nonlinear residual. A line search or other acceptance test can still be needed. Likewise, a prior density specified in one coordinate must be transformed with its Jacobian when a Bayesian analysis changes coordinates.

Worked example

Compare two proposals against an exact one-channel fit

A direct sensor predicts \(y=p\), with reading 1 and initial \(p=4\). The direct correction is \(-3\), reaching the exact fit immediately. The log-coordinate correction is \(-3/4\), giving \(4e^{-0.75}=1.889\). Both reduce the original error of 3, but the two linearizations have different finite remainders. The logarithmic model is nonlinear even for this linear physical sensor.

The asymmetric physical interval retains the bounds specified in logarithmic units.

Expand the residual around a trial parameter

Let \(r_i(p)\) be predicted minus observed data after whitening. Near \(p_k\), \(r(p_k+d)=r(p_k)+Jd+O(\|d\|^2)\), where \(J_{ij}=\partial r_i/\partial p_j\). Minimise the squared norm of this linear expression to obtain the Gauss–Newton step. The exact Hessian of \(\tfrac12\|r\|^2\) is \(J^\mathsf{T}J+\sum_i r_i\nabla^2 r_i\). Gauss–Newton omits the second term. It is especially accurate near a good fit with small residuals. With substantial residuals or severe model curvature it can predict the wrong improvement even if the Jacobian is computed perfectly.

Compute the step from a QR or SVD solve \(Jd\simeq-r\). Verify a derivative independently using a directional difference \([r(p+hv)-r(p-hv)]/(2h)\) and compare with \(Jv\) across a few \(h\). Errors that shrink and then grow as \(h\) decreases are consistent with truncation followed by roundoff. A disagreement stable across several step sizes often signals inconsistent units, residual signs or parameter ordering.

Work one update by hand

For \(g(t;p)=e^{-pt}\), observe \(y(1)=0.50,\ y(2)=0.25\) with equal standard deviations. Start at \(p=0\). The residuals are 0.50 and 0.75 and the Jacobian column is \((-1,-2)^\mathsf{T}\). The least-squares step is \(d=-J^\mathsf{T}r/(J^\mathsf{T}J)=0.4\). At \(p=0.4\) the two predictions are about 0.670 and 0.449. The residual shrinks, but a single linear step falls short of the exact parameter \(\ln2\). Recompute the derivative at the new point before the next update. Fitting the logarithms instead would change the implied measurement-noise model unless the weights are transformed appropriately.

Limit poor steps and enforce physical scales

A damped step minimises \(\|r+Jd\|^2+\mu\|Dd\|^2\). The diagonal scale \(D\) makes a step of one natural parameter unit comparable across components. Large \(\mu\) restricts the step strongly; small \(\mu\) approaches the ordinary Gauss–Newton solve. Evaluate the true objective at the trial point and compare its actual decrease with the decrease predicted by the linear residual model. A step with negative actual decrease should be rejected or shortened. Repeatedly tiny steps with a large gradient are evidence of a stalled solve, not convergence.

For a strictly positive rate, one can write \(k=e^\theta\); then \(\partial g/\partial\theta=k\,\partial g/\partial k\). This transformation enforces positivity and represents relative changes naturally. It also changes what a penalty on parameter distance means. Distinguish damping of an individual iteration from regularisation in the final objective: damping may decrease toward zero as a local method converges even when no penalty on the final parameters is intended.

The limits of a local uncertainty ellipse

At a well-determined full-rank fit with correctly scaled independent errors, \((J^\mathsf{T}J)^{-1}\) approximates parameter covariance in a local linear model. For \(g(p)=p^2\) and observed positive \(y\), either root has nonzero slope and a narrow local interval can arise around each. That interval misses the other root entirely. Bounds, multiple starts, profile fits and independent predictions give different checks on global uniqueness and nonlinearity. Distinct parameter values with similar residuals are a result to report; selecting the one reached by the first start can hide a scientifically important ambiguity.

Eliminate linear coefficients from a nonlinear fit

A decay amplitude may be unknown alongside its time constant. For fixed positive rate \(k\) the vector of predictions is \(a v(k)\), with \(v_i(k)=e^{-kt_i}\) and an unknown scalar amplitude \(a\). Assume the data have already been whitened, applying the same transformation also to \(v\). Minimising the squared residual at fixed \(k\) is an ordinary one-dimensional projection. Differentiating with respect to \(a\) gives \(v^T(av-y)=0\), so \(a(k)=v^Ty/(v^Tv)\) whenever the column is nonzero. Substitution leaves a scalar objective \(F(k)=\|a(k)v(k)-y\|^2/2\).

To compute its derivative, differentiate the complete residual \(r=a v-y\). The chain rule gives \(F'=r^T[a'v+av']\). But the optimal amplitude has already made \(r^Tv=0\), so the first term vanishes exactly. Thus the profile derivative is \(a r^Tv'\), even though \(a(k)\) changes as the rate changes. This cancellation requires an accurately solved inner projection. If an approximate amplitude leaves a visible residual along the column, ignoring its derivative can yield the wrong outer step. Several linear columns can be handled by the same projection argument while their rank remains fixed.

Diagnose the effect on three observations

Take times 0, 1 and 2 seconds, observed amplitudes \(y=(4,2,1.5)^T\), and trial rate \(k=\log2\) per second. Then \(v=(1,1/2,1/4)^T\), \(v^Tv=21/16\), and \(v^Ty=43/8\). Hence \(a=86/21\). Subtract the observations from the predictions to obtain \(r=(2,1,-10)^T/21\). Its product with the design column is proportional to \(2+1/2-10/4=0\), checking that no amplitude adjustment alone improves the fit. The value of the objective is \(105/(2\cdot21^2)=5/42\).

Now \(v'=(0,-1/2,-1/2)^T\) in seconds, so \(r^Tv'=3/14\). Multiplying by the fitted amplitude gives \(F'=43/49\) per unit rate in the chosen amplitude scaling. The positive derivative says that slightly reducing \(k\) lowers the local discrepancy. This sign has a physical explanation: the late observation lies above the fitted curve and a smaller decay rate raises the relative late amplitude. The amplitude will readjust simultaneously to keep the projection optimal.

What if all three acquisition times are the same? Every \(v(k)\) is a nonzero multiple of the same constant column. Changing \(k\) rescales that column and the best amplitude compensates perfectly. The profiled objective is then flat although the inner projection is perfectly well defined. Eliminating a linear nuisance coefficient improves the structure of a search but cannot cure missing information about decay rates when the timing protocol transmits only one measurement direction.

One can diagnose that limiting design before taking data by projecting the derivative column \(v'\) perpendicular to \(v\). Only this perpendicular component can change the fitted curve after amplitude compensation. At identical times it is identically zero because \(v'=-tv\). With distinct times it usually survives. Under approximately constant additive noise, inspecting its magnitude across candidate sampling schedules gives a quantitative way to choose the times that distinguish a changed shape from a changed amplitude.

The projected derivative has units of amplitude times time, so compare candidate schedules using the same time and amplitude scales.

Chapter 6 · Design an informative experiment and test its predictions

Choose observations that separate parameters, then check uncertainty against the behaviour of new data.

From first principles

Derive the information supplied by an observation

Near a reference parameter, suppose a reading of known standard deviation \(s_i\) changes by \(a_i^T\delta p\). Under independent, zero-mean Gaussian errors, the local squared mismatch for a parameter perturbation is the sum of the squared responses in noise units. Collecting terms gives the information matrix,

\[\sum_i\frac{(a_i^T\delta p)^2}{s_i^2}=\delta p^T H\delta p,\]
\[H=\sum_i\frac{a_i a_i^T}{s_i^2}.\]

If some nonzero direction is perpendicular to every sensitivity row, the experiment has no local information in that direction. A new independent reading with sensitivity \(a\) and noise deviation \(s\) adds a positive semidefinite term,

\[H_{\rm new}=H+\frac{aa^T}{s^2}.\]

The new reading is most informative for combinations to which it responds strongly relative to its noise. In the full-rank linear Gaussian model \(H^{-1}\) is the parameter covariance. For a nonlinear experiment the rows depend on the reference parameters, so a prospective design should be checked across the range in which the experiment may operate.

Derivation and applications

Value a new observation for a specified prediction

Suppose a calibrated inverse experiment already supplies a full-rank linear estimate with parameter covariance \(C\). An independent additional reading would measure \(a^Tx\) with noise variance \(s^2>0\). The information matrices add, giving \(C_{\rm new}^{-1}=C^{-1}+aa^T/s^2\). The decision concerns a prediction \(z=c^Tx\), so compare its variance before and after the proposed observation. Multiplying out the following candidate inverse verifies the rank-one update,

\[C_{\rm new}=C-\frac{Ca\,a^TC}{s^2+a^TCa}.\]

The denominator is the total predicted variance of the new reading. Its numerator connects the uncertain target and the proposed noise-free measurement through the existing covariance. Applying \(c^T\) and \(c\) to either side gives

\[\operatorname{var}_{\rm old}(z)-\operatorname{var}_{\rm new}(z)=\frac{(c^TCa)^2}{s^2+a^TCa}.\]

A candidate with excellent precision can have zero value for this target if the covariance-weighted cross term vanishes. Normalize by the initial prediction variance to express fractional gain as a squared correlation with the actual noisy reading,

\[\frac{\Delta\operatorname{var}(z)}{c^TCc}=\frac{(c^TCa)^2}{(c^TCc)(s^2+a^TCa)} .\]

The fraction is between zero and one by the covariance inequality. These formulas presume independently acquired data and an unchanged linear model. Reusing a correlated reading as if independent overstates the gain and can distort a sensor-placement decision.

Worked example

Choose between an indirect and a direct reading

Let \(C=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and target \(z=x_2\). Measuring \(x_1\) with \(s=0.5\) removes \(1^2/(4+0.25)=0.235\) from target variance 1. Measuring \(x_2\) with the less precise \(s=1\) removes \(1/(1+1)=0.500\). The second location gives the more precise final target despite its larger detector noise.

Second application

Identify a target-blind but accurate sensor

A separate model has \(C=\operatorname{diag}(9,1)\), and the target is \(x_1+x_2\) with variance 10. A candidate measures \(x_1-9x_2\) with \(s=0.1\). The cross term is \(9-9=0\), so target variance stays 10. A noisy direct measurement of the target with \(s=3\) instead reduces variance by \(100/(10+9)=5.26\), leaving \(4.74\).

Express design as a change in sensitivity

For independent whitened observations a candidate measurement with row derivative \(j\) adds \(j^\mathsf{T}j\) to the local information matrix. A repeat of the same measurement increases information along its existing direction, while a row with a new orientation can raise rank. After defining physically meaningful parameter scales, one can maximise the smallest eigenvalue to protect the weakest direction, or maximise the determinant to shrink a local uncertainty volume. If the scientific target is a single prediction \(h(p)\), minimising \(\nabla h^\mathsf{T}(J^\mathsf{T}J)^{-1}\nabla h\) at full rank may select a different design. Choose a criterion for the quantity actually needed.

Derive temperature and time sensitivities

Consider \(c=c_0e^{-kt}\) with \(k=k_r\exp[-(E/R)(1/T-1/T_r)]\). Estimate \(\theta=\ln k_r\) and \(E\), taking \(c_0\) as known. At fixed \(T\) the derivatives are

\[\partial_\theta c=-ktc,\qquad \partial_E c=(ktc/R)(1/T-1/T_r).\]

Within one temperature series all sensitivity rows are parallel in this two-parameter space, however many sampling times are used. Exactly at \(T_r\) the second component vanishes. Temperatures on opposite sides of the reference rotate the rows in opposite directions and can reduce parameter correlation. For equal absolute concentration uncertainty, the factor \(kt e^{-kt}\) peaks at \(kt=1\). A wider temperature range changes the rate as well as the orientation, so a time schedule informative at one temperature can miss the decay at another.

Compare an inference with three numerical experiments

Next restore the original noise and narrow the spacing between temperatures from 5 K to 1 K. The standard error reaches 1.4300 kJ/mol. Validate the model where it will be used

Hold out an entire temperature curve to test the predicted temperature dependence. Randomly omitting isolated time points from every curve mostly tests interpolation within already represented conditions; it also risks leakage if neighbouring errors are correlated. Fit only the training data, make the held-out prediction with its uncertainty, and plot signed prediction errors against time. If different temperatures systematically show opposite curvature errors, revisit the rate model before reporting more digits in \(E\).

A reproducible inference records the forward equations, units, noise law, parameter scales, data split, residual plots and the specific prediction the fit supports. Parameter identifiability is tied to the observations available. Adding a second measurement condition can change the answer more than switching between two competent optimisation algorithms.

Choose a design for the prediction that will be reported

Consider a linear calibration \(y=a+bx+\epsilon\) on an instrument with two available settings \(x=-1,+1\). Assume independent errors of common known variance \(\sigma^2\). Any number of repetitions at the same setting estimates its mean response. Denote the two setting means by \(m_-\) and \(m_+\). The calibration then predicts the value at any target \(t\) by linear interpolation or extrapolation, \(Q(t)=(1-t)m_-/2+(1+t)m_+/2\). This expression follows by equating the coefficients of the constant and linear functions, both of which must be reproduced exactly.

With \(n_-\) and \(n_+\) readings, the variance is \(\sigma^2[(1-t)^2/(4n_-)+(1+t)^2/(4n_+)]\). For a fixed budget \(N=n_-+n_+\), minimise over positive group sizes. Taking the derivative with respect to \(n_-\), or applying Cauchy–Schwarz, gives \(n_-:n_+=|1-t|:|1+t|\). The absolute values matter outside the calibration interval, where one interpolation weight becomes negative while its contribution to variance remains positive. This result assumes the slope law is trusted at the target and both settings cost the same.

Allocate twelve readings for a target near one endpoint

At \(t=1/2\) the target weights are \(1/4\) and \(3/4\). The optimal division of twelve readings is therefore three at the negative setting and nine at the positive setting. For unit measurement variance, the resulting prediction variance is \(1/(16\cdot3)+9/(16\cdot9)=1/12\). Splitting the readings equally instead gives \((1+9)/(16\cdot6)=5/48\). The first design reduces this particular variance by one fifth. Both designs can reconstruct the full line because both endpoints have been observed.

If the target were a tight joint estimate of intercept and slope, the answer would change. The information matrix in these two parameters has diagonal entries \(N\) and off-diagonal entries \(n_+-n_-\) for unit variance. Its determinant is \(N^2-(n_+-n_-)^2=4n_-n_+\), maximised by a six–six design. For the three–nine design the determinant is 108 versus 144 when balanced. Giving up joint precision here buys precision specifically for the proposed positive-side prediction. A design decision cannot be assessed without knowing which quantity is intended for publication.

As a further check, allow the physical calibration to contain an unmodelled quadratic term \(d x^2\). At both design points that contribution equals the same constant \(d\), so it is absorbed into the fitted intercept. At \(t=1/2\) its actual contribution is only \(d/4\). Every design using the two endpoints alone therefore incurs prediction bias \(3d/4\), however small its nominal variance. An additional interior validation setting can reveal this defect in a way that more repeated endpoint measurements cannot.

A further constraint may come from instrument startup time. If changing between settings is expensive while taking repeated readings is cheap, run the readings in groups but randomise or balance their order across independent sessions. Otherwise a gradual drift can become indistinguishable from the slope. The independent-error variance derivation offers no protection against an unmodelled time effect confounded with the chosen temperature or excitation.

Chapter 7 · Bayesian inversion and the information supplied by a prior

Combine an explicit distribution for the parameters with a noise model. Calculate exactly which uncertainties an observation reduces.

From first principles

Derive a posterior from a prior and a noisy reading

Suppose the scalar parameter \(x\) has Gaussian prior mean \(m_0\) and variance \(v_0>0\), and a sensor reads \(y=x+\epsilon\), with independent Gaussian error of variance \(s^2>0\). Bayes’ rule multiplies the prior density by the probability of obtaining the reading at each trial \(x\). Its negative log, apart from constants, is

\[-\log p(x\mid y)=\frac{(x-m_0)^2}{2v_0}+\frac{(y-x)^2}{2s^2}+\mathrm{constant}.\]

The coefficient of \(x^2/2\) gives the inverse posterior variance. Matching the linear coefficient after completing the square determines the posterior mean,

\[v_1=\left(\frac1{v_0}+\frac1{s^2}\right)^{-1},\]
\[m_1=v_1\left(\frac{m_0}{v_0}+\frac{y}{s^2}\right).\]

Greater precision means larger weight. An extremely uncertain reading leaves the prior nearly unchanged, while an extremely precise measurement concentrates the posterior near the reading. These statements describe the specified prior and instrument model. They depend on the reliability of both inputs.

Derivation and applications

Predict a future reading using the full posterior uncertainty

A posterior can describe both an unknown state and measurements still to come. Suppose current data give \(x\mid y\sim N(m,C)\). A new independent instrument will observe \(y_*=a^Tx+\epsilon_*\), with known zero-mean Gaussian noise variance \(s_*^2\). Conditional on a fixed \(x\), the only variability is instrumental. Averaging over the posterior makes the expected new reading

\[\operatorname{E}[y_*\mid y]=\operatorname{E}_{x\mid y}[\operatorname{E}(y_*\mid x)]=a^Tm .\]

Apply the law of total variance. The expected conditional variance is the new instrument variance, and the variance of its conditional mean is the uncertainty in the latent signal,

\[\operatorname{var}(y_*\mid y)=\operatorname{E}_{x\mid y}[s_*^2]+\operatorname{var}_{x\mid y}(a^Tx)=s_*^2+a^TCa .\]

The new reading is Gaussian because it is a sum of independent Gaussian contributions. Two future readings along the same vector \(a\) share the latent uncertainty while retaining independent measurement errors. For \(n\) such readings of equal precision, their predicted mean has

\[\operatorname{var}(\overline y_*\mid y)=a^TCa+\frac{s_*^2}{n}.\]

Increasing the number of future repeats reduces only the noise of their average in this prediction. Once actually observed, those data can be used to update the state further. A predictive check is most informative when the new observations were excluded from the original inference and the instrument model applies under the future conditions.

Worked example

Compare a latent interval and a future reading

A posterior for one temperature has mean \(20.0\,^\circ\mathrm C\), variance \(0.16\,\mathrm{K^2}\). A new thermometer has deviation \(0.30\,\mathrm K\). Its predicted reading deviation is \(\sqrt{0.16+0.09}=0.50\,\mathrm K\). A new reading of \(21.0\,^\circ\mathrm C\) lies two predictive standard deviations above the mean. Dividing its discrepancy by the latent deviation \(0.40\,\mathrm K\) would assess a different quantity.

Second application

Forecast the precision of an averaged observation

A separate model predicts a linear contrast with \(a^TCa=0.09\), and a single future reading has noise variance \(1.44\). Averaging 16 readings gives predictive deviation \(\sqrt{0.09+1.44/16}=0.424\). Even infinitely many future repeats would leave a prior-to-measurement predictive deviation \(0.300\). To reduce it to \(0.350\) would require \(n\ge1.44/(0.350^2-0.09)=44.31\), hence at least 45 readings.

From one answer to a distribution

In an experiment with \(y=Ax+\epsilon\), let the unknown have a Gaussian prior with mean \(m_0\) and covariance \(C_0\), and let measurement error be independent Gaussian with mean zero and covariance \(\Gamma\). The prior describes the state of knowledge before seeing these data. Bayes' rule multiplies the likelihood by that prior and normalises over parameters. For positive-definite covariances the negative log posterior, up to constants, is

\[\tfrac12(y-Ax)^\mathsf{T}\Gamma^{-1}(y-Ax)+\tfrac12(x-m_0)^\mathsf{T}C_0^{-1}(x-m_0).\]

The minimiser is the maximum a posteriori estimate. This objective has the same algebra as Tikhonov regularisation centred on a background. The probabilistic interpretation adds assumptions: if the distributions are taken seriously their scales and correlations imply uncertainty, not just a convenient stabiliser for a solver. A guessed smoothness penalty cannot be given calibrated probability intervals without justifying its scale.

Complete the square

Expanding the quadratic in \(x\) gives precision \(C_{\rm post}^{-1}=C_0^{-1}+A^\mathsf{T}\Gamma^{-1}A\) and mean \(m_{\rm post}=C_{\rm post}(C_0^{-1}m_0+A^\mathsf{T}\Gamma^{-1}y)\). Since the exponent is quadratic and the precision positive definite, the posterior is Gaussian and its mean is also its mode. For implementation, factor and solve the precision system. When data dimension is much smaller than parameter dimension, the equivalent data-space update can be cheaper:

\[m_{\rm post}=m_0+C_0 A^\mathsf{T}(AC_0A^\mathsf{T}+\Gamma)^{-1}(y-Am_0).\]

The same matrix multiplying the data discrepancy determines how far knowledge moves from the prior. A reading much more precise than the prior prediction has high influence in its observed direction; an observation compatible with a broad noise distribution does less. In this linear Gaussian setting posterior covariance depends on design and noise, while the posterior mean also depends on the realised data values.

Recover a sum and ask about the difference

Take two independent prior components with means zero and variances 1. Observe their sum as 3 with noise variance 1. The predicted observation variance is \(1+1+1=3\), so the update gives \(m_{\rm post}=(1,1)\). The posterior covariance is \(I-\frac13\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Thus the sum has posterior variance \(2/3\), down from 2, while the difference still has variance 2. A unique best estimate of the difference exists (zero), but here the measurement supplied no information about it. Reporting only the point estimate would conceal that distinction.

Interpret prior correlations and check their consequences

If nearby temperatures have positively correlated priors, observing one cell can reduce uncertainty in another even when its forward sensitivity is zero. That reduction is conditional on the correlation model. Change the correlation length, rerun the update and examine the target prediction. Before training, draw plausible prior fields or use representative quantiles and pass them through the forward operator. If almost every predicted data set violates basic instrument ranges or physical constraints, revisit the prior or parameterisation. This prior-predictive check uses no fitted residual and can uncover a consequential modelling error before a numerical optimiser converges.

Condition a second update on what the first already revealed

Suppose two observation errors share a known source, so processing each reading as an independent likelihood risks counting some information twice. For a scalar parameter and common response coefficient one, write \(y_1=x+e_1,\ y_2=x+e_2\). Let the errors be jointly Gaussian with variances \(v_1,v_2\), covariance \(c\), and independence from the prior parameter. Subtract \(\alpha=c/v_1\) times the first reading from the second. The transformed second error \(e_2-\alpha e_1\) has zero covariance with \(e_1\). Gaussianity strengthens that fact to independence. Its variance is \(v_2-c^2/v_1\) and its response coefficient is \(1-\alpha\).

The data can now be processed sequentially with the same result as a full joint update. If the new transformed coefficient is small, its effect is small even if the reading itself appears precise. The assumption that the original covariance is positive definite guarantees a positive residual variance. Perfectly correlated errors require a separate treatment of any exact difference equation. With non-Gaussian errors, decorrelation alone does not establish independent likelihoods, even when a linear unbiased estimator remains available.

Compare an independent replicate with a correlated one

Start with prior \(x\sim N(0,4)\). Two readings, 2 and 3, have unit marginal error variances and covariance \(1/2\). After the first observation, the precision rises from \(1/4\) to \(5/4\) and the precision-weighted mean rises from zero to 2. Thus the intermediate posterior has variance \(4/5\) and mean \(8/5\). The adjusted second datum is \(3-(1/2)2=2\), with observation coefficient \(1/2\) and noise variance \(1-1/4=3/4\). Its contribution to precision is \((1/2)^2/(3/4)=1/3\), much smaller than the unit contribution of an independent replicate.

The final precision is \(5/4+1/3=19/12\). The adjusted datum adds \((1/2)2/(3/4)=4/3\) to the weighted mean, giving posterior variance \(12/19\) and mean \((2+4/3)(12/19)=40/19\). The mean is about 2.11 and the standard deviation about 0.795. Incorrectly treating both original readings as independent would give variance \(4/9\), mean \(20/9\), and standard deviation \(2/3\). The same pair of readings has therefore yielded a more confident answer solely by suppressing part of the noise model.

An independent check uses the average and difference of the two observations. Their respective signal coefficients are one and zero; equal marginal variances make these two errors uncorrelated and hence independent. The average is 2.5 with variance \((1+1+2\cdot0.5)/4=0.75\) and therefore adds precision \(4/3\) to the prior. The difference remains available for checking the joint error model, even though it supplies no information about \(x\). Both routes give the same posterior when each part of the evidence is used exactly once.

For the numbers used here the difference \(3-2=1\) has variance \(1+1-2(1/2)=1\). It is one standard deviation from the expected zero difference. Thus there is no substantial conflict between these two readings under their correlation model, even though treating them as independent would exaggerate the information available about their shared underlying value.

Chapter 8 · Predictions, parameter profiles and nonlinear uncertainty

Calculate intervals for a quantity of interest, then find where a linear covariance approximation loses the geometry of a nonlinear fit.

From first principles

Propagate fitted uncertainty to a reported quantity

Suppose a fit gives a parameter estimate \(m\) with covariance \(C\), and the quantity to be reported is \(z=h(p)\). Near \(m\), expand a differentiable \(h\) to first order. Writing \(g=\nabla h(m)\), each small parameter change contributes through its directional sensitivity,

\[h(m+\delta p)-h(m)\simeq g^T\delta p,\]
\[\operatorname{var}(z)\simeq g^TCg .\]

Correlations can either reduce or enlarge the propagated variance. For a genuinely nonlinear quantity or a large parameter region, a local line may miss the full shape. One alternative holds the target value fixed and finds the best fit compatible with it. With an objective \(\Phi\), define

\[\Phi_{\rm prof}(z)=\min_{p:\,h(p)=z}\Phi(p).\]

Comparing this constrained objective with its unconstrained minimum tracks compensating changes in the nuisance parameters. A confidence level for its increase requires an explicit statistical model; tracing an objective curve alone supplies no probability calibration.

Derivation and applications

Derive a profile interval using the covariance of a linear fit

Suppose a Gaussian linear experiment gives unconstrained estimate \(m\) and strictly positive covariance \(C\), with correctly known noise scale. Its negative log likelihood relative to the optimum is the quadratic distance \(\tfrac12(p-m)^TC^{-1}(p-m)\). To profile a reported scalar \(z=c^Tp\), require the candidate parameters to satisfy that equality. Introduce a multiplier and differentiate with respect to the vector,

\[C^{-1}(p-m)+\lambda c=0,\]
\[p=m-\lambda Cc .\]

The constraint determines \(\lambda=(c^Tm-z)/(c^TCc)\). Substitution gives both the parameter combination selected at each target value and the profile height,

\[p_z=m+Cc\frac{z-c^Tm}{c^TCc},\]
\[2[\Phi_{\rm prof}(z)-\Phi(m)]=\frac{(z-c^Tm)^2}{c^TCc}.\]

This reproduces the usual propagated Gaussian variance while showing exactly how the other fitted coordinates move along the profile. For a monotone function \(q=g(z)\), imposing \(q\) fixes the corresponding \(z=g^{-1}(q)\). The profile then transforms without approximation,

\[2\Delta\Phi(q)=\frac{[g^{-1}(q)-c^Tm]^2}{c^TCc}.\]

An interval selected by a likelihood-ratio threshold maps by applying \(g\) to its two endpoints. It can be asymmetric even when the underlying likelihood in \(p\) is exactly quadratic. If \(g\) is not one-to-one, every feasible branch must be considered and the smallest objective chosen.

Worked example

Track the nuisance displacement along a profile

Take \(m=(2,4)^T\), \(C=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), and target \(z=p_1+p_2\). The target mean is 6 and variance 3. At \(z=9\), \(Cc=(1.5,1.5)^T\), giving constrained state \((3.5,5.5)^T\). The twice-log-likelihood increase is \(3^2/3=3\). Holding the second parameter fixed would impose a different and more restrictive path.

Second application

Transform an interval for a positive output

A second model estimates \(z=\ln(q/q_0)\) with mean \(0.4\) and deviation \(0.3\); let \(q_0=10\). At threshold \(2\Delta\Phi=4\), the interval in \(z\) is \([-0.2,1.0]\). Mapping its endpoints gives \(q\in[8.19,27.18]\), around the maximum-likelihood value \(10e^{0.4}=14.92\). A symmetric interval about that value would lose the exact profile geometry.

Push uncertainty through the prediction

Often a user needs tomorrow's temperature or a time to threshold, rather than all fitted coefficients. For a linear target \(h(x)=c^\mathsf{T}x\) and Gaussian posterior, \(h\) is Gaussian with mean \(c^\mathsf{T}m\) and variance \(c^\mathsf{T}C c\). Include covariance terms: positively correlated errors raise uncertainty in a sum and reduce it in a difference. For a new noisy measurement add its own independent measurement variance. Reporting this predictive interval as the uncertainty of the noise-free underlying response would mix two questions.

For a smooth nonlinear \(h\), expand about the mean \(m\). The first-order variance is \(\nabla h^\mathsf{T}C\nabla h\); the leading correction to the mean is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}[H_h(m)C]\). If the gradient vanishes, first-order variance propagation can return zero despite substantial uncertainty. For example, if \(x\) is centred normal with variance \(s^2\) and \(h=x^2\), then \(\mathbb Eh=s^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}h=2s^4\), using the Gaussian fourth moment. The linear approximation at zero would miss both.

A positive rate and a nonlinear lifetime

Suppose \(\theta=\ln(k/k_{\rm unit})\) is normal with mean \(m\) and standard deviation \(s\). A decay lifetime is \(t_*=1/k=e^{-\theta}/k_{\rm unit}\). Its median is \(e^{-m}/k_{\rm unit}\); its mean is larger by \(e^{s^2/2}\). A central 95% interval is \([e^{-m-1.96s},e^{-m+1.96s}]/k_{\rm unit}\). These endpoints need not be equally far from the median. For median 10 s and \(s=0.5\), the interval is approximately [3.75, 26.6] s and mean 11.33 s. Transform samples or quantiles if strong nonlinearity makes symmetric error bars misleading.

A profile follows compensating changes

Let the fit have parameters \((p,q)\). To profile \(p\), fix it temporarily at each trial value and minimise the objective over \(q\). The resulting curve reflects how much the other parameter can compensate. Holding \(q\) fixed instead gives a slice that is generally steeper and gives deceptively narrow intervals. For the sum-of-squares surface \(F=(p+q-2)^2+0.04(q-1)^2\), minimising gives \(q=(2.04-p)/1.04\). The resulting profile is \(0.04(p-1)^2/1.04\). A slice at the joint optimum's \(q=1\) is just \((p-1)^2\). Both have minimum at one but radically different curvature.

Read the level as well as the curve

A profile likelihood is a maximisation over nuisance parameters. A Bayesian marginal instead integrates their probability. Even when their modes agree the two objects can differ in shape in nonlinear models. A likelihood-ratio threshold yields approximate confidence sets under regularity assumptions and enough informative data. Boundaries, multiple modes and poorly identified nuisance parameters may invalidate a familiar chi-square cutoff. Report the objective convention and how the threshold was calibrated, e.g. by simulated repetitions of the full experiment. A plot of the raw profile and representative predicted curves along it remains interpretable even when an asymptotic confidence level is questionable.

Invert a test to put an interval on a ratio

A scientifically relevant quantity may be \(q=\mu_y/\mu_x\), for example the response per unit excitation. Estimates \(\hat x,\hat y\) can each have nearly Gaussian uncertainty even when their ratio is highly asymmetric. Suppose for now that they are independent normals with known variances \(s_x^2,s_y^2\), and that the true denominator is nonzero. To test a proposed ratio \(q\), consider the contrast \(\hat y-q\hat x\). If the proposed ratio is correct, that contrast has mean zero and variance \(s_y^2+q^2s_x^2\). The source of difficulty, the uncertain denominator, now enters the reference variance explicitly.

Collect all values whose absolute standardised contrast is at most a normal quantile \(z\). Squaring and expanding gives the quadratic inequality

\[(\hat x^2-z^2s_x^2)q^2-2\hat x\hat y q+(\hat y^2-z^2s_y^2)\le0.\]

This inversion gives a confidence set because it contains the true ratio exactly when the correctly standardised contrast falls in the specified acceptance region. Its shape need not resemble a symmetric error bar. If the leading coefficient changes sign, the accepted set may be unbounded. A covariance between \(\hat x\) and \(\hat y\) changes the variance to \(s_y^2+q^2s_x^2-2q\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat x,\hat y)\), so the preceding coefficients require modification.

A precise reading divided by an uncertain excitation

Use \(z=2\), corresponding to about 95.45 percent coverage for the stated normal model. Set \(\hat x=2,\hat y=4,\ s_x=0.5,\ s_y=1\). The quadratic coefficients are 3, −16 and 12. Its roots are \((16\pm\sqrt{112})/6\), about 0.903 and 4.431. The accepted set is the closed interval between them because the polynomial opens upward. The point estimate is two. The interval extends considerably farther upward than downward because a smaller plausible excitation can produce a much larger ratio. A symmetric linearised interval loses this asymmetry.

Now keep the observations and numerator uncertainty fixed but raise \(s_x\) to 1.5. The resulting inequality is \(-5q^2-16q+12\le0\), whose roots are \((-16\pm\sqrt{496})/10\), about −3.827 and 0.627. The accepted set consists of the two exterior rays. It includes very large positive and negative values. For such values the standardised contrast approaches \(|\hat x|/s_x=4/3\), inside the threshold two, precisely because the data cannot rule out an excitation arbitrarily close to zero.

An investigator with independent physical information that the excitation is strictly positive may intersect the set with the resulting permitted parameter space. A numerical routine that simply discards negative draws or clips ratios at a convenient maximum is instead changing the uncertainty analysis. The unbounded set is a measurable statement about what an experiment failed to localise, and identifies the denominator measurement as an appropriate target for redesign.

When \(\hat x^2=z^2s_x^2\) exactly, the quadratic term disappears. The confidence set is then ordinarily a half-line determined by a linear inequality. This is the transition between bounded and exterior solutions. A robust implementation must handle it directly instead of dividing by a leading coefficient that may be zero or extremely small.

Chapter 9 · Adjoint gradients through a forward solver

Differentiate a scalar objective with respect to many parameters without recomputing a separate perturbed solution for each one.

From first principles

Differentiate a scalar objective through a linear state equation

Suppose the state \(u\) solves \(K(p)u=f(p)\) for a given parameter vector, with nonsingular \(K\), and a scalar objective \(J(u,p)\) measures mismatch. A small parameter perturbation changes the state through both the load and the operator. Differentiating the balance gives

\[K\,du=df-(dK)u,\]
\[dJ=J_u^Tdu+J_p^Tdp .\]

To obtain a scalar derivative without computing every state sensitivity, solve the transpose system \(K^T\lambda=J_u\) once. Substitution eliminates \(du\),

\[dJ=J_p^Tdp+\lambda^T[df-(dK)u].\]

Each parameter then contributes through its derivatives of \(f,K,J\). The transpose is required even if the forward solver is more familiar; it moves the scalar objective’s weights backward through the linear response. A directional finite difference of the complete objective is an independent check on a computed adjoint gradient.

Derivation and applications

Propagate a terminal measurement backward through continuous time

Consider a scalar state satisfying \(\dot u=a(t)u+p\,b(t)\) over \(0\le t\le T\), with known initial state independent of \(p\). A sensor observes \(u(T)\), giving loss \(J=\tfrac12[u(T)-y]^2\). Assume continuous coefficients and exact evolution for this calculation. The forward derivative \(w=\partial u/\partial p\) satisfies an inhomogeneous initial-value problem, and the objective derivative depends on its endpoint,

\[\dot w=a(t)w+b(t),\]
\[w(0)=0,\]
\[\frac{dJ}{dp}=[u(T)-y]w(T).\]

Instead of solving for every possible forcing sensitivity separately, introduce a terminal weight \(\lambda(T)=u(T)-y\). Choose its evolution to cancel the term proportional to \(w\) in the derivative of \(\lambda w\). The product rule gives

\[\dot\lambda=-a(t)\lambda,\]
\[\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda w)=\lambda b(t).\]

Integrate over the interval. The initial product vanishes because \(w(0)=0\), and the terminal product equals the desired gradient. Thus

\[\frac{dJ}{dp}=\int_0^T\lambda(t)b(t)\,dt .\]

One backward weight suffices for many scalar parameters entering through different forcing shapes. A parameter-dependent initial condition would add \(\lambda(0)\partial_pu(0)\). A discrete time integrator approximating the continuous equations also introduces integration error; differentiating the actual discrete steps gives the derivative of the computed loss at that finite step size.

Worked example

Reconstruct a sensitivity in a decaying reservoir

For \(a=-1,\ b=1,\ T=\ln 2,\ u(0)=0,\ p=4\), the terminal state is \(4(1-e^{-T})=2\). With observation \(y=1.5\), the terminal adjoint is \(0.5\). Earlier weights are \(\lambda(t)=0.5e^{-(T-t)}\), so \(dJ/dp=0.5(1-e^{-T})=0.25\). Direct differentiation of \(\tfrac12(p/2-1.5)^2\) at four confirms the result.

Second application

Compare two controls observed only at the endpoint

For \(\dot u=-u\) between two instantaneous unit-gain input pulses, consider their amplitudes as independent parameters. A pulse at time \(t_i\) contributes \(e^{-(T-t_i)}\) to terminal-state sensitivity. At \(T=3\) with terminal residual 2, the gradient for a pulse at \(t=1\) is \(2e^{-2}=0.271\); for a pulse at \(t=2\) it is \(2e^{-1}=0.736\). Later forcing has greater leverage because it has less time to decay.

The state is also a function of the parameters

Suppose a discretised forward model solves \(K(p)u=f(p)\). Data enter through \(Hu\), and observations have been whitened. The objective is \(J=\tfrac12\|Hu-y\|^2+R(p)\). A direct perturbation of a material parameter changes the predicted state even when that parameter never appears in the observation operator. For fixed \(H\), the state derivative contribution is \(g_u^\mathsf{T}du\), with \(g_u=H^\mathsf{T}(Hu-y)\). Differentiating the state equation gives \(K\,du=df-(dK)u\). Solving this equation once per parameter would be prohibitively expensive for a fine material field.

Transpose the solve

Define an adjoint state by \(K^\mathsf{T}\lambda=g_u\). Then \(g_u^\mathsf{T}du=\lambda^\mathsf{T}K\,du=\lambda^\mathsf{T}[df-(dK)u]\). For each parameter component the gradient is

\[\frac{\partial J}{\partial p_j}=\frac{\partial R}{\partial p_j}+\lambda^\mathsf{T}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_j}-\frac{\partial K}{\partial p_j}u\right).\]

This uses one forward and one transposed solve for one scalar objective, independent of the number of gradient components. Forming the contractions for all components still costs work, often local to mesh elements. If sensors or boundary conditions also depend explicitly on parameters, their derivative terms must be included. A symmetric diffusion operator may reuse a factorisation; an advection operator need not equal its transpose.

Work a two-cell example

Consider \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p+1&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(f=(1,0)\), and observe the second component with value \(y=0.4\). At \(p=1\), \(u=(2/3,1/3)\), so the residual is \(-1/15\). The adjoint load is \((0,-1/15)\); solving gives \(\lambda=(-1/45,-2/45)\). Only the first diagonal entry of \(K\) depends on \(p\). With no regulariser the derivative is \(-\lambda_1u_1=2/135\). Check independently: \(u_2=1/(2p+1)\), whose derivative at one is \(-2/9\). Multiplying by the residual gives the same positive gradient. Increasing \(p\) lowers an already underpredicted second reading.

Test the computed gradient in a direction

Pick a physically scaled perturbation \(v\). A correct gradient gives a linear residual \(J(p+hv)-J(p)-h\,g^\mathsf{T}v\) of order \(h^2\) for a smooth problem. Halving \(h\) should roughly quarter this remainder across a useful range. At very small \(h\), forward-solver tolerances and floating-point cancellation can dominate. Repeat with directions touching different components and with the same boundary-condition handling used in production. A close fit to the observed data is a poor gradient test when residuals nearly vanish: a wrong data gradient can then look small by accident. Evaluate at a parameter away from the best fit, solve the state again for each perturbation and compare the resulting objective values. To detect a missing transpose, include a nonsymmetric forward operator in the test. The symmetric two-cell example above can expose a sign error but cannot distinguish the forward matrix from its transpose.

Reverse a sequence of state updates

Suppose the forward solver evolves \(u_{n+1}=G_n(u_n,p)\) for a fixed number of steps, and an objective sums observation losses \(\ell_n(u_n,p)\) from time zero to the final time. The direct chain rule carries a state derivative with respect to every parameter through the entire trajectory. To reverse the calculation, start with \(\lambda_N=\partial_{u_N}\ell_N\) as a column vector. A perturbation \(du_n\) affects both the loss at time \(n\) and the following state by \(G_{n,u}du_n\). Moving the latter action across a dot product gives the recursion \(\lambda_n=\partial_{u_n}\ell_n+G_{n,u}^T\lambda_{n+1}\).

The parameter gradient is the sum of explicit derivatives of all losses, the initial term \((\partial_p u_0)^T\lambda_0\), and the contributions \(G_{n,p}^T\lambda_{n+1}\) from every step. Each reversed step needs the state information used by its forward counterpart. Storing the whole trajectory makes that information available immediately. Recomputing segments between stored checkpoints trades extra forward evaluations for memory. These formulas differentiate the actual discrete transitions, so an adaptive solver with parameter-dependent step rejection or event switches needs a careful interpretation of the differentiated computation.

Make the transpose observable in a two-step calculation

Take a two-coordinate forward transition \(G(u,p)=Mu+pe_2\), with \(M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(u_0=0\), and \(p=1\). Two steps give \(u_1=(0,1)^T\) and \(u_2=(2,2)^T\). Observe the first coordinate only at the end, with target value one. The quadratic loss is one half and its final state derivative is \(\lambda_2=(1,0)^T\). One reverse step gives \(\lambda_1=M^T\lambda_2=(1,2)^T\). The two parameter contributions are \(e_2^T\lambda_2=0\) and \(e_2^T\lambda_1=2\), so the gradient is two.

The zero contribution from the last injection has a physical meaning: the injected second coordinate can influence the first only during a subsequent transition. The first injection has time to do that through the off-diagonal element two. Using \(M\) in place of \(M^T\) in the backwards calculation would instead keep \(\lambda_1=(1,0)^T\), predicting zero total sensitivity. This example exercises a distinction that disappears when every state matrix is symmetric.

For a scalar parameter perturbation \(h\), solving both steps again gives terminal first coordinate \(2(1+h)\) and loss \(J(1+h)=(1+2h)^2/2\). The remainder after subtracting the base loss and the predicted linear term is exactly \(2h^2\). For \(h=0.1\) and 0.05 the remainders are 0.02 and 0.005. The erroneous zero gradient instead gives 0.22 and 0.105. The two sequences have very different observed convergence factors even though they use identical forward trajectories and observation data.

More generally an individual parameter may enter the map at several times with different signs. The corresponding gradient terms must be accumulated rather than overwritten during the backward pass. In this example overwriting after the earliest step happens to return the correct value because the last contribution is zero. Adding a simultaneous observation of the final second coordinate makes both contributions nonzero and provides a complementary regression case for the time-index accumulation.

For a terminal loss summing both coordinate discrepancies, the adjoint load contains both sensor contributions before the first reverse transition.

Recognise an incorrect gradient by the rate of its remainder

Verify the reduced gradient for \(J(p)=(1/(p+2)-0.6)^2/2\) at \(p=1\). For each perturbation solve the scalar equilibrium again and subtract the original objective and the candidate linear term. The sign of the state sensitivity is essential.

Chapter 10 · Iteration as a regulariser

Derive the spectral action of a matrix-free iteration and explain why more iterations can eventually make an inverse estimate worse.

From first principles

Derive a matrix-free correction and its singular-mode filter

For measured data \(b\), minimize \(\tfrac12\|Ax-b\|_2^2\). Its negative gradient is \(A^T(b-Ax)\), so a step of fixed length \(\omega\) requires just one forward and one transpose application. Starting from zero gives the Landweber iteration,

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+\omega A^T(b-Ax_k),\]
\[x_0=0 .\]

For a singular pair \(Av_j=\sigma_j u_j\) with \(\sigma_j>0\), project onto \(v_j\). Each coefficient then follows a scalar geometric recurrence. Summing it gives

\[v_j^T x_k=\left[1-(1-\omega\sigma_j^2)^k\right]\frac{u_j^Tb}{\sigma_j}.\]

Choosing \(0<\omega\le1/\|A\|_2^2\) makes every bracket grow monotonically from zero toward one. Strongly sensed components appear early. Weakly sensed components require many steps, and their final coefficient can greatly amplify noise. This separation of timescales explains why a finite stopping iteration can regularize a noisy reconstruction.

Derivation and applications

Bound the noise admitted by a finite iteration count

Suppose the data are corrupted by an unknown vector \(e\) with \(\|e\|_2\le\delta\). Compare two Landweber runs starting at zero, one with exact data and one with noisy data, with fixed \(0<\omega\le1/\|A\|_2^2\). Orthogonality of the singular vectors makes the worst possible data amplification at iteration \(k\ge1\) the largest singular-mode gain

\[g_k(\sigma)=\frac{1-(1-\omega\sigma^2)^k}{\sigma},\]
\[\sigma>0 .\]

Put \(t=\omega\sigma^2\in[0,1]\). The numerator lies below one and also below \(kt\), since the sum \(1+(1-t)+\cdots+(1-t)^{k-1}\) contains \(k\) terms each at most one. These two estimates control opposite ends of the spectrum,

\[0\le g_k(\sigma)\le\min\{1/\sigma,\ k\omega\sigma\}.\]

The largest value of this upper envelope occurs where its increasing and decreasing branches meet. At \(\sigma=1/\sqrt{k\omega}\), both equal \(\sqrt{k\omega}\). The noise passed to the reconstruction is therefore bounded by

\[\|x_k(b+e)-x_k(b)\|_2\le\delta\sqrt{k\omega}.\]

If that crossover is outside the actual singular spectrum, the bound can be conservative. This estimate describes noise only: the bias remaining after an early stop depends on the true solution’s distribution among singular directions. A useful stopping rule considers both effects and the quantity being reported.

Worked example

Budget the iterations allowed by a noise target

In scaled coordinates let \(\delta=0.010,\ \omega=0.25\), and require the noise-induced solution change to be at most \(0.050\). The envelope guarantees this up to \(k=(0.050/0.010)^2/0.25=100\). At 25 steps the guarantee is \(0.025\); quadrupling the count doubles the guaranteed noise allowance.

Second application

Compare the envelope to an actual weak mode

For another run take \(\omega=1,\ k=10,\ \sigma=0.10\). The exact gain is \([1-0.99^{10}]/0.10=0.956\), smaller than the spectrum-independent bound \(\sqrt{10}=3.16\). A perturbation of \(0.020\) entirely along this data mode produces error \(0.0191\) after ten steps. A fully inverted fit would amplify the same perturbation to \(0.200\).

Multiply by the operator without storing its matrix

For large imaging systems a forward projection \(Bx\) and its adjoint \(B^\mathsf{T}r\) may be available as procedures while a matrix with one column per pixel is too large to store. Landweber iteration starts from zero and uses the least-squares gradient:

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+\omega B^\mathsf{T}(b-Bx_k).\]

An implementation requires both procedures to be adjoints in the same inner products. Check \(\langle Bv,w\rangle=\langle v,B^\mathsf{T}w\rangle\) on a variety of inputs; quadrature weights in a discretised integral can change the correct transpose. For the Euclidean norm choose \(0<\omega<2/\sigma_{\max}^2\) to ensure contraction in every nonzero singular direction. Choosing \(\omega\le1/\sigma_{\max}^2\) additionally avoids alternating scalar transients in these directions.

Derive the filter after a finite number of steps

Let \(z_{j,k}=v_j^\mathsf{T}x_k\), \(\beta_j=u_j^\mathsf{T}b\). The recurrence becomes \(z_{j,k+1}=(1-\omega\sigma_j^2)z_{j,k}+\omega\sigma_j\beta_j\). Summing its geometric series at \(x_0=0\) gives

\[z_{j,k}=\left[1-(1-\omega\sigma_j^2)^k\right]\frac{\beta_j}{\sigma_j}.\]

Components with large singular values reach their least-squares values quickly. Small-singular-value components remain suppressed for many iterations because \(1-(1-\omega\sigma_j^2)^k\simeq k\omega\sigma_j^2\) while \(k\omega\sigma_j^2\) is small. Stopping early is therefore a choice of spectral filter. Changing the initial guess affects the pattern retained in weak or null directions; with initial zero the iteration never invents a null-space component.

Regularise with an artificial evolution time

There are two clocks in a time-dependent inverse experiment. One describes the physical process being measured. A second, entirely artificial clock can limit how far we move towards a data fit. For a fixed real matrix \(A\) and fixed observed data \(b\), evolve the negative least-squares gradient from zero,

\[\dot x(t)=A^T(b-Ax(t)),\qquad x(0)=0 .\]

The data misfit \(F=\|Ax-b\|_2^2/2\) satisfies \(dF/dt=-\|A^T(Ax-b)\|_2^2\le0\). Monotone improvement of the fit alone does not establish improvement of the reconstruction. In singular coordinates \(b_j=u_j^Tb\), each positive singular value gives the scalar equation \(\dot x_j+\sigma_j^2 x_j=\sigma_j b_j\). Multiplying by \(e^{\sigma_j^2t}\) and integrating from zero gives

\[x_j(t)=\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_j^2t}}{\sigma_j}\,b_j .\]

A null component remains zero. A mode reaches half of its unregularised amplitude at \(t=(\log2)/\sigma_j^2\). At a fixed finite \(t\), weak modes have small noise gain approximately \(t\sigma_j\). At very long times the gain tends to \(1/\sigma_j\). This is asymptotic regularisation, with stopping time acting as a spectral filter. As the noise bound decreases one can allow longer runs, subject to continued noise control. A single fixed noisy measurement offers no justification for sending \(t\) to infinity.

Two modes fitted on different clocks

Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/4)\) and \(b=(1,0.04)^T\). At \(t=16\log2\), the weak exponential is exactly \(1/2\) while the strong exponential is \(2^{-16}\). The reconstruction is \((1-2^{-16},\,0.08)^T\). The fully inverted weak datum would give \(0.16\). The strong measurement is fitted to within \(2^{-16}\approx0.0000153\) in amplitude while half the weak amplitude is still suppressed. If some of the \(0.04\) is noise, the same factor suppresses that contribution.

Forward Euler with step \(h\) in this artificial time recovers the Landweber iteration. It replaces each remaining exponential by \((1-h\sigma_j^2)^k\). To make every positive mode decay we need \(0<h<2/\sigma_{\max}^2\). An explicit step beyond this limit can diverge even though the original evolution dissipates misfit. Comparing stopping iterations therefore requires reporting \(h\) as well as \(k\).

See semiconvergence in a two-mode experiment

Let \(B=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.1)\), true \(x=(1,0)\) and observed \(b=(1,0.02)\). Set \(\omega=1\). After one step the estimate is \((1,0.002)\), very close to truth, and the first mode is already exact. After 100 steps the second coordinate is \(0.2(1-0.99^{100})=0.1268\). At convergence it is 0.2. The residual shrinks steadily toward zero while the actual reconstruction has deteriorated after the first step. With a genuinely nonzero second component the error curve may initially improve for longer. Which iteration is best depends on the unknown signal and noise, so a solver's normal residual criterion is not a suitable scientific stopping rule by itself.

Select an observable stopping test

When a data error bound is known, stop the first time the residual falls below a noise-compatible level. With independent validation measurements monitor their predictive error across iterations. A fast Krylov method can bring useful modes in much sooner but may also reach noise-dominated directions sooner. Compare algorithms by scientific accuracy and forward/adjoint applications at a defensible stopping point. If a prior-weighted variable or preconditioner changes how quickly individual modes enter, the early-stopped result changes as well. Record that choice alongside the step size, starting state and stopping rule so the reconstruction can be interpreted.

Read the polynomials selected by a Krylov inverse

Landweber iteration applies a predetermined residual polynomial once the step size and count are chosen. Conjugate gradients on the normal equations instead choose each update from a growing search space using the actual data. With \(x_0=0\), write \(g=B^Tb\) and \(C=B^TB\). The first Krylov approximation is \(x_1=\alpha g\). Minimising \(\|b-\alpha Bg\|^2\) gives \(\alpha=(Bg)^Tb/\|Bg\|^2=\|g\|^2/\|Bg\|^2\) when \(g\ne0\). The first step is thus a data-dependent scaling of the backprojected measurements. It uses only products by the forward operator and its transpose.

At iteration \(k\), the corrections lie in the span of \(g,Cg,\ldots,C^{k-1}g\). In singular coordinates the solution coefficient is a polynomial in \(\sigma_j^2\) times \(\sigma_j b_j\). In exact arithmetic, once the span contains every excited singular direction the least-squares fit is exact in the measured range. Faster convergence of the data residual is valuable for well-determined components. It can also admit a noise-dominated direction much more abruptly than an investigator expects from watching just the residual.

See the second iteration introduce a spurious peak

Let \(B=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.05)\), true state \((2,0)^T\), and observed \(b=(2,0.10)^T\). The second reading is entirely noise in this constructed test. Then \(g=(2,0.005)^T\) and \(Bg=(2,0.00025)^T\). The optimal scalar is \((4+0.000025)/(4+0.0000000625)\approx1.00000623\). The first state is approximately \((2.0000125,0.00500003)^T\), closely matching the known truth. Its predicted second reading is only about 0.000250, and the total residual is consequently just below 0.10. A discrepancy threshold of 0.11 would accept this result.

Both nonzero singular directions are excited by the data. The second Krylov space is the whole plane because \(g\) and \(Cg=(2,0.0000125)^T\) are independent. Its minimiser is therefore the exact inverse state \((2,2)^T\), with zero data residual. The second true coordinate is zero, so this last step increases the state error from about 0.005 to two. The improvement in the objective is an improvement in fitting the particular noise draw. It has supplied no additional physical information about a weakly measured component.

For a real experiment the true state is unavailable, so a plot of state error cannot choose the iteration. Use measured uncertainty, predicted quantities on held-out readings or credible prior constraints to decide about stopping. If independent repeats show the same signed second reading with uncertainty far below 0.10, the case for treating it as pure noise would weaken. A stopping threshold calibrated for the present example should then change with the measurement evidence.

The data-based early step also changes under a rescaling of the parameter coordinates. A different scaling changes the shape of the first search space after backprojection, although the final unregularised solution expressed in the original coordinates remains the same. For that reason the iteration number by itself does not fully specify an iterative regulariser. Record the scaling, preconditioner and starting estimate as part of the reconstruction.

Chapter 11 · Constraints, sparse signals and edge-preserving penalties

Derive a solution at a bound, a soft-threshold rule and the behaviour of a total-variation penalty at a material interface.

From first principles

Derive thresholding from an absolute-value penalty

A coefficient that can physically vanish can be estimated by balancing squared measurement mismatch against a penalty on its absolute magnitude. For a scalar reading \(y\), consider \(F(x)=\tfrac12(x-y)^2+\lambda|x|\) with \(\lambda\ge0\). On either open half-line the derivative is ordinary, giving candidates

\[x=y-\lambda\quad(x>0),\]
\[x=y+\lambda\quad(x<0).\]

When neither candidate has the assumed sign, the kink at zero must be checked. The left derivative at zero is \(-y-\lambda\) and the right derivative is \(-y+\lambda\). A convex minimum occurs there if these enclose zero, namely if \(|y|\le\lambda\). Combining the cases gives

\[x_*=\operatorname{sign}(y)\max(|y|-\lambda,0).\]

The shrinkage creates exact zeros and biases surviving amplitudes toward zero. Applying a similar penalty to differences between adjacent field values instead of to the field itself favours neighbouring sites that share one value while permitting isolated jumps.

Derivation and applications

Shrink a two-dimensional gradient while preserving its direction

At an image pixel or a mesh cell the inferred gradient can have two measured components. If the physical surface has no preferred coordinate axes, a rotationally invariant local penalty is proportional to the Euclidean length of the gradient vector. Consider the local optimization with equally scaled and equally precise components,

\[\min_{g\in\mathbb R^2}\ \frac12\|g-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|g\|_2,\]
\[\lambda\ge0 .\]

For a fixed trial length \(r=\|g\|\), the data mismatch is minimized by making \(g\) parallel to \(y\), since this maximizes their dot product. For \(y\ne0\) write \(g=r y/\|y\|\). The remaining one-dimensional constrained problem is

\[\min_{r\ge0}\ \frac12(r-\|y\|)^2+\lambda r,\]
\[r_*=\max(\|y\|-\lambda,0).\]

Thus both gradient components are attenuated by a common factor; if \(y=0\), the solution is zero directly. The componentwise absolute-value penalty gives a different rule,

\[g_*=\max(1-\lambda/\|y\|,0)y,\]
\[(g_{\rm component})_i=\operatorname{sign}(y_i)\max(|y_i|-\lambda,0).\]

The first expression is for \(y\ne0\). A local gradient shrinkage step is used inside some total-variation algorithms, but arbitrary independently shrunk gradient vectors need not be differences of one global image. A full reconstruction must enforce consistency with the field and the measurement equations. Different component units or uncertainties likewise require a corresponding metric.

Worked example

Compare an inclined edge under two penalties

For measured gradient \(y=(3,4)\) and \(\lambda=2\), the isotropic solution has length \(5-2=3\) and vector \((1.8,2.4)\); its angle is unchanged. Applying componentwise shrinkage gives \((1,2)\) with angle \(63.4^\circ\), compared with the measured \(53.1^\circ\). The choice of penalty can therefore affect an estimated orientation.

Second application

Test whether a detection threshold depends on the grid axes

A second gradient has length \(1.2\) with \(\lambda=1\). Along an axis, both rules return length \(0.2\). Rotating it by \(45^\circ\) gives coordinates \((0.849,0.849)\). The isotropic rule still returns length \(0.2\), whereas componentwise thresholding returns zero because neither coordinate reaches one. An orientation-dependent disappearance can therefore be introduced by the estimator itself.

Physical constraints change the optimality test

An attenuation coefficient cannot be negative. In a convex inversion with lower bound zero, the unconstrained least-squares normal equations need not hold at a valid constrained solution. Consider minimising a differentiable \(F(x)\) with \(x_j\ge0\). At a free component the derivative must be zero. At a zero component only positive feasible perturbations exist, so the derivative must be nonnegative. Negative reconstructed cells cannot generally be repaired by clipping after inversion because the forward operator couples neighbouring parameters.

For example minimise \(\tfrac12[(x_1+x_2-1)^2+(x_1+1)^2]\) under \(x_i\ge0\). The unconstrained solution is \((-1,2)\). Clipping gives \((0,2)\), which overpredicts the first observation. On the active face \(x_1=0\), the minimising \(x_2\) is 1. At \((0,1)\) the gradient is \((1,0)\) and the bound conditions hold. The price of feasibility appears in the data residual; it is important to show which observations the constrained model cannot fit.

Derive the soft-threshold rule

For a scalar coefficient with observation \(b\), minimise \(\tfrac12(x-b)^2+\lambda|x|\). On \(x>0\) the stationary point is \(b-\lambda\), feasible there only when \(b>\lambda\); on \(x<0\) it is \(b+\lambda\). At zero, the admissible slopes of the absolute value range from \(-1\) to 1, so zero solves the condition when \(|b|\le\lambda\). Together these give \(x=\operatorname{sign}(b)\max(|b|-\lambda,0)\). Even retained coefficients shrink by \(\lambda\), a real amplitude bias. With \(b=(-0.2,0.7,2.4)\) and \(\lambda=0.5\), the estimate is \((0,0.2,1.9)\) for an identity forward map.

Penalise changes in a field

In one dimension discrete total variation is \(\sum_j|x_{j+1}-x_j|\). A single large jump and several monotone small steps spanning the same overall change have equal total variation. In contrast a squared difference penalty favours spreading the jump over several sites. That explains why total variation can preserve an abrupt edge when the data localise it. It can also create nearly constant plateaus in a field that really changes smoothly.

For two observed values \(b_1,b_2\), use the variables \(m=(x_1+x_2)/2,\ d=(x_2-x_1)/2\). With objective \(\tfrac12\sum(x_i-b_i)^2+\lambda|x_2-x_1|\), the optimal mean is the observed mean and half-difference is the soft-thresholded observed half-difference with threshold \(\lambda\). When \(|b_2-b_1|\le2\lambda\) the two estimated levels fuse. This concrete loss of resolution should be reported when interpreting a uniform patch in a reconstructed image.

Make an edge claim testable

Generate test fields both with a sharp jump and with a smooth ramp. Pass them through the same sensor model and noise level, then fit with the same strength-selection procedure used for real data. Measure contrast bias and edge-location error separately; an image can put an edge in the correct place but suppress its amplitude. For a nonnegative material field inspect the gradient at active zeros alongside the residual. The regulariser and the bounds each supply information that the instrument may not have measured.

Follow total variation through three coupled cells

Consider three ordered cell values observed directly with equal independent precision. Minimise \(\tfrac12\sum_i(x_i-b_i)^2+\lambda(|x_2-x_1|+|x_3-x_2|)\). Introduce edge variables \(s_1,s_2\) equal to the signs of nonzero differences. At a zero difference either variable may take any value between minus one and one. Differentiation with these admissible subgradients gives the three balances \(x_1-b_1-\lambda s_1=0,\ x_2-b_2+\lambda(s_1-s_2)=0,\ x_3-b_3+\lambda s_2=0\). A candidate solution must satisfy all three balances and must agree with the sign choices that produced it.

Adding the balances eliminates both internal edge forces. Consequently the reconstructed values have exactly the same sum as the observations, regardless of the penalty strength. The quadratic part is strictly convex, so any feasible sign assignment satisfying the balances proves that the state is the unique minimiser. Adding lower bounds can introduce reactions and break the sum identity. Unequal precision also changes which weighted average is conserved. The calculation depends on the observation operator being the identity; after blurring, the first derivative of the data term already couples all cells.

Derive the complete fusion path for a short profile

Take \(b=(0,1,5)^T\). For small positive \(\lambda\), both differences remain positive, so \(s_1=s_2=1\). The equations give \(x=(\lambda,1,5-\lambda)^T\). The first two coordinates meet at \(\lambda=1\), while the second and third are still unequal. Assume those first two now form a plateau of height \(a\), with \(s_2=1\). Adding their balances gives \(2a-1-\lambda=0\), hence \(a=(1+\lambda)/2\). The first equation then requires \(s_1=a/\lambda=(1+\lambda)/(2\lambda)\), which lies in the permitted interval exactly when \(\lambda\ge1\).

The third coordinate stays \(5-\lambda\). Equating it to the plateau gives \((1+\lambda)/2=5-\lambda\), or \(\lambda=3\). Beyond this value all three cells coincide, and sum conservation fixes them at two. The required subgradients are \(s_1=2/\lambda\) and \(s_2=3/\lambda\), both admissible for \(\lambda\ge3\). This completes the path without numerical optimisation. At strengths 0.5, 2 and 4 the respective profiles are \((0.5,1,4.5)\), \((1.5,1.5,3)\) and \((2,2,2)\).

The thresholds record when the data imbalance inside a proposed plateau can be supported by the admissible edge subgradients. A flat patch at the second strength is therefore compatible with unequal raw values of zero and one. Conversely, a visible third-cell edge at that strength has already lost much of its amplitude. A reconstruction report that uses one threshold for declaring the existence of a jump and another for estimating its amplitude should explain both decisions.

Checking the final all-fused solution using only its observed mean would miss the second edge constraint. For example at strength two that constant candidate would require \(s_2=1.5\), outside the admissible unit interval, despite reproducing total mass perfectly. The failed local balance tells which boundary of the candidate plateau has to reopen. This provides a simple hand diagnostic for more complicated one-dimensional reconstructions when an apparently reasonable flat interval has been proposed.

Chapter 12 · A reconstruction audit from observations to a defensible claim

Combine residuals, resolution, uncertainty and independent predictions to decide what one spatial experiment establishes.

From first principles

Audit a recovered contrast in measurement units

Suppose two equally calibrated channels sense neighbouring state values with symmetric cross-talk \(0\le a<1/2\). Their noiseless observations are \(y_1=(1-a)x_1+ax_2\) and \(y_2=ax_1+(1-a)x_2\). Subtracting removes the common level and reveals how strongly the desired contrast \(d=x_1-x_2\) appears in the readings,

\[y_1-y_2=(1-2a)d .\]

Independent zero-mean channel noise of common standard deviation \(s\) gives difference variance \(2s^2\). Inverting only this observable therefore yields the data-based estimate and uncertainty

\[\widehat d=\frac{y_1-y_2}{1-2a},\]
\[\operatorname{sd}(\widehat d)=\frac{\sqrt2\,s}{|1-2a|}.\]

The noise is amplified as the channels approach identical response. A useful resolution audit reports the measured difference, the calibrated contrast gain and the relevant uncertainty sources. If the gain itself is uncertain or channel noise is correlated, those effects must enter the audit before a physical contrast is claimed.

Derivation and applications

Quantify signal leakage into an estimate of a local field

A reconstructed pixel is often a weighted average of several true cells. A resolution audit can predict that mixing and bound its effect using independent knowledge of admissible field gradients. Consider a linear model \(y=Ax+\epsilon\) with zero-mean measurement error of covariance \(\Sigma\), and a chosen linear estimator for the target cell \(j\), \(\widehat x_j=w^Ty\). Define its resolution weights by the response to unit signals in individual true cells,

\[k^T=w^TA,\]
\[\operatorname{E}\widehat x_j=\sum_i k_i x_i,\]
\[\operatorname{var}\widehat x_j=w^T\Sigma w .\]

To reproduce a uniform field exactly, require \(\sum_i k_i=1\). The bias relative to the target is then a sum of differences from that cell. Suppose independently supported spatial smoothness gives \(|x_i-x_j|\le Ld_{ij}\), where \(d_{ij}\) is cell distance. Taking absolute values yields

\[|\operatorname{E}\widehat x_j-x_j|=\left|\sum_i k_i(x_i-x_j)\right|\le L\sum_i |k_i|d_{ij}.\]

This weighted distance has units of length even when resolution weights have negative lobes. If Gaussian measurement noise is justified, a conservative two-sided interval with at least the usual 95 percent coverage for the true cell is

\[\widehat x_j\ \pm\left[L\sum_i|k_i|d_{ij}+1.96\sqrt{w^T\Sigma w}\right].\]

The bias bound is added to the statistical margin because it represents an unknown fixed error, not an independent random draw. The guarantee depends on the supplied smoothness information and calibrated forward model. At a genuine sharp interface the assumed gradient bound may be inappropriate.

Worked example

Audit a local mean on a uniform grid

Three true cell centres lie \(0.5\,\mathrm m\) apart. An estimator of the middle temperature has weights \((0.2,0.6,0.2)\), output noise deviation \(0.10\,\mathrm K\) and independently verified gradient bound \(2.0\,\mathrm{K/m}\). Its effective weighted distance is \(0.20\,\mathrm m\) and maximal mixing bias is \(0.40\,\mathrm K\). An estimate of \(18.0\,^\circ\mathrm C\) has interval half-width \(0.40+1.96(0.10)=0.596\,\mathrm K\).

Second application

Compare a sharper reconstruction at a noisy boundary

An alternative three-cell estimator has weights \((-0.1,1.2,-0.1)\) at distances \(1,0,1\,\mathrm m\). For \(L=0.50\,\mathrm{K/m}\) its bias bound is \(0.10\,\mathrm K\). With noise deviation \(0.30\,\mathrm K\), the total half-width is \(0.688\,\mathrm K\). A smoother estimator of the same target with weighted distance \(0.50\,\mathrm m\) and noise deviation \(0.12\,\mathrm K\) gives \(0.25+0.235=0.485\,\mathrm K\). The sharper-looking estimator yields the wider interval for this stated field class.

Set a question that can fail

Two adjacent thermal zones are blurred by the sensor matrix \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.55&0.45\\0.45&0.55\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Let the observations be \(y=(21.08,20.92)\) °C with independent standard deviations 0.10 K. The engineering question is whether the first zone is at least one kelvin hotter than the second. Work in mean and difference coordinates. The mean passes unchanged, while the difference is attenuated by 0.10. Before fitting, predict that uncertainty in the contrast will dominate this decision.

Derive the data-only estimate and interval

The observed mean is 21 °C, with standard deviation \(0.10/\sqrt2=0.0707\) K. The estimated true contrast is \(\hat d=(21.08-20.92)/0.10=1.60\) K. Its standard deviation is \(\sqrt2(0.10)/0.10=1.414\) K. For known Gaussian noise, a two-sided 95% data-only interval is about \([-1.17,4.37]\) K. The inverse values \((21.8,20.2)\) exactly reproduce both readings. Nevertheless, even the sign of the contrast is uncertain at this level. The exact residual consumed both degrees of freedom and cannot test the instrument model.

Compare a prior-based inference on the same scale

Assume an independent prior for the contrast \(d\sim N(0,1\ \mathrm{K}^2)\), leaving the mean very weakly constrained. In contrast coordinates the data variance is 2 and the observed estimate is 1.60. Adding precisions gives posterior variance \(1/(1+1/2)=2/3\) K² and mean \((2/3)(1.60/2)=0.533\) K. The probability \(P(d>1\mid y)\) is \(1-\Phi[(1-0.533)/\sqrt{2/3}]\simeq0.28\). The prior changed both the centre and the width; this probability is explicitly conditional on the zero-centred prior. Repeating with a wider prior shows how much the conclusion depends on that assumption.

Buy information in the direction the decision needs

Suppose one additional test is available. Another independent repeat with the same blurred sensor reduces the data-only standard deviation of the contrast by \(\sqrt2\), to 1.0 K. A new differential sensor reading \(d+\epsilon\) with standard deviation 0.50 K and errors independent of the original instrument directly adds precision 4 K⁻² to the existing 0.5 K⁻², giving combined standard deviation \(1/\sqrt{4.5}=0.471\) K. This pre-data comparison depends on geometry and precision alone, not on an unknown future reading. Use held-back calibration objects to verify that the new sensor's stated precision and averaging model hold near an actual interface.

Communicate the final claim

Retain the observable mean and the uncertain contrast as separate reported quantities. State the noise law and model assumptions behind any interval. Where a numerical solver contributed, distinguish verified numerical precision from the statistical precision of the data. The difference is also visible in the temperature-design case: making the optimisation converge more tightly cannot restore information lost when all temperatures are close to the reference. A successful analysis may conclude that a particular observation needs redesign while still supplying an accurate prediction of another quantity.

Propagate a spatially varying calibration error

The preceding contrast calculation assumes a known blur and unbiased independent channel errors. Add two possible calibration terms: an offset \(b\) shared by both readings and a differential offset \(c\) that enters the first reading as \(+c/2\) and the second as \(-c/2\). The measurement equations then give mean reading equal to the true mean plus \(b\) and mean random error. Their difference is \(0.10d+c+e_1-e_2\). A shared offset cancels exactly from the contrast, whereas a differential offset is amplified tenfold when the contrast is reconstructed.

If the differential offset has an independently calibrated mean zero and known standard deviation \(s_c\), variance propagation gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat d)=(2s_e^2+s_c^2)/0.10^2\) for equal individual standard deviations \(s_e\). Treating the offset as random is appropriate for an ensemble of independently calibrated experiments with the stated calibration model. Within one installation it is a common unknown across all repeated exposures. If only a deterministic bound on \(c\) is justified, its effect is better reported as a bounded systematic displacement in addition to random sampling uncertainty.

Test the decision after averaging a hundred exposures

Suppose the original pair is observed one hundred independent times with the same differential calibration. The observed average difference remains 0.16 K, individual channel noise is 0.10 K per exposure, and calibration tests support \(|c|\le0.04\) K. The standard deviation of the averaged difference from independent channel noise alone is \(\sqrt{2}\,0.10/10=0.01414\) K. In physical contrast units the random standard deviation is 0.1414 K. The possible systematic error is \(0.04/0.10=0.40\) K in either direction, undiminished by the additional exposures.

A conservative one-sided 95 percent lower limit under Gaussian independent channel noise subtracts both the worst allowed positive offset and the sampling margin. In contrast units it gives \(1.60-0.40-1.645(0.1414)\approx0.967\) K. Even with one hundred exposures this calculation falls slightly below the one-kelvin engineering threshold. Omitting the differential offset would give a lower limit about 1.367 K and reverse the decision. The discrepancy originates in the calibration model, although all raw measurements and all random-noise calculations are unchanged.

What new evidence would change the result? A reference specimen known to have zero contrast estimates \(c\) directly through the observed channel difference. If calibration tightens the guaranteed bound to 0.01 K, the same calculation gives \(1.60-0.10-0.233\approx1.267\) K. If the reference itself has uncertain contrast, propagate that uncertainty through its own blur factor before claiming the tighter bound. This comparison assigns the new measurement a clear purpose: it reduces the component of uncertainty aligned with the decision, rather than collecting more observations of a systematic offset that cancels in neither channel difference nor repeated averages.

An orientation-reversal test can also distinguish true contrast from a fixed differential channel offset. Reversing a specimen of equal geometry reverses the sign of its true contrast but preserves the channel offset. Half the difference of the two measured contrasts then isolates the blurred physical contrast, provided the specimen is stable and both orientations sample comparable regions.

Design the reversal and propagate its uncertainty

Write the normal and reversed orientation mean differences as \(D_+=0.10d+c+\epsilon_+\) and \(D_-=-0.10d+c+\epsilon_-\). Solving for the two unknowns gives \(\hat d=(D_+-D_-)/0.20\) and \(\hat c=(D_++D_-)/2\). The independent information comes from changing the sign of the physical response while keeping the offset fixed. Repeating the normal orientation alone produces another row proportional to the first and cannot separate the two quantities, however many times it is repeated.

Suppose each orientation is averaged over 100 exposures with the same independent channel noise as before. The standard deviation of either orientation difference is 0.01414 K. If the two averages have independent random errors, then \(\operatorname{sd}(\hat d)=\sqrt{2}(0.01414)/0.20=0.10\) K and \(\operatorname{sd}(\hat c)=0.010\) K. With readings 0.18 K and −0.14 K, one obtains \(d=1.60\) K and \(c=0.02\) K as estimates. The one-sided lower contrast limit is \(1.60-1.645(0.10)\approx1.435\) K. Unlike the earlier bounded-offset calculation, this inference estimates the shared differential offset from the design itself.

If the specimen cools differently between orientations, or rotating it changes the sensor footprint, the two signs might no longer describe equivalent regions. A reversal carried out in alternating order across independent blocks can expose drift. Check the recovered offsets for consistency across these blocks before claiming that orientation has separated the physical contrast from an instrument artifact.

Chapter 13 · Choosing regularisation when the true answer is hidden

Derive two parameter choices from different information, then see how an excellent fit to sensor data can restore the noise in a weakly visible mode.

Each observation competes with a penalty

In orthogonal singular coordinates, let \(d_i=\sigma_i x_i+\eta_i\). For a candidate \(\lambda>0\), minimise \(\sum_i(\sigma_i x_i-d_i)^2+\lambda^2\sum_i x_i^2\). Setting each derivative to zero gives \(\widehat x_i=\sigma_i d_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)\). Predicted data are \(H_i d_i\), where \(H_i=\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)\). These factors range from zero to one. Their sum is the effective number of fitted data directions.

If an independently obtained bound for the whitened noise norm is \(\delta\), compare \(\sum_i[(1-H_i)d_i]^2\) with \(\delta^2\). The residual rises as \(\lambda\) increases. A discrepancy choice seeks the largest smoothing consistent with the stated noise level. If there is no crossing, either the search range, model or noise estimate needs examination.

A prediction criterion without a noise bound

For a linear smoother \(\widehat d=Hd\), deleting observation \(i\) gives the leave-one-out residual \(r_i/(1-H_{ii})\) under the usual fixed-design ridge fit. Generalised cross-validation replaces the individual leverages by their mean. This produces the computable quantity

\[\mathrm{GCV}(\lambda)=\frac{\|d-\widehat d\|^2/n}{[1-\operatorname{trace}(H)/n]^2}.\]

The denominator penalises fits that spend nearly one degree of freedom per datum. The criterion estimates predictive performance under assumptions on noise and design. A selected value need not minimise the error in the unknown parameter, and highly unequal leverages can defeat the approximation.

A sensor that barely sees a mode

Take sensitivities \(1,0.01\), true parameters \((1,1)\) and data \((1,0.02)\). Unregularised inversion returns \((1,2)\) with zero residual. A perturbation of one hundredth in the second observation doubled the second parameter. With \(\lambda=0.01\) the second gain is \(\sigma/(\sigma^2+\lambda^2)=50\), so the same datum yields 1. This exact agreement with the hidden truth is accidental.

For \(\lambda=0.1\), the second estimate drops to about 0.0198. A weak direction has been almost removed. Meanwhile the first estimate remains about 0.9901. A single residual norm does not tell you which parameter direction was suppressed.

Inspect an entire regularisation path

Three synthetic sensors have sensitivities \(1,0.2,0.02\), true parameters all equal to one, and an adjustable perturbation only in the last sensor. This deliberately uneven noise makes it possible to test when the GCV assumptions are plausible. Inspect parameter error and data residual over a logarithmic range of \(\lambda\). The result reports the best GCV value on the sampled grid and the residual at that choice.

Chapter 14 · Learning a drifting temperature from a sequence of readings

Derive the scalar Kalman update by completing the square and see how process uncertainty controls memory of the past.

Propagate a distribution through time

A drifting offset follows \(x_k=x_{k-1}+w_k\). A thermometer reports \(z_k=x_k+v_k\). Assume independent, zero-mean Gaussian process increments of variance \(Q\) and sensor noise of variance \(R\), independent at each time and of the initial state. Before the next reading, the mean stays at \(m^-=m_{k-1}\) and the variance adds, \(P^-=P_{k-1}+Q\).

The negative log posterior, up to constants, is \((x-m^-)^2/(2P^-)+(z_k-x)^2/(2R)\). Collect the coefficient of \(x^2\) and its linear term. The result can be written in two equivalent forms:

\[P_k=(1/P^-+1/R)^{-1},\]
\[m_k=m^-+K(z_k-m^-),\]
\[K=P^-/(P^-+R).\]

Thus the fraction of the innovation used is a model prediction. When sensors are noisy the gain falls. When the model permits rapid drift, uncertainty grows between readings, giving the new measurement a larger role. These are variances, so a stated standard deviation must be squared first.

A reading with units and a repeated reading

A prior says \(20.0\pm2.0\) °C (one standard deviation), with negligible intervening drift. A sensor of standard deviation 1 °C reads 23 °C. The gain is \(4/(4+1)=0.8\), giving \(22.4\) °C and variance \(0.8\) °C². A second independent reading of 23 °C gives gain \(0.8/1.8\) and mean \(22.6667\) °C. Treating a duplicate record as independent would falsely claim the same information gain.

In a long random walk with \(Q=0.25\) and \(R=1\) in squared units, a steady posterior variance satisfies \(P=(P+Q)R/(P+Q+R)\). Rearrangement gives \(P^2+QP-QR=0\), so the physically positive root is about 0.3904. New uncertainty replenishes the information squeezed out by measurement.

For a reproducible illustration, a fixed synthetic thermometer trace contains a smooth periodic disturbance and a step in the hidden temperature. It is deliberately held fixed while the filter's assumed \(Q\) and \(R\) change. Compare the lag just after the step with the wiggle once the temperature settles.

Chapter 15 · When a single uncertainty interval hides two explanations

Start from a squared-amplitude measurement, derive the full posterior and design a second observation capable of identifying the sign.

Symmetry is an identifiability statement

A sensor measures \(d=x^2+\eta\) with Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(s\). A symmetric Gaussian prior of variance \(\tau^2\) puts equal weight on opposite signs. Bayes' rule gives the density

\[p(x\mid d)\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{(d-x^2)^2}{2s^2}-\frac{x^2}{2\tau^2}\right].\]

The exponent is even, so the posterior mean is exactly zero whenever the integral exists. That mean may make an extremely poor prediction of the observed quantity. To locate modes, differentiate the negative log density to get \(x[2(x^2-d)/s^2+1/\tau^2]\). Two off-centre modes appear precisely when \(d>s^2/(2\tau^2)\). At smaller \(d\), the prior and noise merge the explanations into one central mode.

Numbers and competing summaries

Set \(d=1\), \(s=0.2\), and \(\tau=2\). The modes are at \(\pm\sqrt{0.995}\), close to \(\pm0.9975\). The density ratio of \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) is \(\exp[-1/(2s^2)+1/(2\tau^2)]\), about \(4.2\times10^{-6}\). The reported mean of zero sits in a very low-density region.

For \(d=-0.05\), there is no real parameter that exactly fits the measured amplitude squared. A noisy sensor can still produce this value. The distribution has a single mode at zero, and there is no justification for taking the square root of the negative datum or discarding the observation.

Buy a measurement that identifies the sign

Assume an independent second sensor reports \(b=x+\epsilon\) with known standard deviation 0.5. Add \(-(b-x)^2/(2\cdot0.5^2)\) to the log density. The original two signs now receive different weights. Both curves in the plot are independently normalised by quadrature on the displayed interval; the sign probability is conditional on that interval. Compare the observation \(b=0\) with \(b=1\).

Chapter 16 · How model error masquerades as a fitted physical parameter

Project an omitted spatial effect into the fitted parameter space. Work out when residual checks reveal the omission and when they are silent.

A misspecified instrument

Suppose \(d=A x_*+b+\eta\), with zero-mean measurement noise and a deterministic but omitted contribution \(b\). Assume \(A\) has independent columns. Least squares gives \(\widehat x=(A^TA)^{-1}A^Td\). Taking expectations leaves the parameter bias \(A^+b\). The fitted residual is \((I-AA^+)(b+\eta)\). Thus the same model error separates into a component that biases the parameters and an orthogonal component that remains visible to residual checks.

For a single sensitivity column \(a\), decompose \(b=b_\parallel+b_\perp\), where \(b_\parallel=a(a^Tb)/(a^Ta)\). Repeating the measurement reduces independent random error; the deterministic projection remains. Error bars computed solely from measurement variance can become more misleading as more readings are acquired.

Calibration with an omitted offset

Fit \(d_i=m t_i\) at \(t=(1,2,3)\) to observations actually generated by \(d_i=2t_i+0.3\). The unmodelled constant has nonzero projection onto \(t\). The bias is \(0.3(1+2+3)/(1+4+9)=0.12857\), giving fitted slope 2.12857. The residuals at the three points are approximately 0.17143, 0.04286 and −0.08571. Their signed trend suggests a missing intercept. At \(t=6\) the model predicts 12.7714 while the actual law gives 12.3; an apparently modest training residual has grown into a substantial extrapolation error.

Now use a discrepancy exactly proportional to time, \(b_i=0.1t_i\). The fitted slope becomes 2.1 with zero residual at every training point. Additional times under the same model cannot separate the true slope from this contribution. A separate calibration that suppresses the extra physical effect can.

Rotate the missing physics

In a two-reading instrument, the model sensitivity is the unit horizontal vector. Vary the magnitude and direction of the omitted contribution. Observe parameter bias and visible residual separately, and locate cases with the same discrepancy norm but completely different fit diagnoses.

Chapter 17 · Predicting a new measurement when the fitted parameters are correlated

Propagate the full posterior covariance to a quantity of interest, distinguish latent and measured uncertainty and select the observation that reduces the uncertainty of the actual prediction.

One model supplies many possible targets

For parameters \(\theta\) with posterior mean \(m\) and covariance \(C\), a linear target \(s=h^T\theta\) has mean \(h^Tm\). Subtract the mean and expand the quadratic product to obtain \(\operatorname{Var}(s)=h^TCh\). An independent sensor with variance \(R\) changes the variance of a future measured value to \(h^TCh+R\). Repeated fits of noisy datasets and repeated physical measurements are distinct sources of variation.

A candidate new observation \(z=a^T\theta+\epsilon\) with variance \(R_a\) has covariance \(h^TCa\) with the target. For a Gaussian posterior the conditional covariance update is \(C_+=C-Caa^TC/(a^TCa+R_a)\), found either by completing the square or projecting out the optimal multiple of the innovation. The target variance decreases by

\[\Delta\operatorname{Var}(s)=\frac{(h^TCa)^2}{a^TCa+R_a}.\]

A precise sum and an uncertain difference

Let \(C\) have diagonal entries 1 and correlation \(-0.9\). A target \(h=(1,1)\) has variance \(2-1.8=0.2\). The difference \(h=(1,-1)\) has variance 3.8. Ignoring correlations would predict 2 for both. With sensor variance 0.25, a newly measured sum has variance 0.45 although the latent sum variance remains 0.2.

For the target sum, measuring the first coordinate with variance 0.25 reduces variance by \((1-0.9)^2/(1+0.25)=0.008\). Measuring the sum itself at the same variance reduces it by \(0.2^2/(0.2+0.25)=0.0889\). These two sensors have differently normalised sensitivity vectors. Their stated noise levels refer to their own output units and must be calibrated before a cost comparison.

Choose the measurement direction

For a fairer geometric comparison, restrict both measurement and prediction directions to unit length. Fix the target along the normalised sum. Sweep candidate observation angles and read the remaining prediction variance. Change the parameter correlation: when does a coordinate sensor improve the target appreciably?

Applied study

Infer parameters from noisy observations and use sensitivity to design a more informative experiment.

From predictions to parameter estimates

A forward problem takes material properties and predicts a response. In an inverse problem the responses have been observed and one or more material properties are unknown. Suppose a model predicts \(g_i(p)\) at observation \(i\), where \(p\) is a vector of parameters. Write \(y_i\) for the measured value and \(s_i>0\) for its estimated standard deviation. With independent Gaussian measurement errors of known variance, the maximum-likelihood fit minimises

\[\chi^2(p)=\sum_i r_i(p)^2,\qquad r_i(p)=\frac{g_i(p)-y_i}{s_i}.\]

The weights put observations with different units or precision on a comparable scale. A small residual says that the chosen parameter fits the observations. To know whether it is the only plausible value, examine how the predictions change as the parameter moves.

Fit a spring and inspect the residuals

A spring in its elastic range has force \(F=kx\). For equally precise force measurements at accurately known extensions, minimising the sum of squares gives \(\sum_i x_i(kx_i-F_i)=0\), hence \(\hat k=\sum_i x_i F_i/\sum_i x_i^2\). At extensions of 1, 2 and 3 mm the measured forces are 1.1, 1.9 and 3.0 N. The fitted stiffness is \(13.9/14=0.9929\) N/mm. Plotting the signed residuals against extension can reveal curvature or a preload that this model omits.

If an unknown preload \(b\) is allowed, fit \(F=b+kx\). The columns of the design matrix are then \(1\) and \(x_i\). Measurements at a single extension can determine \(b+kx\) but cannot distinguish \(k\) from \(b\), however many times the measurement is repeated. Multiple extensions add independent information. For a general linear model a QR or singular-value decomposition solves this least-squares problem without squaring the condition number by explicitly forming the normal equations.

Design a measurement for a nonlinear model

For a nonlinear response, approximate the residual near a trial point by \(r(p+\Delta p)\simeq r(p)+J\Delta p\), with \(J_{ij}=\partial r_i/\partial p_j\). A Gauss–Newton step minimises the norm of this linear expression. A damped step can also penalise the size of \(\Delta p\) when the local linear model gives a poor prediction. Small singular values of the appropriately scaled Jacobian identify parameter combinations that the data barely constrain.

Consider first-order decay \(c(t,T)=c_0e^{-k(T)t}\) with an unknown reference rate \(k_r\) and activation energy \(E\). Write \(k(T)=k_r\exp[-(E/R)(1/T-1/T_r)]\). At \(T=T_r\) the factor multiplying \(E\) vanishes. A complete decay curve at that temperature can estimate \(k_r\), but carries no information about \(E\). Taking measurements on either side of \(T_r\) changes that sensitivity. For a locally linear, full-rank, well-scaled problem with unit-variance normalised errors, \((J^\mathsf{T}J)^{-1}\) approximates parameter covariance. The diagonal estimates uncertainty under the assumed model and noise law.
