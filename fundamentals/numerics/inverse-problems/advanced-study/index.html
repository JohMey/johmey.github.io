Green functions, the Poisson kernel and what a small electrode can influence

Electrostatic field theory and measurement

A Green function is the response of an entire boundary-value problem to a unit source. Once that response is known, a continuous charge distribution and a patterned boundary voltage can both be treated by integration. The two integrations require different kernels.

Define a point response with its boundary conditions

Let \(\Omega\) be a vacuum domain with boundary on which the voltage is given. Use the convention \(-\nabla_{\mathbf y}^2G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\delta^3(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\) and \(G=0\) on the boundary as a function of \(\mathbf y\). In free space \(G=1/(4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|)\) if decay is required at infinity. This definition places no permittivity in \(G\); physical charge enters as \(\rho/\epsilon_0\). A different frequent convention puts \(4\pi\) in the defining equation, changing all subsequent prefactors. Comparing the near-source flux to unity is a straightforward way to fix which convention is being used.

Begin with two differentiable functions \(u,v\). The difference \(\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v-v\nabla u)=u\nabla^2v-v\nabla^2u\) has no mixed-gradient term. Integrate this identity with \(u=V(\mathbf y)\),

\[v=G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y).\]

The source equation for \(G\) samples \(V\) at \(\mathbf x\), while Poisson's equation for \(V\) supplies a volume density. With the outward normal taken from \(\Omega\),

\[V(\mathbf x)=\int_\Omega G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\frac{\rho(\mathbf y)}{\epsilon_0}\,d^3y-\oint_{\partial\Omega}V(\mathbf y)\partial_{n_{\mathbf y}}G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,dA_{\mathbf y}.\]

The \(G\partial_n V\) boundary term vanishes because \(G=0\) there. If the singularity causes concern, exclude a small sphere about \(\mathbf x\), apply the ordinary identity, and shrink the sphere. The inward normal on the excised sphere produces the same \(V(\mathbf x)\) term. The representation separates charges in the volume from voltages maintained by electrode sources; it does not combine them into one fictitious material distribution.

Solve the half-space and differentiate with respect to source position

Consider \(\mathbf x=(\mathbf X,h)\) with \(h>0\) above the plane \(z=0\). For an interior source \(\mathbf y=(\mathbf Y,z)\), denote its reflection by \(\mathbf y^*=(\mathbf Y,-z)\). The grounded half-space Green function is

\[G=\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{|\mathbf X-\mathbf Y|^2+(h-z)^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{|\mathbf X-\mathbf Y|^2+(h+z)^2}}\right].\]

It vanishes as the source coordinate approaches the plane. The outward domain normal there is \(-\hat{\mathbf z}\); therefore the coefficient of a prescribed boundary voltage is

\[P_h=-\partial_nG=\partial_zG|_{z=0}.\]

Differentiating the two terms gives equal positive contributions:

\[P_h(\mathbf X-\mathbf Y)=\frac{h}{2\pi[|\mathbf X-\mathbf Y|^2+h^2]^{3/2}} .\]

Its units are inverse area, while \(G\) has inverse length in this three-dimensional convention. For no interior charge \(V(\mathbf X,h)=\int P_h(\mathbf X-\mathbf Y)f(\mathbf Y)\,d^2Y\). The kernel is positive. Integrate over the whole plane using \(2\pi s\,ds\); the result is one. Hence a constant boundary voltage is reproduced, and an arbitrary voltage bounded between two numbers produces values between the same bounds inside. The integral solves the bounded half-space problem with the far-field condition appropriate to the data. Demanding decay for a nonzero constant boundary value would be inconsistent.

A detector at height \(h\) takes a weighted average over a neighbourhood of width comparable with \(h\). The kernel has algebraic tails, so its support is not a sharp circular footprint. The contribution from radii smaller than \(b\) is \(1-h/\sqrt{b^2+h^2}\). Half the total weight lies within \(b=\sqrt3h\). Ninety per cent requires

\[b=\sqrt{99}h\simeq9.95h.\]

These quite different radii show why a full-width-at-half-maximum measurement of the kernel cannot by itself define the region that contains most of its influence.

A finite driven electrode and a reciprocity experiment

Fix a circular disk of radius \(b\) in the plane at voltage \(V_d\) while grounding the rest of the plane, with ideal thin insulating seams. Directly over its centre,

\[V(0,h)=V_d\left(1-\frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2+b^2}}\right),\]
\[E_z(0,h)=V_d\frac{b^2}{(h^2+b^2)^{3/2}} .\]

At \(h\to0^+\) the electrode voltage is recovered at its centre. For \(h\gg b\) the voltage scales as \(V_d b^2/(2h^2)\). A small electrode surrounded by ground does not behave at long distance as an isolated charged electrode with a \(1/h\) potential. Its surrounding return charge makes a combined disturbance whose far field has a different order. At the sharp ideal edge the boundary voltage jumps, and local fields there require an actual seam geometry if one wants a bounded peak or a breakdown prediction.

For \(b=5.0\) mm, \(V_d=4.0\) V and \(h=5.0\) mm the axial voltage is 1.17 V and field is \(283\) V/m. A separate experiment grounds every electrode and places a point charge \(q\) at that observation point. Green reciprocity relates the net charge on the disk to the previous weighting potential \(w=V/V_d\): \(Q_d=-q\,w\). With \(q=20\) pC the disk collects \(-5.86\) pC. Direct integration of the image-plane surface charge \(\sigma(s)=-qh/[2\pi(s^2+h^2)^{3/2}]\) gives the same result. It is precisely \(-qP_h\). Thus the boundary kernel has both a voltage-producing interpretation and a charge-collection interpretation measurable with different instrumentation.

Recover the kernel of an electrode strip

A boundary pattern invariant in \(y\) should not require a two-dimensional source integral. Integrate \(P_h\) over the unused coordinate. With lateral separation \(s=X-X'\), the result is

\[p_h(s)=h/[\pi(s^2+h^2)].\]

It integrates to one over the real line. A strip between \(-b,b\) at \(V_d\) gives on its central normal \(V(0,h)=2V_d\arctan(b/h)/\pi\). At \(h=b\) the value is half the applied voltage, distinct from the circular electrode's \(1-1/\sqrt2\). The strip extends in one more direction and occupies a larger angular range of boundary influence.

For sinusoidal boundary data \(f(X)=\cos kX\) there is an independent way to determine the result without evaluating a difficult oscillatory integral. Translational symmetry and linearity preserve the sinusoid. Solving Laplace's equation with vertical factor \(Z(h)\) gives

\[Z''=k^2Z.\]

The bounded decaying solution with unit boundary amplitude is \(e^{-|k|h}\). Thus the Fourier transform of the strip kernel is \(e^{-|k|h}\). Convolving twice with heights \(h_1,h_2\) is equivalent to convolution once with height \(h_1+h_2\). This composition property checks a numerical propagation code without requiring separate analytic solutions for many different boundary patterns.

Which symmetries does an influence table possess?

Exchange source and detector in the defining equations for \(G(\mathbf x,\cdot)\) and \(G(\mathbf z,\cdot)\). Their difference identity gives \(G(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)-G(\mathbf z,\mathbf x)=0\), since both vanish on the same boundary. Symmetry extends to scalar or symmetric-tensor positive permittivity when the operator is defined as \(-\nabla\cdot\epsilon\nabla\). It refers to exactly the same material, boundary preparation and domain in both experiments. Replacing one experiment's grounded conductor by a floating conductor changes the Green function.

On a nonuniform mesh it matters whether influence entries correspond to equal point charges or equal charge densities in unequal cells. If a finite-volume solver stores sources as densities, a column's total injected charge scales with its cell volume. An unweighted table of sampled voltages can look nonsymmetric even when the discrete physics is reciprocal. Form the matrix with unit total source and consistent detector averaging, or retain the correct volume-weighted inner product. Test positivity for positive sources as a second check. Symmetric influence entries by themselves cannot expose a common incorrect normalisation or a misplaced outer boundary.

A kernel that reproduces every harmonic around a circle

In a two-dimensional circular domain of radius \(a\), prescribe a voltage \(f(\phi')\) around the wall. For a detector at \((r,\phi)\) let \(t=r/a<1\). The angular Fourier component with order \(m\) is multiplied by \(t^{|m|}\) on continuation into the disk. This follows directly from the regular radial power solution of Laplace's equation. Substitute the Fourier coefficient integral into the series and interchange the integral and the absolutely convergent series at fixed \(t<1\). The boundary response per unit angle is then

\[P_t(\delta)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}t^{|m|}e^{im\delta}=\frac{1-t^2}{2\pi(1-2t\cos\delta+t^2)},\]
\[\delta=\phi-\phi' .\]

To obtain the rational form, separate the \(m=0\) term and sum the two conjugate geometric series \(te^{\pm i\delta}/(1-te^{\pm i\delta})\). The result is positive for every interior point. Integrating it over angle leaves only the constant Fourier mode, so it is normalised to unity. At the centre the kernel is simply \(1/(2\pi)\), recovering the exact mean-value theorem for harmonic functions. Near the wall it becomes very sharply concentrated around the detector's azimuth while retaining a small nonlocal tail. Using prescribed boundary data as a flat average at every interior point would reproduce only the centre reading.

For a concrete experiment put a short arc of half-angle \(\alpha\) centred on \(\phi=0\) at 1 V and ground the remaining circular boundary. Along \(\phi=0\) the potential equals the integral of \(P_t\) between \(-\alpha,\alpha\). With \(u=\tan(\phi'/2)\) one has \(\cos\phi'=(1-u^2)/(1+u^2)\), \(d\phi'=2du/(1+u^2)\). Substitution gives the weight

\[w(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\!\left[\frac{1+t}{1-t}\tan(\alpha/2)\right] .\]

At \(t=0\) it gives \(\alpha/\pi\), exactly the fraction of the circumference occupied by the arc. For a 60°-wide arc \(\alpha=\pi/6\), hence the centre reads \(1/6\) V. At \(t=0.6\) on the centreline it reads about 0.522 V. Placing a small positive line charge there while grounding the whole cylindrical wall induces \(-0.522\) times the line charge on that arc by the same reciprocity argument used on the flat disk. The angular share depends on position inside the circle even though every point on the conductor has the same actual grounded voltage.

An independent check takes the limit of a very large circle with the probe only a distance \(h=a-r\) from the nearest boundary point. For small angular displacement \(\delta=s/a\), \(1-2t\cos\delta+t^2\simeq(h/a)^2+(s/a)^2\) and \(1-t^2\simeq2h/a\). The angular probability element \(P_t(\delta)d\delta\) therefore tends to \(h\,ds/[\pi(h^2+s^2)]\), precisely the translation-invariant strip kernel obtained by integrating the half-space formula over one coordinate. Curvature corrections can be estimated by increasing \(a/h\) at fixed physical arc width and comparing both predictions.

Why an insulated Green function needs a zero-mode subtraction

Consider the simpler interval \(0<x<L\) with prescribed zero flux at both ends. We would like a point response satisfying \(-G''=\delta(x-s)\), \(G'(0)=G'(L)=0\). Integrate this proposed equation over the interval: the left-hand side is \(-G'(L)+G'(0)=0\), whereas the right-hand side is 1. The attempted kernel violates compatibility. Physically a sealed insulating region cannot take a net free test charge without changing its prescribed outer flux. Adding an arbitrary constant to \(G\) cannot fix this contradiction.

A useful alternative removes the constant source mode and fixes the remaining voltage reference. Define \(-G_N''=\delta(x-s)-1/L\), \(G_N'(0)=G_N'(L)=0\), and \(\int_0^L G_N(x,s)\,dx=0\). Away from the point the curvature is \(+1/L\). The left derivative must be \(x/L\) to vanish at zero, the right derivative \(x/L-1\) to vanish at \(L\). They jump by \(-1\) at \(x=s\). Integrating, imposing continuity and subtracting the mean gives the compact symmetric expression

\[G_N(x,s)=\frac{x^2+s^2}{2L}-\max(x,s)+\frac L3 .\]

Differentiating verifies both end conditions and the jump. Integrating the quadratic part and the piecewise maximum over \(x\) verifies the mean condition. Its sign can be negative because the response includes an opposite uniform return source and uses a zero-mean reference. Unlike the positive grounded Dirichlet kernel, it is not a positive voltage weight to use for a maximum-principle test.

Apply a neutral pair of sheets \(+\sigma\) at \(s_1=0.2L\) and \(-\sigma\) at \(s_2=0.7L\). Their voltage is \(\sigma[G_N(x,s_1)-G_N(x,s_2)]/\epsilon\). The two artificial uniform return sources cancel exactly. To the left of both sheets the field vanishes, between them it equals \(+\sigma/\epsilon\), and to the right it vanishes again. The potential drops by \(0.5L\sigma/\epsilon\) across the two sheets. This matches direct Gauss-law jumps, and the zero-mean potential fixes the constant that Gauss's law could not determine. For more complex closed domains numerical solvers implement the same idea by projecting out the constant eigenvector and checking the compatible total charge before inverting. An arbitrary tiny diagonal regularisation of a singular matrix can hide a physically impossible net-source input.

Construct a point response when the medium itself screens charge

A linear ionic or phenomenological screening model between two grounded parallel electrodes leads in one dimension to \([-\partial_x^2+\kappa^2]V=\rho_{\rm imposed}/\epsilon\), \(0<x<L\). Here \(\rho_{\rm imposed}\) excludes the mobile linear response already represented by \(\kappa^2V\). We seek \(G_\kappa(x,s)\) with zero voltage at both ends. Away from its unit source the equation has hyperbolic solutions. Use \(A\sinh(\kappa x)\) on the left to vanish at zero and \(B\sinh[\kappa(L-x)]\) on the right to vanish at \(L\). Continuity at \(x=s\) removes one constant. Integrating across the source gives \(G'_\kappa(s^+)-G'_\kappa(s^-)=-1\); the finite mass term integrates to zero in a shrinking source interval.

To solve transparently use the functions \(u(x)=\sinh(\kappa x)\),

\[v(x)=\sinh[\kappa(L-x)].\]

Their Wronskian \(u'v-uv'\) is \(\kappa\sinh(\kappa L)\), independent of \(x\). Then continuity, the derivative jump, and symmetry under \(s\leftrightarrow x\) are manifest in

\[G_\kappa(x,s)=\frac{\sinh(\kappa x_<)\sinh[\kappa(L-x_>)]}{\kappa\sinh(\kappa L)},\]
\[x_<=\min(x,s),\]
\[x_>=\max(x,s).\]

For \(\kappa\to0\), expand each hyperbolic sine to first order. The result is \(x_<(L-x_>)/L\), the grounded unscreened response. When both observation and source lie many screening lengths from every boundary, the kernel tends to \(e^{-\kappa|x-s|}/(2\kappa)\), the infinite-line screened solution. Thus one formula contains both geometric screening by grounded conductors and material screening by mobile charges.

At the midpoint source and detector coincide. The finite kernel reduces to \(\tanh(\kappa L/2)/(2\kappa)\). Compared with the unscreened midpoint value \(L/4\), its ratio is \(2\tanh(\kappa L/2)/(\kappa L)\). At \(\kappa L=2\) a fixed inserted sheet therefore creates only 76.2% of its unscreened midpoint voltage. A naïve replacement of plate separation by the screening length would give a constant ratio and miss the smooth small-\(\kappa\) crossover. For long gaps the measured voltage saturates at fixed sheet charge, because the countercharge response near the sheet now sets the local scale.

Check the convolution using a uniform positive imposed charge density \(\rho_0\). Without integrating the kernel directly, seek a constant particular solution plus a symmetric homogeneous correction. The result is

\[V(x)=[\rho_0/(\epsilon\kappa^2)]\{1-\cosh[\kappa(x-L/2)]/\cosh(\kappa L/2)\}.\]

Twice differentiation reconstructs the source and zero endpoints. In the small-\(\kappa L\) limit it tends to \(\rho_0x(L-x)/(2\epsilon)\). A numerical integration of the point response against a uniform source should match this independent closed solution throughout the interval, including the near-electrode regions. It is a stronger test than checking the point kernel at the centre alone.

Integrate the original equation across the gap. The sum of outward displacement fluxes equals imposed charge minus \(\epsilon\kappa^2\int V\,dx\), the magnitude of the compensating mobile charge. For the uniform source the fraction of imposed charge balanced by electrode rather than mobile charge is \(2\tanh(\kappa L/2)/(\kappa L)\). For a long gap this fraction falls like twice the screening length divided by gap width, because only the two boundary regions communicate appreciably with the electrodes. A current meter attached to the electrodes and a chemical measurement of accumulated mobile ions can therefore close the same source budget independently.

Design a differential collecting electrode from its weighting functions

Two concentric electrodes supply one signed position signal

A grounded plane is divided into a central disk of radius \(a\), a surrounding annulus from \(a\) to \(2a\), and the remainder. A small charge moves along the central normal. Two charge amplifiers measure the disk and annulus. Construct an output that vanishes at \(h=a\), then find its local height sensitivity. Why can that output alone not distinguish uncertain charge from height away from the null?

Derive and check the result

Let \(w_a=1-h/\sqrt{h^2+a^2}\) and \(w_{2a}=1-h/\sqrt{h^2+4a^2}\). The disk reads \(Q_d=-qw_a\); the annulus reads \(Q_r=-q(w_{2a}-w_a)\). An output \(S=Q_d-\gamma Q_r\) vanishes at \(h=a\) if \(\gamma=(1-1/\sqrt2)/(1/\sqrt2-1/\sqrt5)=1.127\) approximately. Both readings are negative for positive \(q\), so it is their difference, not their sum, that rejects the reference position. The ideal zero does not depend on the magnitude of any nonzero \(q\).

Differentiate with respect to height using \(w_b'=-b^2/(h^2+b^2)^{3/2}\). At the null \(a\,w_a'=-1/(2\sqrt2)\) and \(a\,w_{2a}'=-4/(5\sqrt5)\). Hence

\[a\,d(S/q)/dh=-(1+\gamma)a w_a'+\gamma a w_{2a}'\simeq0.349.\]

For \(q=10\) pC, \(a=2.0\) mm, a small upward displacement of 0.10 mm produces approximately \(+0.175\) pC. Finite differences of the full two-disk formula at heights on both sides of \(a\) check the linear slope. For displacements that are no longer small compared with \(a\), use the full expression rather than extrapolating that single sensitivity.

An additional sum channel \(Q_d+Q_r=-q w_{2a}\) permits a dimensionless output \(S/(Q_d+Q_r)\) that cancels the magnitude and sign of \(q\) while retaining height dependence. This ratio becomes noise sensitive far from the electrodes, where collected charge is small. A null by itself can also mean that there is no charge at all, so a usable position detector checks the sum channel at the same time. The exercise combines electrostatic reciprocity, electronic weighting and a finite signal-to-noise constraint while requiring only two precomputed boundary responses.

How much charge belongs to an annular collector?

An annular electrode with inner radius \(h\) and outer radius \(3h\) lies in a grounded plane. A charge \(q\) sits at height \(h\) over its centre. Derive its induced charge. If a numerical integration omits all radii beyond \(3h\), where does the missing charge go?

Work through the solution

Subtract two circular weights. The annulus receives \(Q/q=-[1/\sqrt2-1/\sqrt{10}]=-0.3909\). The central disk receives \(-(1-1/\sqrt2)q=-0.2929q\). The whole plane should sum to \(-q\), so the part beyond the outer edge supplies

\[-q/\sqrt{10}=-0.3162q.\]

Nothing has been lost by the physical system; a finite integration patch excludes a considerable piece of the long-tailed kernel. Radial extrapolation with the analytic far tail or an expanding integration domain distinguishes missing area from poor quadrature within the patch.

Use resistance to a modelling mistake

A student proposes \(G\) itself as the weight for setting electrode voltages on the plane. Another replaces it by \(2P_h\), arguing that an image doubles the response. Predict two different tests that diagnose these errors.

Work through the solution

The first proposal gives zero contribution since the Dirichlet Green function is zero at the boundary. It confuses volume source response with a boundary normal derivative. The second produces twice the prescribed constant voltage because the kernel would integrate to two. Independent electrostatic reciprocity would assign \(-2q\) to the whole grounded plane around a charge \(q\), violating the charge sum derived from the real and image fields. For a numerical test, set successively a constant, a sinusoid, and a bounded disk on the same plane. Those cases examine normalisation, spectral attenuation, and spatial tails separately.

Normalised spherical harmonics and the field of an arbitrary three-dimensional source

Electrostatic field theory and measurement

An axial voltage scan can determine Legendre coefficients only if the source is rotationally symmetric. A general spherical electrode or molecule needs angular modes with two indices. Their normalisation fixes the conversion from a measured multipole coefficient to a physical voltage.

Separate three coordinates without imposing axial symmetry

In spherical coordinates the source-free Laplace equation is \(r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_r V)+r^{-2}\Delta_\Omega V=0\), where \(\Delta_\Omega=(\sin\theta)^{-1}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta)+(\sin^2\theta)^{-1}\partial_\phi^2\). Assume \(V=R(r)Y(\theta,\phi)\). Separation requires

\[-\Delta_\Omega Y=\lambda Y.\]

Multiply by \(Y^*\) and integrate over the unit sphere. Integration by parts has no actual surface boundary for globally smooth fields and gives

\[\lambda\int |Y|^2d\Omega=\int|\nabla_\Omega Y|^2d\Omega\ge0.\]

The constant mode has eigenvalue zero.

Separate azimuth as \(e^{im\phi}\). Continuity after a full rotation forces integer \(m\). With \(u=\cos\theta\), the remaining equation becomes

\[[(1-u^2)P']'+[\lambda-m^2/(1-u^2)]P=0.\]

Requiring regular behaviour at both poles yields \(\lambda=\ell(\ell+1)\) with integers \(\ell\ge|m|\). The corresponding angular functions are the associated Legendre functions. For each \(\ell\) there are \(2\ell+1\) independent \(m\)'s, all with identical radial behaviour. A rotation mixes coefficients within this subspace but cannot convert a dipole into a quadrupole.

Fix one convention and verify its first modes

Choose \(P_\ell(1)=1\) and for \(m\ge0\) include the Condon–Shortley phase in \(P_\ell^m(u)=(-1)^m(1-u^2)^{m/2}d^mP_\ell/du^m\). Define

\[Y_{\ell m}=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}\,P_\ell^m(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},\]
\[Y_{\ell,-m}=(-1)^mY_{\ell m}^* .\]

With this convention \(\int Y_{\ell m}^*Y_{\ell'm'}\,d\Omega=\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}\), using \(d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\). In particular \(Y_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi}\),

\[Y_{10}=\sqrt{3/(4\pi)}\cos\theta,\]
\[Y_{11}=-\sqrt{3/(8\pi)}\sin\theta e^{i\phi}.\]

To check the dipole normalisation independently,

\[\int \cos^2\theta\,d\Omega=4\pi/3.\]

For \(Y_{11}\),

\[\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3.\]

The complex basis is an accounting device. A real potential obeys \(v_{\ell,-m}=(-1)^m v_{\ell m}^*\), leaving \(2\ell+1\) real angular degrees of freedom at each order.

On a sphere of radius \(a\) suppose segmented electrodes prescribe \(f(\theta,\phi)\). Its coefficients are \(v_{\ell m}=\int Y_{\ell m}^* f\,d\Omega\). These have units of volts because the angular functions and solid angle are dimensionless. The radial equation \((r^2 R')'-\ell(\ell+1)R=0\) has solutions \(r^\ell,r^{-\ell-1}\). For an empty interior, regularity selects \(V_{\rm in}=\sum v_{\ell m}(r/a)^\ell Y_{\ell m}\). For empty exterior decaying at infinity,

\[V_{\rm out}=\sum v_{\ell m}(a/r)^{\ell+1}Y_{\ell m}.\]

Nonuniform prescribed surface voltage requires electrically distinct electrode patches or a specified ideal boundary; one continuous metal conductor could not sustain that static variation.

Represent a tilted dipole in the laboratory frame

Impose \(f=V_0\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}\) with \(\hat{\mathbf p}=(\sin\alpha,0,\cos\alpha)\). In the rotated frame with polar axis along \(\hat{\mathbf p}\) this is a pure \(m=0,\ell=1\) pattern. In the laboratory it is \(V_0[\cos\alpha\cos\theta+\sin\alpha\sin\theta\cos\phi]\). Using the explicit harmonics,

\[v_{10}=V_0\sqrt{4\pi/3}\cos\alpha,\]
\[v_{11}=-V_0\sqrt{2\pi/3}\sin\alpha,\]
\[v_{1,-1}=+V_0\sqrt{2\pi/3}\sin\alpha .\]

The sum of their squared magnitudes is \(4\pi V_0^2/3\), independent of tilt. The interior solution simplifies to \(V_0\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf r/a\), giving uniform \(\mathbf E=-V_0\hat{\mathbf p}/a\). At \(a=40\) mm, \(V_0=2.0\) V and \(\alpha=30^\circ\), \(E_x=-25.0\) V/m, \(E_z=-43.3\) V/m. An axis-only fit would infer the correct axial component while losing the transverse one. A full vector field measurement at one interior point distinguishes the rotated dipole from a weaker aligned one.

Tie angular modes to an actual charge distribution

The addition theorem expresses \(P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf s})=[4\pi/(2\ell+1)]\sum_mY_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf r})Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat{\mathbf s})\). Rotational invariance explains its structure: the sum is unchanged by simultaneous rotation of both directions, so it depends only on their angle. Put \(\hat{\mathbf s}\) at the north pole, where only \(m=0\) survives, and the proportionality constant follows from \(Y_{\ell0}\) and \(P_\ell(1)=1\). Checking \(\ell=1\) recovers the dot product of the two unit vectors.

Use the generating series \(1/|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|=\sum_{\ell\ge0}(r_<^\ell/r_>^{\ell+1})P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf s})\). For an observation radius outside every source point, define \(q_{\ell m}=\int\rho(\mathbf s)s^\ell Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat{\mathbf s})\,d^3s\). Inserting the addition theorem into the Coulomb integral gives

\[V(\mathbf r)=\frac1{\epsilon_0}\sum_{\ell,m}\frac{q_{\ell m}}{(2\ell+1)r^{\ell+1}}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf r}) .\]

Unlike boundary voltage coefficients, \(q_{\ell m}\) carries units C m\(^{\ell}\). At \(\ell=0\), \(q_{00}=Q/\sqrt{4\pi}\) and multiplication by \(Y_{00}\) returns \(Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)\), fixing the prefactor. At \(\ell=1,m=0\),

\[q_{10}=\sqrt{3/(4\pi)}p_z.\]

The two transverse coefficients reconstruct \(p_x,p_y\). Measuring the voltage coefficient at two radii can distinguish a decaying exterior multipole from a regular potential imposed by distant equipment, whose local expansion instead grows as \(r^\ell\).

Design an angular quadrature that measures the claimed coefficient

Uniformly spaced latitude rings do not have equal area. In angular integrals substitute \(u=\cos\theta\), giving \(d\Omega=du\,d\phi\). Equal spacing in \(u\) and \(\phi\) already avoids overweighting the polar caps. More accurate quadrature uses Gauss–Legendre nodes in \(u\) with their weights, combined with equispaced azimuthal samples for the Fourier factors. If a known angular pattern contains \(|m|\le M\), at least \(2M+1\) azimuthal samples are needed to distinguish these signed orders on a fixed ring. A higher order present in an unknown pattern can alias into the fitted low orders, so adding more sample angles and reserving independent test directions is informative.

A robust reconstruction checks the constant mode on \(f=1\), the total power \(\sum|v_{\ell m}|^2=\int|f|^2d\Omega\) for an exactly represented pattern, and the predicted angular field at withheld directions. With noisy overdetermined data use a weighted linear least-squares fit to the modes, including weights for sampling geometry or measurement uncertainty as appropriate. The two choices are physically different: one approximates an angular integral, the other quantifies independent noisy observations. State which objective your fit minimises. An angular reconstruction can predict a voltage while still having very poor sensitivity to high-order exterior source moments, whose signals decay rapidly with radius.

A displaced charge supplies an exact all-orders convergence experiment

Place a point charge \(q\) at \(s\hat{\mathbf z}\) within an otherwise empty sphere used as a measurement surface. At radius \(R>s\) the potential is \(q/[4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{R^2+s^2-2Rs\cos\theta}]\). The axial symmetry makes only \(m=0\) coefficients nonzero, giving the exact Legendre series \([q/(4\pi\epsilon_0R)]\sum_{\ell\ge0}(s/R)^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)\). This example exhibits the convergence parameter directly. It is the radius of the enclosing source relative to the measurement radius, not the magnitude of the charge or the number of sampled angles.

On the ray nearest the point charge, \(\theta=0\), every Legendre polynomial is unity. Truncation at order \(L\) there gives a geometric partial sum and a relative error exactly \((s/R)^{L+1}\) when compared with the exact voltage at that point. At \(s/R=0.40\), six retained orders \(\ell=0,\ldots,5\) leave 0.410% relative error. At \(s/R=0.85\) the same approximation leaves 37.7%. Achieving 1% in the latter case requires \(L+1>\ln(0.01)/\ln(0.85)=28.3\), so at least 29 orders. A claim that retaining through quadrupole always suffices has no physical meaning without an observation radius and a desired error.

On the opposite ray \(P_\ell(-1)=(-1)^\ell\), making the terms alternate. The absolute voltage error there has a smaller prefactor \(1/(1+s/R)\), in contrast to \(1/(1-s/R)\) on the near side. For this particular geometric series its relative error magnitude is still \((s/R)^{L+1}\), despite the alternating signs. At other angles the angular polynomial oscillations can produce much smaller error but not a uniformly improved bound. The simple inequality \(|P_\ell(u)|\le1\) gives an absolute bound \( |q|(s/R)^{L+1}/[4\pi\epsilon_0R(1-s/R)]\) for the entire angular surface. For an arbitrary signed source replace \(|q|\) by integrated absolute charge and \(s\) by a radius enclosing all source points. A nearly neutral distribution can have a small exact voltage at some angles, making any comparable relative bound much less informative.

Now rotate the point charge through polar and azimuthal angles \(\theta_s,\phi_s\) without changing its radius. The coefficient is exactly \(q_{\ell m}=q s^\ell Y_{\ell m}^*(\theta_s,\phi_s)\). Summing \(|q_{\ell m}|^2\) over \(m\) gives \(q^2 s^{2\ell}(2\ell+1)/(4\pi)\), by setting the two angular arguments equal in the addition theorem. This rotational invariant is a strong test of a numerical angular projection: a rotation changes individual complex phases, but the total power at each angular order remains fixed. Rotating a sensor array rather than the source should produce the equivalent opposite rotation of the fitted description.

A spherical split electrode drives an interior field without net charge

Construct a spherical boundary of radius \(a\) whose northern half is held at \(+V_0\) and southern half at \(-V_0\), separated by a thin insulating equatorial seam. In the mathematical limit take \(f(u)=V_0\operatorname{sgn}u\), \(u=\cos\theta\). The problem is axisymmetric and changes sign under reflection through the equator, so only odd \(\ell\) occur. Using the Legendre (rather than unit-normalised harmonic) coefficients, the series inside is \(V=\sum b_\ell(r/a)^\ell P_\ell(u)\), where \(b_\ell=(2\ell+1)V_0\int_0^1P_\ell(u)\,du\) for odd \(\ell\). There is no monopole, hence no change to the net charge when the two voltages are applied symmetrically in a symmetric environment.

The first two nonzero coefficients can be found without a general table. \(P_1=u\) gives

\[b_1=3V_0/2.\]

\(P_3=(5u^3-3u)/2\) integrates from zero to one to \(-1/8\), so \(b_3=-7V_0/8\). At the centre all terms with \(\ell\ge2\) have vanishing gradient, leaving an exact central field \(E_z(0)=-3V_0/(2a)\). Even though the electrode voltages jump abruptly at the equator, a single low-order coefficient determines this point measurement exactly. A finely resolved electrode charge density near the seam requires a very different number of modes and a model of the seam width.

Set \(a=30\) mm and \(V_0=2.0\) V. The central field is \(-100\) V/m. At \(z=0.20a\) on the northern axis, the \(\ell=1\) voltage contributes \(0.600\) V and \(\ell=3\) adds \(-0.0140\) V. The first omitted odd term scales as \((0.2)^5\) times its angular coefficient. At \(z=0.9a\) the same rapidly convergent two-term approximation is no longer trustworthy. A student can compare the actual surface voltages estimated from a truncated expansion to predictions at selected interior radii. A converged central field and a conspicuously rounded numerical step can coexist in the same exact analytic model.

For a finite insulating angular seam suppose instead the boundary interpolates continuously and antisymmetrically near \(u=0\). Only a thin band changes relative to the sign function. The dipole coefficient depends on \(\int f(u)u\,du\); the extra weight \(u\) suppresses the influence of perturbations concentrated around the equator. If the band spans \(|u|<\delta\) and the change in potential is bounded by \(V_0\), the dipole coefficient error is at most \(3V_0\delta^2/2\). High-order edge charge can nevertheless change markedly. This estimate shows how a real imperfection can have a small impact on one observable and a large impact on another. It also provides a quantitative tolerance when using the ideal split boundary to predict a centre-field calibration.

Separate material inside a measurement sphere from fields imposed outside it

A laboratory sometimes measures voltages on two concentric source-free spherical surfaces, without knowing whether the disturbance comes from an object inside both surfaces or from distant electrodes outside both. A single angular map determines the coefficient of each measured harmonic at that radius but cannot in general distinguish these two origins. In an annulus lacking sources, each angular component takes the radial form \(v_{\ell m}(r)=A_{\ell m}r^\ell+B_{\ell m}r^{-\ell-1}\). The regular term is compatible with external forcing, and the decaying term with sources inside the inner radius. Both are harmonic in the measurement shell.

For a single \(\ell=1\) component, denote its coefficient of an ordinary \(\cos\theta\) pattern by \(v(r)=a_1(r/R_1)+b_1(R_1/r)^2\), so \(a_1,b_1\) are voltages at the first radius. If two scans give \(v(R_1)=2.00\) V and \(v(2R_1)=1.30\) V, the equations are

\[a_1+b_1=2.00,\ 2a_1+b_1/4=1.30.\]

Multiply the first by one quarter and subtract, giving \(a_1=0.4571\) V and \(b_1=1.5429\) V. At a third radius \(1.5R_1\) the predicted value is \(1.371\) V. A pure internal dipole would have returned 0.50 V on the second sphere, while a pure uniform external field would have returned 4.00 V. Fitting a single dipole to the first reading alone would grossly misdescribe the second.

For general order the determinant of the two normalised equations is \(t^{-\ell-1}-t^\ell\) with \(t=R_2/R_1\). As the two radii approach each other, this determinant tends to zero and the coefficients cannot be separated reliably. Increasing separation improves discrimination of the two radial powers up to the point where one component falls below the measurement noise or additional sources enter the annulus. The monopole case deserves separate attention: its growing branch is just a constant voltage offset, while its decaying branch reveals total charge. A voltage reference common to both scans is therefore indispensable when using their difference to infer monopole charge.

Suppose the field was measured rather than voltage. Radial field coefficients are \(-\ell A_{\ell m}r^{\ell-1}+(\ell+1)B_{\ell m}r^{-\ell-2}\). Measuring both radial field and voltage on one sphere can, in principle, supply the two independent equations needed to separate regular and decaying parts. For \(\ell=0\) the constant reference disappears from the radial field. An independently calibrated force probe then constrains total internal charge even when the voltmeter has a common offset. Numerically estimated radial derivatives from two very close voltage scans, however, share measurement errors with those voltages; treating them as two independent precise observations overstates the information gained.

A final issue is the coordinate origin. Moving it can create higher moments from lower ones for a nonneutral distribution. If the actual source is a displaced point charge and the coordinate origin shifts toward it, the series converges faster at a given observation sphere. If the charge distribution is unknown, choosing the origin solely to minimise apparent high-order coefficients can hide genuine structure or amplify noise. Record the physical origin of the measuring geometry, report withheld predictions, and compare the integrated monopole flux, which is independent of the choice of origin as long as the source remains enclosed. This separates a useful coordinate convention from an inferred physical symmetry.

Derive and check the result

There are \(\sum_{\ell=0}^3(2\ell+1)=16\) independent real degrees of freedom. Sixteen arbitrary readings need not determine them: their locations must give a full-rank observation matrix. Equatorial-only data, for example, miss every axisymmetric odd pattern there. A straightforward overdetermined grid uses uniform azimuths and Gauss–Legendre nodes in \(u=\cos\theta\). For \(L=3\) use at least \(2L+1=7\) equally spaced azimuths so that differences \(m-m'\) up to 6 cannot alias a nonzero azimuthal difference into the constant component of a projection.

After that exact azimuthal sum, only equal \(m\) terms survive. Their products in \(u\) are proportional to \(P_\ell^m(u)P_{\ell'}^m(u)\). Each contains \((1-u^2)^{|m|}\) times a polynomial and is itself a polynomial of degree at most \(\ell+\ell'\le6\). A four-point Gauss–Legendre rule integrates polynomials through degree 7 exactly. Thus four latitudes times seven azimuths, 28 weighted samples, suffice to reconstruct all 16 coefficients for the declared band limit. Both polar caps are represented by quadrature weights; no point must lie precisely on either pole.

If the true field contains order \(m=7\), then \(e^{i7\phi}=1\) at the seven uniformly spaced azimuths starting at zero. Its azimuthal variation can masquerade as a constant on each sampled circle. A second scan shifted by half an azimuthal spacing flips the sign of this harmonic but preserves a true constant, exposing the alias. Unmodelled high latitudinal orders can similarly corrupt low coefficients through the finite radial quadrature. With noisy measurements evaluate withheld off-grid directions and report both the sensitivity to additional modes and the precision assumed in the protected low orders. An exactly integrating grid under a band-limit hypothesis is not a proof that the physical source obeyed that hypothesis.

Capacitance matrices, floating nodes and controlled approximation bounds

Electrostatic field theory and measurement

A multi-electrode device generally has no single capacitance until the other terminals have been connected or isolated. An energy identity determines the matrix that relates voltages to charges, and a complementary flux identity can certify estimates even when the exact field is unknown.

Build the matrix from unit-voltage experiments

Consider \(n\) isolated conductor surfaces embedded in a connected linear dielectric, plus a grounded reference conductor or decay relative to a reference at infinity. There is no free charge in the dielectric. Define the weighting function \(w_j(\mathbf x)\) by putting conductor \(j\) at 1 V, all other conductors and the reference at zero, then dividing by 1 V. For arbitrary voltages \(V_j\), linearity and uniqueness give

\[\phi(\mathbf x)=\sum_jV_j w_j(\mathbf x).\]

The charge on conductor \(i\) is an integral of displacement leaving its metal into the dielectric. Using a normal \(\mathbf n\) pointing out of the dielectric into the metal gives

\[Q_i=\int_{S_i}\epsilon\partial_n\phi\,dA.\]

Therefore

\[Q_i=\sum_j C_{ij}V_j,\]
\[C_{ij}=\int_{S_i}\epsilon\,\partial_n w_j\,dA=\int_\Omega\nabla w_i\cdot\epsilon\nabla w_j\,dV .\]

The last equality follows by multiplying the charge-free field equation for \(w_j\) by \(w_i\) and integrating by parts. The boundary integral selects just conductor \(i\) because its weighting function is unity there and zero on every other conductor. The volume expression proves reciprocity \(C_{ij}=C_{ji}\) for a symmetric dielectric tensor. The diagonal terms are positive, since they integrate the squared fields of each independent one-volt experiment. In an ordinary connected isotropic configuration a positive voltage on \(j\) with \(i\ne j\) grounded puts negative induced charge on \(i\); the corresponding mutual coefficient \(C_{ij}\) is nonpositive.

The stored energy is

\[U=\tfrac12\sum_{ij}V_i C_{ij}V_j.\]

Positive material response makes the reduced matrix with an effective reference positive definite under usual connected boundary conditions. If all conductors including the external closing conductor are retained without fixing a reference, changing all voltages by a common constant produces no field. The full matrix then has a zero common-voltage mode and each row sums to zero. Removing the grounded reference from the matrix removes this gauge null direction; its remaining rows need not sum to zero. Treating both statements as universal would create a spurious constraint on measured terminal data.

Convert a field matrix to a circuit measurement

A two-port device might have reduced matrix in picofarads \(C=\begin{pmatrix}12&-3\\-3&8\end{pmatrix}\) relative to its grounded enclosure. With conductor 1 at 2 V and conductor 2 at 0 V, charges are \(24\) pC and \(-6\) pC. The grounded enclosure takes the compensating \(-18\) pC. Reciprocity predicts that holding conductor 1 at 0 V and conductor 2 at 2 V puts \(-6\) pC on conductor 1. The diagonal 12 pF includes both the coupling to conductor 2 and the path to the enclosure. In an equivalent lumped model the positive pair capacitance between 1 and 2 is 3 pF, while the two separate couplings to ground are 9 and 5 pF. The sign of a Maxwell matrix off-diagonal is opposite to a positive capacitor drawn between two nodes.

Now disconnect conductor 2 after neutralising it. Its total charge stays zero while its voltage becomes an unknown. The second matrix row demands \(-3V_1+8V_2=0\). Thus \(V_2=3V_1/8\), giving apparent capacitance seen at terminal 1 equal to \(12-9/8=10.875\) pF. Grounding conductor 2 would instead yield 12 pF. The change is a measurable effect of the prepared state, not a change in the dielectric constant of the material. If conductor 2 already carries charge \(Q_2^0\), its voltage acquires a constant offset \(Q_2^0/C_{22}\) in addition to the response to \(V_1\).

For several floating nodes write their block equation as \(Q_f=C_{fa}V_a+C_{ff}V_f\). If the reference fixes the potential freedom, solve \(V_f=C_{ff}^{-1}(Q_f-C_{fa}V_a)\) and substitute into the active-node block. The differential active capacitance is the Schur complement \(C_{aa}-C_{af}C_{ff}^{-1}C_{fa}\). It is symmetric and positive for a positive full reduced matrix. With fixed floating charges the constant term shifts active charges but leaves this differential matrix unchanged in a linear medium. An actual cable measurement that results from leaving a neighbouring shield unconnected should be compared to this elimination, not to the original diagonal.

Obtain an upper capacitance bound by trying a voltage

For a two-terminal charge-free dielectric at prescribed voltage difference \(V\), every admissible continuous trial potential satisfying the electrode voltages gives an energy at least as large as the exact electrostatic energy. This follows by writing the trial as exact plus a perturbation vanishing on fixed-voltage electrodes and expanding the energy. The mixed term integrates to zero because the exact displacement is divergence free. If exterior walls are insulated, the exact normal displacement vanishes there and the trial need not be assigned a fixed potential on them. Thus \(C\le 2U_{\rm trial}/V^2\). The fields must genuinely arise as a gradient of one trial voltage. Guessing unrelated components of an electric field does not provide this upper bound.

For a capacitor of plate area \(A\), separation \(d\), and scalar permittivity varying in the gap, choose a linear voltage along the plate normal. The trial energy gives

\[C/A\leq \langle\epsilon\rangle/d,\]

where the average is over the gap volume divided by \(Ad\). For dielectric columns that run parallel to the applied field with no change of material along the field direction, the linear potential can be exact. For layers stacked normal to the field it usually overestimates capacitance. The same arithmetic average thus serves as the actual result or a strict upper bound depending on geometry, even when the material fractions are identical.

A complementary trial with a divergence-free displacement

Fix total plate charges \(\pm Q\) instead, taking no volume free charge. An admissible displacement trial must have the prescribed flux into each electrode, no flux through insulating side walls, continuous normal component across free-charge-free interfaces, and zero divergence inside. It need not correspond to an exact potential-gradient electric field. Consider \(U_D[\mathbf D]=\tfrac12\int\mathbf D\cdot\epsilon^{-1}\mathbf D\,dV\). Write a trial as \(\mathbf D_*+\delta\mathbf D\). The cross term is \(\int(-\nabla \phi_*)\cdot\delta\mathbf D\,dV=0\): integration by parts gives zero bulk divergence and each conductor has constant voltage multiplying zero net flux change. Thus \(U_D\ge Q^2/(2C)\) and

\[\frac{Q^2}{2U_D^{\rm trial}}\ \le C\le\ \frac{2U_V^{\rm trial}}{V^2}.\]

Unlike two competing estimates, these inequalities identify which side contains the exact answer. For the same parallel-plate geometry choose constant displacement \(D_z=Q/A\). It yields \(C/A\geq1/[d\langle1/\epsilon\rangle]\), the harmonic-mean lower bound. For layers stacked normal to the plates that trial is exact. For columns parallel to the field the upper bound is exact. An arbitrarily shaped two-phase composite satisfies both under the stated plate and insulating-side conditions. This gives a strong physical check before solving detailed microstructure on a mesh.

Use measured numbers to rule out a material model

Take equal volume fractions of relative permittivities 2 and 8 in a cell of area \(100\) mm² and thickness 1.0 mm. The harmonic mean is 3.2, arithmetic mean 5.0. Since \(\epsilon_0A/d=0.8854\) pF, the admissible capacitance interval is 2.83–4.43 pF. A measured 3.3 pF is consistent with many internal geometries and does not determine how the two materials are arranged. A calibrated value of 5.2 pF would require examining fringing, area and thickness, parallel cable capacitance, dispersive or conducting response, and the assumed constituent data. Merely refining an interior mesh cannot produce a static value outside the proven interval if it faithfully solves the same ideal model.

For a field computation use the matrix reciprocity relation as one diagnostic and the two variational bounds as another. An electrostatic finite-element potential may produce a reliable energy estimate while numerical differentiation at a sharp electrode edge produces poor charge density. Integrating boundary fluxes gives each terminal charge, but reconstructing \(C_{ij}\) by the mixed energy integral often has a different error sensitivity. Comparing both over a grid sequence can indicate whether the error is concentrated at edges or in the field volume. Symmetrising a noisy measured matrix by averaging its transpose removes an antisymmetric discrepancy but cannot establish positivity or explain an inaccurate common scale.

Construct a complete measurable matrix without numerical differentiation

Three thin spherical conductors have radii \(a<b<c\). The outer one is tied to the reference potential. Homogeneous material of permittivity \(\epsilon_1\) fills the inner annulus \(a<r<b\) and another material \(\epsilon_2\) fills \(b<r<c\). Spherical symmetry gives each annulus its own independent radial displacement field fixed by its electrode voltage difference. Define the ordinary two-sphere gap capacitances \(K_1=4\pi\epsilon_1 ab/(b-a)\),

\[K_2=4\pi\epsilon_2 bc/(c-b).\]

The inner conductor takes \(K_1(V_1-V_2)\). The middle one has two faces, carrying the opposite inner gap charge and an outer gap charge \(K_2V_2\). Thus \(Q_1=K_1V_1-K_1V_2\) and \(Q_2=-K_1V_1+(K_1+K_2)V_2\). Every sign and every term can be read from a physical face.

In vacuum choose \(a=10\) mm, \(b=20\) mm, \(c=40\) mm. Then \(K_1=2.225\) pF and \(K_2=4.451\) pF. Driven at 3.0 V with the middle conductor grounded, the inner sphere carries \(6.68\) pC and the outer gap has zero field. With the middle conductor floating and neutral, \(V_2=K_1V_1/(K_1+K_2)=1.0\) V. The inner sphere now carries \(4.45\) pC. The inner and outer faces of the floating sphere carry exactly opposite charges, although the conductor has a nonzero voltage. Integrating a current meter attached to the conductor during charging need not tell you each face's charge separately.

Now electrically connect the inner two conductors. Their common voltage sets the inner field to zero. The combined conductor has capacitance \(K_2\) to the exterior, independent of \(a\). This third preparation is distinct from grounding or floating the middle shell. Before calculating an experimental 'device capacitance', draw the electrical connections as well as the geometric cross section. In a larger matrix a driven conductor and a driven guard at the same voltage should have their charges combined only if the experimental ammeter actually measures their total current.

One can also recover the two constituent permittivities from just two carefully selected scalar measurements. Ground the middle shell to determine \(K_1\) directly. Then float it neutral and measure \(K_{\rm ser}=K_1K_2/(K_1+K_2)\), giving \(K_2=K_{\rm ser}K_1/(K_1-K_{\rm ser})\). If \(K_2\) is very large, the floating and grounded readings nearly coincide and their difference becomes noise sensitive. An alternative is to drive inner and middle together and measure the outer-gap current directly. The algebra selects a better experimental protocol instead of hiding ill-conditioning in a formula.

Tighten a variational bound without solving the entire field

Take the stacked two-layer capacitor with no space charge, equal layer thickness and isotropic permittivities \(\epsilon_1=2\epsilon_0,\epsilon_2=8\epsilon_0\). A single linear trial produces the arithmetic-mean upper bound. Insert one interior voltage degree of freedom at a point that coincides with the material interface. The resulting piecewise-linear trial space contains the exact solution. Minimising its energy makes the normal displacements equal, reproducing the harmonic-mean capacitance exactly. A global quadratic trial with the same number of extra parameters will usually perform worse because its derivative is continuous while the exact normal field jumps.

To see this quantitatively write \(x=z/d\) on the unit interval and \(V(x)=V_0[x+\eta x(1-x)]\), with the lower half at relative permittivity 2 and upper half at 8. This trial fixes both endpoints. Its dimensionless capacitance upper estimate is

\[c(\eta)=\int_0^1\epsilon_r(x)[1+\eta(1-2x)]^2dx.\]

Integrating gives

\[c(\eta)=5-3\eta+(5/3)\eta^2.\]

The best quadratic trial has \(\eta=0.9\) and \(c=3.65\), compared with 5.00 for the straight line and 3.20 for the exact layered solution. The unrefined constant-displacement lower trial already supplies 3.20, so the two-field comparison certifies an interval before any field-map visualisation.

Why did the smooth trial still overestimate by 14%? At its endpoints it predicts slopes 1.9 and 0.1 times the mean field magnitude, whereas the exact field has slopes 1.6 and 0.4 in the two halves. Optimising a volume energy distributes a global smooth approximation across both layers. It has no mechanism to jump at the known interface, so an error persists even with its one coefficient optimised perfectly. The example explains why fitting material interfaces in a mesh, or using basis functions that admit correct normal derivative jumps, can matter more than merely increasing polynomial degree.

In an irregular two-dimensional composite an exactly divergence-free complementary trial can be constructed from a stream function \(\Psi\): take \(\mathbf D=(\partial_y\Psi,-\partial_x\Psi)\) in Cartesian coordinates. Its divergence vanishes identically. Total flux through an oriented path is a difference of \(\Psi\) at its endpoints, so carefully chosen boundary values enforce required electrode charges and zero leakage on insulating sides. Optimising \(\int\mathbf D\cdot\epsilon^{-1}\mathbf D/2\) over such trial functions tightens the lower capacitance bound. Optimising the independent voltage trial tightens the upper one. The difference between their resulting bounds quantifies what the chosen approximation spaces still fail to resolve.

What an AC bridge measures when a nominally floating terminal leaks

A real high-impedance lead is neither perfectly floating nor an ideal ground at every frequency. Add a leakage resistance \(R\) from conductor 2 of the two-port example to its grounded enclosure. Keep the reduced static capacitance matrix \(C\) fixed, linear and quasistatic at the frequencies of interest. Let terminal 1 be driven sinusoidally and terminal 2 have no other external drive. Using \(e^{i\omega t}\) here for circuit phasors, charge continuity at terminal 2 gives

\[i\omega(C_{21}V_1+C_{22}V_2)+V_2/R=0.\]

The outward conductive current plus the rate of charge stored in the electrode must vanish. Solving for the active terminal admittance gives

\[Y_{\rm in}=i\omega C_{11}+\frac{\omega^2 C_{12}C_{21}}{1/R+i\omega C_{22}} .\]

At low frequency the leakage holds terminal 2 close to ground, so the leading measured capacitance is \(C_{11}\). At high frequency the floating condition is recovered and \(Y_{\rm in}/(i\omega)\to C_{11}-C_{12}C_{21}/C_{22}\). The effective admittance also has a positive real part, since the driven voltage on terminal 2 supplies Joule heat in the leakage resistance. This term persists even if the dielectric itself is lossless. An AC capacitance meter reporting a frequency-dependent loss tangent might therefore be detecting a boundary preparation rather than intrinsic material loss.

Take \(C_{22}=8\) pF and \(R=100\) MΩ. The crossover \(1/(2\pi R C_{22})\) is 199 Hz. At this frequency the quadrature capacitance is halfway between 12.0 and 10.875 pF, namely 11.4375 pF. The corresponding loss conductance divided by \(\omega\) is 0.5625 pF. An independent verification uses the port voltage ratio at crossover,

\[|V_2/V_1|=|C_{21}|/(\sqrt2 C_{22})=0.265.\]

Compute dissipated power as \(|V_2|^2/(2R)\) for peak phasor amplitudes and compare it with \(\operatorname{Re}Y_{\rm in}|V_1|^2/2\); the two results must coincide.

The crossover shifts inversely with the leakage resistance while its two limiting capacitances stay fixed in this simple model. Measuring several shunt resistances or adding a known external one provides a direct way to test this interpretation. If a neighbouring shield is connected to a voltage follower instead, its response includes the follower's own gain and phase; static floating-node elimination is insufficient. Extending the field-derived capacitance matrix by experimentally known terminal circuits makes it possible to distinguish changes in electrostatic geometry from changes in what the surrounding measurement wiring enforces.

Comparing the plots of the two fields does not fix an inadmissible constraint. If both are corrected, their gap gives an actual a posteriori uncertainty interval even without an analytic solution.

Reconstructing a source from noisy voltage measurements

Electrostatic field theory and measurement

A forward electrostatic calculation can be unique and stable while the inverse problem constructed from it is unstable or nonunique. A scanned electrode, a finite collecting area and an explicitly chosen noise level make these statements experimentally testable.

Write down what the instrument actually measures

Pattern a long electrode at \(z=0\) with an unknown voltage \(f(x)\), periodic over length \(L\) for this model. The region above is homogeneous, charge free, and contains a sufficiently weak probe at height \(h\). For a Fourier component \(f_m e^{ik_mx}\), \(k_m=2\pi m/L\), the bounded harmonic solution at height \(h\) is \(f_m e^{-|k_m|h}e^{ik_mx}\). Treat the constant component according to the actual reference. Suppose the probe reports an average over a rectangular window of width \(w\) centred at \(x_j\), plus noise \(e_j\). For uniform probe sensitivity the forward relation is

\[y_j=\sum_m H_m f_m e^{ik_m x_j}+e_j,\]
\[H_m=e^{-|k_m|h}\operatorname{sinc}(k_m w/2),\]
\[\operatorname{sinc}t=\frac{\sin t}t .\]

The exponential is physical propagation from boundary to probe; the sinc factor is averaging by the detector. The latter has exact zeros in this idealised model. For modes at those zeros no noise-free reading at the same width and height can recover the lost amplitude. More closely spaced scan positions increase the sampling bandwidth but cannot undo either such instrumental nulls or the exponential decrease of the true high-frequency signal.

A nonuniform probe shape replaces the sinc by its Fourier transform. Unknown height adds an additional calibration problem: increasing \(h\) and increasing high-frequency boundary amplitudes can yield the same readings if the amplitudes are unrestricted. Before inferring boundary detail, calibrate the probe width and decide whether height is known, measured with uncertainty or jointly estimated using an independent constraint.

A direct inverse amplifies uncertainty in predictable bands

In an ideal complete periodic scan the discrete Fourier transform separates resolvable modes. Let \(\widehat y_m=H_m f_m+\eta_m\). Inverting one coefficient gives \(f_m^{\rm dir}=\widehat y_m/H_m\) whenever \(H_m\ne0\). A perturbation \(\delta\widehat y_m\) becomes

\[\delta f_m=\delta\widehat y_m/H_m.\]

With \(H_m=e^{-|k|h}\), the amplification grows exponentially with spatial frequency. The mode values can be uniquely determined in ideal noiseless data of infinite precision, yet the inverse is unbounded as increasingly fine modes are admitted. Finite data can additionally produce exact nonuniqueness.

For \(h=2.0\) mm and negligible aperture, boundary wavelengths 20, 10 and 4 mm have transfers \(0.533,0.285,0.0432\), respectively. With 1 mV noise in the measured amplitude of each Fourier component, inverse noise levels are \(1.87,3.51,23.1\) mV. A genuine 10 mV boundary ripple at wavelength 4 mm contributes only 0.432 mV at the probe, smaller than that noise. Plotting its raw inverted coefficient with a smooth line does not improve its information content. Reducing height to 1 mm raises that ripple's signal to 2.08 mV and changes the experiment's resolving power before any reconstruction choice is made.

Derive a regularised inverse and expose its bias

Assume coefficients are inferred separately and use a quadratic penalty. For each \(m\), minimise \(J_m=|H_m f-\widehat y_m|^2+\alpha^2 w_m^2|f|^2\) with positive weights \(w_m\) and positive regularisation parameter \(\alpha\). Splitting real and imaginary parts, or differentiating with respect to \(f^*\), gives

\[\widehat f_m=\frac{H_m^*\widehat y_m}{|H_m|^2+\alpha^2w_m^2},\]
\[W_m=\frac{|H_m|^2}{|H_m|^2+\alpha^2w_m^2},\]
\[\mathbb E[\widehat f_m]=W_m f_m\]

for zero-mean measurement noise. The reconstruction suppresses noise near a small transfer by introducing deterministic bias \( (W_m-1)f_m\). Its variance for known mode-noise variance \(s_m^2\) is \(|H_m|^2s_m^2/(|H_m|^2+\alpha^2w_m^2)^2\). Neither low variance alone nor a small data residual is evidence that a spatial ripple was recovered. Setting \(\alpha\) very large produces a smooth, nearly zero estimate regardless of the data. Setting it very small can reproduce noise almost exactly.

For \(w_m=1,\alpha=0.10\) and wavelength 4 mm in the example above, \(W_m\simeq0.157\). A true 10 mV ripple is estimated at only 1.57 mV on average. The inversion gain is about 3.64 rather than 23.1. At 20 mm, the same penalty retains 96.6% of the true amplitude. If the purpose is to quantify a short ripple, improving the experiment can be preferable to changing the penalty. A derivative penalty \(w_m\propto |k_m|\) encodes a stronger preference against fine structure; a constant mode must be handled separately if the voltage reference or the derivative penalty leaves it unconstrained.

What survives away from a uniform grid or simple geometry?

For arbitrary electrode shapes and detector positions, represent source or boundary data in a chosen basis \(f=\sum_j a_j b_j\). Solve the forward field problem for each unit coefficient and sample it exactly as the real probe would, giving matrix entries \(A_{ij}\). A noise covariance \(\Sigma\) weights the misfit through \(\|\Sigma^{-1/2}(A\mathbf a-\mathbf y)\|^2\). For an identity penalty apply the singular-value decomposition to the whitened matrix,

\[\Sigma^{-1/2}A=U S V^*.\]

Each right singular vector is a source pattern; its singular value tells how strongly it appears in the whitened measurement space. Multiplication by \(U^*\) diagonalises the data misfit and reduces the problem to the scalar filters above.

A zero singular value means the chosen sensors are insensitive to an entire source combination. No choice of numerical precision or regularisation changes that observational fact. A small nonzero value means one needs sufficiently small noise and modelling errors to identify the corresponding component. A practical resolution matrix \(R=(A^*\Sigma^{-1}A+\alpha^2I)^{-1}A^*\Sigma^{-1}A\) maps a noiseless true coefficient vector to the regularised estimate. A column of \(R\) shows which neighbouring coefficients respond when one ideal source coefficient is present. Testing such impulse responses describes the reconstruction's spatial smoothing more honestly than quoting the spacing of its output grid.

Use withheld measurements and identifiable physical quantities

Reserve some independently measured positions, heights or probe widths. Fit the rest and use the recovered candidates to predict the withheld measurements by applying the forward operator. The test is more informative when the withheld observations have different sensitivity patterns. A second distant scan can verify low modes while remaining insensitive to every high-frequency ambiguity. A second close scan or a different probe width can fill modes attenuated or nulled by the first. Correlated calibration errors, such as one common voltage offset, should be modelled as such, rather than being counted as many independent observations.

A distinct nonuniqueness occurs when trying to infer an arbitrary three-dimensional charge density solely from exterior voltages. Take any smooth compactly supported potential \(g(\mathbf x)\) inside the source region whose value and derivatives vanish outside. Add \(\delta\rho=-\epsilon_0\nabla^2g\) to a candidate charge distribution. Its induced potential is \(g\) and therefore vanishes at every external detector. Unless additional sign, support or constitutive assumptions constrain the source, infinitely many internal distributions remain consistent with exact exterior data. This is stronger than amplification of measurement noise. Total charge from a closed-surface flux or low multipole moments can still be identifiable when the internal density is not.

See an ill-conditioned inverse in a two-source instrument

Two possible source patches sit symmetrically beneath two voltage probes. A calibrated forward response, in volts of probe signal per chosen source unit, is \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.98\\0.98&1\end{pmatrix}\). Each patch affects both probes almost equally. The sum pattern \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and difference pattern \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) are orthonormal eigenvectors as well as singular vectors, with gains 1.98 and 0.02. The condition number is 99. The instrument localises total source strength much more reliably than it locates its division between the two patches.

Suppose the actual source vector is \((1,0)\). The predictions are \((1.00,0.98)\) V. A measurement perturbation \((+0.01,-0.01)\) V, though small compared with the signal, lies in the weak difference mode. The exact inverse interprets it as source error \((+0.50,-0.50)\), giving a reconstructed distribution \((1.50,-0.50)\). A common perturbation \((+0.01,+0.01)\) instead produces almost negligible source changes \(+0.00505\) each. A single rms error statistic for the voltage readings misses how their spatial correlation changes uncertainty in the reconstruction.

Use an identity Tikhonov penalty \(\alpha=0.10\). The response factors for the two true source directions become

\[W_+=1.98^2/(1.98^2+0.10^2)=0.9975,\]
\[W_-=0.02^2/(0.02^2+0.10^2)=0.0385.\]

In the absence of noise the estimated source from \((1,0)\) is approximately \((0.518,0.480)\). Both patches light up almost equally. This is the resolution matrix in action: the model predicts the observed voltages well yet cannot confidently identify which of the two patches generated them. Under the same opposite noise perturbation the additional reconstructed difference is only \(0.0192\) on each patch in opposite directions. A visually smooth result is reproducible but biased towards nearly equal strengths.

Add a third probe designed to respond as \((0.6,-0.6)\). This linear combination may be implemented physically by a differential electrode, so negative coefficients are meaningful. The squared singular gain of the old difference direction increases from \(0.0004\) to \(0.0004+2(0.6)^2=0.7204\), while the common gain is unchanged. The new difference singular value is 0.849, reducing the ratio of the largest to smallest singular values to about 2.33 if the three channels have comparable independent noise in the specified units. Adding another copy of the first common-sensitive probe would have been much less effective. Designing measurements against the weak singular vector can improve source resolution far more than collecting many redundant readings.

Separate numerical error from height uncertainty and unknown backgrounds

Return to a sinusoidal source with negligible aperture. If its true height is \(h\) but one divides by an assumed transfer computed at \(h+\delta h\), then even exact noiseless data return \(\widehat f/f=e^{|k|\delta h}\). For a 4 mm wavelength and positive height error 0.10 mm, the fractional amplitude bias is

\[e^{0.157}-1=17.0\%.\]

Unlike random noise this bias does not shrink by averaging repeated scans at the same miscalibrated height. A 20 mm calibration pattern at that same error suffers only about 3.2% bias, so testing one long-wavelength standard need not guarantee short-wavelength accuracy.

For an unknown but stable boundary pattern recorded at two known relative probe heights, \(\widehat y_m(h_2)/\widehat y_m(h_1)=e^{-|k_m|(h_2-h_1)}\) for modes above noise when the aperture is unchanged. The source amplitude and common aperture factor cancel. This measures a relative vertical displacement but not the absolute height if the true boundary amplitudes are unknown. Add a calibration electrode of known voltage and wavelength, or an independently measured mechanical height, to determine the absolute scale. Near a sensor's aperture zero the ratio is exceptionally noise sensitive and should not be used for the height fit.

Consider also a slowly varying stray background produced by distant conductors. In a source-free layer of finite thickness a Fourier mode can contain both \(Ae^{-|k|z}\) and \(Be^{+|k|z}\). The growing term is excluded in a bounded half-space continued to infinity, but can represent a field launched from the upper boundary of a finite domain. For a single spatial mode measured at two heights one can solve a \(2\times2\) system for \(A,B\). Its determinant is \(2\sinh[|k|(h_2-h_1)]\), up to a sign fixed by the column order. As the two heights coalesce their rows become nearly dependent. A measurement showing unexpected height scaling may thus indicate the wrong outer boundary in the forward model rather than a faulty inverse algorithm.

A reproducible inverse analysis can be audited along three independent axes. First manufacture noise-free readings from a source whose exact forward voltage is known, then refine only the forward discretisation. Second add noise with a specified amplitude and covariance and repeat enough draws to estimate recovered variation in identified modes. Third perturb physical nuisance parameters such as probe width, height and background independently of the noise draws. For each stage state what quantity is being recovered: total charge, a few spatial coefficients, or a real-space image biased by a chosen penalty. These stages expose different error mechanisms even when they yield similar changes in a plotted voltage trace.

Build an exactly invisible internal charge perturbation

The general construction of a silent source can be made explicit for a spherical support of radius \(a\). Pick a smooth interior potential perturbation \(g(r)=V_g[1-(r/a)^2]^3\) for \(0\le r<a\), and set \(g=0\) outside. At \(r=a\) both the potential and its first radial derivative vanish; its second radial derivative also approaches zero, avoiding unmodelled surface sheets. It is regular at the origin. Therefore \(g\) itself is the potential relative to infinity of the compact charge distribution \(\delta\rho=-\epsilon_0r^{-2}d(r^2g')/dr\). With \(x=r/a\), expansion gives

\[\delta\rho=\frac{\epsilon_0V_g}{a^2}(18-60x^2+42x^4),\qquad 0\le x<1 .\]

The perturbation is positive near the centre and negative in an outer band if \(V_g>0\). Its enclosed charge is

\[-4\pi\epsilon_0r^2g'=24\pi\epsilon_0V_ga\, x^3(1-x^2)^2.\]

It vanishes at both ends of the radius interval and is positive at intermediate radii. Gauss's law outside then gives exactly zero electric field and the chosen reference gives zero exterior voltage. A numerical inverse recovering only zero perturbation from exterior data is observationally consistent with both the absence of charge and this particular structured distribution. Integrating the polynomial with its correct \(4\pi r^2dr\) weight confirms the exact zero total charge independently.

For \(a=20\) mm, \(V_g=0.10\) V, the internal centre voltage differs by 100 mV while every ideal exterior detector, no matter how accurately calibrated, records no difference whatsoever. At \(r=a/2\), \(g=0.0422\) V and the enclosed perturbation charge is \(9.39\times10^{-14}\) C. An internal measurement can therefore break this particular ambiguity. Moving external probes closer while keeping them outside the sphere cannot. Positivity restrictions on the total density can reduce ambiguity, but adding a sufficiently small signed perturbation to a sufficiently positive background can preserve nonnegativity of both candidate densities and still leave their exterior data identical.

This example suggests a practical way to report results when full source reconstruction is impossible. Ask whether the quantity of interest changes along a silent perturbation. Total charge and all external moments stay unchanged here, whereas the central voltage and internal charge profile change. A claim about total charge can be appropriate even when detailed shell-by-shell densities are not identifiable. Conversely a fitted compact central peak may depend on the chosen smoothness penalty or positivity constraints even when every reported exterior measurement is reproduced.

A multiscale numerical exercise can demonstrate the effect reproducibly. Discretise both \(g\) and its associated \(\delta\rho\), solve the independent forward Poisson problem, and evaluate internal and external probes. Coarse grids may show a small exterior signal if source deposition fails to preserve its neutral moments. Refine the source representation, plot the exterior residual separately from the interior voltage difference and compare with the analytic polynomial. A residual that tends to zero while the interior difference remains finite is the expected physical outcome. Using exactly the same discrete operator to manufacture data and to recover them can mask representation errors, so a second mesh, an analytic source integral or a differently implemented forward solver is especially valuable.

Can two independent estimates agree for the wrong reason?

Separate common uncertainty from independent noise in two height scans

A probe measures a 10 mm wavelength mode at heights 1.0 and 2.0 mm with negligible aperture. The two Fourier amplitudes are 5.34 and 2.85 mV, each with independent 0.10 mV random uncertainty, but they share an unknown common voltage gain. Estimate the boundary amplitude and its random error under unit gain. Decide what the agreement can establish about calibration.

Derive and check the result

The two transfers are

\[H_1=e^{-0.2\pi}=0.5335,\]
\[H_2=e^{-0.4\pi}=0.2846.\]

Each individually predicts about 10.0 mV at the boundary. For independent equal-variance amplitude noise, minimise \(\sum(y_i-H_i f)^2\). Its solution is \(f=(H_1y_1+H_2y_2)/(H_1^2+H_2^2)\), and random standard deviation \(0.10/\sqrt{H_1^2+H_2^2}=0.165\) mV. Simply averaging the two directly inverted amplitudes gives the noisier second inversion equal weight and hence a larger variance.

The measured amplitude ratio is about 0.534, as predicted by the additional 1 mm of propagation. A common multiplicative calibration factor cancels from this ratio. If the true voltage gain were 1.10, both unit-gain inversions would agree at about 10 mV even though the physical amplitude would be 9.1 mV. Repeated scans reduce the random uncertainty but cannot remove this systematic scale. A known reference electrode or independent calibrated voltage source is needed.

If instead both probes share a small additive voltage offset in every spatial sample, a full ideal Fourier analysis would place that constant entirely in the zero mode. Incomplete sampling, finite windows or background leakage can mix the offset into the fitted sinusoid. Including a constant nuisance term in a weighted least-squares fit is one way to test that effect directly. Identifying the form of a shared error is as important as quoting its magnitude: common gain, common offset and relative height uncertainty enter the inverse problem in different directions and respond to different calibration experiments.

Which part of a sample changes a four-electrode impedance measurement?

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

Driving one electrode pair and measuring another yields a transfer impedance whose spatial sensitivity can be positive, negative or nearly zero. A second field solution supplies the sensitivity without perturbing every element of a numerical mesh.

Distinguish the current terminals from the voltmeter terminals

Consider a bounded two-phase electrical sample in a benign laboratory characterisation cell. Drive harmonic current \(+I\) through electrode \(a\) and collect \(-I\) through \(b\). Two independently selected electrodes \(c,d\) read the difference \(U_c-U_d\) with negligible voltmeter current. The measured transfer impedance is

\[Z_{cd,ab}=(U_c-U_d)/I.\]

A four-terminal measurement reduces some lead-voltage errors, but finite-area electrodes still alter the field and contact processes can affect it. Reversing either pair reverses the signed transfer reading. Interchanging both pairs leaves it unchanged in a reciprocal linear sample.

Adopt phasors \(e^{i\omega t}\) and a local complex admittivity \(\gamma=\sigma+i\omega\epsilon\), with real positive scalar \(\sigma,\epsilon\) for this first model. The total current density is \(\mathbf J=-\gamma\nabla u\). In the electroquasistatic regime \(\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u)=0\) away from injected current at the boundary. Magnetic induction and propagation are negligible at the chosen cell dimensions and frequencies; checking electrode and cable dynamics remains necessary even when the sample itself is quasistatic. Perfectly conducting measurement electrodes are equipotentials. Inactive electrodes have zero net current, which allows charge redistribution over their finite surfaces; a strictly insulating uncovered wall instead requires zero normal current locally.

Use an outward normal \(\mathbf n\) from the sample. Define positive terminal current into the sample by \(I_i=\int_{S_i}\gamma\partial_n u\,dA\); it equals minus the outward conduction-plus-displacement flux. The pair currents sum to zero, and only voltage differences matter, so fix one reference or a mean voltage. Integrate by parts against any test voltage \(v\) constant on each ideal electrode, giving

\[\int_\Omega\gamma\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dV=\sum_i I_i\,v_i .\]

This is a complex bilinear identity for reciprocity and linear sensitivity. Real average heating is obtained separately by conjugating the physical fields when computing power. Confusing those two products can give an incorrect phase or an incorrect sign for an imaginary admittivity perturbation.

Perturb the sample while keeping injected currents fixed

Let \(\psi_{ab}\) be the potential per unit current driven from \(a\) to \(b\), and \(\psi_{cd}\) the solution when current is instead driven between the measurement electrodes. Using each as test function for the other shows directly that \(Z_{cd,ab}=Z_{ab,cd}\). Now change material admittivity by a small distribution \(\delta\gamma\) and let the drive field change by \(\delta\psi_{ab}\). Holding all currents and electrode geometry fixed, differentiate the weak equation:

\[\int_\Omega\gamma\nabla\delta\psi_{ab}\cdot\nabla v\,dV=-\int_\Omega\delta\gamma\,\nabla\psi_{ab}\cdot\nabla v\,dV .\]

Choose \(v=\psi_{cd}\) and apply the unperturbed measurement-pair equation to \(\delta\psi_{ab}\). Its left side becomes the change in voltage difference per drive current, hence

\[\delta Z_{cd,ab}=-\int_\Omega\delta\gamma(\mathbf x)\,\nabla\psi_{ab}(\mathbf x)\cdot\nabla\psi_{cd}(\mathbf x)\,dV .\]

The minus sign has an immediate check in a conducting bar: increasing conductivity lowers its resistance at fixed current. The sensitivity density is the overlap of the drive and reciprocal fields, not simply the magnitude of the drive field. If the fields are locally perpendicular a small isotropic admittivity perturbation is invisible to first order in this channel. If the fields are opposed the local real sensitivity can have the opposite sign to nearby regions. For tensor perturbations replace the product by \(-\nabla\psi_{cd}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol\gamma\nabla\psi_{ab}\). Reciprocity in the above form requires symmetric material tensors.

A one-dimensional experiment tests the sign, normalisation and scale

Take a uniform real-conductivity bar of length 20 mm, cross-section \(A=100\) mm² and conductivity \(0.20\) S/m. Assume end electrodes cover its faces and ideal voltage taps select the full length in this limiting four-wire experiment. The impedance is

\[L/(\sigma A)=1000\ \Omega.\]

The drive and reciprocal gradients per ampere have magnitude \(1/(\sigma A)\). Increase conductivity in a full cross-section segment 2.0 mm long by \(0.020\) S/m. The first-order formula predicts

\[\delta R=-\ell\delta\sigma/(\sigma^2 A)=-10.0\ \Omega.\]

Direct series integration of \(1/(\sigma A)\) gives a change from 100.0 to 90.91 Ω in the segment, or \(-9.09\ \Omega\).

The 0.91 Ω difference is consistent with truncating at first order for a 10% conductivity change. Recompute at half the material perturbation: the exact change becomes \(-4.762\ \Omega\), only 0.238 Ω away from the first-order \(-5.00\ \Omega\). The residual decreases by about four when the step halves. Repeating at several positive and negative perturbations and taking a centred difference provides a sharper test of the adjoint code. Varying only the mesh resolution with a fixed large material contrast cannot remove a genuine nonlinear remainder.

The same one-dimensional geometry also demonstrates an absolute identifiability limit. Interchanging two equal-length full-cross-section inserts with different conductivities leaves the end-to-end resistance unchanged, since the integral of reciprocal conductivity is independent of ordering. Perfect measurement precision cannot recover the order. A voltage tap between the two inserts or an alternative contact geometry is needed to break that symmetry.

Interpret real and imaginary voltage readings together

Even if each local \(\sigma,\epsilon\) is real, the fields \(\psi\) in an inhomogeneous sample usually carry spatially varying phase. A change \(\delta\sigma\) can therefore change both the real and imaginary transfer impedance, as can \(i\omega\delta\epsilon\). Treat a complex reading as two real measurements with their calibrated covariance. To first order one finite-element cell with piecewise-constant parameters contributes coefficients \(-\int_{\rm cell}\nabla\psi_{ab}\cdot\nabla\psi_{cd}\,dV\) for \(\delta\sigma\) and \(i\omega\) times that coefficient for \(\delta\epsilon\). The units of the two unknowns differ, so rescale with plausible physical parameter changes before comparing singular values.

As a simple frequency check choose a uniform engineered dielectric-conductor composite with \(\sigma=0.010\) S/m and \(\epsilon_r=1000\). At \(f=\sigma/(2\pi\epsilon)\simeq180\) kHz, \(\gamma=\sigma(1+i)\). The same bar geometry then gives \(Z=(L/A)/\gamma=10.0(1-i)\) kΩ, a phase of \(-45^\circ\). At low frequency the phase tends to zero and the real impedance to 20.0 kΩ. A constant cable delay produces phase proportional to frequency independent of the sample parameters, so record a calibrated reference at each frequency before attributing an observed phase change solely to the specimen.

Derive a spatial sensitivity that can be checked without a mesh

Consider a homogeneous, thick conducting calibration block with a flat, electrically insulating top except at four small contacts on the same line. Coordinates are \(x,y\) along the surface and \(z>0\) into the block. If a unit current enters through a point at the surface, it spreads over hemispheres of radius \(r\). Uniform normal current through a hemisphere requires

\[J_r=1/(2\pi r^2).\]

The radial field is \(J_r/\sigma\); integrating from \(r\) to infinity gives potential \(1/(2\pi\sigma r)\). The coefficient differs from a point source in the interior where an entire sphere carries the current. Subtract a corresponding solution for the terminal extracting the current to satisfy conservation.

Let the drive pair be at \(x=\mp a\) and the sense pair at \(x=\mp c\), with positive terminal at negative \(x\) in each case. At surface points separated from the point contacts the baseline transfer impedance becomes

\[Z_0=\frac1{\pi\sigma}\left(\frac1{a-c}-\frac1{a+c}\right)\qquad(0<c<a).\]

For \(a=15\) mm, \(c=5\) mm and \(\sigma=0.20\) S/m this is 79.58 Ω. A 1 mA real current gives 79.58 mV. The same number follows on interchanging the drive and measurement pairs. The large individual contact potentials of an ideal point source are singular at the driving contact itself, yet the transfer potential evaluated at a distinct contact is finite. A two-terminal impedance obtained by setting \(c=a\) is divergent in this point-contact approximation. To make such a measurement calculable one must assign physical electrode radii, contact laws and current distributions.

Place a small spherical inclusion of radius 1.0 mm at \(x=y=0\) and depth \(z\), with conductivity just 0.020 S/m above background. At the centreline the vertical field components cancel in each paired solution. Differentiating the two point-source potentials horizontally gives a gradient magnitude \(a/[\pi\sigma(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}]\) for a drive half-spacing \(a\); replace \(a\) by \(c\) for the reciprocal measurement field. The first-order change in impedance is the product of the two signed gradients times minus the conductivity perturbation and the sphere volume. The centre sample approximates the volume integral when both background gradients vary slowly over the sphere.

At \(z=8\) mm the result is \(-3.86\) mΩ, a \(-3.86\,\mu\mathrm V\) perturbation at 1 mA, about 48 parts per million of baseline. Increasing depth to 16 mm leaves only \(-0.320\) mΩ. A point midway between one source and one sensing contact can experience a very different gradient alignment from a point on the common centreline. This is why a general inclusion shift should be calculated by differentiating the vector field before taking the dot product. Replacing each gradient by its magnitude would forbid the experimentally possible positive changes. A point inclusion moved twice as deep does not, in general, change the answer by one universal inverse power. Each field samples a different combination of depth and contact separation.

Retain unknown contact impedance as a separate physical parameter

A metal current terminal is nearly equipotential internally but can have an interface voltage drop at the sample. Let \(U_i\) be its metal potential, \(u\) the potential on the adjacent sample, \(j_i=\gamma\partial_n u\) positive into the body and \(z_i\) an area-specific contact impedance in Ω m². A simple local interface law is \(U_i-u=z_i j_i\), together with \(\int j_i\,dA=I_i\). At an inactive metal terminal only this integral is zero. Current can enter on one side and return on the other if the sample potential varies appreciably across its face. A high-impedance voltmeter does not by itself make that metal patch equivalent to an absent electrode.

For sample and electrode test fields \((v,W)\), integration by parts and the contact law give

\[B((u,U),(v,W))=\int_\Omega\gamma\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dV+\sum_i\int_{S_i}\frac{(U_i-u)(W_i-v)}{z_i}\,dA=\sum_i I_i W_i .\]

Here the current and potential gauges are chosen consistently on both drive and reciprocity solves. The contact product is as symmetric as the bulk product, so finite reciprocal contact impedances retain equality upon drive/sense exchange. If the contact changes, differentiating \(1/z_i\) introduces a minus, and the minus from differentiating the full field equations cancels it. The additional first-order sensitivity is \(+\sum_i\int\delta z_i j_{i,d}j_{i,m}\,dA\), with both current densities normalised to unit terminal current. In a uniform two-terminal contact of area \(A\), the two normalised currents are \(1/A\) and one contact contributes \(+\delta z/A\), as an ordinary series contact should. This independent limit fixes both the positive sign and the square-metre convention.

For a controlled reconstruction, form one column per candidate sample perturbation and another per uncertain contact coefficient. Weight measured channels by their noise covariance. Two nearly proportional columns then warn that bulk variation and degrading contact cannot be separated by those channels over that bandwidth. Reciprocity catches lead swaps and some drift, but a reciprocal change in a contact can pass this check. Repeat with a different electrode pattern, or calibrate against a homogeneous reference block at the same frequency. To verify a numerical sensitivity, perturb the contact alone by equal positive and negative amounts and compare the centred impedance difference with the surface integral. Then perturb the sample alone and compare with the volume integral. A successful combined test could otherwise conceal equal and opposite implementation errors.

Designing a displacement bridge at its noise limit

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A moving electrode changes two capacitances in opposite directions. Derive the displacement signal, reconstruct its noise in metres and select a carrier voltage consistent with measured mechanical stiffness and electrical losses.

Find the voltage of the moving electrode before calculating sensitivity

Consider three plane electrodes with common active area. At the centred position the middle conductor is separated by \(g\) from each outer electrode and each gap has capacitance \(C_0\). Displacement \(x\) toward the positive drive electrode reduces one gap and increases the other. Use \(s=x/g\), where \(|s|<1\). Drive the outer electrodes with coherent sinusoidal voltages \(+V_d\) and \(-V_d\), with \(V_d\) the rms amplitude of either electrode relative to ground. The middle conductor leads to a high-impedance amplifier. Its cable and input provide a fixed total capacitance \(C_p\) to ground. Leakage is initially negligible over one carrier period; the input draws negligible signal current. All formulas for carrier voltages in this project use rms amplitudes.

\[C_+=\frac{C_0}{1-s},\]
\[C_-=\frac{C_0}{1+s}.\]

The middle node has zero net charge at the carrier frequency. Its instantaneous ac charge is \(C_+(V_n-V_d)+C_-(V_n+V_d)+C_p V_n\). Set the sum to zero and solve the resulting linear equation for \(V_n\). After combining the gap capacitances the result simplifies considerably:

\[\frac{V_n}{V_d}=\frac{C_+-C_-}{C_++C_-+C_p}=\frac{2C_0s}{2C_0+C_p(1-s^2)}.\]

For zero parasitic capacitance the response is exactly \(V_d x/g\) throughout the ideal geometry. Each individual capacitance is strongly curved near an electrode. Charge conservation divides one nonlinear combination by another and removes that curvature from their voltage ratio. Additional capacitance reduces the small-signal gain and introduces an odd cubic term. Let \(\eta=2C_0/(2C_0+C_p)\). Expanding about the centre gives

\[V_n/V_d=\eta s[1+(1-\eta)s^2+\cdots].\]

The derivative at zero is \(K=\eta V_d/g\) in volts per metre. A small-signal calibration taken only at the origin implicitly accepts this cubic distortion at larger displacements.

For \(C_0=2.00\) pF, \(C_p=4.00\) pF, \(g=100\,\mu\mathrm m\) and \(V_d=1.00\) V, \(K=5000\) V/m. A displacement of 100 nm produces \(0.5000\) mV. At 20 μm, the exact signal is \(0.10204\) V against the linear prediction \(0.10000\) V. The 2.04% curvature would be apparent in a calibration over the wider range. A single proportional calibration cannot remove it. A fit to the ratio itself has a physical parameter, \(C_p/(2C_0)\), which can also be checked by measuring total input capacitance with the mechanical element held at centre.

Translate each readout fluctuation into equivalent displacement

Represent the amplifier near the carrier by an input series voltage noise density \(e_n\) and a shunt current noise density \(i_n\). With the drives treated as small-signal grounds for a noise perturbation, the central node presents \(C_T=2C_0+C_p\) at the centre. The impedance magnitude for current fluctuations is \(1/(\omega C_T)\). For mutually independent noise sources and a narrow demodulation band the corresponding input-referred displacement amplitude spectral density is

\[S_{x,e}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{e_n^2+\left(\frac{i_n}{\omega C_T}\right)^2}.\]

Use noise densities and gain referred to the same detection channel. An rms sine amplitude, a peak-amplitude display and a lock-in reading with an unreported normalisation can differ by factors involving \(\sqrt2\). Here noise figures denote the effective one-sided densities referred to the recovered rms carrier channel. For a white spectrum and an equivalent noise bandwidth \(B_e\) the displacement standard deviation equals the density times \(\sqrt{B_e}\). The displayed cut-off frequency of a multi-pole lock-in filter need not equal its equivalent noise bandwidth. A calibration using a known injected sideband measures both the gain convention and the actual band response.

At 100 kHz, \(e_n=10\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\), \(i_n=10\,\mathrm{fA/\sqrt{Hz}}\) and \(C_T=8.00\) pF, current noise produces \(1.99\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\). Adding variances gives \(10.20\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\), or \(2.04\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\) at the stated gain. A 100 Hz equivalent noise bandwidth gives 20.4 pm rms of electrical readout noise. Cable capacitance reduces the voltage made by current noise but also reduces signal gain. In fact \(C_T K=2C_0 V_d/g\), so their ratio for current noise is independent of \(C_p\) in this model. The voltage-noise contribution grows with cable capacitance. Higher carrier frequency initially reduces the current contribution but reaches a plateau set by voltage noise.

Derive backaction for a floating sensing plate

Electrical drive can change the mechanical stiffness of the sensor. For each gap take half the squared rms gap voltage times its capacitance derivative. This follows either by averaging the instantaneous plate pressure over a carrier cycle or by differentiating electrical coenergy with the middle node at its charge-balanced voltage. Mechanical motion must be slow compared with the carrier. Since \(C_-'\) is negative, the two signed forces cancel at the centre. For a small positive \(s\), write \(V_n\simeq\eta V_ds\),

\[C_+'\simeq(C_0/g)(1+2s),\]
\[C_-'\simeq-(C_0/g)(1-2s).\]

The leading imbalance is

\[F=\tfrac12 C_+'(V_d-V_n)^2+\tfrac12 C_-'(-V_d-V_n)^2\simeq\frac{2C_0 V_d^2}{g^2}(1-\eta)x .\]

The electromagnetic stiffness \(K_e=2C_0 V_d^2(1-\eta)/g^2\) subtracts from the mechanical restoring stiffness \(k\). If \(C_p=0\), the equal field magnitudes on both sides cancel exactly for every displacement in ideal plane geometry. To check directly, the positive gap field is

\[[V_d(1-s)]/[g(1-s)]=V_d/g.\]

The negative gap field has the same magnitude. A charge amplifier holding the central electrode at virtual ground would change this condition and have a different backaction; its calibration must use its own circuit. For a finite \(C_p\), the measured change of resonance with carrier voltage can identify electrical coupling independently of the signal voltage.

With \(k=0.020\) N/m the nominal configuration has \(K_e=0.00020\,\mathrm{N/m}\) at 1 V and \(0.00080\,\mathrm{N/m}\) at 2 V. At 2 V the resonance ratio for the same effective mass is

\[\sqrt{1-0.0008/0.020}=0.980.\]

A 2% frequency decrease measures roughly a 4% reduction of stiffness. If the displacement is imposed by a very stiff external positioner this effect changes the force needed to hold position. If the sample is itself a force sensor, the same effect changes the mechanical conversion between applied force and displacement. Quoting an electronic noise floor without specifying which mechanical situation is intended can hide the larger uncertainty.

Choose a feasible drive and include Brownian motion

Raising the carrier at fixed frequency increases displacement signal. It may also heat the dielectrics, exceed amplifier headroom or significantly soften the spring. Suppose each gap at centre has an effective loss tangent \(\tan\delta\), with a real conductance \(\omega C_0\tan\delta\) at the carrier. Its dissipated power is conductance times the squared rms voltage. Add both gaps to find

\[P=2\omega C_0\tan\delta V_d^2.\]

Fixed parasitic capacitance at the stationary middle node has negligible carrier voltage at exact balance in this model. An actual shield or cable dielectric connected directly to one of the drive nodes will have its own loss. A calorimetric or current phase measurement should include that extra load.

With effective \(\tan\delta=0.010\), the two active gaps dissipate 25.13 nW per squared volt at 100 kHz. A 100 nW heat budget gives \(V_d\le1.995\) V. A driver that remains linear to 2.50 V and a softening allowance \(K_e\le0.1k\) permit 2.50 V and 3.16 V respectively. The thermal bound sets the largest practical drive under these assumptions. For the electrical readout alone, the best feasible setting is thus 1.995 V and the electrical floor is about \(1.02\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\). A separate limit on the electric field or measurement amplitude could be introduced as another experimentally justified constraint.

Now let the mechanical element have viscous damping \(b=1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\) at \(T=300\) K. In thermal equilibrium the one-sided Brownian force density is

\[\sqrt{4k_BT b}=1.29\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{N/\sqrt{Hz}}.\]

Far below mechanical resonance its displacement density is this force divided by \(k-K_e\). At 1 V it is \(6.50\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\), already greater than the electrical term. At 1.995 V it is about \(6.70\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\). The combined density adds in quadrature, giving about 6.81 at 1 V and 6.78 at the heat limit. A minimum of the total can occur between them. For an imposed position of a rigid stage the Brownian motion of this compliant probe may be outside the measurement model; for a freely suspended sensing mass it is part of the signal uncertainty. The optimisation criterion follows the physical quantity the instrument measures.

Use the phase and polarity of a calibration to track false signals

Middle-node leakage to ground with conductance \(G\) changes the circuit equation to \([G+i\omega C_T]V_n=i\omega(C_+-C_-)V_d\) at small displacement. The ideal gain acquires a factor \(i\omega C_T/(G+i\omega C_T)\). For a 10 MΩ leakage path and 8 pF, the corner is 1.99 kHz. At 100 kHz the magnitude reduction is about 0.02% and the phase lead about \(1.14^\circ\). At 2 kHz both quadratures matter. A drift in humidity that lowers the resistance can look like a gain change if only the in-phase channel is recorded. Measure a known displacement at two carrier frequencies and fit both quadratures before interpreting it as a change in geometry.

Suppose the two actual drives are \(V_d(1+a)\) and \(-V_d(1-a)\). At centre the output becomes \(\eta V_da\), equivalent in the small-signal calibration to displacement \(ag\). An amplitude imbalance of one part in ten thousand gives a 10 nm offset at \(g=100\,\mu\mathrm m\), many orders above a short-band noise floor expressed in picometres. Reverse the mechanical calibration displacement to isolate odd signal, measure the voltages at the electrode terminals and compare the nulled bridge under exchange of the drive channels. Reversing both carrier phases simply reverses both desired signal and this coherently driven offset; by itself it cannot distinguish them. An offset stable over a short scan can be estimated from two symmetric calibration displacements, whereas a drifting imbalance requires a reference or simultaneous drive measurement.

Find the minimum for a compliant probe instead of guessing at it

It is possible to optimise the free probe analytically up to a scalar root. Write \(y=V_d^2\), \(K_e=hy\), and \(a\) for the readout displacement density at unit drive multiplied by one volt. Let \(d_F=\sqrt{4k_BT b}\) be the Brownian force density. At displacement frequencies well below the resonance, the total variance density has an electrical term falling as \(1/y\) and a mechanical term that grows as the spring softens:

\[S_x(y)=\frac{a^2}{y}+\frac{d_F^2}{(k-hy)^2},\]
\[{dS_x\over dy}=-{a^2\over y^2}+{2h d_F^2\over(k-hy)^3}.\]

On the stable interval the first derivative strictly increases when \(h>0\). The first term starts at negative infinity and the second diverges at the stiffness boundary. Hence there is one unconstrained minimum. Solve a bracketed scalar equation for \(y\), then compare with each experimental upper bound in volts squared. At zero parasitic capacitance the ideal floating plate has \(h=0\); total noise then falls monotonically toward an asymptotic Brownian floor. A numerical routine that divides by \(h\) would fail precisely at this informative physical limit. In either case, a force-noise specification referred to the external sample can produce a different optimum because force conversion multiplies displacement noise by stiffness.

With the baseline values \(a=2.039\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m\,V/\sqrt{Hz}}\), \(h=2.00\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{N/(m\,V^2)}\), \(d_F\simeq1.287\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{N/\sqrt{Hz}}\). Substitution brackets the stationary point between 1.45 and 1.50 V. At roughly 1.47 V the density is about \(6.72\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\), a shallow minimum compared with the heat-limited result. A tiny uncertainty in amplifier noise or in the support damping could shift the exact optimum appreciably while having very little effect on achievable resolution. Reporting a numerically optimised voltage to six decimals would imply a knowledge of the physical system absent from these inputs.

A direct bench experiment can test whether the excess really comes from compliance. Compare the demodulated spectrum with the centre plate tightly held and with it free. The electrical floor should be similar if the clamping keeps cable capacitance and carrier coupling fixed. The free probe should acquire a mechanical resonance which moves with \(V_d^2\), and the low-frequency component should track the inferred static stiffness. A large peak locked to carrier-drive amplitude modulation in both experiments suggests coherent feedthrough. Also check that the noise estimate comes from a frequency band above drift yet below resonance, and that the inferred amplitude density times the square root of the bandwidth predicts repeated stationary readings. These practical checks distinguish a readout-limited displacement estimate from a calibrated measurement of actual thermal motion.

Least squares and orthogonal factorisation

Numerical linear algebra

Fit a straight line to inconsistent observations, derive its residual conditions and explain why an orthogonal factorisation can preserve accuracy better than the normal equations.

From first principles

Derive the orthogonality condition for a fit

With more observations than unknown coefficients, the linear prediction \(Ax\) can miss the observed vector \(b\). Suppose squared residuals carry equal weight and choose coefficients to minimize \(\|b-Ax\|_2^2\). Try any change \(x\mapsto x+t d\), where the direction \(d\) is fixed but the scalar \(t\) can have either sign. Expand with \(r=b-Ax\),

\[\|r-tAd\|_2^2=\|r\|_2^2-2t\,d^TA^Tr+t^2\|Ad\|_2^2 .\]

At a minimum the linear term must vanish in every direction, so \(A^Tr=0\). This says the residual is perpendicular to every vector the model can generate. Rearranging gives the normal equations,

\[A^TAx=A^Tb .\]

If \(A\) has independent columns then the quadratic term is positive for every nonzero direction and the minimizer is unique. A thin QR factorization with orthonormal columns \(Q\) and triangular \(R\) yields the equivalent equation \(Rx=Q^Tb\) and avoids explicitly forming products that can worsen numerical conditioning.

Background · Orthogonal projector
\[P=QQ^\mathsf T\]

Columns of Q must be orthonormal in the stated real inner product. Least squares removes the component of the data orthogonal to the fitted range.

Derivation and applications

Impose one exact calibration condition on a noisy fit

Some data describe noisy readings while another relationship is an exact conservation or calibration requirement. Model noisy data by \(Ax\simeq b\) with independent equal-variance errors, but require \(c^Tx=d\) exactly. Assume \(A\) has full column rank and \(c\ne0\). Write \(H=A^TA\), and let \(x_0\) denote the ordinary least-squares solution. Expanding around \(x_0\) removes the cross term because the residual there is orthogonal to every column of \(A\),

\[\|Ax-b\|^2=\|Ax_0-b\|^2+(x-x_0)^TH(x-x_0).\]

Thus the constrained fit is the closest feasible point in the metric determined by \(H\). Introduce a multiplier \(\lambda\) for the exact condition in half the squared objective. Setting its derivatives to zero and solving the first equation gives

\[H(x-x_0)+c\lambda=0,\]
\[x=x_0-H^{-1}c\lambda .\]

Applying the constraint determines the multiplier and the increase in squared residual,

\[\lambda=\frac{c^Tx_0-d}{c^TH^{-1}c},\]
\[\Delta\|r\|^2=\frac{(c^Tx_0-d)^2}{c^TH^{-1}c}.\]

The denominator is positive. In computation the expression \(H^{-1}c\) denotes a solve, preferably using existing QR factors. An already satisfied constraint leaves the fit unchanged. Directions weakly constrained by the measured data can accommodate an exact calibration with little increase in residual.

Worked example

Reconcile two fitted quantities with an exact total

An ordinary fit gives \(x_0=(2,5)^T\) with \(H=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)\). The correct total is \(x_1+x_2=6\). Now \(H^{-1}c=(0.25,1)^T\), the denominator is \(1.25\), and \(\lambda=0.8\). The adjusted pair is \((1.8,4.2)\). Its extra squared residual is \(4(0.2)^2+(0.8)^2=0.80\), with the less precisely measured second coordinate absorbing most of the correction.

Second application

Enforce a zero offset in a calibration line

Three equally weighted readings at \(t=0,1,2\) are \(1,3,5\). The free fit is \(1+2t\). A separate exact zero calibration demands an intercept of zero. Along that feasible line the slope obeys \((0^2+1^2+2^2)m=0+3+10\), giving \(m=2.6\). The three residuals \(1,0.4,-0.2\) have squared norm \(1.20\), a directly observable cost of the added calibration condition.

Replace exact consistency by an optimisation problem

Three thermometers at different positions need not lie on a common straight temperature profile. Let the rows of \(A\) contain the values of candidate basis functions at those positions and let \(b\) contain the observations. With more independent measurements than unknown coefficients, \(Ax=b\) may have no solution. A least-squares fit chooses \(x\) to minimise \(\|b-Ax\|_2^2\). Equal unweighted terms assume the deviations are comparable in units and importance. If one instrument is much noisier than another, that assumption deserves review before interpreting a fitted slope.

Take any coefficient perturbation \(sd\). Expanding the squared mismatch gives \(\|b-Ax\|^2-2s\,d^TA^T(b-Ax)+s^2\|Ad\|^2\). At a minimum the coefficient of \(s\) must vanish for every \(d\). Hence \(A^TAx=A^Tb\). This is also sufficient: when the linear term vanishes, every perturbation adds a nonnegative square. Geometrically the residual is perpendicular to the column space of \(A\). Full column rank makes the minimiser unique, since no nonzero coefficient perturbation then leaves the fitted values unchanged.

Background · When is a fitted residual independent of the fitted coefficients?

Assume the design matrix has full column rank and the observation errors have covariance \(\sigma^2 I\). Write the parameter error as \(A^+\epsilon\) and the residual as \((I-P)\epsilon\), with \(P=AA^+\). Their cross-covariance is

\[\operatorname{Cov}(\hat x-x,r)=\sigma^2 A^+(I-P)^T=0.\]

The last equality follows because \(I-P\) maps every vector into the orthogonal complement of the columns of \(A\), where \(A^+\) vanishes. For Gaussian errors the two quantities are jointly Gaussian, so zero cross-covariance also gives independence. With non-Gaussian noise their projections may still be statistically dependent. Orthogonality is always an algebraic property of the least-squares fit; independence needs an assumption about how errors were generated.

Fit three imperfect readings

Take positions \(t=-1,0,1\) and deviations from a baseline temperature of \(1,2,5\) K. Fit \(b(t)\simeq c+mt\), using a constant and a linear column. Their dot product is zero because the positions sum to zero. Their squared lengths are three and two. Multiplying the observation vector by each column gives 8 and 4, so the normal equations are diagonal and yield \(c=8/3\) K and \(m=2\) K per position unit.

The predicted values are \(2/3,8/3,14/3\) K. The residual \(b-Ax=(1/3,-2/3,1/3)^T\) has zero sum and zero dot product with \((-1,0,1)^T\), as required. Changing the intercept cannot lower the sum of squares because the positive and negative residuals already balance; changing the slope has the analogous weighted balance. The squared residual norm is \(2/3\) K². This measures fit to these readings. It does not tell whether a line will predict temperatures well outside the sampled interval.

Remove redundant precision loss

If \(A\) has full column rank and singular values \(\sigma_i\), then \(A^TA\) has eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\). Consequently

\[\kappa_2(A^TA)=\kappa_2(A)^2.\]

When two model columns are nearly parallel, forming the products explicitly can lose significant information about their small difference. For example, a straight-line fit on positions clustered around a very large offset has nearly proportional constant and position columns. Centering and scaling positions improves the parameterisation and gives an intercept with a local physical interpretation.

Alternatively, factor \(A=QR\) with \(Q\) having orthonormal columns and \(R\) upper triangular. Decompose the residual into its orthogonal parts inside and outside the column space:

\[\|b-Ax\|^2=\|Q^Tb-Rx\|^2+\|(I-QQ^T)b\|^2.\]

The second term is independent of the fit. Solve \(Rx=Q^Tb\) by back substitution. Householder reflectors build this factorisation using length-preserving transformations. In the three-temperature example, dividing the constant column by \(\sqrt3\) and the position column by \(\sqrt2\) already produces \(Q\). The transformed data are \(8/\sqrt3\) and \(4/\sqrt2\). Dividing by the diagonal of \(R\) recovers exactly the same fit. When the columns are nearly dependent, inspect the rank explicitly, using column pivoting or singular values, before giving a physical interpretation to individual coefficients.

Include measurement weights explicitly

If an instrument gives independent errors with standard deviations \(s_i\), divide row \(i\) of both \(A\) and \(b\) by \(s_i\). The sum of squares now measures squared differences in units of each sensor’s own standard deviation. For two readings 0 and 10 with standard deviations 1 and 3, a constant fit minimises \(c^2+(10-c)^2/9\). Differentiation gives \(c=1\), versus the unweighted mean of 5. A reliable low reading exerts more influence on the weighted result.

This weighting still assumes independence and an appropriate error description. If both readings share an uncertain offset, repeated observations can reduce independent fluctuations without removing the offset. Near a rank deficiency, a small weighted residual likewise cannot establish separate physical identifiability of the fitted coefficients. Examine uncertainty or singular directions in the parameter map as well as the final mismatch.

Derive a prediction after withholding one observation

Let \(A\) have full column rank and use equal independent data variances. The fitted vector is \(\hat y=Hy\) with \(H=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\); implement the projection with a stable factorisation. Suppose row \(i\) is withheld and the reduced design stays full rank. Its new prediction at that location, \(\hat y_i^{(-i)}\), can be obtained without refitting the entire data set. Replace \(y_i\) in the full data by this predicted value. The model fitted to the reduced observations now also satisfies the full normal equations because the added row has precisely zero residual at that fit.

Apply the original hat matrix to the modified vector. Its \(i\)-th fitted value is \(\hat y_i+h_{ii}(\hat y_i^{(-i)}-y_i)\), which must equal \(\hat y_i^{(-i)}\). Rearranging gives

\[y_i-\hat y_i^{(-i)}=(y_i-\hat y_i)/(1-h_{ii}).\]

This relation shows how leverage can mask an error inside the training data. If \(h_{ii}=1\), the denominator vanishes because that observation alone identifies some parameter combination. Withholding it then requires a different rank convention or additional modelling information.

Predict the end of a calibration line from its other readings

Fit \(y=a+bt\) to \((t,y)=(0,0),(1,1),(2,4)\). The mean time is one and the mean response is \(5/3\). The slope from centred data is \([(-1)(-5/3)+(1)(7/3)]/[1+1]=2\), giving intercept \(-1/3\). The fitted values are \((-1/3,5/3,11/3)\) and residuals are \((1/3,-2/3,1/3)\). Taken individually, either endpoint looks better fitted than the midpoint. But each endpoint also has disproportionate influence on the slope used to fit itself.

For a straight line the intercept and centred-time columns are orthogonal, so \(h_{ii}=1/3+(t_i-1)^2/2\). The endpoint leverages are \(5/6\), the central leverage is \(1/3\), and their sum is two, the number of fitted parameters. The leave-one-out residuals are consequently \(2,-1,2\). Verify the last directly: the first two readings define the line \(y=t\); it predicts 2 at the final time, while the held-out observation is 4. For the central reading the two endpoints define \(y=2t\), predicting 2 instead of 1.

The scientific question determines whether this test is relevant. Dropping an endpoint forces extrapolation, whereas dropping the midpoint tests interpolation between two retained settings. Averaging these three squared errors mixes two distinct prediction tasks. If future measurements will be taken only inside the calibrated interval, a separate evaluation there is more informative. Serially correlated measurements also invalidate the interpretation of neighbouring held-out readings as independent validation, although the algebraic leave-one-out identity for a fixed design remains valid.

For these three observations the sum of squared training residuals is

\[1/9+4/9+1/9=2/3.\]

The sum after leaving each observation out in turn is \(4+1+4=9\). The discrepancy arises even with an exactly implemented fitting routine. Each training prediction has already used the observation against which it is compared. The difference becomes acute near the boundary because that observation has supplied much of the information about its own fitted value.

Singular values and minimum-norm solutions

Numerical linear algebra

Resolve a linear measurement into independent directions, compute its pseudoinverse and predict the effect of truncating a weakly observed component.

From first principles

Count what a rectangular measurement can recover

For a real matrix \(A\), the squared output length in a unit input direction \(v\) is \(v^TA^TAv\). Since \(A^TA\) is symmetric and nonnegative, it has orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_j\) with eigenvalues \(\sigma_j^2\ge0\). Each positive eigenvalue defines a unit output direction \(u_j=Av_j/\sigma_j\). Distinct orthogonal input eigenvectors then have orthogonal output directions because

\[u_i^T u_j=\frac{v_i^TA^TAv_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\delta_{ij}.\]

Resolve an input into these vectors and the null space. A component along \(v_j\) is amplified by \(\sigma_j\), while every null component disappears. For measured data \(b\), matching the observed output components gives

\[x_{\min}=\sum_{\sigma_j>0}\frac{u_j^Tb}{\sigma_j}\,v_j .\]

Adding any null vector produces the same prediction but increases the squared norm by its own squared length. This proves the minimum-norm choice. Data perpendicular to all the \(u_j\) remain as a least-squares residual, and noise along a small positive singular value can be amplified substantially.

Background · Singular value
\[Av_i=\sigma_i u_i\]

Input and output directions can live in different spaces. A small positive singular value can cause large inverse amplification.

Derivation and applications

Find the smallest reconstruction in physically scaled coordinates

The minimum-norm solution of an underdetermined fit depends on which units and physical scale define the norm. Consider exact independent constraints \(Ax=b\) with fewer rows than columns, and let \(W\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix of penalties. Minimize \(\tfrac12 x^TWx\) among all feasible states. A reference value could first be subtracted if zero represents an inappropriate baseline. Factor \(W=L^TL\) and express the optimization in the scaled variable \(y=Lx\),

\[Ax=b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (AL^{-1})y=b,\]
\[x^TWx=\|y\|_2^2 .\]

Thus the usual minimum-norm singular-value solution applies to the scaled matrix. An alternative derivation introduces a multiplier \(\lambda\). Differentiating \(\tfrac12x^TWx-\lambda^T(Ax-b)\) gives

\[Wx=A^T\lambda.\]

Substituting in the constraints yields

\[(AW^{-1}A^T)\lambda=b,\]
\[x=W^{-1}A^T(AW^{-1}A^T)^{-1}b .\]

The reduced matrix is positive definite for independent constraint rows. For any feasible perturbation \(h\) with \(Ah=0\), the cross term in the objective vanishes:

\[(x+h)^TW(x+h)-x^TWx=2\lambda^TAh+h^TWh=h^TWh .\]

This establishes uniqueness and explains the role of the weighting. Directions that data leave unresolved are assigned by a stated metric. Changing units while transforming both \(A\) and the metric consistently preserves the physical result.

Worked example

Distribute a measured total with unequal motion costs

A balance gives \(x_1+x_2=10\) and the squared cost is \(x_1^2+4x_2^2\). Stationarity requires \(x_1=4x_2\), yielding \((8,2)\) at cost \(80\). The unweighted minimum-norm pair \((5,5)\) satisfies the balance but has weighted cost \(125\). There is no change in agreement with the measurement.

Second application

Check invariance under a change of coordinate units

Write a second balance \(u+v=6\) and equal physical cost \(u^2+v^2\). If \(w=100v\), the new row is \((1,0.01)\) and the cost is \(u^2+0.0001w^2\). The weighted solution is \(u=3,w=300\), recovering \(v=3\). Applying an unweighted norm to the rescaled coordinates instead gives \(u=6/(1.0001)\simeq5.9994\), exposing a unit-dependent extra assumption.

Find directions that a rectangular map measures separately

For real \(A\), the matrix \(A^TA\) is symmetric positive semidefinite since \(v^TA^TAv=\|Av\|^2\). Choose orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_i\) with positive eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\) and define \(u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i\). Dotting two such vectors shows that the \(u_i\) are orthonormal. Completing both bases gives the singular value decomposition \(A=U\Sigma V^T\). Each \(\sigma_i\) is a gain from one parameter direction into a corresponding output direction. Zero singular values indicate directions that no observation in this model can distinguish.

To minimise \(\|Ax-b\|_2\), express both vectors in these orthogonal bases. For each positive singular value the residual component vanishes by taking \(v_i^Tx=u_i^Tb/\sigma_i\). Output components outside the range remain unavoidable, while input components in the nullspace can take any value without changing the fit. Setting those free components to zero gives the minimum-norm least-squares answer. This derivation identifies the pseudoinverse without assuming either a square matrix or a unique exact solution.

Distinguish an unobserved coordinate from an error

Suppose two instruments read \(b_1=x_1+x_2\) and \(b_2=0.02x_3\), giving readings 4 and 0.06. One singular value is \(\sqrt2\), corresponding to the mean direction of \(x_1,x_2\); another is 0.02 along \(x_3\). The difference \(x_1-x_2\) is unobserved. The minimum-norm exact fit is \((2,2,3)^T\), and every \((2+s,2-s,3)^T\) has identical data. More precision on the mean measurement cannot determine the hidden difference.

A noise change of +0.01 in the second reading raises the reconstructed third parameter by 0.5. If the admissible parameter scale is one, this is a substantial perturbation. Truncating the 0.02 singular direction instead sets \(x_3=0\). Then the residual grows from zero to the entire second reading, but its noise no longer controls the fitted third coordinate. The decision trades bias against uncertainty; it does not prove that the true third parameter is zero.

Assess a reduced measurement model

Keeping the largest \(k\) singular components produces \(A_k=\sum_{i\leq k}\sigma_i u_iv_i^T\). In the spectral norm its error is \(\sigma_{k+1}\): expansion in the right singular basis gives this upper bound, and applying the difference to \(v_{k+1}\) attains it. Any rank-\(k\) alternative must annihilate some unit vector in the span of the first \(k+1\) right singular vectors, on which the original map has norm at least \(\sigma_{k+1}\). Hence no lower-rank replacement improves the worst-case absolute output error.

That best-approximation result concerns forward predictions in a specified norm. Inverting small singular values magnifies noise, so a good low-rank approximation for forward use does not automatically determine the appropriate inverse for parameter recovery. Work with physically scaled parameters and noise-weighted observations before deciding which singular components are meaningful.

Choose an output before discarding a direction

Suppose an instrument observes \(x_1\) accurately and \(0.001x_2\) with uncertainty 0.01. The second variable alone has uncertainty of order ten. The output \(x_1\), however, has no projection onto the poorly observed direction. Its uncertainty receives no contribution from that amplification. Conversely the output \(x_1+x_2\) inherits the full uncertainty. This distinction can change whether a rank decision is acceptable. The spectrum describes the data acquisition matrix, while the projection of the chosen observable onto right singular vectors determines which losses of information affect the reported result.

Prove that truncation is optimal in two matrix norms

When compressing a dense linear map \(A\), decide which discrepancy is being minimised over all approximations of rank at most \(k\). Its spectral norm measures the largest output error over unit inputs. For any rank-\(k\) map \(B\), consider the span of the first \(k+1\) right singular vectors of \(A\). Its dimension exceeds the rank of the restriction of \(B\), so there exists a unit vector \(w\) in this span satisfying \(Bw=0\). Therefore \(\|(A-B)w\|=\|Aw\|\ge\sigma_{k+1}\). Retaining the first \(k\) singular pairs attains this lower bound, as its discarded part has largest singular value exactly \(\sigma_{k+1}\).

For the Frobenius norm, project each column of \(A\) into the column space of a candidate \(B\). Orthogonal projection is the closest approximation with columns in that space. If its projector is \(P\), the captured squared norm is \(\sum_j\sigma_j^2\|Pu_j\|^2\). The weights \(\|Pu_j\|^2\) lie between zero and one and sum to at most \(k\). The largest possible weighted sum assigns weight one to the \(k\) largest squared singular values. Hence the optimal discarded Frobenius norm is \((\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2)^{1/2}\). Both arguments require the norm and coordinate scaling just stated. Entrywise maximum error or application-specific weighted errors generally have different optimisers.

Decide how many responses a reduced map must reproduce

Suppose the four singular values of a unit-scaled instrument model are 8, 3, 2 and 1. A rank-two approximation has best possible worst-case gain error two and Frobenius error \(\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5\). Its relative squared Frobenius loss is \(5/(64+9+4+1)=5/78\), approximately 6.41 percent. Describing this as a loss of just 6.41 percent can be misleading if the experiment deliberately excites the third singular direction. For that unit input, the exact output has length two and the compressed output is zero, giving complete loss of the signal of interest.

Suppose instead inputs have equal independent unit variance in all four right singular coordinates. Then the expected squared error in the output is the sum of the discarded squared gains, exactly five. If the third coordinate is excited with variance one hundred while the others retain variance one, the error from truncating that coordinate alone becomes 400. Under that different usage distribution the meaningful objective is \(\|(A-B)C^{1/2}\|_F\), where \(C\) is the input covariance.

In these diagonal coordinates the weighted gains become \(8,3,20,1\). The best rank-two design for expected output error now retains directions three and one, and the discarded expected squared error becomes

\[3^2+1^2=10.\]

The ranking changed because the physical question changed. It did not change the optimality theorem for the unweighted matrix; it changed which matrix correctly represents the intended error measure.

When an input covariance is singular, some directions are never excited under the stated design. The weighted objective can be indifferent to large errors in just those directions. A later change of operating regime may activate them, so a reduced model qualified for one input ensemble should retain the conditions under which its ranking was derived.

Decide whether a low-rank approximation is worthwhile

The best rank-\(k\) approximation discards the tail of the singular spectrum. Its relative Frobenius error is the square root of discarded squared singular values divided by total squared singular values, with the square root applied to the complete ratio.

Removing unequal scales before an iteration

Numerical linear algebra

Derive the change of variables behind diagonal preconditioning, then measure what it can and cannot repair in a two-spring system.

Coordinates in the energy

Suppose one displacement is measured in millimetres and the other in metres. A steepest-descent direction depends on the Euclidean lengths of the coordinates. The physical minimum does not. Let \(A\) be symmetric positive definite, \(D=\operatorname{diag}(A)\), and introduce \(y=D^{1/2}x\). Substituting \(x=D^{-1/2}y\) in the energy produces

\[\widetilde A=D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2},\]
\[\widetilde b=D^{-1/2}b.\]

The diagonal of the new matrix is one. Its off-diagonal entry is the original coupling divided by the geometric mean of the two direct stiffnesses. This is a dimensionless measure of interaction. The transformed gradient step maps back to \(x_{j+1}=x_j+\alpha D^{-1}(b-Ax_j)\).

Choosing a common step

For any eigenvector of the transformed matrix the iteration multiplies the error by \(1-\alpha\lambda\). With eigenvalues in \([m,M]\), equalise the two extreme absolute factors by taking \(1-\alpha m=-(1-\alpha M)\). This gives \(\alpha=2/(M+m)\) and worst-case contraction \((M-m)/(M+m)\). If \(m\) approaches zero the direction barely moves, however accurately the linear algebra is performed.

Two quite different causes of a slow solve

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(100,1)\), an optimal unscaled step shrinks the error by only \(99/101\). Diagonal scaling gives the identity. One step of length one then solves both components exactly. This is the effect of unlike units or stiffness scales.

For a unit-diagonal matrix with coupling 0.95, scaling leaves the matrix unchanged. Its eigenvalues are 1.95 and 0.05. Each optimal step can retain 95% of the error. The nearly free opposing motion is part of the problem itself. A preconditioner that represents the coupling, or a different physical constraint, is needed for faster convergence in that direction.

Derive the time and frequency responses of a relaxing polarisation, locate the loss maximum and decide whether a measured sample contains more than one relaxation process.

From a delayed polarisation to a constitutive equation

A slab of insulating material is placed between parallel electrodes. At first the measured charge increases very quickly when a small voltage is switched on. It then continues to rise slowly even though the applied voltage is fixed. A constant real relative permittivity can describe the final charge but cannot predict this record. The experiment reveals at least two relevant time scales. For a measurement window that resolves only the slower process, treat the fast electronic response as instantaneous and describe the slower polarisation \(P\) by its approach to an equilibrium value.

At fixed weak field the equilibrium slow polarisation is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E\), with \(\Delta\epsilon>0\). Assume the rate of change is proportional to the distance from that equilibrium, with one positive relaxation time \(\tau\). Then

\[\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E(t).\]

This is a phenomenological material law. Its assumptions include linear response about the prepared state, a time-independent \(\tau\), and a sample whose constituents can be described by one dominant relaxation. Testing those assumptions is part of the experiment.

For \(E(t)=E_s\) switched on at zero from an unpolarised state, solve using the integrating factor \(e^{t/\tau}\). The result is

\[P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E_s(1-e^{-t/\tau}).\]

After one \(\tau\), 63.21% of the slow response has developed; after \(3\tau\), 95.02%. Measuring an absolute fraction requires subtracting the fast initial jump and knowing the eventual plateau. A long baseline drift can look like a second relaxation if those references are not controlled.

Derive the complex dielectric response

Now use peak phasors with time dependence \(e^{-i\omega t}\). Differentiation contributes \(-i\omega\), so

\[P/E=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon/(1-i\omega\tau).\]

Include the high-frequency relative permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\), where “high” means above the slow process but below additional electronic resonances. The real and imaginary parts of relative permittivity are

\[\epsilon'=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{\Delta\epsilon}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\]
\[\epsilon''=\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.\]

At fixed field amplitude the polarisation current is

\[\dot P=-i\omega P.\]

The mean electrical work per unit time and volume is

\[\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E^*\dot P)=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon''|E|^2.\]

Loss therefore has positive \(\epsilon''\) in the chosen time convention. Using \(e^{+i\omega t}\) reverses the sign of the imaginary part of permittivity but leaves mean absorbed energy positive when the current convention is changed consistently.

Set \(u=\omega\tau\). Differentiating \(u/(1+u^2)\) gives \((1-u^2)/(1+u^2)^2\). Hence \(\epsilon''\) peaks at \(u=1\), with height \(\Delta\epsilon/2\), and the real part lies halfway between its low- and high-frequency plateaux. The peak identified here is in the imaginary *permittivity*. A plot of dissipated power at fixed field includes an additional factor of frequency and need not have a peak at the same location.

Tie a frequency sweep to a step test

Suppose a material has \(\epsilon_\infty=2\), \(\Delta\epsilon=6\), and a measured loss peak at 1 kHz. Then \(\tau=1/(2\pi\,1000)\simeq159\ \mu\mathrm s\). At the peak the relative permittivity is \(5+3i\) in our convention. A time-domain voltage step should show a slow charge transient reaching 63.2% of its added charge in roughly 159 μs. This comparison connects two experiments that weight electrode offsets and parasitic impedances differently.

An independent shape check follows by eliminating \(u\). Let \(X=\epsilon'-\epsilon_\infty\) and \(Y=\epsilon''\). From the formulas \(X^2+Y^2=\Delta\epsilon X\). Thus \((X-\Delta\epsilon/2)^2+Y^2=(\Delta\epsilon/2)^2\): a single Debye relaxation traces a semicircle in the complex plane. Departures from a semicircle can reflect several relaxation times, spatially inhomogeneous samples, leakage or imperfect fixture correction. They do not uniquely identify the microscopic mechanism.

Identify mobile charge with a transverse field

Fields and the response of matter

Derive a Hall-voltage measurement from transverse force balance, reverse current and field to reject offsets and explore what fails when two carrier types conduct.

Choose signs from a drawing

A rectangular conducting sample has its long axis along \(x\), width \(w\) along \(y\), and thickness \(t\) along \(z\). A uniform magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\) is applied. An external circuit maintains conventional current \(I>0\) in \(+x\). High-impedance transverse probes draw negligible current. Define the measured signal as \(V_H=V(y=0)-V(y=w)\). For uniform transverse field this convention gives

\[V_H=E_yw.\]

Reversing the voltmeter leads reverses the reported sign; writing a carrier sign without a lead convention is incomplete.

With one charge carrier species of number density \(n\) and signed charge \(q\), the drift velocity satisfies

\[j_x=nqv_x=I/(wt).\]

If the transverse velocity vanishes, its magnetic force component is \(-qv_xB\), opposed by \(qE_y\). Thus \(E_y=v_x B=j_xB/(nq)\). The resulting port measurement is

\[V_H=\frac{IB}{nq t}.\]

The width cancels for a uniform bar; changing the contact geometry or using a current source with a nonuniform injection profile can spoil that simplification near the terminals. The cancellation also exposes the sign. For electrons \(q<0\), positive conventional current means that the mobile charge drifts in \(-x\). The inferred transverse field is negative for positive \(B\) in the chosen convention.

Reconstruct density and mobility from separate observations

Take \(t=0.50\) mm, \(I=5.0\) mA, \(B=0.20\) T and a transverse signal of \(-1.25\) mV. The inferred signed Hall coefficient is

\[R_H=tV_H/(IB)=-6.25\times10^{-4}\ {\rm m^3/C}.\]

Using singly charged electrons gives

\[n=1/(e|R_H|)\simeq1.00\times10^{22}\ {\rm m^{-3}}.\]

A longitudinal voltage measurement, after correcting for probe separation, can yield the material conductivity. If \(\sigma=80\) S/m, the single-carrier mobility is

\[\mu=\sigma |R_H|=0.050\ {\rm m^2/(V\,s)}.\]

These operations use different pieces of information. The longitudinal measurement alone gives only the product \(n|q|\mu\). The transverse measurement supplies a sign and, in the one-carrier model, the density independently of mobility. This is an inverse experiment whose two observables have different sensitivities, not a more precise measurement of the same scalar.

One can derive the same result from a stationary Drude equation \(0=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)-m\mathbf v/\tau\). The longitudinal drag gives

\[E_x=mv_x/(q\tau);\]

force balance in \(y\) gives the transverse field just obtained. In a homogeneous single-carrier sample, \(E_y/E_x=q\tau B/m\), the signed Hall angle tangent. At fixed sample and field, doubling \(I\) doubles both voltages in this linear regime and leaves their ratio unchanged. Significant current-dependent apparent density can indicate heating or nonlinear contacts.

Isolate the transverse voltage from a misaligned contact

Practical side contacts seldom sit at exactly the same \(x\). A fraction of the longitudinal voltage can leak into the transverse pair. Write the readout at a fixed current as \(V_\mathrm{m}(B)=V_0+\alpha V_{xx}+V_H(B)\). A background that is even in field cancels in \([V_\mathrm m(B)-V_\mathrm m(-B)]/2\). For example, readings of 3.05 mV and 5.55 mV at \(+B,-B\) return \(-1.25\) mV Hall signal and 4.30 mV of even background. Taking a single measured sign would have diagnosed the wrong carriers.

When a Hall density stops being a particle count

With electrons and holes, longitudinal conductivities add. In a weak magnetic field their transverse contributions also depend on squared mobilities. For positive mobilities \(\mu_e,\mu_h\) and electron and hole densities \(n,p\), the two-carrier weak-field coefficient in the sign convention above is \(R_H=(p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2)/[e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2]\). To see where the square appears, note that one factor of mobility converts the longitudinal field into drift; a second converts the resulting transverse magnetic force into a drift contribution. Summing species currents and imposing zero total transverse current gives the denominator management through the summed longitudinal conductivity.

Equal electron and hole densities with equal mobilities give zero Hall signal while current still flows readily. A single-species inversion would suggest unbounded density. At equal densities but unequal mobilities, the faster species can set the sign even though neither species is more numerous. Additional field dependence, temperature variation and independent structural information are needed before treating a Hall number as a carrier census.

A zero-average voltage can store measurable energy

Connect a capacitor \(C\) across a resistor \(R\) at a uniform absolute temperature \(T\), with no external drive. Ideal circuit equations predict that any deliberately deposited charge decays on time scale \(RC\). At thermal equilibrium, microscopic fluctuations continually exchange charge and energy with the capacitor. Symmetry gives zero mean voltage, but the stored energy involves \(V^2\). In the classical limit a quadratic degree of freedom has mean energy \(k_BT/2\), giving \(\langle V^2\rangle=k_BT/C\). The resistor sets the time scale of equilibration; equilibrium mean-square voltage does not depend on resistance in this ideal model.

Check the dimensions: \(k_BT\) is an energy in joules and capacitance has units coulombs per volt. Their ratio is volts squared. At \(T=300\) K and \(C=100\) pF the RMS voltage is \(\sqrt{k_BT/C}\simeq6.44\ \mu{\rm V}\). A voltmeter that averages the signed voltage for a long time reports a value near zero. Measuring the noise requires an RMS, variance or appropriately calibrated spectral measurement.

Derive the same variance from a spectral measurement

Use a one-sided voltage power spectral density per hertz at positive frequencies. The classical Johnson–Nyquist open-circuit result for a resistor is \(S_R(f)=4k_BTR\) V²/Hz. Its square root is an amplitude spectral density in V/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\), not a voltage variance. Use an equivalent series voltage noise source and the RC transfer function \(H(f)=1/[1+2\pi i fRC]\). A stationary linear system multiplies the voltage power spectrum by \(|H|^2\), so

\[S_C(f)=\frac{4k_BTR}{1+(2\pi fRC)^2},\qquad\]

\int_0^\infty S_C(f)\,df=\frac{k_BT}{C}.

The integral follows by \(u=2\pi RCf\) and \(\int_0^\infty du/(1+u^2)=\pi/2\). It furnishes a detailed check on the factor four, the use of one-sided density, the conversion from hertz to angular frequency and the amplitude-versus-power distinction. If your result disagrees by a constant factor of two or \(2\pi\), audit these conventions before assigning a new noise temperature.

For \(R=10\) kΩ, \(T=300\) K, the low-frequency amplitude spectral density is approximately 12.9 nV/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). The RC corner at \(C=100\) pF is \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\simeq159\) kHz. The open-circuit low-frequency density rises with \(R\) while the bandwidth shrinks in inverse proportion. Their integrated product gives the earlier resistance-independent variance.

Include a real observation bandwidth

Suppose a first measurement adds an ideal low-pass cutoff \(B\). The recorded fraction of equilibrium variance is \(F=(2/\pi)\arctan(B/f_c)\). Half of the noise variance lies below the RC corner. That may be surprising if the corner was introduced as the frequency at which the spectral density reaches half its zero-frequency value. These two properties coincide for this single-pole spectrum for different mathematical reasons.

At \(B=0.1f_c\), \(F\simeq0.06345\), so the recorded RMS is about 25.2% of the infinite-band RMS. At \(B=10f_c\), \(F\simeq0.93655\), giving about 96.8% of the RMS. In an experiment, taking a square root one step too early changes this comparison. A real detector transfer function replaces the sharp cutoff by an integral of \(S_C(f)|H_\mathrm{det}(f)|^2\).

Background · How does a longer voltage exposure alter the measured noise?

An equilibrium resistor–capacitor circuit has voltage covariance \(C_v(s)=(k_BT/C)\exp(-|s|/\tau)\), where \(\tau=RC\). Suppose a digitiser returns the mean voltage during an exposure \(T_m\). Squaring a sample of this mean measures a different variance from averaging the instantaneous voltage squared. In the classical stationary model the two-time integral becomes

\[\mathrm{var}(\bar v)=\frac{2}{T_m^2}\int_0^{T_m}(T_m-s)C_v(s)\,ds=\frac{2k_BT}{C}\frac{u-1+e^{-u}}{u^2},\]
\[u=\frac{T_m}{\tau}.\]

Short exposures recover \(k_BT/C\). At ten correlation times only about 18% of that variance remains. For very long exposures the variance decreases as \(2\tau/T_m\). Taking more closely spaced samples of one exposure cannot undo that averaging.

A physical check and a calibration check

An RC time-domain experiment provides a second observable beyond integrated power. Under the same single-pole equilibrium model the voltage autocovariance is

\[\langle V(t)V(t+\tau)\rangle=(k_BT/C)e^{-|\tau|/(RC)}.\]

Its variance at zero lag agrees with equipartition, and its decay time is obtained from the homogeneous charge-relaxation equation. Repeating the measurement with a known extra parallel capacitance lowers the RMS and lengthens the correlation time. Seeing only one of those changes suggests that the observed noise may include substantial amplifier input voltage or an independent background.

The classical approximation assumes \(hf\ll k_BT\) over the relevant band and a resistor that can be treated as frequency independent. A MHz room-temperature experiment is far inside this regime. At sufficiently high frequencies or cryogenic temperatures, a quantum noise model, an explicit definition of the measured ordering of the spectrum and the detector's energy exchange are needed. Extending an ideal classical white source to infinite frequency is only permissible as an approximation inside the convergent low-frequency RC calculation; it is not a microscopic model at arbitrarily high frequency.

Move the reference plane of a wave measurement

Numerical field laboratory

Recover a load reflection coefficient from a noncontact voltage measurement and explain why identical amplitude data can represent physically different terminations.

Specify which plane defines the load

A uniform lossless transmission line has real characteristic impedance \(Z_0=50\ \Omega\). Drive it sinusoidally at one frequency. Choose peak phasors with time factor \(e^{i\omega t}\) and measure distance \(d\) from the load towards the source. Incident and reflected waves move in opposite directions. Taking the load as phase origin, the ratio of the reflected to incident voltage at the load is

\[\Gamma_L=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0).\]

The ratio at a sensor plane a distance \(d\) away is \(\Gamma_\mathrm{in}=\Gamma_Le^{-2i\beta d}\). The factor two is the phase difference of two opposite travellers. Moving the sensor does not change a physical resistor.

For a real resistive load, \(\Gamma_L\) is real and ranges from −1 for a short to +1 for an open. A 75 Ω load gives +0.2 and a \(100/3\) Ω load gives −0.2. These loads have the same reflected power fraction of four per cent. Distinguishing them requires phase information or positioning the sensor at more than one known location.

Derive the envelope and the ambiguity

The local voltage is the sum of incident and reflected phasors. Factor out the incident phase and take the magnitude:

\[\frac{|V(d)|^2}{|V^+|^2}=1+|\Gamma_L|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\{\Gamma_Le^{-2i\beta d}\}.\]

Its maxima and minima have amplitude ratios \(1+|\Gamma|\) and \(1-|\Gamma|\) for a passive load, giving \(\mathrm{VSWR}=(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)\). The distance between successive maxima is \(\lambda/2\), because the measured intensity involves a relative wave number \(2\beta\). A measured ratio 1.5 yields \(|\Gamma|=0.2\) but leaves its sign and, for general impedances, its phase undetermined. Extremum positions contain information that a ratio discards.

At one eighth of a wavelength from a 75 Ω load the phase rotation is \(-\pi/2\), giving \(\Gamma_\mathrm{in}=-0.2i\). If a researcher plugs that complex coefficient directly into \(Z=Z_0(1+\Gamma)/(1-\Gamma)\), the inferred impedance is about \(46.15-19.23i\ \Omega\). That is the correct input impedance at the sensor plane. To infer the actual element multiply back by \(e^{2i\beta d}\) before the inversion. The recovered load is then 75 Ω with zero imaginary part.

A high-slope position is sensitive to phase; a stationary amplitude extremum is useful when estimating the reflectance magnitude.

Recover a complex reflection from several amplitude measurements

If \(|V^+|\) is independently calibrated, sample squared voltage at \(d=0,\lambda/8,\lambda/4,3\lambda/8\). Denote normalised intensity by \(I_d\). Writing \(\Gamma=x+iy\) gives \(I_0-I_{\lambda/4}=4x\) and \(I_{\lambda/8}-I_{3\lambda/8}=4y\) with the convention above. Constant detector offsets cancel in these differences. A multiplicative gain error remains and must be calibrated. A mismatch in the assumed wavelength rotates or distorts the reconstructed coefficient rather than merely adding random noise.

For \(\Gamma=0.2\), the four intensities are 1.44, 1.04, 0.64 and 1.04. The reconstruction returns \(x=(1.44-0.64)/4=0.2\), \(y=0\). The observation probes have provided both quadratures without a phase-sensitive receiver. Estimate a cavity linewidth from a finite, biased record

Numerical field laboratory

Connect the time and frequency descriptions of one damped mode, then diagnose why a weak detector background systematically biases the inferred quality factor.

Know whether the detector reads field or energy

After a drive switches off, let a single cavity mode have complex analytic amplitude \(a(t)=a_0e^{-\kappa t/2}e^{-i\omega_0t}\) for \(t\ge0\). The energy is proportional to \(|a|^2\), giving \(U=U_0e^{-\kappa t}\). Its instantaneous decay rate \(-U'/U=\kappa\) implies

\[Q=\omega_0 U/P_\mathrm{loss}=\omega_0/\kappa.\]

A phase-sensitive receiver measuring field amplitude sees a time constant twice as long as the energy lifetime. Before fitting the data, ask how the measurement transducer converts the field into a reported number.

Fourier transforming the causal decaying amplitude gives an integral proportional to \(1/[\kappa/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)]\), up to the time convention. Its squared magnitude has half-power points at \(|\omega-\omega_0|=\kappa/2\). Therefore the full power linewidth in hertz is \(\Delta f=\kappa/(2\pi)\). A measurement of either the energy decay time \(\tau_U=1/\kappa\) or the spectral width can test the other without having to calibrate the absolute detector gain.

At \(f_0=2.0\) GHz and \(\tau_U=0.50\ \mu\mathrm s\), we obtain \(\kappa=2.0\times10^6\) s⁻¹, \(\Delta f\simeq318.3\) kHz and \(Q\simeq6283\). A field receiver would report an amplitude time constant of 1.0 μs. Substituting that time directly into \(Q=2\pi f_0\tau_U\) would double the apparent quality.

Derive the two-point fit with a detector offset

Let a power detector report \(S(t)=A e^{-\kappa t}+B\), with a constant background \(B>0\). In the absence of background two samples separated by \(\Delta t\) give

\[\widehat\kappa=\log[S(t)/S(t+\Delta t)]/\Delta t.\]

With background, the logarithmic derivative is

\[-\frac{d}{dt}\log S=\kappa\frac{Ae^{-\kappa t}}{Ae^{-\kappa t}+B}.\]

It starts below the true rate and tends to zero in the tail. A straight line fitted to late log data can therefore look impressively precise while overestimating the lifetime. Increasing the acquisition duration can worsen this bias if the entire record is forced into a straight-line model.

In the experiment set \(b=B/A=0.04\). A two-point fit from one true lifetime to two has dimensionless rate \(\log[(e^{-1}+0.04)/(e^{-2}+0.04)]\), approximately 0.844. Its apparent lifetime is about 18.5% high. The source, walls and coupling have not changed. Only the interpretation of detector output has changed. Move the start of the fit towards three lifetimes and inspect the increasing gap from the ideal log line.

Design a second independent inference

With three equally spaced, noiseless readings \(S_0,S_1,S_2\) of one exponential plus a constant, subtract consecutive values before taking a logarithm. If \(a=e^{-\kappa\Delta t}\), the differences are \(A'(1-a)\) and \(A'a(1-a)\), giving \(a=(S_1-S_2)/(S_0-S_1)\). This cancels the constant background. At an interval of one lifetime, a first signal of unit energy and background 0.04 gives approximately \(1.04,0.407879,0.175335\). Their ratio of differences recovers \(e^{-1}\). In a real noise floor two nearly equal readings make this division unreliable. Use multiple windows or fit the raw signal with a measured noise model.

Finite spectral resolution creates a separate challenge. Multiplying the measured field by a rectangular window of duration \(T\) convolves its spectrum with a sinc-like response of width of order \(1/T\). For the 318 kHz linewidth in the example, \(T=1\) μs gives frequency bins spaced by 1 MHz in a basic Fourier transform. Zero padding can interpolate its curve, but cannot restore the unmeasured late-time signal. A parametric fit may extract a sub-bin frequency or decay rate by using its model assumptions; its uncertainty then depends on signal-to-noise ratio and on whether the one-mode theory is adequate.

Check the single-mode model before refining its fit

If two nearby modes reach the same detector, their fields add before squaring. The result contains an interference term oscillating at their frequency difference on top of a decaying envelope. A scalar positive background cannot explain that alternating residual. Test by narrowing the excitation band, moving the pickup to a symmetry node of one mode, or examining a complex receiver's quadratures. A model with two exponentials has more degrees of freedom and can fit noise as well as signal; require consistency across independent records.

An honest linewidth report specifies the measured quantity, acquisition window, background treatment, coupling state, reference frequency and uncertainty. A fit to stored energy measures total loss. Inferring internal absorption alone additionally requires a known port coupling or a measurement that changes it separately, as in the course's cavity project.

Numerical field laboratory

Design a small-signal experiment to estimate resistance and inductance, propagate amplitude and phase uncertainty and decide when a distributed field model is needed.

A measured impedance is one complex number

A laboratory sensing coil is driven by a small sinusoidal current at angular frequency \(\omega\). In its simplest usable range, a series model predicts \(Z=R+i\omega L\). The voltage across a port defines the sign of current entering the positive terminal. With peak phasors, complex power is

\[S=VI^*/2=|I|^2(R+i\omega L)/2.\]

Thus the real part measures average irreversible loss. Magnetic energy at peak current is \(L|I|^2/2\); reactive power equals \(\omega\) times this peak energy. These equalities supply two routes to the same parameters provided parasitic capacitance and frequency-dependent losses remain negligible.

At 100 Hz, a coil with \(R=20\ \Omega\) and \(L=40\) mH has reactance \(2\pi(100)(0.040)=25.13\ \Omega\), magnitude 32.12 Ω and phase about 51.49 degrees. A 10 mA peak current produces about 0.321 V peak and 1 mW average resistive loss. The peak magnetic energy is only \(2\,\mu\mathrm J\). Check that the reactive power obtained from the phase and current is about 1.26 mvar. Confusion between RMS and peak phasors easily introduces a factor two while still leaving the impedance ratio correct.

Derive what an amplitude error and a timing error do

Suppose the readout gives magnitude \(M\) and phase \(\phi\). Then \(R=M\cos\phi\), \(L=M\sin\phi/\omega\). For small independent uncertainties and known \(\omega\), differentiate both expressions. The relative first-order perturbations are

\[\frac{\delta R}{R}=\frac{\delta M}{M}-\tan\phi\,\delta\phi,\qquad\]

\frac{\delta L}{L}=\frac{\delta M}{M}+\cot\phi\,\delta\phi .

The phase uncertainty must be expressed in radians. For independent random errors their variances add with the squared coefficients; the signed equations above describe individual perturbations. Near zero phase even a very accurate amplitude tells little about inductance, while near 90 degrees inferring resistance requires exceptional phase accuracy. A near-45-degree measurement balances these two sensitivities when the uncertainty budget treats them similarly.

If the phase uncertainty is 0.5° and relative magnitude uncertainty is 1%, the 45-degree standard deviation in either recovered parameter is approximately \(\sqrt{0.01^2+0.00873^2}=0.0133\), or 1.33%. At five degrees the relative inductance contribution from phase alone is \(\cot5^\circ \times0.00873\simeq10\%\). Taking data at more digits does not improve phase calibration.

Measuring magnetic core loss by a hysteresis loop and by calorimetry

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A closed loop on a graph of magnetic induction against field encloses an area with units of energy per unit volume. A thermometer measures kelvins. Bring these two observations onto the same scale, account for the winding heat, and determine when the two experiments actually test the same material loss.

Infer the field and the induction from independent windings

Wind a toroidal specimen with a primary winding of \(N_p\) turns and a lightly loaded pickup of \(N_s\) turns. Let \(A\) be the representative iron cross-sectional area, \(\ell\) the mean magnetic path length and \(V_c=A\ell\) the magnetic volume. Neglect a gap and leakage initially. Ampère's law around a path through the core gives

\[H\simeq N_pi_p/\ell.\]

This relation is accurate when the toroid is slender enough that the spread of path radii is small and the pickup current is negligible. In a thick toroid \(H(r)=N_pi_p/(2\pi r)\), so one point on a nominal loop can actually represent a range of local field values.

Faraday's law supplies the second observable. Choose winding polarity so the reported voltage is

\[v_s=N_sA\,dB/dt.\]

Integrating the voltage gives the change in cross-section-averaged magnetic induction. The pickup cannot determine a constant induction offset, because static flux induces no voltage. A closed loop area is insensitive to adding a constant to \(B\) or \(H\), but a dc offset in the voltage integrates into a time-dependent ramp and prevents the reconstructed trajectory from closing. In steady periodic operation one can subtract the voltage mean over a complete period before integration. Applying that correction to a genuinely evolving magnetisation process would remove part of a real flux change.

Multiply the two measured field quantities in the appropriate order. The field work supplied per unit material volume on a small induction change is \(H\,dB\). The source term associated with the magnetising flux is \(i_p v_{p,{\rm induced}}\,dt\). Using \(v_{p,{\rm induced}}\simeq(N_p/N_s)v_s\), its value is

\[i_pN_pA\,dB=V_cH\,dB.\]

Integrating around a settled cycle gives the magnetic loss

\[W_c=V_c\oint H\,dB,\]
\[P_c=fV_c\oint H\,dB.\]

The field can absorb positive work and return some of it later in the same cycle. The area measures the algebraic remainder. Reversing the polarity of a single signal flips the loop orientation. A passively heated specimen must receive positive net energy when signs are defined for power entering the core. Simply taking the absolute value of all instantaneous products would count reversible storage twice and can grossly overestimate the heat.

Work an elliptical loop from time-domain signals

Suppose a nearly sinusoidal field is \(H=H_p\cos\omega t\) and the induction lags it by a positive angle \(\delta\), \(B=B_p\cos(\omega t-\delta)\). Differentiate the latter and multiply by \(H\). Over one cycle \(\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\) averages to zero, and \(\cos^2\omega t\) averages to one half. The resulting mean power density is \(\omega H_pB_p\sin\delta/2\), giving \(\oint H\,dB=\pi H_pB_p\sin\delta\). With zero lag the two signals trace a line and the area vanishes, even if the peak values are large. A saturation-distorted loop need not be elliptical; the original time integral remains valid with synchronised data.

Take \(H_p=120\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_p=0.200\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta=12^\circ\). The energy per cycle and volume is \(15.68\,{\rm J/m^3}\). A toroid with \(\ell=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(A=2.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\) and \(V_c=40\,{\rm cm^3}\) dissipates \(0.627\,{\rm mJ}\) per cycle. At \(f=200\,{\rm Hz}\) its power is \(0.125\,{\rm W}\). With \(N_p=100\), the necessary current peak is \(0.240\,{\rm A}\). With \(N_s=50\), the pickup voltage has peak \(N_sA(2\pi f)B_p=2.51\,{\rm V}\). These last two predictions allow the raw oscilloscope traces to be checked before a calculated loss in watts is trusted.

For sampled periodic data use \(w=\sum_n(H_n+H_{n+1})(B_{n+1}-B_n)/2\), including the segment from the last point back to the first. This trapezoidal line integral computes an oriented polygon area. Plot the starting and ending points before adding the closing segment. Connecting widely separated endpoints by hand hides drift and can generate a fictitious wedge whose area depends on acquisition timing. Repeat at higher sampling rates to see whether corners or switching features carry appreciable extra area. When a periodic excitation has harmonics the time increment must resolve the highest significant harmonic in both measured signals.

Compare a thermal measurement with the magnetic area

Consider first a calorimeter that receives heat only from the core. Let the heat capacity of specimen, holder and sensor be \(C_{\rm eff}\). Let the leakage conductance to an ambient bath be \(G\), and let \(\Delta T=T-T_a\). The thermal balance \(C_{\rm eff}\dot{\Delta T}=P_c-G\Delta T\) predicts a steady offset \(P_c/G\) and an initial heating slope \(P_c/C_{\rm eff}\). A small thermometer lag or poor thermal contact can make the earliest measured slope misleading even though environmental losses are then small. A separate electrical heater placed where the core deposits its heat can calibrate the effective thermal response.

For \(C_{\rm eff}=20\,{\rm J/K}\), \(G=0.015\,{\rm W/K}\), and a \(2.0\,{\rm K}\) rise above ambient, the magnetic loss \(0.125\,{\rm W}\) would give a slope of about \(0.00477\,{\rm K/s}\). The heat already leaking to the bath is \(0.030\,{\rm W}\), too large to discard in a ten-percent comparison. If the winding is inside the same calorimeter, its Ohmic heat must enter the balance as well. With primary resistance \(1.50\,\Omega\) and the computed sinusoidal current, winding heating is

\[(0.240/\sqrt2)^2(1.50)=0.0432\,{\rm W}.\]

The expected slope of the combined package at the same temperature is now \(0.00693\,{\rm K/s}\). Subtraction of winding heat recovers the core result only when the estimated resistance represents the winding at its operating temperature and frequency.

A thermal estimate drawn from a whole interval is often more robust than a derivative from two adjacent temperature samples. Integrating gives

\[Q_{\rm deposited}=C_{\rm eff}[\Delta T(t_2)-\Delta T(t_1)]+G\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Delta T\,dt.\]

Divide by the duration for mean power, then remove other measured heat sources. A drifting ambient must be recorded explicitly because the calorimeter stores energy relative to its own absolute temperature. If the core and coil equilibrate slowly with each other, a single fitted heat capacity can depend on the fitting time window. A two-temperature model or a longer dwell then supplies an independent diagnostic.

Separate reversible exchange from hysteretic and induced currents

A single measured loop at finite frequency gives the total energy dissipated by the core at that prescribed waveform and temperature. The loop can include irreversible domain processes, eddy currents and other rate-dependent effects. It does not by itself split these contributions. To probe their different scaling hold the peak induction and waveform shape fixed, then change frequency. Under a restricted low-frequency model the quasi-static hysteresis energy per cycle is approximately constant, while classical thin-lamination eddy-current power scales as \(f^2B_p^2\). Its energy per cycle therefore scales roughly as \(f\). Plotting the measured loss per cycle and volume against \(f\) tests whether such an intercept-plus-slope description fits the chosen range.

Optical trapping and Brownian calibration of a dielectric bead

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

An image of a bead remaining near a focus does not establish the strength of its trap. Derive the force pulling a small dielectric sphere into a bright region, compare it with the forward force from scattering and learn what a motion record can tell you about stiffness, drag and temperature. The model here uses an inert dielectric sphere in a homogeneous Newtonian liquid.

From two dielectric boundaries to the energy of an induced dipole

A sphere of radius \(a\) and relative permittivity \(\epsilon_b\) sits in a liquid of relative permittivity \(\epsilon_m\). Assuming the sphere is small compared with a wavelength in the liquid, solve its local electric problem in an almost uniform applied field \(E\). Rotational symmetry about the field admits an interior potential proportional to \(r\cos\theta\). The exterior consists of the imposed potential \(-Er\cos\theta\) and an induced dipolar term proportional to \(\cos\theta/r^2\). Equality of potential and of normal displacement at the surface gives the local-field and dipole factors

\[E_{\rm in}=\frac{3\epsilon_m}{\epsilon_b+2\epsilon_m}E,\]
\[p=\alpha E,\]
\[\alpha=4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_m a^3\frac{\epsilon_b-\epsilon_m}{\epsilon_b+2\epsilon_m}.\]

The field doing the polarising work is the external field in the host at the particle position before inserting it. To turn that work into a potential, build up the dipole from zero rather than assuming a permanent dipole moment. Quasistatic assembly gives

\[-\int_0^E\alpha E'\,dE'=-\alpha E^2/2.\]

For a harmonic peak field average the square over an optical period. In a nearly lossless liquid of refractive index \(n_m\), the peak field is related to travelling-wave intensity by \(I=n_m\epsilon_0c|E|^2/2\). The resulting conservative force is

\[U=-\frac{\operatorname{Re}\alpha}{4}|E|^2=-\frac{\operatorname{Re}\alpha}{2n_m\epsilon_0 c}I,\]
\[\mathbf F_{\rm grad}=-\nabla U .\]

The two factors of two have different origins: induced-dipole assembly and time averaging of a sinusoid. Using a permanent-dipole potential with a peak field would miss both. A bead of higher refractive index than the liquid has positive real polarizability and is attracted to increasing intensity. A lower-index inclusion reverses this local tendency. For large particles, strong absorption or rapidly structured fields, a dipole with real static polarizability no longer specifies the force; a field or multipole calculation of the complete momentum balance is required.

Investigate the small-sphere dipole response and its dependence on particle size

Compute a transverse stiffness and test how local it is

Assume the beam near its focal plane has intensity \(I(x)=I_0e^{-2x^2/w^2}\), with power \(P\), waist \(w\) defined at \(1/e^2\) intensity and \(I_0=2P/(\pi w^2)\). Put \(U_0=\operatorname{Re}\alpha I_0/(2n_m\epsilon_0c)>0\). The potential measured above its minimum is \(U_0[1-e^{-2x^2/w^2}]\). Expanding to second order and equating its leading term to \(\kappa_xx^2/2\) gives

\[\kappa_x=4U_0/w^2.\]

Stiffness therefore scales linearly with power, as the third power of sphere radius and as the inverse fourth power of waist at fixed indices. Each scaling applies within the stated approximations.

For a 40 nm radius bead with \(n_b=1.59\), take \(n_m=1.33\), \(w=0.65\) micrometres, vacuum wavelength 1064 nm, \(P=90\) mW and \(T=298\) K. The index contrast factor is 0.1252 and the waist intensity is \(1.36\times10^{11}\) watts per square metre. The predicted depth \(3.03\times10^{-20}\) J is 7.36 times the thermal energy. The lateral stiffness is \(2.87\times10^{-7}\) N/m. A displacement of 50 nm then requires approximately 14.3 fN of transverse balancing force. At a displacement of half a waist, the exact restoring force is the harmonic estimate multiplied by \(e^{-1/2}=0.607\); a Hookean calibration cannot be extrapolated that far.

Extract stiffness from fluctuations or from a relaxation time

Near a stable point in one Cartesian direction, let \(x\) denote displacement from the mean and \(\zeta\) the viscous drag. At sufficiently low frequencies the inertial term is negligible and the overdamped Langevin equation is

\[\zeta\dot x=-\kappa x+\xi.\]

At equilibrium the fluctuating force has zero mean and white covariance \(2k_BT\zeta\delta(t-t')\) over the frequencies considered. The deterministic relaxation time is

\[\tau=\zeta/\kappa.\]

Multiplying the equation at positive delay by \(x(0)\) and averaging removes the uncorrelated later noise and gives the decaying covariance. Equipartition sets its initial value.

\[C(t)=\frac{k_BT}{\kappa}e^{-|t|/\tau},\]
\[f_c=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi\zeta},\]
\[S_x^{(1)}(f)=\frac{4k_BT\zeta}{\kappa^2+(2\pi f\zeta)^2},\quad f\ge0 .\]

The stated spectrum is one-sided in ordinary frequency. Its integral from zero to infinity equals \(C(0)\); doubling another time would double the variance. In an unbounded fluid with viscosity 1.00 mPa s, Stokes drag is \(6\pi\eta a=7.54\times10^{-10}\) N s/m. The default predicts \(f_c=60.5\) Hz and rms displacement 120 nm. The latter is roughly 0.18 waist, so the outer part of a long thermal trajectory may sample appreciable anharmonicity. A finite-depth well in an unbounded bath does not have a globally normalisable trapped equilibrium. These local equilibrium predictions apply to a sufficiently long-lived, locally sampled trapped state.

If a voltage detector gives \(V=gx\), the spectral corner and normalised autocorrelation time do not depend on the displacement gain \(g\). They determine only \(\kappa/\zeta\). Equipartition using a calibrated spatial variance gives \(\kappa\) independently of drag, provided mechanical fluctuations are in equilibrium and sufficiently resolved. If the viscosity or nearby-wall drag is uncertain, combining a known stage oscillation with the Brownian spectrum can determine further calibration quantities. Keep a stage-driven coherent peak out of the noise integral used for equipartition.

Account for exposure, sample size and unobserved motion

A camera typically reports the position averaged during an exposure of duration \(t_e\). Its variance is the double integral of the covariance over the exposure interval divided by \(t_e^2\). Evaluating the exponential local model and introducing \(u=t_e/\tau\) gives a multiplicative factor \(2(u-1+e^{-u})/u^2\). At \(u=1\) the reported physical-motion variance is only 0.736 of the instantaneous variance. Interpreting it as the full variance would overestimate stiffness by approximately 36 percent. Independent pixel-localisation noise adds additional variance with the opposite sign of bias.

Estimate that localisation noise with an effectively immobilised reference bead at a comparable brightness. Test the averaging correction by changing exposure duration at constant sampling frequency where possible. A camera that reports overlapping exposures has correlated measurement errors and needs a different covariance model. A long series of closely spaced frames also contains fewer independent thermal configurations than its frame count suggests. Compare estimates across several blocks substantially longer than the relaxation time; residual low-frequency drift can dominate even when high-frequency position precision looks excellent.

A separate check uses a known uniform liquid flow. At a steady small displacement \(x_s\), force balance gives

\[\kappa x_s=\zeta u_{\rm flow}.\]

For the default drag and stiffness a flow of 10 micrometres per second gives an offset of 26.3 nm. Repeat with reversed flow and use half the displacement difference to cancel an unknown zero-force position. Near a wall, the local flow at the bead and wall-corrected drag may differ from a stage velocity and the unbounded Stokes value. Those effects alter the mechanical calibration even if the optical field is unchanged.

A known stage oscillation calibrates position in a fluid

Brownian fluctuations alone determine the product of several unknown calibration parameters. A weak sinusoidal translation of the sample cell adds a known mechanical signal at a chosen frequency. Work in laboratory coordinates with a fixed optical trap and with fluid moving uniformly at velocity \(u(t)\) near the bead, well away from walls in the idealised model. The drag is now \(-\zeta(\dot x-u)\). Superposing a stage displacement \(A\cos\omega t\) on thermal fluctuations gives a deterministic response whose complex amplitude satisfies \((\kappa-i\omega\zeta)\widetilde x=-i\omega\zeta A\) in the negative-exponential convention. Therefore its amplitude ratio is

\[\frac{|\widetilde x|}{A}=\frac{\omega\tau}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2\tau^2}} .\]

At the trap corner the bead displacement amplitude is the known stage amplitude divided by the square root of two. A calibrated 80.0 nm stage oscillation there produces a predicted bead amplitude of 56.6 nm. If a position-sensitive receiver records a coherent peak amplitude of 0.170 V, its displacement gain is 3.01 mV/nm. At very low drive frequencies the fluid velocity is small for fixed stage amplitude and the trap holds the bead nearly stationary. At high enough frequency the bead follows the fluid displacement within the overdamped model, but finite hydrodynamic, optical and electronic bandwidths eventually matter.

After determining the gain, convert the surrounding voltage spectrum into spatial units and use the area of the stochastic fluctuation spectrum to find stiffness. Exclude the calibrated line and account for the finite spectral averaging window; a narrow drive that leaks into neighbouring bins can inflate the inferred thermal variance. Dividing measured stiffness by the independently determined corner rate then gives drag, which can be compared with the known viscosity and sphere radius. This avoids assuming free-space Stokes drag in the first step of a near-wall measurement.

There are two limiting systematic effects that a good fit to a single drive frequency cannot resolve. A frequency-dependent stage calibration changes the actual fluid displacement, and motion of the optics relative to the cell generates a direct detector background even with the bead immobilised. Repeat the drive above and below the corner, compare coherent phase as well as amplitude and acquire a trapped and immobilised reference. Correct conversion should explain the entire complex response with one spatial gain and one corner time. A single calibration number obtained at one frequency can hide a phase lag in the stage controller or in the optical position channel.

One last cross-check reverses the stage drive phase by half a period. The coherent bead response must reverse, while thermal background power remains unchanged. A component that fails this reversal could come from ambient vibration unrelated to the stage. Averaging the two traces with and without a sign change separates these contributions before either is used in an optical-force calibration.

Coupled optical and mechanical resonances

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A vibrating mirror can imprint its position on an optical field, while the stored light pushes back on the mirror. An apparent pair of peaks can indicate two hybrid modes, interference at a readout port or two unrelated resonances. Starting from energy in a moving cavity, derive the coupled response and learn which measurements establish a coupling strength and its uncertainty.

From a moving boundary to a force per photon

Take a nearly empty Fabry–Pérot cavity of optical length \(L\) with one moving end. A longitudinal resonance has \(\omega_c=m\pi c/L\) for integer \(m\), neglecting frequency-dependent mirror phase. For a small extension \(x\), \(\delta\omega_c=(\partial\omega_c/\partial x)x\) with signed slope \(G_x=-\omega_c/L\). At fixed photon number \(n\) the optical energy is \(n\hbar\omega_c(x)\). Its derivative gives the radiation-pressure force on the coordinate. For this mirror \(F=-n\hbar G_x>0\) pushes outward. The derivative must be at fixed photon number. Differentiating a driven steady-state photon number as though it were a closed-system energy would incorrectly include work exchanged with the pump.

For a 10 mm cavity resonant near 1064 nm the frequency slope magnitude in ordinary frequency is about 28.2 MHz per nanometre. A static shift of 10 pm therefore changes the optical resonance by approximately 282 kHz. One photon exerts a mean force \(\hbar\omega_c/L\simeq1.87\times10^{-17}\) N. An equivalent classical derivation counts a momentum change twice per normal reflection for each relevant round trip. The two descriptions yield the same mean force. The mirror displacement is the properly normalised mode coordinate; in a flexural device the optically sampled position and the total physical mass are generally different from centre-of-mass displacement and mass.

Introduce a single mechanical eigenmode with effective mass \(m_{\rm eff}\), angular frequency \(\Omega_m\) and displacement scale \(x_{\rm zpf}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m_{\rm eff}\Omega_m)}\). The single-photon coupling magnitude is \(g_0=|G_x|x_{\rm zpf}\). This normalisation is convenient even when the observed vibrations are entirely classical. For \(m_{\rm eff}=10\) ng and \(\Omega_m/(2\pi)=1.0\) MHz, \(x_{\rm zpf}=9.16\times10^{-16}\) m and \(g_0/(2\pi)=25.8\) Hz for the stated optical geometry. The small displacement scale explains why a coherent pump is normally used to make the interaction measurable.

How a laser couples frequencies that are otherwise far apart

An optical carrier oscillates at hundreds of terahertz, whereas the mirror may vibrate at only megahertz. Direct comparison of these two bare frequencies would never suggest a resonant exchange. A strong coherent pump supplies a phase reference and converts a mechanical displacement into optical sidebands. Move into a frame rotating at the pump frequency and write the cavity amplitude as its large steady part plus a small perturbation. The leading frequency-shift interaction is proportional to photon number times displacement. Expanding the intensity around the large pump keeps terms linear in both the probe field and displacement. The resulting coupling is \(G=g_0\sqrt{n_c}\), where \(n_c\) is mean intracavity pump photon number.

Tune the pump about one mechanical frequency below the optical cavity resonance. In the resolved-sideband and weak-fluctuation limit a near-resonant process moves one excitation between the mechanical mode and an upper optical sideband. The rapidly rotating opposite-sideband terms average out. Now the two coupled frequencies are an optical sideband offset and the mechanical frequency, measured relative to a common rotating reference. The large carrier frequency has already been accounted for by the pump. The pump phase can be absorbed into the mode definitions, so \(G\) may be chosen positive for this isolated pair.

If instead the pump is placed near the upper sideband, the surviving interaction creates or annihilates excitations in pairs. The passive exchange equations below cease to apply and strong pumping can produce amplification or instability. A bare two-oscillator machine driven mechanically near a shared natural frequency often has analogous exchange equations without a carrier pump. Identical matrices can describe different energy supplies; identify the drive and rotating reference before assigning a literal photon-to-phonon energy conversion.

Derive complex poles and the effect of unequal damping

Let \(a,b\) be small energy-normalised optical and mechanical complex amplitudes. In a frame where the uncoupled optical sideband has zero frequency, let the mechanical detuning be \(\delta\). Let \(\kappa,\Gamma\) denote energy linewidths in angular frequency, so amplitude decay rates are half these values. With an optical probe \(s\) at offset \(\omega\), the linear system is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\kappa/2-i\omega&iG\\ iG&\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\delta)\end{pmatrix}\binom{a}{b}=\binom{s}{0}.\]

Zeros of the determinant give autonomous complex mode frequencies. In the lossless case the eigenvalues are \(\delta/2\pm\sqrt{G^2+(\delta/2)^2}\). Far off resonance each branch approaches a bare mode. At zero detuning the two eigenvectors have equal optical and mechanical weights and the splitting is \(2G\). The eigenvectors rotate continuously through the avoided crossing, an important discriminator from two uncoupled lines simply passing through one another.

At zero detuning with losses, completing the square in the determinant gives

\[\omega_\pm=-i(\kappa+\Gamma)/4\ \pm\sqrt{G^2-(\kappa-\Gamma)^2/16}.\]

The real parts separate once \(G>|\kappa-\Gamma|/4\). The shared imaginary part above that threshold corresponds to a hybrid energy linewidth \((\kappa+\Gamma)/2\). Resolving two intensity features on a given instrument is a stronger, readout-dependent condition. The dimensionless cooperativity \(C=4G^2/(\kappa\Gamma)\) is yet another measure, comparing interaction against the product of dissipation scales. A value greater than one does not automatically prove two resolved normal-mode poles in a device with very unequal linewidths.

Use frequency numbers divided by \(2\pi\) consistently from here for examples. With optical linewidth 240 kHz, mechanical linewidth 40 kHz and coupling 160 kHz, the real pole separation is

\[2\sqrt{160^2-50^2}=304\,\mathrm{kHz}.\]

Both hybrid energy linewidths are 140 kHz, and cooperativity is 10.7. If coupling falls to 45 kHz, cooperativity is still about 0.844 and no real zero-detuning pole separation appears. At 50 kHz the square root reaches zero. Near that threshold even a small linewidth or detuning calibration error has a large effect on a coupling estimate inferred from a single splitting.

Compute what an optical or mechanical receiver actually sees

Invert the matrix. With \(D=(\kappa/2-i\omega)[\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\delta)]+G^2\), the small amplitudes are \(a=s[\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\delta)]/D\) and \(b=-iGs/D\). The poles are shared. Their numerators are different, so the two channels can show different apparent maxima. A port in reflection also has a directly reflected field that interferes with the leaking intracavity amplitude. Its intensity can display a narrow dip or asymmetric feature without a matching resonance-height pattern inside the cavity. A legitimate forward model must describe the measured port.

\[a(t)=a_0e^{-\kappa t/2}\cos Gt,\]
\[b(t)=-ia_0e^{-\kappa t/2}\sin Gt.\]

Their normalised total energy decays as \(e^{-\kappa t}\), while energy moves back and forth between optical sideband and vibration. The first full transfer occurs at \(t=\pi/(2G)\) if the rotating-wave approximation holds. For \(G/(2\pi)=100\) kHz, this is 2.50 microseconds. With a shared energy linewidth of 20 kHz in ordinary frequency the surviving energy fraction then is

\[e^{-2\pi(20\,{\rm kHz})(2.50\,\mu{\rm s})}=0.730.\]

A gated excitation shorter than the exchange period but long enough to avoid unwanted modes can prepare an approximately optical initial perturbation. Independent detection of an optical revival and a delayed mechanical response tests coherent exchange more strongly than the presence of two peaks alone. Comparing times of maxima directly with one half the reciprocal measured optical linewidth misidentifies the exchange physics. Check the decay of the sum of appropriately calibrated energies and the relative phase of the exchange. Electrical or optical feedthrough can produce a prompt apparent response with no mechanical build-up.

Infer a coupling with propagated measurement errors

When the two hybrid pole frequencies have been obtained from a justified fit and zero bare detuning is established, the coupling follows from the measured real separation \(S\):

\[G=\sqrt{S^2/4+(\kappa-\Gamma)^2/16}.\]

All quantities here can be expressed in the same ordinary-frequency units. If \(S=300\pm8\) kHz, \(\kappa=240\pm10\) kHz and \(\Gamma=40\pm4\) kHz, the estimate is 158.1 kHz. Independent small errors give

\[\partial G/\partial S=S/(4G)=0.474,\]
\[\partial G/\partial\kappa=-\partial G/\partial\Gamma=(\kappa-\Gamma)/(16G)=0.0791.\]

Combining in quadrature gives about 3.9 kHz uncertainty. A common frequency calibration can correlate all three inputs, and a fitted detuning and splitting from one scan may also be correlated. Use their covariance rather than multiplying independent error bars blindly.

A second inference varies intracavity pump power. Since \(G^2=g_0^2 n_c\), plot the squared fitted coupling against independently calibrated \(n_c\). A nonzero intercept could indicate an additional direct coupling, baseline fitting bias or a mode incorrectly assigned at low power. The linewidths themselves can vary with pump power because of heating or backaction, so fit or measure them across the scan. Incident optical power is not identical to stored photon number; cavity coupling, detuning and loss determine their relation.

A measured frequency shift and linewidth change reveal optical feedback

The same instrument can be operated below the resolved splitting regime and read out a dispersive shift of the mechanical response. Algebraically eliminate the optical amplitude in the two-mode equations while driving the mechanical mode weakly. Its inverse susceptibility acquires a self-energy contribution proportional to the optical susceptibility. Let \(x\) be probe frequency measured from the bare optical sideband and use the same linewidth convention as above. In the mechanical denominator the additional term is

\[\Sigma(x)=\frac{G^2}{\kappa/2-ix}=\frac{G^2(\kappa/2+ix)}{(\kappa/2)^2+x^2}.\]

Its real part adds to the amplitude damping, while its imaginary part shifts the resonant condition. At very weak coupling one can evaluate it at the bare mechanical frequency \(x=\delta\), because variation across the narrow mechanical peak is small. Near an avoided crossing or when the mechanical peak samples a large change in optical susceptibility, solve the coupled poles directly; simply adding a linewidth and shift then neglects the very hybridisation being tested.

In ordinary-frequency units choose \(G=10\) kHz, optical linewidth 200 kHz, mechanical linewidth 2 kHz and detuning \(+100\) kHz. At that offset the optical amplitude half-width is 100 kHz, giving \(\operatorname{Re}\Sigma=0.50\) kHz and \(\operatorname{Im}\Sigma=0.50\) kHz. The full mechanical linewidth increases from 2.0 to approximately 3.0 kHz. Its centre moves upwards by about 0.50 kHz with the adopted matrix signs. Moving to minus 100 kHz reverses the shift and preserves the added damping in this symmetric model. At zero detuning the predicted shift vanishes but the added linewidth is largest.

A scan on both sides is more discriminating than measuring broadening alone. A background rise in mechanical temperature changes integrated thermal noise and may change intrinsic loss, but need not reproduce this odd shift and even broadening pattern. Measure intrinsic linewidth far from the optical response and subtract that baseline. An unknown constant frequency offset can be removed with the two symmetric detunings, but an optical power dependence of cavity frequency can move the actual symmetry centre. Monitor the optical resonance during the scan.

The effective susceptibility describes classical feedback through the delayed optical field. It does not by itself specify the final mechanical temperature. A bath occupation or noise spectrum is also required to convert a damping change into an energy distribution. A driven response peak that broadens and lowers in amplitude is evidence for a changed susceptibility; thermal cooling additionally requires a calibrated measurement of the fluctuating mechanical displacement and its optical transduction.

Scattering from a central potential and what angular data can recover

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A detector records angles, whereas the force law refers to separations that were never directly observed. Derive the forward map from incoming impact parameter to angle, distinguish the several trajectories a detector may count in one angular bin, and invert sufficiently complete signed deflection data under stated hypotheses.

Build the deflection integral from a radial turning point

Use relative mass \(\mu\), asymptotic relative speed \(v_\infty\), incoming energy \(E=\mu v_\infty^2/2>0\) and impact parameter \(b\ge0\). With a central interaction that tends to zero at infinity, angular momentum is \(\ell=\mu v_\infty b\). For a single repulsive encounter let \(r_0\) be the closest approach. Energy gives

\[\dot r^2=v_\infty^2[1-U(r)/E-b^2/r^2];\]

\(r_0\) is the outer turning root reached from infinity. Dividing \(\dot\varphi=\ell/(\mu r^2)\) by the radial speed and integrating over the outgoing half trajectory gives

\[\Phi(b)=\int_{r_0}^\infty \frac{b\,dr}{r^2\sqrt{1-U(r)/E-b^2/r^2}},\]
\[\chi(b)=\pi-2\Phi(b).\]

The straight, free trajectory sweeps \(\Phi=\pi/2\) between closest approach and infinity and gives zero deflection. For repulsive bend away from the centre this sign convention gives positive \(\chi\). A weak attraction can give negative signed deflection even though a detector reporting the angle between incoming and outgoing velocities may supply only its magnitude. If the path winds by more than a complete turn, angular data modulo \(2\pi\) also lose winding information. The square-root singularity in the integral for an ordinary turning point is integrable. A substitution \(r=r_0+s^2\) makes the near-endpoint quadrature regular. A double turning point requires separate analysis because it can make the angular advance diverge.

Incoming trajectories with parameters between \(b\) and \(b+db\) represent annular area \(2\pi b\,db\) for an axially symmetric beam. At a detected polar angle \(\theta\) the solid angle of a ring is \(2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\). Conservation of incoming counts gives

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\sum_j\frac{b_j}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db_j}{d\theta}\right|.\]

The sum includes all incoming impact parameters reaching that angular bin. Correcting measured counts requires incident flux, target number, angular acceptance and efficiency. The formula is a density in solid angle. Its apparent divergence at an isolated angle need not imply an infinite number in a finite acceptance bin. Integrate the density against the actual bin solid angle or use the preimage areas of the bin directly.

Solve a hard sphere and an inverse-square repulsion

A hard sphere of effective exclusion radius \(a\) specularly reverses the incoming normal velocity at the impact surface. The angle between incoming and outgoing directions is \(\theta=2\arccos(b/a)\) for \(b<a\). Therefore \(b=a\cos(\theta/2)\),

\[|db/d\theta|=(a/2)\sin(\theta/2).\]

The factors cancel to give \(d\sigma/d\Omega=a^2/4\), independent of polar angle, and total cross section \(\pi a^2\). Equal polar angular bins do not give equal counts: their solid angles differ by \(\sin\theta\). This geometric distinction often accounts for a measured histogram that does not look uniform even for a constant differential cross section.

For \(U(r)=K/r\) with \(K>0\), the orbit is a repulsive hyperbola. The analytic relation is

\[\tan(\theta/2)=K/(2Eb).\]

It can be derived from the energy and the angle through the deflection integral: put \(u=b/r\) so that \(\Phi=\int_0^{u_0}du/\sqrt{1-(K/Eb)u-u^2}\). Complete the square and evaluate the arcsine at the root to get

\[\Phi=\pi/2-\arctan[K/(2Eb)].\]

Define \(d=K/(2E)\); then \(b=d\cot(\theta/2)\),

\[d\sigma/d\Omega=d^2/[4\sin^4(\theta/2)].\]

High precision near the forward direction is physically limited by finite beam size, detector acceptance and any screening of the long-range interaction.

For \(d=2.0\,{\rm mm}\) in an abstract mechanical analogue, a deflection of \(60^\circ\) corresponds to \(b=3.46\,{\rm mm}\), and the differential cross section is \(16\,{\rm mm^2/sr}\). The cross section for deflections greater than \(60^\circ\) is the area \(\pi b^2=37.7\,{\rm mm^2}\). The differential value and integrated area answer different laboratory questions. Doubling energy at fixed interaction halves \(d\), quarters the cross section into a fixed angular interval and reduces the deflection at a fixed incoming \(b\). Measuring only a total count without specifying angular threshold is inadequate for a long-range interaction.

Recover impact parameter from angular counting under monotonicity

Suppose the deflection decreases continuously from \(\pi\) at \(b=0\) to zero at large \(b\). Integrating counts for all angles at least \(\theta\) then gives precisely the incident disk \(\pi b(\theta)^2\). The recovered map satisfies

\[b(\theta)^2=2\int_\theta^\pi(d\sigma/d\Omega)(\theta')\sin\theta'\,d\theta'.\]

Applying this formula to constant hard-sphere intensity gives \(b^2=a^2(1+\cos\theta)/2\), the geometrical result. It also exposes the information needed from a detector: a finite upper cutoff can leave the reconstruction with an unknown disk-area offset.

Failing monotonicity, the same cumulative counts give the total area of all incoming regions producing deflections above threshold, which can be a collection of annuli. No unique \(b(\theta)\) follows from that one number. Near a smooth extremum \(\theta(b)=\theta_r+c(b-b_r)^2/2+\cdots\), two branches contribute inverse square-root factors proportional to \(1/\sqrt{|\theta-\theta_r|}\). A finite bin contains finite counts because integrating that singularity gives a square root. Tagging the incoming impact parameter in a controlled beam or measuring trajectory time delay can separate branches invisible in a final-angle histogram.

Invert a signed deflection function at one energy

Assume smooth \(U(r)\) with \(\rho(r)=r\sqrt{1-U(r)/E}\) strictly increasing on the radial interval sampled by the trajectories. The turning condition is

\[\rho(r_0)=b.\]

Write \(W(\rho)=\ln[r(\rho)/\rho]\) and transform the deflection integral to \(\Phi=b\int_b^\infty[d\ln r/d\rho]\,d\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2-b^2}\). The term from \(d\ln\rho/d\rho\) gives the free angle \(\pi/2\); the remainder has \(\chi=-2b\int_b^\infty W'(\rho)\,d\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2-b^2}\). This is an Abel-type map. Multiply by \(1/\sqrt{b^2-s^2}\) and integrate from \(b=s\) to infinity. Interchanging the integrals produces the inner integral

\[\int_s^\rho\frac{b\,db}{\sqrt{b^2-s^2}\sqrt{\rho^2-b^2}}=\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

Substitute \(b^2=s^2+(\rho^2-s^2)\sin^2\alpha\) to check the identity. With \(W(\infty)=0\) the inversion is \(W(s)=\pi^{-1}\int_s^\infty\chi(b)\,db/\sqrt{b^2-s^2}\). Recover \(r=s e^{W(s)}\) and finally \(U(r)=E[1-(s/r)^2]\) parametrically. This derivation explains the restrictions. A trajectory only samples radii outside its turning point, the signed angle must retain branch information, and the map to \(\rho\) must be single valued. Unknown large-\(b\) tails influence the integral even when only comparatively small radii are of interest.

Derive the weak-deflection formula from a straight incoming trajectory

When the deflection is small one can calculate the transverse impulse along the undeflected line before evaluating a nonlinear radial integral. Put the incoming velocity along \(x\) and the impact point at \(y=b>0\), so the unperturbed trajectory is \(x=v_\infty t\), \(r=\sqrt{x^2+b^2}\). The transverse force is \(-U'(r)b/r\). Dividing its time integral by the incoming momentum \(\mu v_\infty\) gives the signed leading deflection

\[\chi(b)\simeq-\frac{b}{E}\int_b^\infty\frac{U'(r)\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-b^2}} .\]

The factor follows from replacing \(dt\) by \(dx/v_\infty\), using symmetry between positive and negative \(x\), and the identity \(dx=r\,dr/\sqrt{r^2-b^2}\). A decreasing positive potential gives a positive angle, consistent with repulsive scattering in the chapter's convention. The approximation neglects longitudinal momentum change at leading order; a finite speed change far from the interaction would also be inconsistent with elastic energy conservation. Near a turning barrier or for a large angle the original deflection integral must replace this straight path.

An analytically tractable finite-range model is \(U(r)=U_0\exp[-r^2/(2a^2)]\). Along the incoming straight line the transverse force is \((U_0 b/a^2)\exp[-(x^2+b^2)/(2a^2)]\). The Gaussian \(x\) integral gives

\[\chi(b)\simeq\sqrt{\pi/2}(U_0/E)(b/a)\exp[-b^2/(2a^2)].\]

A central hit has zero transverse force by symmetry if the particle crosses through the weak smooth potential rather than being reflected. Distant particles see little interaction, so the deflection must peak at an intermediate impact parameter. Differentiation places the maximum at \(b=a\), with height \(\sqrt{\pi/(2e)}U_0/E\).

For \(U_0/E=0.10\) the peak is about \(0.0760\,{\rm rad}=4.36^\circ\), comfortably small for this leading calculation. Two impact-parameter families can contribute to one smaller nonzero angle. For example \(b=0.50a\) gives \(0.0553\,{\rm rad}\); a trajectory with \(b\) between \(1.5a\) and \(1.7a\) produces a similar deflection on the declining branch. A detector counting more events near four degrees may be recording the merging of these branches, rather than a particularly large local force at the turning separation. Reconstructing \(b(\theta)\) from monotonically accumulated disk areas would be invalid for this smooth repulsive bump even though every encounter is elastic.

Measure the energy dependence to distinguish the weak limit. For fixed \(b/a\) the leading angle scales as \(1/E\). Doubling energy halves the predicted peak angle. A finite spread of incident energies therefore broadens the observed rainbow before detector angular resolution is considered. A position-tagged incident beam can furnish \(\chi(b)\) point by point; a detector measuring final angles alone mixes weakly and more strongly penetrating paths. Adding incoming-position information changes what inverse problem is identifiable, without improving the detector's angular precision.

Limit a scattering inference using finite range and measurement bandwidth

The Abel reconstruction requires data to arbitrarily large \(b\). A finite experiment measures up to \(b_{\max}\), and the unmeasured tail contributes an integral to \(W(s)\). For the example of a known asymptotic \(\chi=C/b\), the tail from \(b_{\max}\) to infinity evaluates to \(C\,\arcsin(s/b_{\max})/(\pi s)\) for \(s<b_{\max}\). Substitute \(b=s/\cos\alpha\) to obtain this result. For \(s\ll b_{\max}\), it approaches \(C/(\pi b_{\max})\). Setting the tail to zero therefore introduces an offset across the reconstructed log-radius even at small \(s\). Bounded angular noise and uncertainty in the tail model should be propagated separately.

As a numerical illustration take \(C=5.0\,{\rm mm}\),

\[b_{\max}=100\,{\rm mm},\]
\[s=20\,{\rm mm}.\]

The missing contribution is about \(0.0160\), corresponding to roughly a \(1.6\%\) multiplicative error in reconstructed radius if other terms were exact. At that same \(s\), the leading whole weak-tail value \(C/(2s)=0.125\) would be biased appreciably by this omission. A model with a short exponential tail would give a quite different correction. Adding a physically motivated tail with an uncertainty range is more informative than extending a noisy measured curve by an unrestricted polynomial.

The lower integration endpoint has a square-root weight. Uniform samples in \(b\) can be wasteful near a selected reconstruction radius and coarse interpolation there can dominate the result. Substitute \(b=s\cosh u\) to obtain

\[db/\sqrt{b^2-s^2}=du.\]

The Abel integral becomes a straightforward integral of \(\chi(s\cosh u)\) over \(u\), with a finite upper limit if the data stop at \(b_{\max}\). This substitution removes a numerical singularity but adds no information about missing impact parameters. Validate a reconstruction by inserting its inferred potential back into the original forward integral and predicting angular measurements withheld from the fit. Agreement with the very data used in the inversion alone can conceal an incorrect tail or a misassigned branch.

Convert centre-of-momentum angles into laboratory angles

An ordinary collision experiment often launches a particle of mass \(m_1\) into a stationary particle of mass \(m_2\). In Newtonian elastic scattering the centre-of-mass speed is \(V=m_1v_{\rm in}/(m_1+m_2)\), while the projectile's speed in that frame is \(u_1=m_2v_{\rm in}/(m_1+m_2)\). Rotate that velocity by the centre-of-mass angle \(\chi\) in the scattering plane, then add the centre velocity back. The outgoing laboratory velocity components are \(V+u_1\cos\chi\) longitudinally and \(u_1\sin\chi\) transversely. Consequently \(\theta_{\rm lab}=\operatorname{atan2}(\sin\chi,m_1/m_2+\cos\chi)\). The centre-of-mass solid-angle density derived from the impact parameter does not transfer unchanged to a lab angular histogram.

If \(m_1=m_2\), the identity \(1+\cos\chi=2\cos^2(\chi/2)\) gives \(\theta_{\rm lab}=\chi/2\) except when the projectile stops and loses a well-defined direction. A heavy projectile can be restricted to a narrow forward cone. Let \(r=m_1/m_2>1\). Differentiating the laboratory angle gives

\[d\theta_{\rm lab}/d\chi=(1+r\cos\chi)/(1+2r\cos\chi+r^2).\]

It vanishes at \(\cos\chi=-1/r\). At that angle \(\sin\theta_{\max}=1/r\); hence \(r=2\) limits the projectile to \(\theta_{\max}=30^\circ\). Two centre-of-mass angles can generate the same nonzero laboratory angle below this maximum. This laboratory-frame fold may appear even for a monotone centre-of-mass repulsive potential.

Energy helps separate the branches. The outgoing laboratory projectile energy divided by its incident energy is \([m_1^2+m_2^2+2m_1m_2\cos\chi]/(m_1+m_2)^2\). For \(m_1=2m_2\), head-on reversal in the momentum frame gives energy fraction \(1/9\), while forward scattering gives one. Both can land near the laboratory beam direction. A detector reporting angle only cannot distinguish a barely deflected fast projectile from a strongly deflected slow one emerging along almost the same line. Recording time of flight to a downstream detector or measuring the recoil supplies the missing information. Instrumental and kinematic multiplicity should therefore be addressed before attributing a two-branch angular signal to an exotic force law.

Ask what a flight-time measurement adds to angle data

At a given impact parameter choose a large comparison sphere of radius \(R\) enclosing a short-range potential. The transit time from its incoming boundary to the outgoing boundary is \(t_U(R,b)=2v_\infty^{-1}\int_{r_0}^R dr/\sqrt{1-U/E-b^2/r^2}\). The free reference at the same \(b\) is \(t_0=2\sqrt{R^2-b^2}/v_\infty\). Their difference is a classical time delay under a precisely specified pair of surfaces. It combines a different path length with the altered speed along the path. A repulsive barrier can cause either a shorter geometrical penetration or longer dwell near a turning point, so the sign cannot be inferred merely from the word repulsive.

For an impenetrable sphere of radius \(a>b\), the particle moves at free speed on each straight segment. The radial turning point is replaced by specular contact at \(a\), so \(t_{\rm hard}=2[\sqrt{R^2-b^2}-\sqrt{a^2-b^2}]/v_\infty\). The delay is \(-2\sqrt{a^2-b^2}/v_\infty\). Using \(a=3.0\,{\rm mm}\), \(b=1.8\,{\rm mm}\), and \(v_\infty=2.0\,{\rm m/s}\), one finds \(-2.4\,{\rm ms}\). The hard sphere takes less transit time between the two points where the respective trajectories cross the large sphere. Those points differ from the endpoints of the free line, so this negative delay implies no acceleration above the fixed free speed.

For a potential with a true long-range \(1/r\) tail the simple free-reference difference can acquire a logarithmic dependence on the comparison radius. A reported time delay then needs its reference geometry and tail model, just as a measured forward angle needs its angular acceptance. In the inverse problem, combining deflection and delay can help separate long dwell near a nearly unstable circular orbit from a large geometrical bend at an ordinary turning point. A large angle alone contains insufficient information to assign the time spent close to the scattering centre.

Reconstruct a finite-depth potential well from two deflections

A circularly symmetric square well provides a forward problem in which the trajectory is straight in each region and changes direction at the potential boundary. Let \(U(r)=-U_0<0\) for \(r<a\) and zero outside. A particle with \(b<a\) enters with speed \(v_\infty\) and travels inside at \(v_{\rm in}=v_\infty\sqrt{1+U_0/E}\equiv n v_\infty\). Angular momentum conservation across the radial impulse gives

\[v_\infty\sin i=v_{\rm in}\sin r_i,\]

where \(i\) and \(r_i\) are angles of the velocity to the normal outside and inside. Thus \(\sin i=b/a,\ \sin r_i=b/(na)\). At each crossing the direction bends toward the centre. The magnitude of net deflection is \(2(i-r_i)\), with negative signed deflection in the convention used for attraction here.

Take \(n=1.25,\ b=0.60a\). Then \(i=36.87^\circ\), \(r_i=28.69^\circ\), and the total deflection has magnitude \(16.35^\circ\). As \(b\) approaches \(a\) from below, the magnitude approaches \(2[\pi/2-\arcsin(1/n)]\), about \(73.7^\circ\). For \(b>a\) the trajectory does not touch the ideal sharp boundary and is undeflected. A smooth finite-width transition removes the ideal discontinuity at grazing incidence and can generate trajectories that linger near the outer part of the well. Examining a well with measured edge width is therefore different from trying to fit all grazing events with a perfectly sharp step.

If \(a\) is known, an incoming position tag and a measured deflection at one \(b\) determine \(n=\sin i/\sin(i-|\chi|/2)\), hence \(U_0/E=n^2-1\). The preceding numbers give

\[U_0/E=0.5625.\]

For an independently known \(E\) this determines the well depth. If \(a\) is unknown too, one angle supplies only one equation for two parameters. Multiple impact parameters, an independent threshold for entering the well, or a measured time of flight can supply the additional scale. Near \(b=0\) the deflection is approximately \(2(b/a)(1-1/n)\), so a series of very small impact parameters measures mainly that one combination; broad coverage is needed to resolve \(a\) and \(n\) individually.

The time inside the well is \(2a\cos r_i/(n v_\infty)\), because the interior chord has offset \(b/n\) and speed \(nv_\infty\). At the same \(b\), the time between intersections of the free path with the circle is \(2a\cos i/v_\infty\). For \(n=1.25,b=0.6a\), their ratio is \(\cos28.69^\circ/[1.25\cos36.87^\circ]\approx0.877\). The faster interior motion crosses a longer chord in less time. For grazing free paths the reference chord becomes very short and the comparison can reverse. Reporting which part of the trajectory the timing equipment resolves is therefore as important as measuring the dwell accurately.

Propagate uncertainties from a detector ring to an inferred force

Suppose \(N\) counts are recorded in an angular ring and the product of incident fluence, target count and calibrated efficiency is \(C\). The estimate of bin-averaged cross section is \(N/(C\Delta\Omega)\), with \(\Delta\Omega=2\pi[\cos\theta_{\rm low}-\cos\theta_{\rm high}]\) for full azimuth. Approximately independent counting events have fractional standard error \(1/\sqrt N\), but the fluence and efficiency create correlated scale errors across angular bins. When integrating bins to reconstruct \(b^2(\theta)\), a shared five-percent normalisation uncertainty does not shrink just because the number of angular bins grows. It propagates into an approximately \(2.5\%\) scale uncertainty in the recovered impact parameter.

For an ideal hard sphere, let an experiment integrate the area feeding angles greater than \(90^\circ\) and find \(14.1\pm0.7\,{\rm mm^2}\). Since this is \(\pi b(90^\circ)^2=\pi a^2/2\), the inferred radius is \(\sqrt{2(14.1)/\pi}=3.00\,{\rm mm}\) with uncertainty approximately \(0.075\,{\rm mm}\) for small errors. If the unobserved backward final ten degrees are simply omitted, an extra solid angle \(\Delta\Omega=2\pi[1-\cos10^\circ]\) is missed. Its small size for a smooth hard-sphere law can be estimated, but a general inverse analysis cannot assume that backward intensity is smooth without testing it.

Is multiplying each measured bin centre by the angular density there sufficient for a steep forward-scattering law?

Work through the solution

A finite bin measures an integral against solid angle, not the value at its centre. For a rapidly varying \(\sin^{-4}(\theta/2)\) density near zero, the lower-angle part of the bin can dominate its count. Use the exact preimage annulus \(\pi[b(\theta_{\rm low})^2-b(\theta_{\rm high})^2]\) or integrate the model numerically over the detector's angle-dependent efficiency. Repeat with narrower bins as a check. If the lower acceptance overlaps the direct beam, separate the unscattered population and account for its instrumental angular spread before assigning a cross section to small deflections.

Work directly in counts for a finite annular detector

For the repulsive inverse-square example take \(d=2.0\,{\rm mm}\) and a full-azimuth ring that accepts polar angles from \(55^\circ\) to \(65^\circ\). The expected incident area is \(\pi d^2[\cot^2(27.5^\circ)-\cot^2(32.5^\circ)]\), about \(15.4\,{\rm mm^2}\). Multiplying by an incident fluence of \(100\) particles per square millimetre and efficiency \(0.80\) predicts about \(1230\) counts. A Poisson fluctuation scale is then roughly \(35\) counts. The exact integral supplies a meaningful reference even when the plotting software reports a differential value at the centre of the bin.

Moving both angular boundaries one degree outward decreases the count because the repulsion sends fewer particles through larger angles. The change can be calculated by reevaluating the two cotangents, keeping the fluence and efficiency fixed. This sensitivity allows a calibration uncertainty in the angular zero to be propagated into a count prediction without treating it as a force uncertainty. If fluence is independently measured the dependence on \(d^2\) can then estimate the interaction scale. If fluence is itself unknown, a single count measures only their product; independent calibration or measurements across energies are needed to separate them.

An incoming uniform beam scatters from a hard disk in three-dimensional impact geometry with effective radius \(3.0\,{\rm mm}\). What area feeds the polar bin from \(60^\circ\) to \(90^\circ\)?

Work through the solution

The accepted impact parameters run from \(a\cos45^\circ\) to \(a\cos30^\circ\). The difference of their squared radii is \(9(0.75-0.50)=2.25\,{\rm mm^2}\), giving area \(2.25\pi=7.07\,{\rm mm^2}\). Relate a counted coincidence to an operator

Denote unit measurement directions by \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\). Outcome projectors are \((1+s\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)/2\) and \((1+t\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b)/2\), for \(s,t=\pm1\). \[p(s,t|\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\tfrac14(1-st\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),\]
\[E(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b.\]

Summing over either outcome leaves one half for the other observer, independent of the remote angle. Covariance can change while the individual counting rates remain constant. At an angle of 60 degrees the probabilities of the two equal-outcome combinations sum to one quarter, and of opposite outcomes to three quarters. Their signed difference gives −0.5. The lab models spin-axis angles; linear polarisation analysers give a doubled angle in their effective two-state representation.

The quantum prediction combines expectation values measured on different subensembles with independently chosen setting pairs. Use coplanar angles \(a=0,\ a'=\pi/2,\ b=\theta,\ b'=-\theta\).

\[S=E(a,b)+E(a,b')+E(a',b)-E(a',b')=-2(\cos\theta+\sin\theta).\]

Follow the amplitudes through both pulses

Immediately before the second pulse each state has population one half; measuring population here cannot reveal detuning. Choose a second pulse equivalent to projecting onto \((|g\rangle+e^{-i\phi}|e\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Then one output population is

\[P(\tau,\phi)=\tfrac12[1+\cos(\Delta\tau-\phi)].\]

The sign of the phase follows our definitions of detuning and the reference pulse. Shifting the reference phase makes it possible to check that sign. A measurement using only \(\phi=0\) cannot distinguish \(\Delta\) from \(-\Delta\). Coherence is the off-diagonal density-matrix element that the second pulse converts into an observable population difference.

Derive two envelopes with different origins

A homogeneous decay time \(T_2\) multiplies the off-diagonal element by \(e^{-\tau/T_2}\). If instead the detuning of each experimental shot is constant during that shot and drawn from a Gaussian distribution of mean \(\bar\Delta\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_\Delta\), average the complex phase, not its absolute value. Completing the square in the Gaussian integral gives

\[\langle e^{i\Delta\tau}\rangle=e^{i\bar\Delta\tau}e^{-\sigma_\Delta^2\tau^2/2}.\]

With both independent mechanisms the measured contrast is \(C(\tau)=\exp[-\tau/T_2-\sigma_\Delta^2\tau^2/2]\). One mechanism gives an initial logarithmic slope, the other adds curvature. A single low contrast measured at just one delay cannot establish which one caused it.

Transport coefficients from equilibrium fluctuations

Statistical mechanics from microscopic counting to material response

How can a fluid in equilibrium reveal the diffusion constant that would govern a spreading drop, or the viscosity that would resist an applied shear? Derive an exact connection between displacements and velocity correlations, generalise it to stress and heat currents and test how a finite record can bias each number. The assumptions behind convergence matter as much as the prefactor.

Integrate the velocity correlation before taking a long-time limit

Observe one Cartesian coordinate of a tagged particle in a homogeneous equilibrium fluid with zero mean drift. The displacement over time \(t\) is the integral of its velocity. Square that integral, average over the equilibrium ensemble and use stationarity to replace the two-time expectation by \(C_v(t_1-t_2)\). For a scalar component in a time-reversal-symmetric equilibrium state this correlation is even. Integrate over the two triangles of the time square:

\[\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^2\rangle=2\int_0^t(t-s)C_v(s)\,ds,\]
\[\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\langle\Delta x^2(t)\rangle=\int_0^t C_v(s)\,ds .\]

If the correlation is integrable and boundary effects remain absent, the long-time slope yields a diffusion coefficient \(D=\int_0^\infty C_v(s)ds\). In \(d\) isotropic dimensions one may use \(D=(1/d)\int\langle\mathbf v(s)\cdot\mathbf v(0)\rangle ds\) or the mean-square-displacement slope divided by \(2d\). Using the full vector dot product without division by dimension gives an overestimate. Directional diffusion in an anisotropic material requires tensor components rather than a forced scalar average.

The derivation is kinematic and does not assume a Markovian motion. A fluid with velocity memory or an oscillating solid still obeys the finite-time relation. Those systems need not exhibit the same long-time proportionality to time. A bound oscillator can have persistent or alternating velocity correlations and a bounded displacement. An observation window in which the mean square grows over a fraction of an oscillation is insufficient to claim diffusion.

A solvable benchmark from ballistic to diffusive motion

For one coordinate take the underdamped Langevin equation \(m\dot v=-\zeta v+\xi\), with zero-mean white noise satisfying \(\langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle=2k_BT\zeta\,\delta(t-t')\). In equilibrium \(\langle v^2\rangle=k_BT/m\). Condition on \(v(0)\) and integrate for positive time. The later random force has no correlation with the initial velocity, giving \(C_v=(k_BT/m)e^{-t/\tau_v}\), where \(\tau_v=m/\zeta\). Its area and the resulting displacement are

\[D=\frac{k_BT}{\zeta},\]
\[\langle\Delta x^2(t)\rangle=2D[t-\tau_v(1-e^{-t/\tau_v})].\]

At \(t\ll\tau_v\), the constant and linear terms inside the brackets cancel and displacement is ballistic:

\[\langle\Delta x^2\rangle\simeq(k_BT/m)t^2.\]

At \(t\gg\tau_v\) the slope approaches \(2D\). Fitting a line through the origin at times only a few relaxation times overestimates the influence of the negative intercept in this long-time expression and underestimates \(D\). Comparisons at multiple lags distinguish crossover curvature from a true anomalous power law.

For an inert sphere of radius 0.50 micrometres and density \(2000\) kg per cubic metre in water with \(\eta=1.00\) mPa s at 298 K, its bare mass is \(1.05\times10^{-15}\) kg and Stokes drag \(9.42\times10^{-9}\) kg/s. The estimate \(\tau_v=0.111\) microseconds is far faster than ordinary video tracking. \(D=0.437\) square micrometres per second and the one-coordinate rms displacement over 10 ms is 0.0935 micrometres. Hydrodynamic added mass and memory affect very short-time behaviour; the simple exponential is an analytical reference, not a claim that a water suspension remains Markovian down to arbitrary times.

Relate a weak imposed force to equilibrium motion

In the same soluble model a small constant force \(F\) turns the mean velocity into \(F(1-e^{-t/\tau_v})/\zeta\), so the mobility \(b=\lim\langle v\rangle/F=1/\zeta\). Together with equilibrium diffusion this gives the Einstein relation \(D=k_BTb\). A broader equilibrium argument considers a dilute suspension in a weak conservative potential \(U(x)\). Its current is

\[j=-D\,\partial_x n+bFn,\]

with \(F=-\partial_xU\). The Boltzmann distribution \(n\propto e^{-U/k_BT}\) must carry zero current; substitution gives the same relation without assuming exponential velocity relaxation.

Linear response connects the full weak-force impulse response to an equilibrium correlation. For a perturbation coupled as \(-F(t)x\), classical equilibrium response of velocity has causal kernel \(C_v(t)/(k_BT)\) for \(t>0\) under the usual microreversibility assumptions. Integrating the response kernel for a constant weak force gives

\[b=(1/k_BT)\int_0^\infty C_v(t)dt,\]

which recovers the kinematic diffusion relation. Strong forcing, an active bath or a nonequilibrium steady state need not obey this simple equilibrium identity.

A direct check compares independently measured weak sedimentation or calibrated drift with freely diffusing trajectories. For the previous bead a force 10 fN would produce a mean drift about 1.06 micrometres per second in the ideal Stokes regime. During a 10 ms frame its mean drift is about 0.011 micrometres, much smaller than rms Brownian motion, so many frames or many particles are needed. A single short jagged record can appear to move against the force. Use equal positive and negative applied forces to cancel constant stage drift in the estimated mobility.

Stress and heat current carry their own normalisations

In a homogeneous isotropic laboratory fluid, define \(P_{xy}\) as a spatially averaged off-diagonal microscopic stress, with units Pa. Its equilibrium mean is zero in an isotropic state without imposed shear. A weak velocity gradient couples to momentum transport. The Green–Kubo expression for dynamic shear viscosity is

\[\eta=\frac{V}{k_BT}\int_0^\infty\langle P_{xy}(t)P_{xy}(0)\rangle\,dt .\]

If a program outputs an extensive stress moment \(J_{xy}=V P_{xy}\), the prefactor is \(1/(Vk_BT)\). Multiplying an already extensive current by volume again is a common orders-of-magnitude error. Check dimensions: pressure squared times time times volume divided by energy gives pascal seconds. Kinetic and interaction contributions to microscopic stress must both be included according to the model; a calculation with ideal kinetic flux alone need not reproduce the viscosity of a dense fluid.

Similarly for a component of total heat current \(J_{Q,x}\) with units energy times length per time, the thermal conductivity in an isotropic equilibrium single-component system is

\[\kappa=\frac{1}{Vk_BT^2}\int_0^\infty\langle J_{Q,x}(t)J_{Q,x}(0)\rangle\,dt .\]

Here \(k_BT^2\) means \(k_B\) times \(T^2\). If a heat-flux density is used instead, the volume prefactor moves as for stress. Heat current must exclude energy carried by a net mass flow as appropriate to the chosen thermodynamic constraint. In mixtures several conserved species currents can couple, and the measured conductivity at zero mass flux can differ from one unprojected current coefficient. The meaning of the measured flux and the imposed condition must be specified before comparing coefficients.

For an illustrative volume \(10^{-24}\) cubic metres at 300 K, assume one stress autocorrelation \(C_P(t)=(2.0\times10^6\ {\rm Pa})^2 e^{-t/(2.0\,{\rm ps})}\). Its area is 8.0 square pascal seconds. Multiplication by \(V/(k_BT)\) gives viscosity \(1.93\times10^{-3}\) Pa s. Doubling the simulated volume of an otherwise similar homogeneous fluid generally reduces the variance of its volume-averaged stress, keeping the physical transport coefficient intensive. Holding the same numerical stress variance while merely changing the prefactor would describe a different equilibrium fluctuation model.

Find a correlation integral's useful time window

An estimate at lag \(s\) averages over pairs of samples separated by that lag. In a record of duration \(T_{\rm rec}\), the number of available pairs decreases with lag. Overlapping time origins strongly correlate the estimates, so plotting one error bar per raw pair exaggerates precision. Integrating an estimated correlation to the maximum possible lag frequently accumulates late random noise rather than useful signal. Conversely stopping too early omits a physical tail.

For a positive single-exponential reference, integrating to three correlation times retains \(1-e^{-3}=95.0\) percent of the area. Five times retain 99.3 percent. A correlation containing a small, much slower component can look essentially flat on the main decay scale while contributing appreciably to the area. Divide a long run into blocks, compare running integrals and check several window lengths. Report the variation between independent blocks and any explicit tail model used beyond the measured interval.

At short lags, finite exposure averages correlated positions or velocities. Uncorrelated localisation errors in position increase nonzero-lag displacement variance by a constant \(2s_x^2\) per coordinate. Numerically differentiating those same noisy positions creates large artificial velocity variance and negative correlations between adjacent increments. An apparent negative velocity lobe can therefore be physical backscattering or a measurement artefact. Comparing a raw displacement fit with the velocity integral after independently characterising detector noise is more informative than differentiating first and interpreting every feature as fluid memory.

A forced shear wave gives a second viscosity measurement

An equilibrium stress correlation estimates viscosity without imposing any flow. A separate driven measurement can test that coefficient against a directly observed penetration depth. Take a Newtonian fluid filling \(y>0\), driven by a laterally oscillating plate at \(y=0\) with speed \(U_0\cos\omega t\). Neglect boundaries at large \(y\), compressibility and nonlinear convective effects in this spatially uniform tangential shear. The momentum equation reduces to

\[\partial_tu=\nu\partial_y^2u,\]

with kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\eta/\rho\).

Seek \(u=\operatorname{Re}[U_0e^{-\lambda y-i\omega t}]\) and choose the square root with positive real part. Then \(\lambda=(1-i)/\delta\),

\[\delta=\sqrt{2\nu/\omega}.\]

Both amplitude decay and phase lag carry information: at distance one penetration depth the amplitude is \(e^{-1}\) of the wall speed and the lag magnitude is one radian. A single complex velocity map can determine viscosity from either slope and use their agreement to test the simple model.

For a fluid of density \(1000\) kg per cubic metre and measured penetration depth 0.126 mm at 20 Hz, \(\nu=\omega\delta^2/2\simeq1.00\times10^{-6}\) square metres per second and \(\eta\simeq1.00\) mPa s. A stress-fluctuation integral yielding a value twice as large would predict a penetration depth larger by \(\sqrt2\), approximately 0.178 mm. Such a comparison can discriminate a missing component or volume factor in the equilibrium calculation if density, temperature and fluid composition match.

At high enough frequency the fluid may have a complex frequency-dependent viscosity rather than the zero-frequency coefficient from a Green–Kubo area. In linear response that generalisation is the one-sided Fourier transform of the equilibrium stress correlation with appropriate prefactors. A single-exponential stress memory gives a frequency-dependent response with a characteristic roll-off and phase lag. Comparing a high-frequency penetration measurement directly to a zero-frequency area without checking the timescale can therefore produce a genuine difference, not a statistical failure.

Remove a centre-of-mass mode only when the observable requires it

In a finite periodic particle simulation total momentum may be exactly conserved. If the initial centre of mass has a random velocity and no thermostat removes it, every particle's measured laboratory coordinate includes the common drift \(V_{\rm CM}t\). Its mean-squared displacement gains a ballistic term \(\langle V_{\rm CM}^2\rangle t^2\). In a canonical ensemble the centre-of-mass velocity variance in one direction is \(k_BT/M_{\rm tot}\). Though it falls with system size, at sufficiently long times its quadratic contribution exceeds the linear diffusive part.

For 1000 equal particles of mass \(m\), an equilibrium centre-of-mass mode contributes one thousandth of the single-particle instantaneous velocity variance. In a long-time autocorrelation this small constant never integrates to a finite diffusion coefficient. Subtract centre-of-mass displacement or work in a zero-total-momentum ensemble before taking the long-time integral of an internal tracer's motion. In a real sample with externally imposed drift, decide whether the physical quantity sought is diffusion about that drift or absolute laboratory displacement; the subtraction has a physical meaning rather than merely a cosmetic improvement.

Conversely, indiscriminately subtracting the mean velocity of a tiny observation subregion separately at every time can remove genuine hydrodynamic long-wavelength motion. A strongly applied single-particle thermostat can alter current correlations and suppress transport even while maintaining the correct static temperature histogram. Check the thermostat strength and system size; compare an equilibrium-prepared weakly thermostatted trajectory with an energy-conserving production segment where appropriate. Maintaining the correct one-time distribution is not sufficient to preserve all dynamical correlation functions.

The distinction also matters when removing an estimated mean stress before integrating its fluctuations. The true shear mean may vanish by symmetry, but a short noisy record has a nonzero sample mean. Subtracting a slowly varying trend on the same timescale as a genuine stress tail can erase physical viscosity. Use independent samples, separately measured drifts and explicit sensitivity to the subtraction window. A persistent nonzero off-diagonal stress under nominal isotropic conditions may indicate incomplete equilibration, a deformed box or an unrelaxed solid rather than a fluid transport coefficient.

Heat and particle currents can share fluctuations

Consider two thermodynamic fluxes \(J_1,J_2\) driven weakly by conjugate forces \(X_1,X_2\), chosen so entropy production density is \(J_1X_1+J_2X_2\). Linear response writes \(J_i=\sum_jL_{ij}X_j\). Near time-reversal-symmetric equilibrium, with flux parities chosen appropriately and no magnetic-field reversal complications, reciprocal relations give

\[L_{12}=L_{21}.\]

The symmetric part must be positive semidefinite so that entropy production is nonnegative for every small force. Thus \(L_{11},L_{22}\ge0\) and \(L_{12}^2\le L_{11}L_{22}\).

Suppose a thermal measurement enforces \(J_1=0\) by allowing a compensating force \(X_1\) to develop, as when a concentration or electrochemical gradient cancels a coupled particle flux. Solving the first row gives

\[X_1=-L_{12}X_2/L_{11}.\]

Substitution into the second yields response coefficient \(L_{22}-L_{21}L_{12}/L_{11}\). The measured constrained heat response can therefore be smaller than the coefficient at fixed \(X_1=0\). The difference has a simple operational origin in the boundary condition enforced during the experiment.

Take consistent normalised coefficients \(L_{11}=4,L_{22}=3,L_{12}=L_{21}=2\). At zero first force the heat flux slope is three. At zero first flux it is \(3-4/4=2\). Both are positive and consistent with the same equilibrium correlation matrix. Comparing a simulated heat-current autocorrelation corresponding to the unconstrained coefficient against a zero-particle-flux experiment requires the mixed-current correlation too. Apparent disagreement by a factor of one and a half can arise entirely from this distinction.

Green–Kubo cross-correlations must use properly defined conjugate fluxes and units. Redefining heat current by adding a constant multiple of particle current changes the matrix entries but must leave a physically defined zero-particle-flux heat measurement invariant when the forces are transformed consistently. This reference invariance is analogous to subtracting a per-particle energy reference in grand-canonical covariance. It provides a powerful test of a multicomponent transport implementation without relying on any empirical transport value.

A lattice trajectory provides an exactly known correlation integral

For a one-dimensional nearest-neighbour walk with steps of length \(a\) every interval \(\Delta t\), suppose successive step directions have correlation \(\langle s_{j+k}s_j\rangle=r^k\) with \(|r|<1\), and zero mean. Squaring the sum of \(n\) steps gives

\[a^2[n+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)r^k].\]

In the large-\(n\) limit divide by \(2n\Delta t\) to obtain

\[D=a^2(1+r)/[2\Delta t(1-r)].\]

The uncorrelated random-walk value is recovered at \(r=0\).

With \(a=1\) micrometre and \(\Delta t=0.10\) s, uncorrelated diffusion is five square micrometres per second. Persistence \(r=0.5\) raises it to fifteen, while anticorrelation \(r=-0.5\) lowers it to \(5/3\). All three walks have the same step-size variance, so an estimate based only on one-frame displacement misses the entire correlation effect. The finite-lag autocorrelation sum and a long-time displacement fit should recover the same value on a long simulated record.

The discrete formula also clarifies the special zero-lag weight in numerical integration. An integral approximated from sampled symmetric correlations gives half weight to the zero lag on a one-sided trapezoidal rule. A careless double count of the instantaneous self term predicts twice the uncorrelated diffusion. Benchmark a numerical estimator with this exact walk before applying it to molecular data with unknown late tails and noisy velocity derivatives.

Measure an off-diagonal diffusion coefficient after rotating the sample

In an anisotropic sheet a tracer's two-dimensional displacement covariance need not align with the camera axes. Let its long-time principal diffusion coefficients be \(D_1,D_2\) and the first principal axis make angle \(\theta\) with laboratory \(x\). In matrix form the laboratory tensor is \(R\,{\rm diag}(D_1,D_2)R^{\mathsf T}\), with \(R\) the planar rotation. At sufficiently large lag in an effectively unbounded drift-free region, the displacement covariance matrix tends to \(2D t\). An off-diagonal covariance is a measurable correlation between displacement components even if the noise is independent in the material principal directions.

Take \(D_1=2.0\) and \(D_2=0.50\) square micrometres per second with \(\theta=30^\circ\). Laboratory components are \(D_{xx}=1.625\), \(D_{yy}=0.875\) and \(D_{xy}=0.650\), in the same units. At 0.10 s the cross covariance is 0.130 square micrometres. Rotating the physical sheet by ninety degrees interchanges the two diagonal values and reverses the sign of the off-diagonal one. An instrument-fixed camera readout artefact would not generally follow this material rotation.

To infer the principal axes from data, fit each covariance component versus lag using a common drift subtraction and independent position calibration. Diagonalise the symmetric fitted diffusion matrix. Positive semidefiniteness requires

\[D_{xy}^2\le D_{xx}D_{yy}.\]

A strong violation indicates incompatible estimates, unit conversion errors or statistical uncertainty, not a physically permissible strongly anisotropic diffusion tensor. Near-degenerate principal values make the orientation poorly determined even when the mean diffusion coefficient is precise. Report uncertainties on eigenvalues and orientation separately.

A finite field of view introduces a further bias. Fast particles along the high-diffusion direction leave the image sooner, and a tracking pipeline retaining only long trajectories can favour slow or fortuitously confined paths. Compare different fields of view and minimum track durations, or fit a measurement model incorporating censoring. A physical material anisotropy should persist after correcting these effects; apparent anisotropy aligned with the short direction of a rectangular observation window demands particular scrutiny.

A weak calibrated force along different axes supplies an independent check through the tensor Einstein relation under equilibrium conditions. If the diffusion tensor predicts off-axis mean drift, the mobility experiment should observe its sign and magnitude after reversing the force. A discrepancy could reveal active forcing, nonstationarity, an incorrect temperature or a mismatch between the mobility and displacement observables. It cannot be diagnosed from a scalar mean square alone.

Another rotational invariant is the trace of the diffusion tensor. In the example its value stays 2.50 square micrometres per second for every sample orientation, while the determinant stays one square micrometre to the fourth per second squared. A comparison of two camera orientations therefore provides numerical checks independent of the fitted angle. A drift mistakenly left in the displacement covariance can violate the expected linear dependence on lag, making the inferred effective eigenvalues grow with the selected fitting window. Subtract the ensemble mean displacement at each lag and record that mean separately as a flow measurement. Conversely do not subtract an individual best-fit line from every short trajectory without modelling the resulting loss of long-time fluctuations; that procedure can bias both diffusion coefficients downward.

The variance slope is \((0.064-0.024)/(0.050-0.010)=1.00\) square micrometre per second, so \(D=0.500\) square micrometres per second. The intercept is 0.014 square micrometres, equal to twice the single-frame localisation variance. The implied rms localisation error is \(\sqrt{0.007}=0.0837\) micrometres. Forcing the fit through the origin at the shortest lag would give \(D=1.20\), more than twice the inferred value.

Acquire more lags to test the linear model. A static drift contributes a term growing as \(t^2\) if its mean displacement is not removed, while finite exposure can lower the physically measured short-lag variance. Two points always define a line and cannot independently validate either noise model.

Tutorial · Estimate a hidden slow tail in viscosity

A stress correlation is modelled as \(A[e^{-t/\tau}+0.02e^{-t/(40\tau)}]\). What fraction of its true area comes from the low-amplitude tail? How much does a cutoff at \(5\tau\) capture?

Weight a correlation by its duration

The fast and slow areas are \(A\tau\) and \(0.80A\tau\). The slow contribution is 44.4 percent of total. Integration to five fast times captures \(A\tau[(1-e^{-5})+0.80(1-e^{-5/40})]\simeq1.087A\tau\), or about 60.4 percent. Visual disappearance of the dominant peak therefore does not establish convergence of the transport coefficient.

The slow contribution must be supported by independent long blocks or a physically motivated model. A fitted tiny constant baseline can generate arbitrarily large apparent area if extrapolated indefinitely. Subtracting a sample mean estimated from the same short record can conversely suppress a genuine slow mode. Compare records much longer than the proposed tail time before trusting the extrapolation.

Tutorial · Is an algebraic tail compatible with ordinary diffusion?

Suppose a measured long-time velocity correlation remains positive and scales as \(t^{-\alpha}\). Determine when its Green–Kubo area exists and infer the displacement growth for \(0<\alpha<1\) and for \(\alpha=1\). Why would a finite periodic simulation require care?

Integrate the time-dependent slope before naming a diffusion coefficient

For \(\alpha>1\) the tail integral converges. For \(0<\alpha<1\), the running area grows as \(t^{1-\alpha}\), so displacement variance grows as \(t^{2-\alpha}\) under the stated persistent positive tail. At \(\alpha=1\), the running area grows logarithmically and the displacement variance is of order \(t\log t\). Sign oscillations, finite-size termination or another late-time crossover can alter these conclusions.

A periodic box admits only discrete long-wavelength modes and may cut off a hydrodynamic tail. Repeating at larger sizes and comparing the range before signals traverse the box helps identify a bulk regime. Unwrap particle coordinates crossing periodic boundaries before calculating a displacement. A wrapped coordinate can falsely saturate and imitate a confined particle even in a freely diffusing periodic fluid.
