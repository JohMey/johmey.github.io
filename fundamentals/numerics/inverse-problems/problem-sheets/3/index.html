Nonlinear estimates and experimental design

Distinguish a successful optimisation step from a reliable parameter estimate and a prediction that generalises.

Problem sheet 3 · Chapters 5 and 6

1. One step, two possible answers2. Which two measurement conditions?3. Read a fit as a prediction

1. One step, two possible answers

For \(r(p)=p^2-4\), (a) compute the Gauss–Newton steps from \(p=1\) and \(p=-1\). (b) Compare the actual and linear predicted residuals after the positive-start step. (c) Explain what happens from \(p=0\), and why one local standard error cannot describe all possibilities.

Inspect the slope at each starting point

The step is \(-r/(2p)\) for \(p\ne0\). It is \(+1.5\) at 1 and \(-1.5\) at −1. From 1 the linear model predicts residual zero, whereas \(r(2.5)=2.25\). Its magnitude still decreased from 3. At zero the derivative vanishes, and the linear model predicts no improvement in any direction. The two separated exact solutions \(+2,-2\) produce distinct local uncertainty regions unless physics fixes the sign.

2. Which two measurement conditions?

In dimensionless whitened coordinates each design may add two rows. Compare \(J_A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(J_B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-0.5\\1&0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). (a) Determine each information matrix and rank. (b) Find the uncertainty of the second parameter under design B and after repeating the whole design four independent times. (c) Relate the calculation to measuring decay only at a reference temperature.

Compute the information added by each row

Design A gives \(\operatorname{diag}(2,0)\), rank one. Design B gives \(\operatorname{diag}(2,0.5)\), rank two. In the local linear unit-noise model the second parameter has variance 2, hence standard deviation \(\sqrt2\). Four independent copies multiply information by four and halve that standard deviation. At the reference temperature the Arrhenius activation derivative vanishes. More sampling at that one temperature cannot recover the missing direction.

3. Read a fit as a prediction

Use the temperature-design experiment. (a) Compute the narrow-to-wide standard-error ratio for \(E\) at fixed noise. (b) Compare the wide-range fit error with its own standard error using the known 115 kJ/mol truth. (c) Design a validation that tests a new temperature and one that tests noise calibration.

Change a single condition per comparison

The ratio is \(1.4300/0.3636=3.93\). For the wide, lower-noise fit the signed error is \(114.0367-115=-0.9633\) kJ/mol, about \(-2.65\) reported standard errors. This is one draw; it does not estimate the long-run coverage fraction. To assess transfer, hold out a complete temperature series, fit the others and predict that entire curve. To assess noise calibration, repeat the same design with many independent seeds and compare scatter or interval coverage against the local uncertainty calculation.
