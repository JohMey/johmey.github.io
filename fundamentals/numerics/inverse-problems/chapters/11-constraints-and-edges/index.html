Physical constraints change the optimality test

An attenuation coefficient cannot be negative. In a convex inversion with lower bound zero, the unconstrained least-squares normal equations need not hold at a valid constrained solution. Consider minimising a differentiable \(F(x)\) with \(x_j\ge0\). At a free component the derivative must be zero. At a zero component only positive feasible perturbations exist, so the derivative must be nonnegative. Negative reconstructed cells cannot generally be repaired by clipping after inversion because the forward operator couples neighbouring parameters.

For example minimise \(\tfrac12[(x_1+x_2-1)^2+(x_1+1)^2]\) under \(x_i\ge0\). The unconstrained solution is \((-1,2)\). Clipping gives \((0,2)\), which overpredicts the first observation. On the active face \(x_1=0\), the minimising \(x_2\) is 1. At \((0,1)\) the gradient is \((1,0)\) and the bound conditions hold. The price of feasibility appears in the data residual; it is important to show which observations the constrained model cannot fit.

Derive the soft-threshold rule

For a scalar coefficient with observation \(b\), minimise \(\tfrac12(x-b)^2+\lambda|x|\). On \(x>0\) the stationary point is \(b-\lambda\), feasible there only when \(b>\lambda\); on \(x<0\) it is \(b+\lambda\). At zero, the admissible slopes of the absolute value range from \(-1\) to 1, so zero solves the condition when \(|b|\le\lambda\). Together these give \(x=\operatorname{sign}(b)\max(|b|-\lambda,0)\). Even retained coefficients shrink by \(\lambda\), a real amplitude bias. With \(b=(-0.2,0.7,2.4)\) and \(\lambda=0.5\), the estimate is \((0,0.2,1.9)\) for an identity forward map.

Penalise changes in a field

In one dimension discrete total variation is \(\sum_j|x_{j+1}-x_j|\). A single large jump and several monotone small steps spanning the same overall change have equal total variation. In contrast a squared difference penalty favours spreading the jump over several sites. That explains why total variation can preserve an abrupt edge when the data localise it. It can also create nearly constant plateaus in a field that really changes smoothly.

For two observed values \(b_1,b_2\), use the variables \(m=(x_1+x_2)/2,\ d=(x_2-x_1)/2\). With objective \(\tfrac12\sum(x_i-b_i)^2+\lambda|x_2-x_1|\), the optimal mean is the observed mean and half-difference is the soft-thresholded observed half-difference with threshold \(\lambda\). When \(|b_2-b_1|\le2\lambda\) the two estimated levels fuse. This concrete loss of resolution should be reported when interpreting a uniform patch in a reconstructed image.

Make an edge claim testable

Generate test fields both with a sharp jump and with a smooth ramp. Pass them through the same sensor model and noise level, then fit with the same strength-selection procedure used for real data. Measure contrast bias and edge-location error separately; an image can put an edge in the correct place but suppress its amplitude. For a nonnegative material field inspect the gradient at active zeros alongside the residual. The regulariser and the bounds each supply information that the instrument may not have measured.

Follow total variation through three coupled cells

Consider three ordered cell values observed directly with equal independent precision. Minimise \(\tfrac12\sum_i(x_i-b_i)^2+\lambda(|x_2-x_1|+|x_3-x_2|)\). Introduce edge variables \(s_1,s_2\) equal to the signs of nonzero differences. At a zero difference either variable may take any value between minus one and one. Differentiation with these admissible subgradients gives the three balances \(x_1-b_1-\lambda s_1=0,\ x_2-b_2+\lambda(s_1-s_2)=0,\ x_3-b_3+\lambda s_2=0\). A candidate solution must satisfy all three balances and must agree with the sign choices that produced it.

Adding the balances eliminates both internal edge forces. Consequently the reconstructed values have exactly the same sum as the observations, regardless of the penalty strength. The quadratic part is strictly convex, so any feasible sign assignment satisfying the balances proves that the state is the unique minimiser. Adding lower bounds can introduce reactions and break the sum identity. Unequal precision also changes which weighted average is conserved. The calculation depends on the observation operator being the identity; after blurring, the first derivative of the data term already couples all cells.

Derive the complete fusion path for a short profile

Take \(b=(0,1,5)^T\). For small positive \(\lambda\), both differences remain positive, so \(s_1=s_2=1\). The equations give \(x=(\lambda,1,5-\lambda)^T\). The first two coordinates meet at \(\lambda=1\), while the second and third are still unequal. Assume those first two now form a plateau of height \(a\), with \(s_2=1\). Adding their balances gives \(2a-1-\lambda=0\), hence \(a=(1+\lambda)/2\). The first equation then requires \(s_1=a/\lambda=(1+\lambda)/(2\lambda)\), which lies in the permitted interval exactly when \(\lambda\ge1\).

The third coordinate stays \(5-\lambda\). Equating it to the plateau gives \((1+\lambda)/2=5-\lambda\), or \(\lambda=3\). Beyond this value all three cells coincide, and sum conservation fixes them at two. The required subgradients are \(s_1=2/\lambda\) and \(s_2=3/\lambda\), both admissible for \(\lambda\ge3\). This completes the path without numerical optimisation. At strengths 0.5, 2 and 4 the respective profiles are \((0.5,1,4.5)\), \((1.5,1.5,3)\) and \((2,2,2)\).

The thresholds record when the data imbalance inside a proposed plateau can be supported by the admissible edge subgradients. A flat patch at the second strength is therefore compatible with unequal raw values of zero and one. Conversely, a visible third-cell edge at that strength has already lost much of its amplitude. A reconstruction report that uses one threshold for declaring the existence of a jump and another for estimating its amplitude should explain both decisions.

Checking the final all-fused solution using only its observed mean would miss the second edge constraint. For example at strength two that constant candidate would require \(s_2=1.5\), outside the admissible unit interval, despite reproducing total mass perfectly. The failed local balance tells which boundary of the candidate plateau has to reopen. This provides a simple hand diagnostic for more complicated one-dimensional reconstructions when an apparently reasonable flat interval has been proposed.
