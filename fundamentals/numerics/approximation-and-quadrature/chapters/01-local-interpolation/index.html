Piecewise interpolation and error bounds

Construct a continuous profile from sensor values, prove a local interpolation bound and distinguish sampling error from measurement uncertainty.

Reconstruct from the information available

A line of thermometers supplies values \(y_i\) at increasing coordinates \(x_i\). It does not determine a unique continuous function: any function vanishing at every sensor can be added to a candidate profile. Choosing piecewise straight segments asserts that the unresolved curvature between consecutive readings is small enough for the intended measurement. This assumption may be excellent for predicting a mean temperature and poor for estimating a local heat flux, since differentiation increases the influence of measurement noise.

For \(x\in[x_i,x_{i+1}]\), let \(s=(x-x_i)/h_i\), where \(h_i=x_{i+1}-x_i>0\). The unique affine function matching both readings is \(I_hy=(1-s)y_i+sy_{i+1}\). It follows by writing an unknown intercept and slope, imposing the two endpoint values and eliminating both unknown coefficients. The weights are nonnegative and sum to one. The interpolated value lies between adjacent readings, constants are reproduced, and bounded additive errors of size \(\epsilon\) at the two sensors change the interpolant by at most \(\epsilon\). These properties can fail when extrapolating a considerable distance outside the measured interval.

Why the error is quadratic

Suppose \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable on a panel. Fix an interior point \(t\), and choose a coefficient \(c\) so that \(g(x)=f(x)-I_h f(x)-c(x-x_i)(x-x_{i+1})\) also vanishes at \(t\). It already vanishes at both panel endpoints. Rolle’s theorem applied twice gives a point \(\xi\) where \(g''(\xi)=0\). The interpolant has zero second derivative there, so \(c=f''(\xi)/2\). Evaluate the identity at \(t\) to obtain

\[f(t)-I_h f(t)=\frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(t-x_i)(t-x_{i+1}).\]

The product is nonpositive within the interval and has greatest magnitude \(h_i^2/4\) at its midpoint. Thus the uniform error is at most \(h_i^2\max|f''|/8\). If every panel width is halved, an upper bound based on the same curvature falls by four. When curvature is positive, the segment overestimates the function. Knowledge of the curvature sign supplies information that an absolute error bound would discard.

Turn the bound into a sampling decision

On the unit interval consider a temperature \(T=290+8x^2\) K, with \(x\) given numerically in metres. On the first panel of length 0.25 m the endpoint readings are 290 and 290.5 K. Their average is 290.25 K. The actual midpoint is 290.125 K, an overestimate of 0.125 K. The bound \(16(0.25)^2/8=0.125\) K is attained because the curvature is constant. A second panel of the same width has the same maximum interpolation error even though its temperature change is different.

Now allow an uncertainty of 0.03 K at every source node. A conservative total bound is \(2h^2+0.03\) K for \(h\) in metres. Achieving 0.05 K requires \(h\leq0.10\) m, or at least 11 measurements including both ends. No amount of interpolation onto a denser display grid changes this source requirement. For the slope, two readings with opposite errors can change the estimated derivative by \(2\epsilon/h\), which grows when \(h\) shrinks. Choosing sensor spacing therefore depends on the quantity to be estimated. A spacing suitable for accurate point values may require additional smoothing before slopes inferred from noisy data are useful.

Interpret a finite sensor footprint

Sometimes the observation is already a mean over a finite sensor footprint. For a symmetric footprint of width \(w\) centred at \(x\), the mean of a smooth field is \(f(x)+f''(x)w^2/24+O(w^4)\), by Taylor expansion and symmetry. For the quadratic temperature above this correction is exactly \(16w^2/24\) K when \(w\) is measured in metres. With a 0.10 m footprint it is about 0.0067 K above the point value. Interpreting a cell mean as a point reading therefore adds another positive bias before the chord interpolation even begins. This distinction becomes important when comparing a temperature probe with simulation values stored as cell averages.

Distribute measurements according to the curvature they must resolve

An equally spaced grid spends the same sampling budget in every part of a record. Suppose instead that the trajectory is twice continuously differentiable on each of several known regions, with a certified bound \(M_i\) for \(|f''|\) on region \(i\). Over a panel of width \(h_i\) the largest linear interpolation error is at most \(M_i h_i^2/8\). Thus keeping every local bound below a specified tolerance \(\epsilon\) requires \(h_i\le\sqrt{8\epsilon/M_i}\) whenever \(M_i>0\). A region with four times the curvature bound needs twice as many panels per unit length.

For a fixed total number of panels the reverse question is also helpful. In a region of length \(L_i\) with \(n_i\) equal panels its bound is \(M_iL_i^2/(8n_i^2)\). To minimise the largest such bound over positive continuous allocations, equalise those dominant local bounds. Otherwise moving some resolution from a region with a smaller bound to the largest can improve the maximum. The resulting allocation satisfies \(n_i\propto L_i\sqrt{M_i}\). Counts must then be rounded and their actual bounds checked. Known transitions in curvature should occur at grid nodes if each local bound is to apply to its entire panel.

Place nine panels across a changing acceleration

A displacement history lasts three seconds. In the first two seconds its acceleration magnitude is bounded by 2 m/s², and in the last second by 18 m/s². The relative panel allocations are \(2\sqrt2:\sqrt{18}=2:3\). Nine panels cannot achieve exactly that ratio. Try four panels in the first interval and five in the second. Their widths are 0.5 and 0.2 s, giving error bounds \(2(0.5)^2/8=0.0625\) m and \(18(0.2)^2/8=0.0900\) m. Moving one panel to the late region instead gives bounds \(2(2/3)^2/8=0.1111\) m and \(18(1/6)^2/8=0.0625\) m. The four–five allocation has the smaller worst guarantee.

Nine equally spaced panels have width one third second throughout and include the transition at two seconds. Their late bound is 0.25 m, almost three times the nonuniform worst bound. This comparison is about a guaranteed maximum between observations, not only a discrepancy at selected test locations. Piecewise quadratic motions with constant accelerations attain the midpoint bound on each panel; slopes and positions can be matched at the transition without altering either local error.

The reasoning assumes acceleration bounds independent of the sampled readings. Estimating them from those same coarse readings can miss a short acceleration pulse entirely. If endpoint measurements also contain error bounded by 0.03 m, the convex interpolation weights add at most 0.03 m to each panel bound. The chosen allocation then certifies 0.12 m. Refining the grid further can reduce curvature error but cannot eliminate the measurement floor without better data.

If the allowable total error were 0.10 m, leaving 0.03 m for sensor uncertainty would allow at most 0.07 m for curvature. Four early panels already meet that condition. The late section would require at least six panels because \(18/(8\cdot6^2)=0.0625\), giving a ten-panel acquisition plan.
