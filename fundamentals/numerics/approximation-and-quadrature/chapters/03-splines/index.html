Hermite pieces and cubic splines

Build a cubic from endpoint values and slopes, then determine missing curvature information by joining adjacent pieces smoothly.

Four conditions on a single panel

Line segments reproduce values but change slope abruptly at a sensor. A trajectory planner may require a smooth velocity as well as an accurate position. On a panel of width \(h\) let \(s=(x-x_i)/h\). A cubic polynomial has four coefficients. Specifying \(y_i,y_{i+1}\) and slopes \(d_i,d_{i+1}\) determines it uniquely. A convenient basis writes the interpolant as

\[H=(2s^3-3s^2+1)y_i+(-2s^3+3s^2)y_{i+1}+h(s^3-2s^2+s)d_i+h(s^3-s^2)d_{i+1}.\]

To reconstruct these terms, seek a cubic multiplying \(y_i\) with value one at \(s=0\), zero at \(s=1\), and zero slope at both ends. The four linear conditions give \(2s^3-3s^2+1\). The other basis functions follow by imposing a unit on their own datum and zero on the remaining three. The factors of \(h\) convert derivatives with respect to physical \(x\) into derivatives with respect to dimensionless \(s\). Dropping these factors would make the interpolant change shape when switching between metres and centimetres.

For positions \(y_0=0,y_1=1\) m over one second with both endpoint velocities zero, \(H=3s^2-2s^3\) m. At half time the position is 0.5 m but the speed is 1.5 m/s, greater than the mean speed of 1 m/s. Imposing rest at both ends forces the intervening speed to exceed the mean somewhere. The piece alone leaves endpoint accelerations \(+6\) and \(-6\) m/s². A smooth join to a subsequent segment therefore needs more than identical endpoint positions.

Recover slopes when only values are measured

A cubic spline fits one cubic on every interval, requiring continuity of both first and second derivatives at interior nodes. Let \(M_i=S''(x_i)\). Since a cubic has a linear second derivative, on panel \(i\) interpolate \(M_i\) and \(M_{i+1}\) linearly and integrate twice. Use the two measured endpoint values to fix the integration constants. On equal panels the resulting right-hand slope at the left endpoint is \((y_{i+1}-y_i)/h-h(2M_i+M_{i+1})/6\). The left-hand slope at the right endpoint is \((y_{i+1}-y_i)/h+h(M_i+2M_{i+1})/6\).

Equating these expressions at an interior join gives \(M_{i-1}+4M_i+M_{i+1}=6(y_{i-1}-2y_i+y_{i+1})/h^2\). This tridiagonal system relates each undetermined curvature to the neighbouring data. Two additional end conditions are needed. A natural spline imposes zero curvature at both endpoints. A clamped spline uses given endpoint slopes instead. Choose those conditions from what the experiment actually controls.

Examine the influence of endpoint assumptions

Fit a natural spline at \(x=0,1,2\) to \(y=0,1,0\). The end curvatures are zero and the interior equation gives \(4M_1=6(0-2+0)=-12\), hence \(M_1=-3\). On the first panel \(S''=-3x\). Two integrations give \(S=-x^3/2+ax+b\). The endpoint values set \(b=0,a=1.5\), yielding \(S(0.5)=0.6875\) compared with 0.5 from the straight chord. Symmetry gives zero slope at the summit and a reflected cubic on the second interval.

Smoothness alone cannot establish the true midpoint value. A sensor measuring a sharp corner would support a piecewise linear description, and monotone data can require a slope limiter to avoid artificial extrema in a cubic fit. Likewise, setting endpoint curvature to zero in a mechanical fit asserts a moment condition only under the appropriate beam model. Report the end conditions with the fitted curve, especially when derivatives near the ends are used as physical observables.

Use independent endpoint information as a check

Suppose the three displacement values in the example come with an independent measurement of the initial slope, \(S'(0)=1\) rather than 1.5. Imposing a natural left endpoint as well as this slope would overdetermine that end. One reasonable choice is a mixed spline, clamped on the left and natural on the right. For \(h=1\), the slope formula at the left reads \(1-(2M_0+M_1)/6=1\), giving \(2M_0+M_1=0\). The interior equation gives \(M_0+4M_1=-12\), since \(M_2=0\).

Solving yields \(M_1=-24/7\) and \(M_0=12/7\). A measured slope at one end has changed curvatures inside the interval. That coupling across segments distinguishes a global spline fit from interpolating each segment independently with prescribed slopes at every node.

Why the natural spline minimises bending

Consider all twice continuously differentiable interpolants of the same tabulated values on an interval. For a natural cubic spline \(S\), each panel is cubic, \(S'\) and \(S''\) agree across knots, and \(S''=0\) at both endpoints. Let a competing interpolant be \(S+w\). The perturbation \(w\) vanishes at every knot including the endpoints. Expanding the squared curvature integral gives \(\int(S''+w'')^2=\int(S'')^2+2\int S''w''+\int(w'')^2\). To prove that \(S\) minimises this integral, account for every boundary term in the cross product.

Integrate the cross product by parts twice on each panel. The interior integral containing \(S''''w\) vanishes because the panel is cubic. The term \(S'''w\) vanishes at every panel endpoint since \(w=0\) at each knot. The remaining interface contributions \(S''w'\) cancel between adjacent panels because both factors are continuous. At the outer boundaries \(S''=0\). Thus the cross term vanishes exactly. Equality of the objectives requires \(w''=0\) throughout; a globally affine perturbation vanishing at two distinct knots is zero. This proves uniqueness within the admissible class and explains the natural endpoint condition as part of a variational statement.

Calculate the bending energy on an uneven grid

Suppose the interpolation points are \((0,0),(1,2),(3,2)\). The panel widths are one and two, and the secant slopes are two and zero. For a natural cubic write \(M_i=S''(x_i)\), so \(M_0=M_2=0\). Continuity of first derivative at the central knot gives \(2(1+2)M_1=6(0-2)\), hence \(M_1=-2\). The bending moment changes linearly from zero to −2 on the first panel and from −2 to zero on the second. The longer panel contains twice the squared curvature integral because it sweeps the same range of curvature over twice the distance.

Specifically the first contribution is \(\int_0^1(-2x)^2dx=4/3\). On the second panel \(S''=x-3\), giving \(\int_1^3(x-3)^2dx=8/3\). Total energy is four. Integrating the first curvature twice and fitting its endpoints gives \(S=7x/3-x^3/3\); at the central knot the slope is \(4/3\), neither of the adjacent straight-line secant slopes. On the second panel write \(t=x-1\). Then \(S=2+4t/3-t^2+t^3/6\), which returns to two at \(t=2\). The curve rises above the equal second and third data values in between.

Minimum bending therefore does not guarantee monotonicity between nearly flat measurements or an absence of overshoot. If flat endpoints describe a known plateau rather than merely two equal observations, a shape constraint changes the admissible functions. The preceding proof still explains why the natural interpolant won its own curvature competition, but gives no reason to prefer it after physical constraints have changed.

For these data the overshoot can even be located analytically. On the second panel the derivative vanishes when \(4/3-2t+t^2/2=0\), giving the interior root \(t=2-2/\sqrt3\), about 0.845. A measured value taken near \(x=1.845\) would therefore help discriminate the minimum-bending curve from a physical plateau. Taking another measurement at either already constrained endpoint would do less to resolve that ambiguity.
