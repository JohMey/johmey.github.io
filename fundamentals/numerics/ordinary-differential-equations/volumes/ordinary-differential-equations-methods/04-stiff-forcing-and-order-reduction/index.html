Why stiff stability does not guarantee the expected order

A differential equation is constructed to have a smooth, known solution and a rapidly damped error mode. Use it to distinguish stability of the homogeneous equation, tracking of a moving target and the effect of off-grid stage forcing.

Choose an exact solution before increasing stiffness

Let \(\lambda<0\), let \(\phi(t)\) be a smooth specified function, and solve the Prothero–Robinson model \(y'=\lambda[y-\phi(t)]+\phi'(t)\), \(y(0)=\phi(0)\). The exact solution is \(y(t)=\phi(t)\) for every \(\lambda\). For an initial mismatch \(e_0\), the error evolves \(e(t)=e_0e^{\lambda t}\). Thus the observed slow solution and fast relaxation timescale can be varied independently. This makes the model an incisive verification case for a stiff integrator: changing \(\lambda\) leaves the exact waveform fixed while altering how errors created by discrete stages are damped.

A stability function \(R(h\lambda)\) describes propagation of errors for the homogeneous test \(y'=\lambda y\). A-stability constrains \(|R(z)|\le1\) over the left half-plane. L-stability additionally requires \(R(z)\to0\) as \(|z|\to\infty\) in the stable stiff limit. Neither definition alone describes how a method evaluates a time-dependent forcing at its internal stages. Stage order and stiff accuracy can matter when those stage errors are multiplied by large \(|\lambda|\). Observing bounded output on a homogeneous damping test does not establish a method's full formal order on a forced stiff problem.

Calculate the error recurrence of backward Euler and the trapezoidal rule

Write \(e_n=y_n-\phi_n,\ z=h\lambda\). Backward Euler evaluates the whole right side at \(t_{n+1}\). Collect terms after subtracting the exact target:

\[(1-z)e_{n+1}=e_n+\phi_n-\phi_{n+1}+h\phi'_{n+1}.\]

The forcing defect begins at \(h^2\phi''_{n+1}/2\). Its effect on the next error is divided by \(1-z\). The rapidly decaying homogeneous mode is also divided by that factor. This can make backward Euler track a particular smooth imposed target surprisingly well in the very stiff regime even though its classical nonstiff order is one. Its behaviour should be assessed in the desired observables and step–stiffness range, rather than ranked solely by the formal method label.

For the trapezoidal rule the corresponding equation is

\[(1-z/2)e_{n+1}=(1+z/2)e_n+\phi_n-\phi_{n+1}+\tfrac h2(\phi_n'+\phi_{n+1}').\]

The defect is the trapezoidal quadrature error in the derivative and begins at order \(h^3\). However, its homogeneous factor tends to \(-1\) when \(z\to-\infty\), so a pre-existing fast error can survive as a sign-alternating numerical component for many steps. With \(z=-100\) the factors are \(1/101=0.00990\) for backward Euler and \(-49/51=-0.96078\) for trapezoid. Both schemes are A-stable. Only the first immediately damps this unresolved mode. Event resets, inconsistent initial preparation or changing external loads can seed it.

A midpoint constraint can force endpoint drift

Implicit midpoint uses \(y_{n+1}-y_n=h[\lambda((y_n+y_{n+1})/2-\phi_m)+\phi'_m]\). For fixed \(h\) and large negative \(\lambda\), finite numerical states require the stage average to approach \(\phi_m\). Thus \(y_{n+1}\simeq2\phi_m-y_n\). The endpoints inferred from this interior constraint need not land on \(\phi_{n+1}\). Compare with trapezoid, which shares exactly the same scalar homogeneous stability function but evaluates two different times and states for nonlinear or time-dependent forcing.

With \(y_n=\phi_n\) and \(\phi(t)=t^2\), the limiting endpoint error is

\[2\phi(t_n+h/2)-\phi_n-\phi_{n+1}=-h^2/2.\]

The exact finite-\(z\) midpoint error starting from the target is \(zh^2/[4(1-z/2)]\). The trapezoidal derivative quadrature, in contrast, is exact for this quadratic target and keeps \(e_n=0\) at every step started exactly on it. Their common stability function did not predict their different forced response. In the midpoint example an alternating error can cancel at some output times, so inspecting only the final point at an even number of steps may obscure the largest per-step error. Compare all sampled times and separate propagated from newly injected defects.

Separate injected forcing error from a starting mismatch

This experiment uses the target t squared with step 0.1 s. It propagates the exact error recurrences of backward Euler, trapezoid and midpoint rather than estimating the difference by subtracting two large trajectories. The reference also includes the exponentially decaying initial mismatch. Move the stiffness control until a time step contains a hundred fast relaxation times. With a nonzero initial error, trapezoid and midpoint preserve a visible alternating component even though the exact mismatch essentially disappears after one step.

Set the initial mismatch to zero to isolate what an initially accurate trajectory gains from stage forcing. For this one quadratic target the trapezoidal derivative quadrature is exact and introduces no error, while midpoint still misses the endpoint because it constrains the value at the middle of the interval. Try a less stiff value and compare the peak and endpoint errors over all times. The demonstration is deliberately a manufactured exact target; substituting a cubic target would remove trapezoid’s special cancellation while keeping the same homogeneous stability function.

Tutorial · compare error mechanisms at fixed work

Compute a forced stiff step exactly

Set \(h=0.10,\lambda=-1000,\phi=t^2,\ y_0=0\). Compute errors after the first step for backward Euler, trapezoid and midpoint.

Work through the solution

Here \(z=-100\), \(\phi(h)=0.01\). Backward Euler defect is \(h^2\), giving \(e_1=0.01/101=9.90\times10^{-5}\). Trapezoid yields zero error on this target. Midpoint gives \(z h^2/[4(1-z/2)]=-0.004902\), so its predicted \(y_1\) is only 0.005098. The exact target is smooth in all three experiments; the fast stiffness multiplies the way its curvature is sampled within the step.

Check an unresolved initial-layer perturbation

Keep \(z=-100\) and introduce an initial error of 0.01. How large is the propagated part after two steps, excluding new forcing defects?

Work through the solution

The exact propagated part is \(0.01e^{-200}\), negligible here. Backward Euler gives

\[0.01/101^2=9.80\times10^{-7}.\]

Both trapezoid and implicit midpoint give \(0.01(49/51)^2=0.00923\) with restored positive sign after the second step. Negative sign at alternate steps can be invisible if the code saves every second state. Monitoring stage and consecutive-step output helps identify this persistence.

Design a refinement that distinguishes ordinary order from stiff forcing error

Why are a study at fixed \(\lambda\) with \(h\to0\) and one at fixed \(h\lambda=-100\) different experiments? What should be plotted?

Work through the solution

At fixed \(\lambda\), sufficiently small \(h\) eventually resolves the fast timescale and standard smooth-solution order can reappear. Holding \(h\lambda=-100\) changes the differential equation's error-relaxation scale at every refinement, deliberately keeping that fast mode unresolved while the exact target stays fixed. Plot maximum error over all physical sample times, the homogeneous amplification and representative stage defects for both sequences. Report the exact initial mismatch as well as \(\phi\). Otherwise a measured order can conflate target tracking, unremoved fast transients and cancellation at one favourable output time.
