Dynamics and numerical time integration · 1. Find a crossing between accepted time steps

An integrator can approximate a trajectory accurately yet report a poor switch time. Construct a continuous interpolant, relate a state error to an event-time error and inspect what happens when a trajectory merely touches a threshold.

An event is a root of a composite function

Let \(y'=f(t,y)\) and let a sensor or change of dynamics be described by \(g(t,y)=0\). Along an exact trajectory the scalar event function is \(h(t)=g(t,y(t))\). A simple event at \(t_*\) satisfies \(\dot h_*=g_t+g_yf\ne0\). If approximate trajectory or sensor evaluation introduces error \(\delta h\), linearisation around the crossing gives \(\delta t\simeq-\delta h/\dot h_*\). A small state error can therefore imply a much larger time error if the crossing is nearly tangential. Reporting an event timestamp to machine precision after bisecting a low-order interpolant is not equivalent to knowing the physical crossing time that accurately.

Consider a dimensionless unit-frequency oscillator with \(y=\cos t,\ y'=-\sin t\) during the first half cycle. Its first falling crossing of threshold \(a\in(0,1)\) occurs at \(t_*=\arccos a\), with slope \(-\sqrt{1-a^2}\). At \(a=0.5\), a positive vertical interpolation error \(10^{-3}\) can delay the crossing by about \(0.00115\) time units. At \(a=0.9999\) the same linear estimate is about 0.0707, comparable with or larger than the event time itself; the simple linear local approximation then also fails. At precisely \(a=1\) the surface is tangent at the maximum and sign-change tests can miss it altogether.

Construct a state and slope interpolant on one accepted interval

Suppose samples of state and derivative are available at \(t_n,t_{n+1}=t_n+h\). Write \(s=(t-t_n)/h\) and choose a cubic \(p(s)\) matching \(p(0)=y_n,p(1)=y_{n+1},p_s(0)=hf_n,p_s(1)=hf_{n+1}\). Solving these four linear conditions gives

\[p=(2s^3-3s^2+1)y_n+(s^3-2s^2+s)hf_n+(-2s^3+3s^2)y_{n+1}+(s^3-s^2)hf_{n+1}.\]

For an exact scalar trajectory sufficiently smooth in time, the Hermite error is \(y(t)-p(t)=y^{(4)}(\xi)(t-t_n)^2(t-t_{n+1})^2/4!\), bounded by \(h^4\max|y^{(4)}|/384\). Linear interpolation instead has error bounded by \(h^2\max|y''|/8\). Combine either bound with a known lower bound on \(|\dot h|\) in a bracket to bound event-time error. In an actual ODE solve, \(y_n,f_n\) are numerical, so interpolation and integrated state errors both contribute. A high-order interpolant cannot repair incorrect endpoint data or sample a discontinuity assumed smooth.

Bracket on the physical trajectory and restart after a transition

At each accepted interval compare event values at both ends. For a simple crossing of the requested direction, solve \(g(t,p(t))=0\) inside that interval using a bracketed root finder. The bracket controls the algebraic root-finding error; independent time-step refinement measures trajectory error. If the dynamics change at the event, truncate the old step there, apply any specified reset \(y^+=R(t_*,y^-)\) and restart the integrator under the new right side. Propagating the old smooth interpolant through a change of dynamics can corrupt both state and subsequent event ordering.

If a surface stays exactly active over a finite interval, repeated detection at the same time can stall the integration. A model of contact, hysteresis or directional rearming may be physically appropriate, depending on what the event means. Merely nudging the time forward by an arbitrary tolerance can skip a nearby second event. Similarly two opposite crossings within one large accepted interval may yield equal endpoint signs. A maximum step guided by known physical timescales, supplementary extrema detection or an analytically enclosed event function can be needed. Accuracy control on the smooth state alone does not guarantee detection of every specified surface crossing.

Compare interpolation errors independently of the propagation error

The calculator uses exact cosine endpoint samples and their derivatives, then locates the first falling crossing on a uniform time grid by linear or cubic Hermite interpolation. The curves show error against step spacing on a logarithmic vertical scale. Their irregular minima occur when a grid point happens to coincide with the event. Look for the error envelope over several spacings. At threshold 0.5 the exact result is 1.047198 s. Moving the threshold changes both the crossing slope and its position relative to the sampled grid.

Distinguish uncertainty in the threshold from time discretisation

Two clocks can agree on an event and still report a poorly determined arrival time if the physical threshold is uncertain. For a calibrated level \(a\) the falling crossing solves \(\cos t_*=a\), so implicit differentiation gives \(dt_*/da=-1/\sqrt{1-a^2}\). A level error of \(0.002\) near \(a=0.5\) produces a timing uncertainty of about \(0.00231\) s for the unit-frequency benchmark. At \(a=0.9\) the same device gives \(0.00459\) s. Close to the turning point the trace is almost horizontal. Reducing the root finder's tolerance cannot remove that physical loss in sensitivity.

If the numerical trajectory also has a known absolute state error bound \(\eta\), divide that bound by a lower bound on the crossing speed in the bracket. The result is a deterministic timing allowance. An independent random threshold error has a standard deviation and calls for a statistical interpretation. Adding those numbers in quadrature would need a probability model for both. Reporting the two separately preserves what the measurement and the solver actually established.

Dynamics and numerical time integration · 2. A delayed feedback model needs a history, not one starting value

A controller responds to the measured displacement one time delay ago. Construct its solution in successive intervals, track propagated derivative discontinuities and derive the point at which increasing delay can destabilise negative feedback.

Specify the additional data an ordinary initial value omits

For \(t\ge0\) consider \(y'(t)=-a y(t-\tau)\) with positive constant gain \(a\) and delay \(\tau>0\). The derivative at the start depends on \(y(-\tau)\); an initial value \(y(0)\) alone is insufficient. Supply a history \(y(t)=\phi(t)\) on \([-\tau,0]\), continuous at zero for a continuous state. Two preparations sharing \(y(0)\) but different earlier values can immediately develop different slopes. This is why treating a delay as a new algebraic coefficient in an ODE solver without a history buffer changes the physical problem.

Take constant history \(\phi=1\). For \(0\le t\le\tau\), the delayed input is one and \(y=1-at\). For \(\tau\le t\le2\tau\), the derivative is \(-a[1-a(t-\tau)]\); integrate from \(t=\tau\) to get \(y=1-at+a^2(t-\tau)^2/2\). On the next interval another integral gives \(y=1-at+a^2(t-\tau)^2/2-a^3(t-2\tau)^3/6\). Each new interval solves an ordinary forced equation with known earlier right-side data. This is the method of steps. A smooth-looking initial history can nevertheless mismatch derivatives: here the left derivative at zero is zero while the equation supplies \(-a\) on the right.

Trace how a step mismatch propagates through the delay

At \(t=\tau\) continuity of \(y\) at zero makes \(y'\) continuous, but \(y''(t)=-a y'(t-\tau)\) inherits the earlier jump in \(y'\). At \(2\tau\) the discontinuity appears in the third derivative. High-order adaptive ODE error formulas assume smooth derivatives within an interval. Marking known breakpoints and integrating separately across the delay intervals avoids applying a high-degree interpolant through an unacknowledged loss of smoothness. With several delays, sums of delay lengths can generate additional event locations and their bookkeeping matters. A discontinuity in the physical forcing can also generate new fronts of reduced regularity.

Within a method-of-steps solver, values \(y(t-\tau)\) usually lie between stored previous mesh points. Dense output supplies their interpolation. Its error feeds directly into the next interval's right side and can limit global accuracy even when the ODE time-step rule has higher formal order. Maintain enough history to cover the largest delay and requested outputs, and preserve the known one-sided behaviour at breakpoints. If a trial step is longer than the delay its stages may refer to the current unknown interval; an algorithm must either constrain that step or solve the resulting within-step interpolation problem consistently. A generic lookup of the last stored value instead creates an unintended zero-order-held feedback law.

Derive a delay-induced change of oscillatory stability

For a constant-coefficient retarded equation try \(y=e^{st}\). Substitution gives \(s+a e^{-s\tau}=0\). Unlike a polynomial characteristic equation, this transcendental equation generally has infinitely many roots. A pair crosses the imaginary axis when \(s=i\omega\), giving \(a\cos(\omega\tau)=0,\ \omega-a\sin(\omega\tau)=0\). For positive \(\omega\), the first crossing is at \(\omega=a,\ a\tau=\pi/2\). With negligible delay the model behaves like the stable ODE \(y'=-ay\). The crossing indicates that negative feedback acting on old information can become destabilising; stability of the zero-delay ODE is no guarantee for arbitrary delay.

To check crossing direction, differentiate \(s+a e^{-s\tau}=0\) at constant \(a\), obtaining \(ds/d\tau=-s^2/(1+\tau s)\) after using the characteristic equation. At \(s=ia\) its real part is \(a^2/[1+(a\tau)^2]>0\). Increasing delay moves this pair into the unstable half-plane. A numerically observed sign-changing transient alone does not prove instability, as stable delay systems can oscillate while decaying. Compare an envelope over successive periods with the characteristic roots and refine both time discretisation and history interpolation. An unstable numerical discretisation can otherwise imitate a feedback-induced growth.

Compare a stored history with the method-of-steps solution

The delay is the time unit and the history on the preceding interval is one. The calculator integrates an explicit Euler update with an integer number of steps per delay so it can read the delayed state exactly at a stored grid index. The comparison curve evaluates the exact piecewise polynomial. Its kth contribution starts after k minus one delay intervals and has coefficient given by the kth power of minus the dimensionless feedback divided by k factorial. This follows by repeatedly integrating the previous interval’s polynomial, so it is independent of the Euler recurrence.

At feedback one, inspect the exact values after one, two and three delays before refining the grid. Euler already integrates the constant derivative on the first interval exactly, while errors enter on later intervals. Change the feedback across π/2 and compare the oscillation envelope. Six displayed delays are useful for checking the calculation but can be too short to measure slow growth close to threshold. A stable-looking short record does not replace the characteristic-root analysis. Because the grids here align with the delay, this experiment isolates stepping error and does not test a general off-grid history interpolator.

Dynamics and numerical time integration · 3. Solving a two-point boundary problem without guessing the start slope

When endpoint conditions act on different sides of an interval, shooting can amplify a tiny initial error. Construct a cubic collocation system, test its continuous differential residual and quantify which boundary-value solution the residual actually certifies.

Sensitivity of shooting need not be sensitivity of the physical solution

Take \(y''=\kappa^2y\) on \(0\le x\le1\), with \(y(0)=1,\ y(1)=0\). The solution \(y=\sinh[\kappa(1-x)]/\sinh\kappa\) decays from the left. To shoot from the left, introduce unknown slope \(s\), yielding endpoint \(y(1)=\cosh\kappa+(s/\kappa)\sinh\kappa\). The correct slope is \(-\kappa\coth\kappa\) and its error is magnified by \(\sinh\kappa/\kappa\) at the far end. For \(\kappa=20\) this factor is about \(1.21\times10^7\). Even an initial slope accurate to \(10^{-10}\) can cause a far-end mismatch of order \(10^{-3}\). The mathematical boundary solution can remain well behaved while this particular representation suffers cancellation of large growing and decaying components.

A collocation boundary solver instead treats approximate states at many locations as simultaneous unknowns and enforces both boundary conditions directly. Write the equation as first-order system \(Y'=(v,\kappa^2 y)^T=F(x,Y)\), \(Y=(y,v)\). On each panel \([x_i,x_{i+1}]\) choose a cubic Hermite polynomial using unknown endpoint states and endpoint slopes fixed by \(F_i,F_{i+1}\). This already imposes the differential equation at both endpoints. Require the polynomial derivative to satisfy the ODE also at the midpoint. Matching yields two equations for each panel, with the boundary equations closing the overall algebraic system.

Derive a residual with only three right-side evaluations per panel

For panel width \(h\), Hermite interpolation at the midpoint gives \(Y_m=(Y_i+Y_{i+1})/2+h(F_i-F_{i+1})/8\). Its derivative there is \(p'_m=3(Y_{i+1}-Y_i)/(2h)-(F_i+F_{i+1})/4\). Equating this with \(F_m=F(x_i+h/2,Y_m)\) yields

\[Y_{i+1}-Y_i-\frac h6(F_i+4F_m+F_{i+1})=0 .\]

This is a three-point Lobatto collocation relation, resembling Simpson quadrature with a midpoint state determined by the same cubic. Neighbouring panels share endpoint states and slopes, constructing a continuously differentiable piecewise approximation for a smooth right side. The nonlinear algebraic Jacobian has local block structure apart from boundary conditions and a few global unknowns. Dense finite differencing of the entire state would discard that structure and can dominate a large adaptive solve.

As a simple independent test take the scalar ODE \(y'=\lambda y\). With \(z=h\lambda\), substitution of \(Y_m\) yields a one-panel transfer \(R(z)=(1+z/2+z^2/12)/(1-z/2+z^2/12)\), agreeing with \(e^z\) through \(z^4\). The symmetry \(R(-z)=1/R(z)\) checks the derivation. At large negative \(z\), \(R(z)\to1\), so the local solution formula is not strongly damping for unresolved stiff modes. Global enforcement of boundary conditions and adaptive spatial resolution remain important: a stable algebraic solve need not resolve a very thin physical layer.

Interpret a continuous residual through a stability estimate

A collocation residual vanishes at the points where it was imposed, so checking only those values tests the nonlinear algebraic solve, not the differential approximation between points. Evaluate the continuous defect \(d(x)=p'(x)-F(x,p(x))\) at independent points or use its suitably weighted panel norm. Refine where the residual is large, especially where the solution changes rapidly, and enforce the boundary conditions independently. A mesh with many uniform far-interior points can inefficiently resolve a solution having a layer of thickness \(1/\kappa\) near a single wall.

For a scalar linear elliptic boundary problem \(-y''+\kappa^2 y=f\) with zero endpoint error, an approximation with smooth enough derivatives has error governed by \(-e''+\kappa^2e=r\). If \(\|r\|_\infty\le\epsilon\), a maximum-principle argument gives \(\|e\|_\infty\le\epsilon/\kappa^2\) for \(\kappa>0\): at an interior positive error maximum, \(-e''\ge0\), and similarly for the negative minimum. This bound links a differential residual in the same formulation to solution error under the stated boundary conditions. A residual in a differently scaled first-order system requires its own inverse stability estimate. Near a nonunique boundary solution an apparently tiny defect can correspond to a much larger state error.

Reconstruct the entire cubic boundary solution

The calculator solves the same linear collocation equations as the written panel derivation. For this constant matrix, its growing and shrinking eigendirections decouple and one panel applies the stated rational transfer to each. The numerical field shown between nodes is the actual cubic Hermite interpolant through those collocation states and their slopes. The reference is the independently evaluated hyperbolic-sine boundary solution. Increase the layer parameter at fixed panel count first and look just inside the left boundary. Both curves hit the imposed endpoints even on a visibly inadequate mesh.

Then double the number of panels. The displayed maximum difference is measured on 241 independent evaluation locations and is a sampled diagnostic, not a guaranteed global norm. For large layer parameter the shooting endpoint amplification can be enormous even while the collocation solution becomes accurate upon mesh refinement. At only a few cells across the layer the cubic may bend below zero. A solution satisfying the nonlinear algebraic conditions of the chosen discretisation need not preserve positivity of the continuum field at every point.

Dynamics and numerical time integration · 4. Why stiff stability does not guarantee the expected order

A differential equation is constructed to have a smooth, known solution and a rapidly damped error mode. Use it to distinguish stability of the homogeneous equation, tracking of a moving target and the effect of off-grid stage forcing.

Choose an exact solution before increasing stiffness

Let \(\lambda<0\), let \(\phi(t)\) be a smooth specified function, and solve the Prothero–Robinson model \(y'=\lambda[y-\phi(t)]+\phi'(t)\), \(y(0)=\phi(0)\). The exact solution is \(y(t)=\phi(t)\) for every \(\lambda\). For an initial mismatch \(e_0\), the error evolves \(e(t)=e_0e^{\lambda t}\). Thus the observed slow solution and fast relaxation timescale can be varied independently. This makes the model an incisive verification case for a stiff integrator: changing \(\lambda\) leaves the exact waveform fixed while altering how errors created by discrete stages are damped.

A stability function \(R(h\lambda)\) describes propagation of errors for the homogeneous test \(y'=\lambda y\). A-stability constrains \(|R(z)|\le1\) over the left half-plane. L-stability additionally requires \(R(z)\to0\) as \(|z|\to\infty\) in the stable stiff limit. Neither definition alone describes how a method evaluates a time-dependent forcing at its internal stages. Stage order and stiff accuracy can matter when those stage errors are multiplied by large \(|\lambda|\). Observing bounded output on a homogeneous damping test does not establish a method's full formal order on a forced stiff problem.

Calculate the error recurrence of backward Euler and the trapezoidal rule

Write \(e_n=y_n-\phi_n,\ z=h\lambda\). Backward Euler evaluates the whole right side at \(t_{n+1}\). Collect terms after subtracting the exact target:

\[(1-z)e_{n+1}=e_n+\phi_n-\phi_{n+1}+h\phi'_{n+1}.\]

The forcing defect begins at \(h^2\phi''_{n+1}/2\). Its effect on the next error is divided by \(1-z\). The rapidly decaying homogeneous mode is also divided by that factor. This can make backward Euler track a particular smooth imposed target surprisingly well in the very stiff regime even though its classical nonstiff order is one. Its behaviour should be assessed in the desired observables and step–stiffness range, rather than ranked solely by the formal method label.

For the trapezoidal rule the corresponding equation is

\[(1-z/2)e_{n+1}=(1+z/2)e_n+\phi_n-\phi_{n+1}+\tfrac h2(\phi_n'+\phi_{n+1}').\]

The defect is the trapezoidal quadrature error in the derivative and begins at order \(h^3\). However, its homogeneous factor tends to \(-1\) when \(z\to-\infty\), so a pre-existing fast error can survive as a sign-alternating numerical component for many steps. With \(z=-100\) the factors are \(1/101=0.00990\) for backward Euler and \(-49/51=-0.96078\) for trapezoid. Both schemes are A-stable. Only the first immediately damps this unresolved mode. Event resets, inconsistent initial preparation or changing external loads can seed it.

A midpoint constraint can force endpoint drift

Implicit midpoint uses \(y_{n+1}-y_n=h[\lambda((y_n+y_{n+1})/2-\phi_m)+\phi'_m]\). For fixed \(h\) and large negative \(\lambda\), finite numerical states require the stage average to approach \(\phi_m\). Thus \(y_{n+1}\simeq2\phi_m-y_n\). The endpoints inferred from this interior constraint need not land on \(\phi_{n+1}\). Compare with trapezoid, which shares exactly the same scalar homogeneous stability function but evaluates two different times and states for nonlinear or time-dependent forcing.

With \(y_n=\phi_n\) and \(\phi(t)=t^2\), the limiting endpoint error is \(2\phi(t_n+h/2)-\phi_n-\phi_{n+1}=-h^2/2\). The exact finite-\(z\) midpoint error starting from the target is \(zh^2/[4(1-z/2)]\). The trapezoidal derivative quadrature, in contrast, is exact for this quadratic target and keeps \(e_n=0\) at every step started exactly on it. Their common stability function did not predict their different forced response. In the midpoint example an alternating error can cancel at some output times, so inspecting only the final point at an even number of steps may obscure the largest per-step error. Compare all sampled times and separate propagated from newly injected defects.

Separate injected forcing error from a starting mismatch

This experiment uses the target t squared with step 0.1 s. It propagates the exact error recurrences of backward Euler, trapezoid and midpoint rather than estimating the difference by subtracting two large trajectories. The reference also includes the exponentially decaying initial mismatch. Move the stiffness control until a time step contains a hundred fast relaxation times. With a nonzero initial error, trapezoid and midpoint preserve a visible alternating component even though the exact mismatch essentially disappears after one step.

Set the initial mismatch to zero to isolate what an initially accurate trajectory gains from stage forcing. For this one quadratic target the trapezoidal derivative quadrature is exact and introduces no error, while midpoint still misses the endpoint because it constrains the value at the middle of the interval. Try a less stiff value and compare the peak and endpoint errors over all times. The demonstration is deliberately a manufactured exact target; substituting a cubic target would remove trapezoid’s special cancellation while keeping the same homogeneous stability function.

Dynamics and numerical time integration · 5. How far does an energy-preserving rotation lag behind?

Integrate uniform circular motion by the implicit midpoint rule. The points stay exactly on the energy circle in exact arithmetic. Derive the angle advanced at each step and measure the accumulated timing error.

From a midpoint balance to a rotation matrix

Write a two-component oscillator as \(v'=\omega Jv\), where \(J\) sends \((x,y)\) to \((-y,x)\). Choosing scaled coordinates makes both components comparable; for a mechanical oscillator one may take displacement and a suitably scaled speed. The generator is skew-symmetric, so \(d(v^Tv)/dt=\omega v^T(J^T+J)v=0\). The exact motion has angular advance \(h\omega\) over an interval \(h\).

Midpoint approximates the velocity at the midpoint of the segment joining the old and new states. This yields \((I-aJ)v_{n+1}=(I+aJ)v_n\) with \(a=h\omega/2\). Since \(J^2=-I\), multiplication by \(I+aJ\) eliminates the inverse and gives

\[R_h=(I-aJ)^{-1}(I+aJ)=\frac{(1-a^2)I+2aJ}{1+a^2}.\]

The coefficients of \(I\) and \(J\) have squares summing to one. They are the cosine and sine of the angle \(\theta_h=2\arctan a\). Thus every update preserves the squared radius. The angle differs from the exact \(h\omega\). Expanding the arctangent gives \(\theta_h=h\omega-(h\omega)^3/12+O((h\omega)^5)\). Over a fixed duration \(T=nh\), the leading accumulated angle error is \(-T\omega^3h^2/12\). This phase lag is especially visible after many periods even when a graph of energy looks perfect.

For comparison, explicit Euler takes the tangent line to its end point. Its amplification is \(\sqrt{1+(h\omega)^2}\). Studying both amplitude and timing identifies different defects of the two updates.

Put a clock on the unit circle

Let \(\omega=2\) rad/s, \(h=0.5\) s and \(v_0=(1,0)\). The half-step parameter is 0.5. The rotation sends the first point to \((0.6,0.8)\) instead of the exact \((\cos1,\sin1)\approx(0.5403,0.8415)\). Both radii are one. The angular deficit is \(1-2\arctan(0.5)\approx0.072705\) rad per update. After ten updates, the numerical clock is behind by 0.72705 rad or about 0.364 s of physical time.

With \(h=0.25\) s over the same five seconds the deficit is \(10-40\arctan(0.25)\approx0.20085\) rad. Its ratio to the coarse-step error is close to one quarter. In the notebook the rotation is built by a linear solve and its angle is measured from a dot product and an oriented area. These are independent ways to check the hand formula. Counting full turns requires retaining unwrapped increments; a final point alone cannot identify the number of completed turns.

Exercise · reverse the sign of time

Show why a forward midpoint update followed by one equal backward update returns to the starting state. Does forward Euler have the same property here?

Work through the solution

Replacing \(a\) by \(-a\) interchanges the two factors in the Cayley formula, so \(R_{-h}=R_h^{-1}\). Their product is the identity. For explicit Euler the product is \((I-h\omega J)(I+h\omega J)=(1+(h\omega)^2)I\). The reversal increases every radius when the angular speed and the step are nonzero.

Exercise · set a phase budget

For \(\omega=2\) rad/s, approximate a time step that keeps the accumulated phase lag below 0.02 rad over six seconds. Give an exact way to check the choice.

The leading magnitude is \(T\omega^3h^2/12=4h^2\). Equating this to 0.02 gives about 0.0707 s. Choosing 90 equal steps over six seconds gives \(h=1/15\) s.
