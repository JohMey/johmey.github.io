Operator splitting and noncommuting flows

Determine how the order of two exact substeps affects a coupled evolution and recover second order by a symmetric composition.

Two solvable subproblems

Suppose an evolution has the form \(u'=(A+B)u\), where the subproblems involving just \(A\) or just \(B\) have inexpensive exact flows. First applying \(A\) for one nominal step and then \(B\) gives \(e^{hB}e^{hA}u_n\). Each operator is advanced over the same physical interval. When \(A\) and \(B\) commute, their exponential product equals the exponential of the sum. For general matrices the missing interaction between simultaneous changes controls the error.

Expand the factors through \(h^2\). The mixed term of their product is \(h^2BA\); that of the exact exponential is \(h^2(AB+BA)/2\). The difference is \(h^2(BA-AB)/2\), the signed commutator term. Reversing the order reverses its sign. Composing a half step of \(A\), a full step of \(B\) and another half step of \(A\) instead balances both mixed terms. The local discrepancy is then order \(h^3\) for fixed bounded matrices, giving global second order over a fixed interval under stable propagation.

Work out two shears

Let \(u=(q,p)^T\), \(A(q,p)=(p,0)\) and \(B(q,p)=(0,-q)\), so the exact motion is a unit-frequency oscillator. Each separate flow is a shear. From \((1,0)\), a drift of length 0.2 followed by a kick produces \((1,-0.2)\). A kick followed by a drift produces \((0.96,-0.2)\). The exact result is \((\cos0.2,-\sin0.2)\approx(0.980067,-0.198669)\). The positions from the two first-order compositions bracket the exact one in this example. Their leading positional deviations are almost equal and opposite.

The symmetric drift–kick–drift composition first leaves the position at 1, changes the momentum to \(-0.2\), then drifts by \(-0.02\). Its final state is \((0.98,-0.2)\). The position error is about \(6.66\times10^{-5}\) while the momentum error is about \(1.33\times10^{-3}\). Halving the step in a one-step test scales the leading momentum discrepancy roughly by eight. When measuring at a fixed final time instead, the number of steps doubles and the expected asymptotic error reduction is approximately four.

What the substeps must share

Both subflows need compatible state variables and boundary data. If each independently applies an external source intended to appear once in \(A+B\), the combined model advances a different equation and cannot converge to the desired solution even with arbitrarily small steps. Conversely a source assigned to neither flow disappears. Check the combined first derivative of the composition at \(h=0\); it must reproduce the complete original right-hand side. This low-cost test catches modelling errors before comparing convergence slopes.

An exact limiting case for a code

Take two diagonal decay matrices with first-coordinate rates −1 and −3 and second-coordinate rates −2 and zero. Each separate flow rescales the coordinates. Their products commute, so starting from \((1,1)\) and integrating to \(t=0.5\) must give \((e^{-2},e^{-1})\) in either order. The equality holds for a single full step as well as any subdivision. This commuting case tests time scaling and state transfer without any expected splitting error. The shear example then tests the distinct question of whether the implementation achieves its intended order when a commutator is present.

Resolve the direction of a nonlinear splitting error

Consider logistic growth \(u'=au-bu^2\) with \(a,b>0\). The separate linear growth flow is \(G_h(u)=e^{ah}u\). The saturation flow solves \(u'=-bu^2\) and is \(D_h(u)=u/(1+bhu)\) for positive initial states. Each flow is exact, positivity preserving and inexpensive. But the saturation strength depends on the current population, which growth changes during a step. Therefore applying growth before saturation generally produces a different result from applying saturation before growth.

Write the two full-step maps explicitly. Growth then saturation yields \(e^{ah}u/[1+bh e^{ah}u]\); saturation then growth gives \(e^{ah}u/(1+bhu)\). For positive \(u,h\) and \(a\) the first denominator is larger and the final value smaller. The exact nonlinear flow has denominator \(1+(b/a)(e^{ah}-1)u\), obtained by solving the linear equation for \(1/u\). Since \(h<(e^{ah}-1)/a<h e^{ah}\), the two orderings bound the exact positive answer on opposite sides. This ordering result uses the particular signs and monotonicity of logistic growth; it is not a generic enclosure theorem for split systems.

Test a physical equilibrium that neither subflow preserves

Set \(a=b=1,\ u_0=1\). The complete model is exactly stationary because growth and saturation cancel. Choose \(h=\log2\), so an isolated growth step doubles the state. Growth followed by saturation gives \(2/(1+2\log2)\approx0.8381\). Saturation followed by growth gives \(2/(1+\log2)\approx1.1812\). Neither result is the stationary true value. Powering either map through many long intervals would move the solution toward a numerical equilibrium different from the physical one, although each individual substep is solved without approximation.

A symmetric growth–saturation–growth step gives \(u_1=2/(1+\sqrt2\log2)\approx1.0100\). Symmetry has cancelled the leading ordering discrepancy without enforcing exact preservation of the steady state. To predict the long-run drift of the first ordering, solve its nonzero fixed-point equation \(u=2u/(1+2hu)\), giving \(u=1/(2h)\approx0.7213\). For the opposite ordering the numerical equilibrium is \(1/h\approx1.4427\). These values expose a persistent bias that a one-step comparison can understate.

A steady physical reference is particularly informative when a splitting method is used to integrate dynamics near a balance of large opposing processes. Compare the numerical equilibrium and the relaxation rate toward it. An implementation can improve its order by a symmetric composition while still requiring much finer steps, or a different assignment of source terms, to maintain the equilibrium accuracy a physical application demands.

A refinement study can use the exact nonzero fixed point formulas to separate local error from accumulated drift. For arbitrary positive \(h\), the growth-first numerical fixed point is \((1-e^{-h})/h=1-h/2+O(h^2)\), while the saturation-first fixed point is \((e^h-1)/h=1+h/2+O(h^2)\). Thus their steady-state biases have opposite signs and are first order even though the individual one-step defects from the physical equilibrium start at second order. An empirical comparison based on the first step and another measured after equilibration should not be expected to return the same power of the step size.

The two steady-state formulas also converge toward the same physical value as the step tends to zero.
