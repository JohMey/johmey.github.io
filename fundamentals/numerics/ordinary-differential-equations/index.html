How the course fits together

  1. Chapters 1–4

    Integrate a rate over one step. Local defects accumulate into global error, additional Runge–Kutta stages cancel Taylor terms, and stiffness can impose a stability limit even on a slowly varying solution. Multistep formulas reuse earlier information.

  2. Chapters 5–8

    Estimate error to select the step size, then ask what structure a time update preserves. Exponential integration evolves linear modes exactly and splitting separates processes that can each be evolved simply.

Further chapters and interactive tutorials

  1. Implicit midpoint, reversibility and the phase of an oscillator

    Imagine integrating a mechanical trajectory forward, saving only its end state and then running the same method backward. Recovering the start is a stronger demand than obtaining a plausibly small one-step error. Even exact recovery does not guarantee that an oscillation peaked at the correct time.

  2. Splitting a stiff relaxation from a slowly varying drive

    A sensor tries to follow a changing reference while a fast relaxation damps departures. An explicit method may oscillate wildly even though the underlying transient decays monotonically. Treating the restoring term implicitly can resolve the slow motion using steps longer than the transient time. Whether those steps measure the transient accurately is a different question.

  3. Why a boundary value problem can be easier than its shooting equation

    A concentration decays across a slab, with concentrations known at both faces. Computing a solution from the first face alone can bring in an exponentially growing mode. Enforcing both faces together gives a different numerical problem with the same differential equation.

  4. How two decaying modes produce a growing signal

    Solve a triangular system exactly, derive the energy growth criterion and separate a real transient from numerical instability.

  5. Locating a grazing event between time steps

    Derive a sensitivity formula for event time, then measure how a harmless error in a trajectory can create or remove two threshold crossings.

Applied study

Distinguish stability, positivity and accuracy when stepping a model forward in time.

How does a short step change a long trajectory?

A well-mixed reactant A converts to B at rate \(kA\). In a closed vessel \(\dot A=-kA\) and \(\dot B=kA\). For \(A(0)=1\), the exact concentration of A is \(e^{-kt}\) in the chosen units, while \(A+B\) remains constant. A numerical scheme can preserve the total exactly and still put the wrong amount in each species.

Integrating the differential equation over one time interval gives \(A_{n+1}=A_n-k\int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}}A(t)\,dt\). Replacing the integrand by its left endpoint yields forward Euler, \(A_{n+1}=(1-z)A_n\) with \(z=kh\). Using the unknown endpoint instead gives backward Euler, \(A_{n+1}=A_n/(1+z)\). Each method replaces the exact amplification factor \(e^{-z}\) by a simpler function \(R(z)\).

Distinguish stability, positivity and accuracy

For the decay mode, forward Euler damps successive values when \(|1-z|<1\), or \(0<z<2\). Keeping an initially positive concentration nonnegative requires the stronger condition \(z\leq1\). At \(z=1.5\) the forward Euler values alternate in sign as their magnitudes shrink. Backward Euler has \(R(z)=1/(1+z)>0\) for every positive step, although a large step can still give a poor quantity of reactant.

Taylor expansion gives \(e^{-z}=1-z+z^2/2+\cdots\). Euler misses a term of order \(h^2\) per step. Over a fixed time interval the number of steps is proportional to \(1/h\), producing first-order global error under stable propagation. Classical four-stage Runge–Kutta (RK4) evaluates intermediate slopes. On this linear problem its factor is \(R_4=1-z+z^2/2-z^3/6+z^4/24\). It first differs from the exponential at order \(h^5\), giving fourth-order global convergence.

Test a hand calculation against the exact decay

Take \(k=0.8\) s⁻¹ and \(h=0.625\) s, giving \(z=0.5\). After two steps, the exact fraction remaining is \(e^{-1}=0.36788\). Forward Euler gives \(0.5^2=0.25\) and backward Euler gives \((1/1.5)^2=0.44444\). Both give positive, decaying sequences, but one consumes too much A and the other too little. The signs follow from \(1-z<e^{-z}<1/(1+z)\) for \(z>0\).

RK4 gives \(R_4(0.5)=0.6067708\), hence \(A_2\simeq0.36817\). Comparing all methods at the same physical time separates time error from a simple difference in how long the reaction has proceeded.

Problem class

Keep the chemical interpretation

With \(k=0.8\) s⁻¹, find the forward Euler upper step for nonnegative concentrations and the upper stability boundary. What happens exactly at the first bound?

Show the calculation

Nonnegativity gives \(h\leq1/k=1.25\) s. The decaying stability interval has \(h<2/k=2.50\) s. At \(h=1.25\) s the method sends all remaining A to B in one step. The exact solution still contains \(e^{-1}\) of the starting A. Thus respecting a nonnegativity bound alone permits considerable error.

Measure an order from two runs

Suppose a step halving reduces the largest RK4 error over the same time interval from \(2.00\times10^{-8}\) to \(1.22\times10^{-9}\) mol/m³. Estimate the observed order. Explain what a mass-balance test would miss.

Show the calculation

Assuming \(E_h\approx C h^p\), take \(p=\log_2(E_h/E_{h/2})\approx4.03\). For these equations the stage increments of A and B are equal and opposite. Their sum can remain at the initial total throughout either run even when both concentrations have sizable individual errors.
