Hamiltonian motion in phase space

Classical mechanics

Construct a Hamiltonian, read a phase portrait and distinguish bounded motion from rotation at a separatrix.

From first principles

Construct Hamilton’s equations by exchanging velocity for momentum

Begin with a smooth Lagrangian \(L(q,\dot q,t)\) for a conservative mechanical system. Assume the momentum definition \(p=\partial_{\dot q}L\) can locally be inverted to give \(\dot q\) as a function of \((q,p,t)\). This regularity is what lets position and momentum serve as independent state coordinates. Define the Hamiltonian and differentiate the product before substituting any equations of motion:

\[H=p\dot q-L,\]
\[dH=\dot q\,dp+p\,d\dot q-L_q\,dq-L_{\dot q}\,d\dot q-L_t\,dt .\]

The two \(d\dot q\) terms cancel by the momentum definition. Comparing the coefficients of the independent differentials gives \(H_p=\dot q\) and \(H_q=-L_q\). Hamilton's principle previously gave the Lagrange equation \(\dot p=L_q\), so

\[\dot q=\partial_pH,\]
\[\dot p=-\partial_qH,\]
\[\partial_tH=-\partial_tL .\]

Along a solution the \(q\) and \(p\) contributions to \(dH/dt\) cancel. For a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H\) is constant, so planar trajectories lie on its level sets. In the elementary case \(L=m\dot q^2/2-U(q)\), the transformation yields \(H=p^2/(2m)+U(q)\).

Derivation and applications

How Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space area

An energy contour shows where a single trajectory may lie. To learn what the dynamics does to nearby initial conditions, follow a small patch of the canonical \((q,p)\) plane. Consider one degree of freedom and a twice continuously differentiable Hamiltonian. Let the system advance by a short time \(\delta t\). To first order the coordinate map is a displacement along the Hamiltonian velocity field:

\[q'=q+H_p\,\delta t,\]
\[p'=p-H_q\,\delta t .\]

The area of a sufficiently small parallelogram changes by the determinant of this map's derivative. Differentiate with respect to both initial coordinates. The product of off-diagonal elements enters only at second order:

\[\det\frac{\partial(q',p')}{\partial(q,p)}=(1+H_{pq}\delta t)(1-H_{qp}\delta t)+H_{pp}H_{qq}\delta t^2=1+O(\delta t^2).\]

For the exact continuous map its area rate is the divergence of the phase velocity. Equality of the mixed second derivatives gives

\[\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=\partial_q\dot q+\partial_p\dot p=H_{pq}-H_{qp}=0\]

for an infinitesimal material area \(A\). Integrating adjacent patches gives the same conclusion for a finite region. The outline can stretch and rotate. Even explicit time dependence of \(H\) does not change this volume result. A friction force imposed independently of the Hamiltonian can change it. The second-order remainder above belongs to a short expansion; repeatedly taking finite steps with an approximate rule can accumulate a spurious change.

Worked example

Track a cloud in a harmonic trap

Prepare a small rectangular set of initial states, \(2.00\) mm wide in position and \(0.0100\) kg m/s wide in momentum. Its area is \(2.00\times10^{-5}\) J s. In a trap of mass \(0.500\) kg and frequency \(\omega=4.00\) rad/s, the exact quarter-period map is \(q'=p/(m\omega)\), \(p'=-m\omega q\). The new widths are \(5.00\) mm and \(0.00400\) kg m/s. Their product retains the original area even though neither width is conserved.

Second application

Diagnose a numerical phase portrait

An explicit Euler step in the same trap uses \(q'=q+p\delta t/m\) and \(p'=p-m\omega^2q\delta t\). Its determinant is exactly \(1+\omega^2\delta t^2\). At \(\delta t=0.0500\) s it expands area by \(1.04\) per step, giving \(1.04^{50}=7.11\) times the initial area after 50 steps. A graph of the growing cloud could look like physical heating. The determinant exposes a discretization error without requiring an exact trajectory for each point.

Position needs a conjugate momentum

The position of a pendulum at one instant does not say which way it will swing. A state needs both a configuration coordinate and a momentum. In Lagrangian mechanics define the canonical momentum by \(p=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q\). It can have different physical dimensions depending on the coordinate. For a particle on a line it has units of linear momentum. For a pendulum angle it has units of angular momentum. The pair \((q,p)\) forms a two-dimensional phase space for one degree of freedom.

Provided the relation between \(p\) and \(\dot q\) can be inverted, define \(H(q,p,t)=p\dot q-\mathcal L(q,\dot q,t)\) with the velocity expressed in terms of \(q,p\). Take a differential. The terms proportional to \(d\dot q\) cancel by the definition of \(p\). Using Lagrange’s equation \(\partial_q\mathcal L=\dot p\) gives \(dH=\dot q\,dp-\dot p\,dq-\partial_t\mathcal L\,dt\). Matching the coefficients produces Hamilton’s equations

\[\dot q=\partial_p H,\qquad \dot p=-\partial_q H.\]

For a system with ordinary quadratic kinetic energy, a velocity-independent potential and fixed constraints, \(H=K+U\). Its change along a solution is \(dH/dt=\partial_tH\) because the two terms containing the derivatives with respect to \(q,p\) cancel. A time-independent Hamiltonian confines the trajectory to a level set.

Use Poisson brackets to test conservation

Hamilton's two equations specify how \(q_i\) and \(p_i\) change. To follow an observable \(A(q,p,t)\), apply the chain rule and substitute \(\dot q_i=\partial H/\partial p_i\) and \(\dot p_i=-\partial H/\partial q_i\). This defines the Poisson bracket,

\[\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+\{A,H\},\qquad \{A,B\}=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_i}\right).\]

Since \(\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}\), a candidate change of phase-space coordinates must preserve these brackets to be canonical. A vanishing bracket with \(H\) certifies conservation for an observable having no explicit time dependence. It is a local calculation and does not require solving an orbit.

For a particle in a two-dimensional trap set \(H=(p_x^2+p_y^2)/(2m)+(k_xx^2+k_yy^2)/2\) and \(L_z=xp_y-yp_x\). The momentum derivatives in the bracket cancel the two kinetic contributions. The remaining coordinate terms give \(\dot L_z=(k_x-k_y)xy\), exactly the torque \(xF_y-yF_x\). With \(k_x=4.00\) N/m, \(k_y=6.00\) N/m and instantaneous position \((0.030,0.040)\) m, angular momentum decreases at \(2.40\times10^{-3}\) N m. At a point on either axis the instantaneous derivative vanishes even for the anisotropic trap. For a conservation law holding on every trajectory the expression must vanish throughout phase space, as it does when \(k_x=k_y\).

Draw energy contours before integrating

A rigid pendulum of mass \(m\) and length \(\ell\) has \(p=m\ell^2\dot q\) and \(H=p^2/(2m\ell^2)+mg\ell(1-\cos q)\). At the bottom \(q=0,p=0\) is a stable equilibrium. Low-energy contours are ellipses to leading order because the cosine potential is quadratic there. Each closed contour returns to its original phase point after one full oscillation. Points of greatest \(|p|\) are fastest and occur at the bottom.

The top \(q=\pi,p=0\) has energy \(2mg\ell\). Its contour separates swinging trajectories from trajectories that keep rotating in one direction. This separating curve is the separatrix. Since angles differing by \(2\pi\) label the same physical position, the full portrait lives on a cylinder. A rotation winds around that cylinder. As a trajectory approaches the separatrix, it spends a long time near the unstable top, so the small-angle period no longer estimates its return time accurately.

Compare thresholds and phase velocities

For \(m=0.50\) kg, \(\ell=0.80\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s², the potential barrier is 7.85 J. A start from the bottom at 5.0 rad/s supplies kinetic energy \(0.5(0.50)(0.80)^2(5.0)^2=4.00\) J. At the turning point \(\cos q=1-4.00/(0.50\times9.81\times0.80)=-0.0194\). The maximum angle is about 91.1°. Energy classifies this as a swing even though a small-angle expansion would be inaccurate at the turning point.

Continuous rotation requires bottom speed above \(\sqrt{4g/\ell}=7.00\) rad/s for this ideal pendulum. At precisely that value the system approaches the upper equilibrium asymptotically. This statement assumes a rigid link that can sustain compression. A pendulum made from a flexible string may lose tension before reaching the top, in which case the assumed circular constraint fails.

Check a numerical phase portrait

For any smooth one-degree Hamiltonian the divergence of the phase velocity is \(\partial_q\dot q+\partial_p\dot p=H_{pq}-H_{qp}=0\). A small patch of initial states can shear and stretch while preserving its phase-space area. Viscous damping would instead contract the patch. Numerical time integration can also produce apparent contraction or growth, so refining the step is essential before interpreting a spiral as evidence of friction.

For the harmonic oscillator, ordinary forward Euler gives \(q'=q+\Delta t\,p/m\) and \(p'=p-\Delta t\,kq\). The determinant of this linear update is \(1+\Delta t^2 k/m\), larger than one at every step. The spurious outward spiral follows from the numerical map alone. Updating momentum first and then using the new momentum for the position gives determinant one. That area property preserves the geometric structure, though it does not by itself guarantee accurate phase for a coarse step. Keep both geometry and timing in view when judging numerical trajectories.

The area inside a closed harmonic-oscillator contour provides another numerical test. Its semiaxes are \(\sqrt{2E/k}\) in position and \(\sqrt{2mE}\) in momentum. Their product times \(\pi\) gives \(2\pi E/\omega\). Increasing oscillator energy therefore expands the area in direct proportion. A phase portrait plotted using velocity replaces every momentum value by its value divided by mass, changing numerical areas and their units. Specify the vertical coordinate before comparing this diagnostic between models with different masses.

A slowly changing spring transports a phase-space area

Suppose an oscillator's spring stiffness can be varied externally during the motion. Its Hamiltonian \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega(t)^2q^2/2\) now depends explicitly on time, so ordinary energy conservation does not apply. At an instantaneous point on a trajectory Hamilton's equations make the terms involving \(\dot p\) and \(\dot q\) cancel in \(dH/dt\). The remaining change is \(dE/dt=\partial H/\partial t=m\omega\dot\omega q^2\). Energy is supplied when the imposed parameter raises the potential at the current displacement.

Take \(|\dot\omega|\ll\omega^2\) so that many cycles occur while the frequency changes by an appreciable fraction. Over one cycle \(\omega\) and \(E\) may then be treated as nearly fixed. Because average potential and kinetic energies of a harmonic oscillator are equal, \(\langle q^2\rangle=E/(m\omega^2)\). Inserting this mean into the work rate gives the averaged relation

\[\frac{dE}{dt}\simeq\frac{E}{\omega}\dot\omega,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac E\omega\right)\simeq0 .\]

The quantity \(J=E/\omega\) is the area enclosed by the instantaneous constant-energy ellipse divided by \(2\pi\). Calling it an adiabatic invariant states both the approximation and the observable. A slowly changing system migrates between contours of different energies in a way that preserves this action to leading order. This mechanical meaning of adiabatic concerns a timescale ratio. The derivation does not assume heat exchange, dissipation or a thermodynamic temperature.

Compare a slow compression with a sudden change

A \(0.500\) kg oscillator has initial angular frequency \(20.0\) rad/s and amplitude \(0.0400\) m. Its energy is \(m\omega_i^2 A_i^2/2=0.160\) J and initial action is \(0.00800\) J s. Raise the frequency smoothly to \(40.0\) rad/s over many periods. Action conservation predicts \(E_f=0.320\) J and final amplitude \(\sqrt{2E_f/(m\omega_f^2)}=0.0283\) m. The external control supplies work even as the maximum displacement decreases.

For comparison, change the stiffness instantaneously at a turning point, retaining the same initial and final frequencies. Position and momentum are continuous, but the potential energy at that position quadruples immediately. The new energy is \(0.640\) J and the amplitude remains \(0.0400\) m. Make the same switch at the zero crossing instead and the energy initially remains \(0.160\) J because the potential vanishes there. These two outcomes distinguish a rapid change tied to an oscillator phase from the nearly phase-independent slow ramp. Recording both position and velocity after the ramp allows energy, action and phase to be assessed separately.

A time-dependent Hamiltonian still carries each small phase-space area element into one of equal area, even during a sudden change of curvature. This exact conservation for an ensemble is distinct from slow conservation of the contour area assigned to a single orbit. A group of uniformly distributed initial phases illustrates the difference: immediately after a sudden spring switch all points occupy the same locations and enclose their original region, but their new energies depend on phase. The switch maps the original single-energy contour onto points that subsequently occupy many different energy contours of the new Hamiltonian.

Nonlinear stiffness and amplitude-dependent period

Classical mechanics

Derive the leading frequency shift of a symmetric nonlinear oscillator and test whether a measured amplitude shift is consistent with a cubic spring.

From first principles

Derive the first amplitude correction to a spring’s frequency

A symmetric elastic restoring force has no quadratic displacement term; the first correction to a linear spring can therefore be cubic. Suppose a conservative oscillator satisfies \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\) with \(k>0\) and \(|\alpha A^2/k|\ll1\) at measured peak amplitude \(A\). Leading motion is approximately \(A\cos\Omega t\). Cube this motion to find which frequencies enter the perturbing force:

\[x^3\simeq A^3[\tfrac34\cos\Omega t+\tfrac14\cos3\Omega t] .\]

To maintain a bounded periodic solution, its fundamental component must balance at first order. Otherwise the remaining fundamental force would resonantly grow the correction. Combine the inertia and restoring-force coefficients at \(\cos\Omega t\):

\[-m\Omega^2A+kA+\tfrac34\alpha A^3\simeq0,\]
\[\Omega\simeq\sqrt{k/m}\left(1+\frac{3\alpha A^2}{8k}\right).\]

The third-harmonic component also distorts the waveform, but reinserting that order-\(\alpha\) distortion into the cubic force affects the fundamental only at the next perturbative order. Positive \(\alpha\) raises frequency with amplitude; negative \(\alpha\) lowers it within the local range where the stable weakly nonlinear model applies.

Derivation and applications

Find the third harmonic generated by a weak cubic spring

A change in oscillation period is one signature of a nonlinear restoring force. The same nonlinearity also changes the shape of the recorded motion. Let an undamped mass obey \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\), with \(k>0\). Release it from rest at the measured peak displacement \(A\). Define \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\), the small signed parameter \(\epsilon=\alpha A^2/k\), and a stretched time \(\tau=\Omega t\). Write \(x/A=\cos\tau+\epsilon u(\tau)\) with \(u(0)=u'(0)=0\) so that \(A\) remains the physical turning point.

Allow the frequency to shift as \(\Omega=\omega_0(1+\epsilon\nu)\). Substitute the expansion into the equation and retain terms only to first order in \(\epsilon\). Primes below denote differentiation with respect to \(\tau\):

\[u''+u=2\nu\cos\tau-\cos^3\tau=(2\nu-3/4)\cos\tau-\tfrac14\cos3\tau .\]

A term driven at the unit frequency would grow proportionally to \(\tau\sin\tau\), contradicting a bounded, periodic correction. Choose \(\nu=3/8\) to remove it. The remaining forcing is at three times the fundamental. A trial \(C\cos3\tau\) has \(u''+u=-8C\cos3\tau\). Add the required homogeneous term to satisfy the turning-point conditions:

\[u(\tau)=\frac{\cos3\tau-\cos\tau}{32}.\]

The predicted displacement therefore contains two lines with linked amplitudes and a shifted fundamental:

\[x(t)\simeq A\!\left[(1-\epsilon/32)\cos\Omega t+(\epsilon/32)\cos3\Omega t\right],\]
\[\Omega/\omega_0\simeq1+3\epsilon/8 .\]

The coefficient sign gives the third harmonic relative to the phase of a release at positive displacement. Squaring an amplitude to obtain a power spectrum erases that sign. The approximation assumes \(|\epsilon|\ll1\) and excludes appreciable dissipation during the observation.

Worked example

Estimate a micrometre-scale distortion

Take \(A=12.0\) mm, \(k=20.0\) N/m and \(\alpha=1.00\times10^4\) N/m³. Then \(\epsilon=0.0720\), so the third-harmonic displacement amplitude is \(12.0(0.0720)/32=0.0270\) mm. The fundamental frequency should increase by \(2.70\%\). With \(\omega_0=10.0\) rad/s, the third-harmonic line lies close to \(30.8\) rad/s.

Second application

Test whether a measured line comes from the spring

At \(A=6.00\) mm on the same apparatus, \(\epsilon\) falls by four and the third-harmonic amplitude falls by eight to \(3.38\) micrometres. The fractional frequency shift becomes \(0.675\%\). A third-harmonic signal that instead halves with amplitude could arise from an already distorted input or readout. The two amplitude scalings plus an independent frequency measurement test the cubic model more strongly than observing a line at three times the frequency.

Start from a reversible force law

A spring that looks linear under small deflection can stiffen as it is displaced further. For an oscillator symmetric under \(x\mapsto-x\), the restoring force expanded about equilibrium has no even powers. Keep the leading correction and write \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\), with \(k>0\) and \(\alpha\) measured in N/m³. Assume the displacement is small enough that neglected fifth-order terms remain unimportant. Positive \(\alpha\) gives a hardening spring. A negative cubic coefficient describes local softening and may need higher-order terms to represent large displacements safely within the model.

The potential is \(kx^2/2+\alpha x^4/4\). Without damping the total energy is conserved and a release at amplitude \(A\) fixes that energy. Unlike a harmonic oscillator, the quadratic curvature at the origin no longer fixes the period for every possible energy. We want a quantitative connection between the amplitude of the motion and its zero-crossing interval.

Obtain the first frequency correction

Try \(x\simeq A\cos\Omega t\) and substitute it into the cubic equation. The identity \(\cos^3 u=(3\cos u+\cos3u)/4\) shows that the nonlinearity introduces a component at the original frequency and a third harmonic. Balance the fundamental coefficient to get \(-m\Omega^2+k+3\alpha A^2/4\simeq0\). Expanding the square root gives

\[\Omega\simeq\omega_0\left(1+\frac{3\alpha A^2}{8k}\right),\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}.\]

The control parameter is \(|\alpha|A^2/k\), the ratio of cubic to linear force at maximum displacement. The small additional third harmonic changes the waveform at first order but only feeds back into this leading frequency result at higher order. Fitting a perfect sinusoid to a high-precision trace may therefore show a residual even when the measured average period follows this formula.

Check the result using the conserved energy

The quarter-period is the time to move from equilibrium to \(A\). Substitution of the energy at the turning point gives an integral over \(dx/\sqrt{2[E-U(x)]/m}\). Let \(x=A\sin u\) to remove the square-root endpoint. The remaining integrand is proportional to \(\{1+[\alpha A^2/(2k)](1+\sin^2u)\}^{-1/2}\). Expanding and averaging over the interval from zero to \(\pi/2\) uses mean \(\sin^2 u=1/2\), yielding \(T\simeq(2\pi/\omega_0)[1-3\alpha A^2/(8k)]\). This agrees with the reciprocal of the harmonic-balance result.

The integral approach generalises to experimentally tabulated conservative forces by integrating them first to obtain a potential. It would need modification for strong damping, where energy varies significantly between the two turning points of one cycle.

Infer stiffness from period data

For \(m=0.50\) kg, \(k=200\) N/m and \(\alpha=4.0\times10^4\) N/m³, \(\omega_0=20.0\) rad/s. At \(A=0.020\) m the force ratio is 0.080, giving a leading frequency increase of 3.0% to 20.6 rad/s. At half this amplitude the predicted increase is 0.75%. Measure the period over several cycles at each amplitude, keeping the amplitude change due to loss small. A plot of frequency against the square of amplitude should be approximately straight near the origin. A nonzero intercept shift under a change of temperature could indicate a change in ordinary linear stiffness instead of a new nonlinear coefficient.

Compute the entire amplitude dependence of a pendulum

A cubic expansion is useful near equilibrium. For a pendulum it is possible to carry the energy calculation beyond every finite Taylor approximation. Take a point mass with fixed length \(\ell\), no friction and a release from rest at angle \(0<\theta_0<\pi\). Its conserved energy gives \(\dot\theta^2=(2g/\ell)(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)\). Use \(\cos\theta=1-2\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Defining \(k=\sin(\theta_0/2)\) reduces the speed magnitude to \(2\sqrt{g/\ell}\sqrt{k^2-\sin^2(\theta/2)}\).

One quarter period is the time to travel between zero angle and the turning point. The reciprocal speed appears to diverge at the latter, but it has an integrable square-root singularity for every ordinary turning angle. To evaluate it stably, set \(\sin(\theta/2)=k\sin u\). The factor \(\cos u\) from differentiating cancels the same factor inside the square root. Four equivalent quarters give

\[T=4\sqrt{\ell/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}}=4\sqrt{\ell/g}\,K(k).\]

Here \(K\) denotes the complete elliptic integral with modulus \(k\). Expanding the integrand at small \(k\) and integrating the first correction gives \(T/T_0=1+k^2/4+\cdots=1+\theta_0^2/16+\cdots\), where \(T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\). The positive correction is the familiar softening: increasing amplitude lengthens the period because the restoring torque grows more slowly than its linear extrapolation.

Decide when a small-angle clock loses accuracy

A pendulum of length \(0.800\) m has \(T_0=1.794\) s at \(g=9.81\) m/s². At a release of \(60.0^\circ\), \(k=\sin30^\circ=0.500\). Evaluating the integral gives \(K(0.500)=1.68575\) and \(T/T_0=2K/\pi=1.0732\), hence \(T=1.926\) s. The weak-angle approximation gives \(1+(\pi/3)^2/16=1.0685\). It misses the exact period by roughly \(0.008\) s per cycle; a many-cycle timing record resolves the accumulated discrepancy.

As \(\theta_0\) tends to \(\pi\), the integrand near \(u=\pi/2\) approaches the reciprocal of a vanishing cosine. The integral diverges logarithmically, expressing the long time spent close to the unstable upper equilibrium. No finite polynomial in amplitude can reproduce that divergence. A numerical timing experiment should therefore locate regular zero crossings rather than demand a turning-point velocity of exactly zero in sampled data. Comparing the crossing intervals with the regularized integral distinguishes numerical resolution error from the genuine increase in physical period.

A way to evaluate the elliptic integral without a special-function library is to form the arithmetic–geometric mean. Begin with \(a_0=1\) and \(b_0=\sqrt{1-k^2}\), then replace the pair by their arithmetic and geometric means. Both converge rapidly to a common \(M\), and \(K(k)=\pi/(2M)\). For the 60° release the first pair is \(1\) and \(0.86603\), the next pair is approximately \(0.93301\) and \(0.93060\), and the subsequent pair agrees near \(0.93181\). Taking the reciprocal of the common value directly gives the period ratio. This procedure supplies an accurate independent benchmark even when the small-amplitude expansion ceases to converge quickly.

Numerical quadrature and the arithmetic–geometric mean should agree at a fixed amplitude. If a period extracted from actual trajectories differs while these two evaluations agree, examine the dynamical time step, how release angle is measured, and whether the observed oscillations have suffered appreciable damping.

Liouville flow and what an ensemble can remember

Classical mechanics

A cloud of identical noninteracting beads is released with slightly different initial positions and momenta. Its position distribution widens. Does the system occupy more phase space, or has its uncertainty merely changed shape?

Follow a small phase-space element

For one canonical degree of freedom the velocity of a point \((q,p)\) is \((\partial_pH,-\partial_qH)\). A short-time map takes \(\boldsymbol z\) to \(\boldsymbol z+\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol z,t)\,dt\). Its Jacobian matrix is \(I+(\partial\boldsymbol v/\partial\boldsymbol z)dt\); expanding the determinant gives \(1+(\nabla_z\cdot\boldsymbol v)dt+O(dt^2)\). Hamilton's equations make the divergence zero because the two mixed second derivatives of a smooth Hamiltonian cancel. Each additional canonical pair contributes a similar cancellation. A time-dependent Hamiltonian may change energy but still preserve the instantaneous phase-space volume along the flow.

Conservation of the number of ensemble members in a moving phase-space box also gives the continuity equation \(\partial_t f+\nabla_z\cdot(f\boldsymbol v)=0\). The vanishing divergence leaves \(df/dt=0\) on each trajectory. A stationary mechanical system can admit \(f=F(H)\) as a stationary distribution because trajectories stay on energy surfaces. Such stationarity alone does not imply that one trajectory explores an entire energy surface. For example, further integrals of motion restrict regular multidimensional trajectories.

Propagate a cloud released into free flight

For mass \(m\), the exact map is \(p=p_0,\ q=q_0+tp_0/m\). Its determinant is one regardless of time. An initially uncorrelated cloud with variances \(s_q^2,s_p^2\) has evolved covariance \(C_{qp}=ts_p^2/m\). The new position variance is \(s_q^2+(ts_p/m)^2\), while the momentum variance stays fixed. Compute the determinant of this covariance matrix:

\[\sigma_q^2\sigma_p^2-C_{qp}^2=s_q^2s_p^2 .\]

For a Gaussian the ellipse at a fixed multiple of the covariance radius thus retains its area while it shears. With \(m=1\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(s_q=1\,\mathrm m\), \(s_p=0.5\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\), and \(t=4\,\mathrm s\), the observed position standard deviation is \(\sqrt5=2.236\,\mathrm m\). The momentum standard deviation remains 0.5 and the covariance becomes \(1.0\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\). Their uncorrected width product is 1.118 in action units. The square root of the covariance determinant is still 0.5. Multiplication of measured marginal widths alone falsely suggests an area increase.

Compare reversible shear and real drag

Now apply a linear drag \(\dot p=-\gamma p\) to each bead with constant \(\gamma>0\). Its phase-space divergence is \(-\gamma\), hence the map determinant after time \(t\) is \(e^{-\gamma t}\). Direct integration gives \(p=e^{-\gamma t}p_0,\ q=q_0+(1-e^{-\gamma t})p_0/(m\gamma)\). The cloud now genuinely loses area in the reduced bead description. A larger Hamiltonian model of bead plus environment can remain volume preserving. Choose a drag rate in the graph and compare the original, freely sheared and damped ellipses for the same observation time. Check there is a smooth zero-drag limit rather than substituting zero into a quotient.

Parametric resonance and Floquet growth

Classical mechanics

A pendulum can gain energy when its length varies periodically even if no additive force acts on it. A modulation near twice a natural frequency amplifies one phase of the oscillation and attenuates another. The stability test concerns the growth of a perturbation over a complete modulation period.

Linearise the timing mechanism

Consider \(\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2[1+h\cos(\Omega t)]x=0\). Here \(h\) is the fractional modulation of the stiffness and \(\gamma\) is the amplitude damping rate. If \(x=\dot x=0\) exactly, the solution stays zero. Small fluctuations or an initial condition provide the seed. Direct differentiation of \(E=[\dot x^2+\omega_0^2(1+h\cos\Omega t)x^2]/2\) yields \(\dot E=-2\gamma\dot x^2-h\omega_0^2\Omega x^2\sin(\Omega t)/2\). Energy transfer depends on the phase of \(x^2\), which naturally contains a component at twice the oscillation frequency.

Find the first instability tongue

A magnetic orbit that spirals outward in a perfect vacuum

Track a charged particle in a prescribed uniform field, derive a rotation update and separate numerical energy growth from a real magnetic force.

Use the work law before solving the trajectory

A nonrelativistic charged particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) moves perpendicular to a uniform, time-independent magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\). There is no electric field and the dilute test particles do not affect the applied field. The Lorentz force gives \(\dot v_x=\omega v_y,\ \dot v_y=-\omega v_x\) for \(\omega=qB/m\). Dotting with \(\mathbf v\) gives zero, so the speed should stay constant. This property holds for either sign of the charge; the direction of rotation changes. It is a better initial test of a trajectory solver than how circular a short segment looks on screen.

Define \(J(v_x,v_y)=(v_y,-v_x)\), so \(J^T=-J\) and \(J^2=-I\). The exact velocity over a time step \(h\) is \([\cos(\omega h)I+\sin(\omega h)J]\mathbf v\). Integration in time gives a circular orbit of radius \(v_\perp/|\omega|\) once the guiding centre is chosen. A magnetic field alone bends a trajectory without changing its kinetic energy; a slowly growing orbit radius at fixed \(B\) and fixed initial speed demands an explanation.

Why forward Euler adds energy at every step

Euler gives \(\mathbf v_+=(I+dJ)\mathbf v\) with \(d=\omega h\). Squaring the norm eliminates the cross term because \(v^TJv=0\), leaving \(\|\mathbf v_+\|^2=(1+d^2)\|\mathbf v\|^2\). No nonzero step has exact unit gain. A geometric picture makes the bias tangible: a tangent step leaves the initial velocity circle on its outside, whatever the point of departure.

After \(N\) steps the speed factor is \((1+d^2)^{N/2}\). With 24 steps per physical orbit, \(d=2\pi/24\simeq0.2618\), so a full orbit multiplies speed by roughly 2.2. At shorter times or smaller steps this can resemble weak physical acceleration. Reducing \(h\) at fixed physical duration reduces the growth. Testing one step at fixed count, rather than comparing over a fixed physical interval, underestimates the cumulative error.

Solve a centred magnetic step exactly

Use the time-centred velocity in the Lorentz force: \(\mathbf v_+-\mathbf v=d J(\mathbf v_++\mathbf v)/2\). Premultiply by the transpose of \(\mathbf v_++\mathbf v\). The left side is the difference of squared speeds and the right side vanishes, so the update preserves speed for every \(d\) in exact arithmetic. Rearranging yields a two-dimensional linear solve; using \(J^2=-I\) turns it into a rotation,

\[\mathbf v_+=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\mathbf v+\frac{2t}{1+t^2}J\mathbf v,\]
\[t=d/2.\]

These coefficients are \(\cos\alpha\) and \(\sin\alpha\) for \(\alpha=2\arctan(d/2)\). This is the magnetic rotation used in the nonrelativistic Boris integrator. For simultaneous electric fields, the usual split step adds two half electric impulses around the magnetic rotation. The current experiment isolates the magnetic part; it makes no claim about a nonuniform electric field or the accuracy of interpolation from a field mesh to particles.

Relativistic particle dynamics and the field tensor

Waves, radiation and relativity

A circular track measures momentum per charge. A clock following the particle registers proper time, and a detector records how much kinetic energy it has gained. We will put these observations in one dynamical equation, solve that equation in uniform fields and exhibit a numerical pusher that can get the energy right while its orbit phase slowly slips.

What the track, clock and calorimeter measure

Consider a particle of invariant mass \(m\), signed charge \(q\) and ordinary velocity \(\mathbf v\). Inertial observers agree on the proper time between neighbouring events on its timelike worldline, \(d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}\). Adopt coordinates \(x^\mu=(ct,x,y,z)\) and metric \(g=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)\). Dividing the displacement by proper time produces a vector of fixed Minkowski norm. Multiplying it by invariant mass gives four-momentum.

\[u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v),\]
\[p^\mu=mu^\mu=(\mathcal E/c,\mathbf p),\]
\[\mathcal E^2-c^2|\mathbf p|^2=m^2c^4 .\]

Energy includes the rest term. Momentum is \(\gamma m\mathbf v\). Differentiating the last equation on a trajectory gives \(d\mathcal E/dt=\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf p/dt\). This identity replaces the nonrelativistic step of dotting \(m\mathbf a\) with velocity. A magnetic field can rotate momentum with fixed magnitude and energy, whereas an electric field can change energy by doing work.

Suppose an electron begins at rest and gains 10.0 keV of kinetic energy across an electrostatic potential difference in vacuum. Its rest energy is 511 keV, giving \(\gamma=1.01957\) and \(v/c=\sqrt{1-1/\gamma^2}=0.1950\). The nonrelativistic estimate is 0.1978. In a modest energy measurement the discrepancy could lie below the experimental spread. For a precise orbit calculation the 1.96 percent change in relativistic cyclotron period is measurable independently of that discrepancy in speed.

A linear map that produces the Lorentz force

Seek a local equation linear in field and four-velocity and covariant under boosts. For the energy component to give \(q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\), and for the spatial components to give the electric force and the oriented cross product, construct the antisymmetric field tensor. With the stated metric and \(\epsilon^{123}=1\), set

\[F^{0i}=-E_i/c,\]
\[F^{i0}=E_i/c,\]
\[F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B_k,\]
\[\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu .\]

Lowering the index on \(u\) reverses the signs of its spatial components. The spatial product becomes \(q\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\), and division by \(\gamma\) converts the proper-time derivative to \(d\mathbf p/dt\). The time component similarly yields \(d\mathcal E/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\). Contract the force with \(u_\mu\). The symmetric product \(u_\mu u_\nu\) annihilates the antisymmetric tensor, which proves that the evolution preserves the mass shell.

To recover Maxwell's kinematic constraints from potentials, put \(A^\mu=(\phi/c,\mathbf A)\) and \(F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu\). Then \(F^{0i}=(\partial_t A_i+\partial_i\phi)/c=-E_i/c\), while the spatial-spatial components are the magnetic curl. Adding a four-gradient to \(A^\mu\) leaves the tensor unchanged because ordinary derivatives commute. The particle action \(S=-mc^2\int d\tau-q\int A_\mu dx^\mu\) gives the same force after integrating variations by parts with fixed endpoints. Gauge changes add only an endpoint term to this action. A trajectory measured with local forces cannot distinguish such potential representatives.

Constant force, variable coordinate acceleration

Put a particle initially at rest in a uniform static electric field along \(x\). Let \(a_0=qE/m\) be signed. Integration of \(dp_x/dt=qE\) gives \(p_x=ma_0t\), so the speed tends to \(c\) even while momentum increases linearly. Proper-time evolution is simplest in terms of rapidity \(\eta\), where \(u^0=c\cosh\eta\), \(u^x=c\sinh\eta\). The two tensor equations imply \(d\eta/d\tau=a_0/c\).

\[t=\frac{c}{a_0}\sinh(a_0\tau/c),\]
\[x=\frac{c^2}{a_0}\{\cosh(a_0\tau/c)-1\},\]
\[\frac{dv_x}{dt}=\frac{a_0}{\gamma^3}.\]

The initial time and position are zero. The work \(qEx=mc^2(\cosh\eta-1)\) equals kinetic energy, which checks both the integration constant and the dimensional factor in the displacement. For an electron use the direction of the electric force as positive to simplify signs. At 20.0 kV/m it gains 1.0 keV in 5.0 cm. The energy gain follows from the static potential even though the speed does not grow exactly as the square root of distance. The field region must last long enough for the particle to cross it and fringe fields can alter the entry trajectory.

Helical motion, radius and period

A uniform magnetic field along \(z\) keeps \(p_z\), energy and \(|\mathbf p_\perp|\) fixed. Write \(p_x+ip_y\) as a complex number. The Lorentz force reduces to a first-order rotation equation and a single time integration gives the corresponding position. With positive \(B\), positive charges starting along \(+x\) initially turn towards \(-y\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}(p_x+ip_y)=-i\frac{qB}{\gamma m}(p_x+ip_y),\]
\[R=\frac{p_\perp}{|q|B},\]
\[T=\frac{2\pi\gamma m}{|q|B}.\]

The helical pitch is \(v_zT\). Radius gives transverse momentum and a calibrated period gives total energy through \(\gamma\). A track seen only in projection along the field gives no direct evidence that longitudinal momentum vanishes. A second view or a timing measurement can resolve this ambiguity.

At 10.0 keV the electron has \(pc=\sqrt{K(K+2mc^2)}=101.6\) keV. With entry perpendicular to a 2.0 mT field its radius is 0.169 m and its period 18.2 ns. Doubling the field halves both radius and period at fixed injection energy. Doubling momentum at fixed field doubles radius but changes period by the ratio of Lorentz factors. These are different tests of a track calibration. A beam with a spread of energies will dephase relative to a narrow timing gate even when every single particle remains in a perfectly uniform field.

A drift frame gives an exact crossed-field solution

For constant crossed fields with \(E<cB\), transform to the frame moving at \(\mathbf v_d=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). There \(E'=0\) and \(B'=\sqrt{B^2-E^2/c^2}\); calculate the transformed initial momentum and integrate a helix. Transform two successive equal-phase events back to the lab. Their perpendicular displacement in the electric-free frame is zero. If the motion has no component parallel to \(\mathbf B\), the entire displacement there is zero, and the average lab cycle velocity is exactly \(\mathbf v_d\). The detailed lab trace need not be circular and the instantaneous lab energy may oscillate.

Take \(E=30.0\) kV/m along \(y\) and \(B=0.50\) mT along \(z\). The drift is along \(+x\) at \(6.00\times10^7\) m/s. A particle injected at that velocity crosses the uniform fields on a straight line because the forces cancel. An initially stationary positive particle instead begins by accelerating along \(+y\). These observations can use the same field with different injected particles. If \(E\) exceeds \(cB\), this electric-free frame is unavailable. Transform to a magnetic-free frame and integrate electric acceleration instead.

Continuous rotation and a discrete experiment

A naive forward step \(\mathbf p_{n+1}=\mathbf p_n+q\Delta t\,\mathbf v_n\times\mathbf B\) adds a vector perpendicular to the previous momentum. Its squared norm increases by the squared magnitude of that kick. The apparent heating will persist in perfectly static uniform magnetic fields. For a purely magnetic update, evaluate the direction using the mean of old and new momenta. At fixed \(\gamma\), the resulting implicit two-dimensional linear map can be solved algebraically and is an exact rotation by a modified angle.

\[\delta=2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega_c\Delta t}{2}\right),\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{qB}{\gamma m}.\]

The sign of rotation follows the earlier complex equation. Dotting the update with the mean momentum gives zero change in squared norm, which verifies the energy property even for relatively large time steps. A common electromagnetic pusher surrounds the magnetic rotation by two electric half-kicks. In spatially varying fields, specify at what time and position each field is sampled, and check spatial interpolation and time convergence. For ultrarelativistic nearly cancelling electric and magnetic fields, preserving rotation in a pure field alone does not ensure cancellation is represented accurately.

Solve the constant tensor without transforming every time step

When uniform electric and magnetic fields are constant in spacetime, the Lorentz equation is a linear constant-coefficient equation for four-velocity as a function of proper time. Nonlinearity in ordinary time hides that simple structure, because the rate at which proper time accumulates changes along the orbit. Define the mixed tensor \(M^\mu{}_\nu=(q/m)F^{\mu\alpha}g_{\alpha\nu}\). In a uniform field it does not vary along the trajectory. A formal solution is then

\[u(\tau)=e^{M\tau}u(0),\]
\[x(\tau)-x(0)=\int_0^\tau e^{Ms}u(0)\,ds .\]

The coordinate formula is written as an integral since \(M\) can be singular, as it is in a purely magnetic field. Blindly replacing this integral by \(M^{-1}(e^{M\tau}-I)\) fails in exactly such simple cases. A power series for the integral or a block matrix exponential treats zero eigenvalues without an artificial inverse. Antisymmetry of the tensor implies \(M^{\mathsf T}g+gM=0\). Differentiating \(e^{M^{\mathsf T}\tau}ge^{M\tau}\) shows that it stays equal to \(g\), making preservation of \(u^\mu u_\mu=c^2\) a structural property of the exact mapping.

For parallel constant fields along \(z\), the matrix decomposes into a boost in the \(ct,z\) subspace and a rotation in \(x,y\). Write \(\alpha=qE/(mc)\), \(\Omega=qB/m\), and denote the initial four-velocity components by \(u_0^\mu\). The proper-time solution is

\[u^0=u_0^0\cosh\alpha\tau+u_0^z\sinh\alpha\tau,\]
\[u^z=u_0^z\cosh\alpha\tau+u_0^0\sinh\alpha\tau,\]
\[u^x+i u^y=(u_0^x+i u_0^y)e^{-i\Omega\tau}.\]

The longitudinal and transverse operations commute because they act in different subspaces. In lab time the transverse rotation slows as the electric field increases energy, although the proper-time rotation frequency stays constant. A particle therefore traces a helix whose transverse radius \(p_\perp/(|q|B)\) is fixed while the pitch evolves. A single measured turn time without a tagged position in the accelerating region is insufficient for an energy reconstruction.

As a numerical test use an electron, quoting magnitudes, with \(B=1.0\) mT, \(E=10.0\) kV/m and initial purely transverse speed \(0.10c\). One magnetic turn takes 35.72 ns in proper time and accumulates electric rapidity magnitude \(|\alpha|\tau\simeq0.2096\). The final Lorentz factor is \(\gamma_i\cosh(0.2096)=1.0272\), giving final kinetic energy about 13.9 keV, compared with 2.57 keV initially. The lab-time turn duration is \(\gamma_i\sinh(|\alpha|\tau)/|\alpha|\simeq36.2\) ns. Reversing the electric field changes the direction of the axial displacement but leaves final energy and turn duration unchanged for this initially transverse particle. Reversing the initial longitudinal velocity would generally break that energy symmetry.

Infer uncertainty and test units in a momentum reconstruction

A track fitter commonly reports field, radius, pitch, time and a fitted covariance matrix. The dynamical relation turns these observed quantities into momentum. Let \(L_z\) denote a signed pitch per turn. With known signed direction of circulation and calibrated absolute field magnitude, \(|\mathbf p|=|q|B\sqrt{R^2+(L_z/2\pi)^2}\). For small errors, logarithmic differentiation gives weights for fractional radius and pitch errors:

\[d\log p=d\log B+w_R\,d\log R+w_L\,d\log |L_z|,\]
\[w_R=\frac{R^2}{R^2+(L_z/2\pi)^2},\]
\[w_L=1-w_R .\]

For \(R=0.20\) m and \(L_z=0.30\) m the radius accounts for 94.6 percent of the squared transverse-plus-longitudinal geometric factor. Independent relative uncertainties of 0.20 percent in field, 0.50 percent in radius and 2.0 percent in pitch yield about 0.525 percent uncertainty in total momentum. A common detector scale controlling both radius and pitch would enter with correlated signs and should not be counted as two independent errors. When the pitch tends to zero, use the original derivatives with respect to \(L_z\); a reported fractional error on a quantity consistent with zero is ill defined.

Differentiating \(K=\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}-mc^2\) gives \(d\log K=(\gamma+1)d\log p/\gamma\). The coefficient approaches two at low energy and one at very high energy. At the approximately 3.70 keV energy of the tutorial the above momentum error would imply about a 1.05 percent energy error. A kinetic-energy estimate from a small difference of nearly equal numbers can also lose numerical precision at much lower energies. Rationalise it as \(K=p^2c^2/(\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}+mc^2)\) when working near rest.

Several independent checks use this reconstruction without assuming the track fitter is correct. The turn time inferred from the fitted \(\gamma\) can be compared with a separate timing channel. Reverse the magnetic field to reverse the direction of curvature while keeping the speed and energy approximately fixed. Translate the beam within a mapped uniform field and look for changing fitted radius that correlates with position, an indication of geometric or field-map bias. Finally plot the residual of the magnetic force law separately for transverse magnitude and angle. A time integrator may conserve momentum magnitude for symmetry reasons even when its field interpolation biases the phase or shifts the guiding centre systematically.

Why momentum and kinetic energy do not gain fixed fractions together

In an electrostatic accelerating gap, the kinetic energy increment for a particle passing between two equipotential surfaces is its charge times the potential drop, regardless of injection energy. This statement follows by integrating the Lorentz power law over the actual path. A magnetic guiding field can alter the path and transit time while doing no direct work. A time-dependent electric field, a missed equipotential endpoint or appreciable radiation changes the simple inference and must be addressed separately.

For an electron entering with 10 keV and acquiring an additional 10 keV, kinetic energy doubles. Its initial momentum times \(c\) is about 101.6 keV and its final value about 144.4 keV. The radius in the same subsequent perpendicular magnetic field increases by a factor approximately 1.42. Doubling the radius would instead require doubling momentum, which demands a substantially greater energy increment at this low initial energy. Comparing radii before and after a known voltage supplies an independent check on the sign and magnitude of a particle's charge-to-mass model.

A measured energy gain below the nominal voltage does not by itself diagnose radiation. The potential actually traversed, finite angular acceptance, entrance or exit fringe fields, collision losses and biases in reconstructing longitudinal momentum can all matter. Repeat with reversed field polarity or several injection energies and compare the kinetic-energy difference rather than the two radii directly.

Connect characteristic roots to decay and oscillation

Near an equilibrium, many closed-loop positioning stages reduce to a constant-coefficient second-order equation. Consider the free response after the reference has become constant. Let \(x\) be deviation from its final value, \(\omega_n>0\) the natural frequency and \(\zeta\) the dimensionless damping ratio. Try an exponential response because time derivatives of an exponential reproduce the same waveform.

\[\ddot x+2\zeta\omega_n\dot x+\omega_n^2x=0,\]
\[x=e^{st}\ \Longrightarrow\ s^2+2\zeta\omega_n s+\omega_n^2=0 .\]

For \(0<\zeta<1\) solving the quadratic produces conjugate roots. Their real part sets the exponential decay rate and their imaginary part sets the observed angular oscillation frequency.

\[s_{1,2}=-\zeta\omega_n\pm i\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},\]
\[x=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}(A\cos\omega_dt+B\sin\omega_dt).\]

Here \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) and the initial displacement and velocity set \(A,B\). A measured period gives \(\omega_d\) while the decaying peak envelope gives \(\zeta\omega_n\). Stable poles describe decay of free modes. The numerator of a forced response also influences how prominently each mode appears.

Derivation and applications

Separate a cubic stability boundary from oscillation frequency

Feedback gain can move an oscillatory pair across the imaginary axis even with every characteristic coefficient positive. Take a continuous linear unity-feedback loop whose open-loop transfer is \(K/[s(s+a)(s+b)]\) with positive \(a,b,K\). The integral mode can represent a position obtained from velocity; the other poles can represent actuator and measurement dynamics. Add unity to the loop transfer and clear its denominator to obtain the closed-loop polynomial.

\[p(s)=s^3+(a+b)s^2+ab\,s+K .\]

A marginal oscillation has \(s=i\omega\) with real nonzero \(\omega\). Separate the real and imaginary parts. The imaginary equation sets the frequency without requiring the gain, while the real equation selects the gain that supports it.

\[K-(a+b)\omega^2=0,\]
\[\omega(ab-\omega^2)=0,\]
\[K_{\rm crit}=ab(a+b).\]

At this gain the cubic factors exactly into a real stable pole and a pair of purely imaginary roots. The cubic Routh first column determines which side of the boundary is stable. Its first two entries are positive, making the sign of the third entry decisive for positive gain.

\[p_{\rm crit}(s)=(s+a+b)(s^2+ab),\]
\[\text{first column: }1,\ a+b,\ ab-\frac{K}{a+b},\ K .\]

Below the threshold all entries have the same sign. Above it there are two sign changes and therefore two right-half-plane roots. The model treats actuator authority as unlimited and omits nonlinear friction; a finite observed oscillation amplitude requires additional physics beyond the pole crossing.

Worked example

Predict the onset period of a positioning loop

Set \(a=2.00\) s⁻¹ and \(b=8.00\) s⁻¹. Marginal gain is \(K_{\rm crit}=160\) s⁻³. The oscillation has frequency \(4.00\) rad/s, hence period \(2\pi/4.00=1.57\) s. At \(K=120\) s⁻³ the third entry is \(16-12=4.00\), giving stability. At \(K=180\) s⁻³ it is \(-2.00\) and the pair grows.

Second application

Recover the two lag rates from a marginal test

Another loop reaches marginality at \(K=60.0\) s⁻³ with a measured oscillation of \(\sqrt{10}\) rad/s. The rates have product \(ab=10.0\) s⁻² and sum \(K/(ab)=6.00\) s⁻¹. These observations cannot describe two positive real lag rates because the maximum product at fixed sum is \(6^2/4=9.00\) s⁻². The failed consistency check flags model structure or calibration before attributing a unique time constant to either stage.

Relate polynomial coefficients to observed motion

For a normalised second-order system, the free response satisfies \(\ddot y+2\zeta\omega_n\dot y+\omega_n^2y=0\). Substitution of \(e^{st}\) gives \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). The real part sets the decay rate. For \(0<\zeta<1\), the imaginary part \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) sets the time between successive extrema. Doubling \(\omega_n\) at fixed \(\zeta\) halves all characteristic times but leaves the fraction of overshoot unchanged.

Assume a unit DC gain, a constant numerator and zero initial displacement and velocity. The step response is \(y=1-e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}[\cos(\omega_dt)+(\zeta\omega_n/\omega_d)\sin(\omega_dt)]\). Differentiate the bracket together with its envelope. The cosine terms cancel and the remaining derivative is proportional to \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_dt)\). The first peak occurs at \(t_p=\pi/\omega_d\). Substituting that time gives the fractional overshoot

\[M_p=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right).\]

Design for a specified peak

Suppose a positioning axis may overshoot a 10 mm command by at most 1 mm. Solving the previous expression for the limiting damping ratio gives \(\zeta=-\ln M_p/\sqrt{\pi^2+(\ln M_p)^2}=0.591\). At \(\omega_n=5\) rad/s the corresponding poles are approximately \(-2.96\pm4.03i\) per second and the first peak occurs at 0.779 s. A nominal oscillatory second-order system with this damping just meets the 1 mm bound. A zero near the dominant poles, a saturated drive or another slow pole changes the transient, so the result should be checked against the full model after the poles are chosen.

A settling specification differs from a peak specification. The absolute normalised error stays below the envelope \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). To guarantee a 2% settling band using this bound, solve \(t\ge-\ln[0.02\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}]/(\zeta\omega_n)\). This conservative time for the example is about 1.40 s. The commonly used \(4/(\zeta\omega_n)\) is a convenient estimate. The envelope bound spells out both the tolerance and its assumptions.

Follow a gain change on a third-order plant

A plant with two lags and an integrator has open-loop transfer \(K/[s(s+1)(s+2)]\) in units normalised to a common time base. Close unity negative feedback. The characteristic polynomial is \(s^3+3s^2+2s+K\). Its coefficients are all positive for \(K>0\), which rules out positive real roots but allows an unstable complex pair. Form the first column of the Routh array: \(1,\ 3,\ (6-K)/3,\ K\). All entries must have the same sign for strict stability. Thus \(0<K<6\).

At \(K=6\) the polynomial factors as \((s+3)(s^2+2)\). The step excites an undamped pair at angular frequency \(\sqrt2\), alongside a decaying component. At \(K=7\) the first-column signs change twice, so two roots have crossed into the right half-plane. The input-output gain alone did not reveal this upper bound. Additional lags allow feedback to arrive out of phase with motion. Chapter 4 reaches the same type of conclusion by examining sine-wave responses, which can often be measured even when coefficients of a polynomial are poorly known.

Find the effect of a finite closed-loop zero

Pole locations specify the available exponential terms of a causal linear response. Their coefficients depend on the numerator and initial condition. To isolate this distinction, choose two positive times \(\tau_1\ne\tau_2\) and a transfer function \(G(s)=(1+T_zs)/[(1+\tau_1s)(1+\tau_2s)]\). It has unit static gain for every \(T_z\), so changing the numerator neither moves the poles nor changes the final unit-step response. For zero initial state and a unit step \(Y(s)=G(s)/s\). Write \(Y=1/s+A/(s+1/\tau_1)+B/(s+1/\tau_2)\).

The initial value \(y(0^+)=0\) gives \(A+B=-1\). The initial-slope theorem gives \(\dot y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}sG(s)=T_z/(\tau_1\tau_2)\); equivalently \(-A/\tau_1-B/\tau_2=T_z/(\tau_1\tau_2)\). Solving and transforming back gives

\[y(t)=1-\frac{\tau_1-T_z}{\tau_1-\tau_2}e^{-t/\tau_1}+\frac{\tau_2-T_z}{\tau_1-\tau_2}e^{-t/\tau_2}.\]

For \(T_z=\tau_1\) one mode has zero coefficient in this particular input–output map. A physical internal state with that mode can still respond to a nonzero initial condition or another input. With \(T_z<0\) the zero lies in the right half-plane and the initial output slope opposes the eventual response. That inverse response can be a serious limitation when interpreting an early excursion as a sign error in the actuator.

Time the undershoot of two competing actuator paths

Consider an actuator that changes a measured quantity through a slow positive effect and an immediate faster opposing effect. Let \(\tau_1=4\) s, \(\tau_2=1\) s and \(T_z=-2\) s. The poles are both stable, and the output approaches one under a unit command. Yet the response is \(y=1-2e^{-t/4}+e^{-t}\), with initial slope \(-0.50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Set \(\dot y=0.5e^{-t/4}-e^{-t}=0\). It follows that \(e^{-3t/4}=0.5\), so the minimum occurs after \(t=4\ln2/3=0.924\,\mathrm s\). Its value is \(1-2(0.794)+0.397=-0.191\).

For a 10 mm final position change this model briefly produces about 1.91 mm motion in the wrong direction. A physical travel limit 1.0 mm behind the starting point would therefore be violated even though the real parts of both poles meet a settling-time target. Compare instead \(T_z=0\) with identical poles and DC gain: the initial slope vanishes and the response rises monotonically. Before changing controller gain to correct an observed undershoot, measure which pathway acts first. Inverting the right-half-plane zero with a causal stable feedforward filter is impossible, since exact inversion would introduce an unstable pole.

For this response the first crossing back through the initial position is later than the minimum. Let \(u=e^{-t/4}\); solving \(1-2u+u^4=0\) gives the trivial root \(u=1\) at release and a further root near 0.544. Thus the measured position remains on the wrong side for about \(-4\ln(0.544)=2.44\) s. A simple diagnostic that samples only once after two seconds would label the overall gain as negative. At one minute the same test reads nearly the full positive change. Recording the full waveform distinguishes an initial inverse response from an overall sensor polarity error. A disturbance entering the two paths at a different location can exhibit yet another zero, which is why a zero should always be quoted for a specified input and output.

Assign the poles of a spring-mounted positioning stage with PID

A positioning loop can measure displacement, accumulate position error and damp measured motion. Let a stage of mass \(m\), viscous damping \(c\) and return spring \(k\) obey \(m\ddot y+c\dot y+ky=u+d\), with actuator force \(u\) and disturbance force \(d\). The controller uses \(e=r-y\), \(\dot z=e\), and \(u=K_pe+K_iz-K_d\dot y\). Differentiating the measured position supplies damping without turning a step in commanded position into an impulsive force command.

To find the natural motion, set \(r=d=0\) and substitute \(u=-K_py+K_iz-K_d\dot y\). Move the feedback terms to the left and differentiate once to eliminate \(z\), using \(\dot z=-y\). The resulting characteristic polynomial is

\[m s^3+(c+K_d)s^2+(k+K_p)s+K_i.\]

The derivative gain adds viscous damping, the proportional gain adds stiffness, and closing an integral loop adds a state. At any stable equilibrium with a constant reference, \(\dot z=0\) requires \(y=r\). The integrator can then supply whatever constant force the spring and disturbance require, within actuator limits. The transient still depends on how the reference enters the controller.

Take \(m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(c=3.0\,\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}\), \(k=5.0\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). Suppose the desired poles are \(-2,-3,-4\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Multiplying the three factors gives the desired polynomial \(2(s+2)(s+3)(s+4)=2s^3+18s^2+52s+48\). Comparing coefficients produces \(K_d=15\,\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}\), \(K_p=47\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), \(K_i=48\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}s^{-1}}\). For a 20 mm command from rest the initial proportional force is \(0.940\,\mathrm N\). At the final resting position with \(d=0\), the spring needs \(0.100\,\mathrm N\). \(K_i z\) supplies it and the proportional contribution is zero. A filtered derivative introduces an additional pole, so this ideal coefficient match must be checked again against the implemented controller bandwidth.

Sampling, saturation and commissioning

Control engineering

Carry a continuous-time design onto a sampled actuator, predict where its operating assumptions fail, and distinguish an observed final value from an equilibrium.

From first principles

Integrate a held command across one sample

A sampled actuator holds its command fixed until the next update. Consider a stable first-order plant \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\) with constant coefficients and sample interval \(h\). Let \(y_k\) be the value at the start of interval \(k\) and \(u_k\) the command held throughout it. During that interval the equilibrium for the applied command is the constant \(Ku_k\). Subtract it and integrate the homogeneous decay over exactly one interval.

\[\frac d{dt}(y-Ku_k)=-\frac{y-Ku_k}{\tau},\]
\[y_{k+1}-Ku_k=e^{-h/\tau}(y_k-Ku_k).\]

Set \(a=e^{-h/\tau}\). The resulting discrete plant is an exact sample-to-sample model under a zero-order hold. For proportional feedback with unit reference scale, substitute \(u_k=g(r_k-y_k)\) to see where the sampled free response pole lies.

\[y_{k+1}=a y_k+K(1-a)u_k,\]
\[z_{\rm cl}=a-K(1-a)g .\]

A sample-to-sample deviation is multiplied repeatedly by \(z_{\rm cl}\), so decay requires \(|z_{\rm cl}|<1\). This condition depends on the time at which the command is applied. A full sample of computational delay changes the state update and its characteristic equation.

Derivation and applications

Quantify the phase and attenuation introduced by a hold

A digital controller sends a piecewise-constant command to a continuous actuator. Even ideal sampling and instantaneous arithmetic do not make the resulting fundamental sinusoid identical to the requested one. Consider samples of a complex sinusoid at period \(h\). A zero-order hold outputs each sample unchanged over the following interval. For times \(t=kh+s\) within interval \(k\), the ratio of held signal to the desired continuous sinusoid is periodic in the within-sample time \(s\).

\[u_h(kh+s)=e^{i\omega kh},\]
\[\frac{u_h(kh+s)}{e^{i\omega(kh+s)}}=e^{-i\omega s},\]
\[0\leq s<h .\]

The zero-frequency Fourier coefficient of this periodic ratio gives the component at the original sinusoidal frequency. The remaining coefficients produce spectral images spaced by the sample frequency. Integrate the ratio over one hold interval and factor out the midpoint phase.

\[H_0(i\omega)=\frac1h\int_0^h e^{-i\omega s}ds=e^{-i\omega h/2}\frac{\sin(\omega h/2)}{\omega h/2}.\]

Below Nyquist the sine factor is positive. The fundamental therefore has phase equivalent to a delay of half a sample and a frequency-dependent gain. For small \(\omega h\), the sine expansion makes the leading attenuation quadratic while phase lag is linear.

\[\arg H_0=-\frac{\omega h}{2},\]
\[|H_0|=1-\frac{(\omega h)^2}{24}+O((\omega h)^4).\]

This calculation concerns the fundamental component of a held command. Additional computation latency and sensor filtering accumulate their own phase. Frequencies approaching Nyquist also require examining the sampled closed-loop dynamics directly.

Worked example

Check a servo's phase allowance

At 50.0 Hz crossover and 1.00 kHz sampling, \(\omega h/2=\pi(50/1000)=0.1571\). The hold contributes \(-9.00^\circ\) and its fundamental magnitude is 0.9959. A further full sample of computation delay contributes \(-18.0^\circ\), giving \(-27.0^\circ\) combined at that frequency. The small gain change would conceal this phase cost in a magnitude-only measurement.

Second application

Choose a sampling interval from a phase budget

At a proposed crossover of 80.0 Hz, allocate at most 6.00° lag to the hold. Since the magnitude of the phase in degrees is \(180fh\), the interval must satisfy \(h\leq6/(180\times80)=0.417\) ms, or a sample rate of at least 2.40 kHz. At this limit the fundamental magnitude is \(\sin(6^\circ)/(6\pi/180)=0.9982\). The resulting condition addresses the hold alone.

Derive the held-input model

A processor reads a sensor every \(h\) seconds and holds its most recent command until the next sample. Solve \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku_k\) exactly from \(kh\) to \((k+1)h\) with this constant input. The result is \(y_{k+1}=a y_k+K(1-a)u_k\), where \(a=e^{-h/\tau}\). This recursion is exact at the sampling instants for the assumed hold. Taking a large Euler step through the continuous closed-loop equation describes a different experiment. A real controller has only the sampled information available when it computes \(u_k\).

With proportional control \(u_k=K_p(r_k-y_k)\) the discrete pole is \(z=a-KK_p(1-a)\). Stability requires \(|z|\lt1\). A negative pole of modulus less than one alternates sign each update while decaying. For \(K=1,\ \tau=1\) s and \(K_p=3\), \(h=0.2\) s gives \(z=0.275\); \(h=0.8\) s gives \(z=-1.203\). The same nominal continuous design gives radically different sampled records. For positive gain the upper gain limit is \((1+a)/[K(1-a)]\). Additional computation and transport delays lower the available margin further.

Why a saturated integrator remembers the past

Let \(v_k=K_p e_k+I_k\) be the requested command, and \(u_k\) its value clipped to the actuator range. An unconstrained integrator adds \(hK_i e_k\) to \(I_k\) at every sample. While an unreachable command holds a heater at its upper limit, positive error keeps accumulating; when the command becomes reachable again the stored integral may keep the heater fully on. This delayed recovery is called windup. Logging only actual heater power hides the growth of the internal demand. Log both signals and the integral state.

A back-calculation update feeds the clipping discrepancy back to the integrator:

\[I_{k+1}=I_k+h[K_i e_k+(u_k-v_k)/T_t].\]

When the request exceeds the upper stop, the second term is negative. At fixed error and fixed clipping level, this update attracts the requested command toward a value consistent with saturation, provided its discrete tracking dynamics are stable. For example, with \(h=0.1\) s, \(K_i=1\), \(e=2\), \(u=5\), \(v=8\), and \(T_t=0.5\) s, the integral increment is \(-0.4\), compared with \(+0.2\) without correction. Tuning \(T_t\) must account for the sample interval and actuator dynamics. Anti-windup cannot make an infeasible steady torque available.

A persistent discrepancy warrants checking the recorded timestamps, the implementation of the step and whether a limit was active. To examine sampling or clipping, explicitly add these mechanisms to the model before comparing with the equations above. The logged linear-loop runs answer the finite-record question directly.

Derive an exact sampled stability boundary with a delayed command

A digital controller may acquire a sample at the start of an interval but only deliver its computed command at the following update. The one-sample computation delay then introduces an additional dynamic state. Consider a first-order plant \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\) with an ideal zero-order hold and sampling period \(T\). Its exact sample-to-sample relation is \(y_{k+1}=a y_k+b u_k\), with \(a=e^{-T/\tau}\) and \(b=K(1-a)\). Assume proportional negative feedback about a zero reference and express gains with dimensions chosen so that \(bg\) is dimensionless.

With negligible computation delay, \(u_k=-gy_k\) yields one pole \(a-bg\), so stability requires \(|a-bg|<1\). With a one-sample delay the applied command is \(u_k=-gy_{k-1}\), giving \(y_{k+1}=a y_k-bg y_{k-1}\). Substitution of a trial sequence \(z^k\) gives the characteristic polynomial \(z^2-az+bg\). For a real monic quadratic \(z^2+c_1z+c_0\), the unit-disc conditions are \(1+c_1+c_0>0,\ 1-c_1+c_0>0,\ 1-c_0>0\). For \(0<a<1,\ b>0,\ g>0\) only the last condition limits increasing gain: \(bg<1\).

Once the delayed poles form a complex pair, their product equals their squared modulus. That makes the bound physically transparent: as \(bg\) reaches one the oscillation no longer decays between samples. This mechanism is invisible if a continuously implemented controller is analysed and merely evaluated at plotting intervals.

Compare two software schedules at the same sampling rate

Take \(K=1\), \(\tau=0.50\,\mathrm s\), and \(T=0.10\,\mathrm s\). The exact coefficients are \(a=0.8187,\ b=0.1813\). Without delay the positive gain limit is \((1+a)/b=10.03\). An input limit may eventually bound the motion or produce a limit cycle, but it does not restore local linear stability around the unsaturated equilibrium. A controller test that records sensor samples and commanded values with separate timestamps can distinguish this delay from a sign error or an incorrectly discretised plant. Reduce the delayed gain to 4.0 and the predicted modulus becomes 0.852; the same clock can operate stably with a different response speed.

To recognise the delayed oscillation in a recorded trace, compute its angle as well as its modulus. At gain seven each real part is \(a/2=0.4094\). Dividing by the modulus 1.126 gives \(\cos\theta=0.364\), so \(\theta=1.20\,\mathrm{rad/sample}\). The apparent oscillation period is about \(2\pi(0.10)/1.20=0.52\) s. By comparison the immediate negative real pole produces a two-sample alternating pattern with period 0.20 s. A controller log showing roughly five samples per growing cycle accords with the delayed implementation, whereas alternating every sample points towards the immediate model operating too aggressively. This interpretation uses the synchronised physical actuator output; a software variable computed today for application tomorrow may have a different timestamp from the hardware.

Why a fast vibration can appear as a slow feedback error

An analogue position sensor may detect a genuine low-frequency displacement and a much faster frame vibration. Sampling can make the two contributions indistinguishable. For a sampling interval \(T_s=1/f_s\), evaluate a complex sinusoid at sample \(n\):

\[e^{i2\pi(f+kf_s)nT_s}=e^{i2\pi fnT_s}e^{i2\pi kn}=e^{i2\pi fnT_s},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.\]

A real cosine is also unchanged by reversal of frequency, so its apparent frequency can be folded into \(0\le f_a\le f_s/2\). At \(f_s=100\,\mathrm{Hz}\), a 92 Hz cosine gives precisely the samples of an 8 Hz cosine with reversed phase angle. If both are present, their sampled amplitudes combine according to phase. Removing 8 Hz in software would remove the true 8 Hz motion as well. Filter the continuous sensor signal before sampling if the higher component must be attenuated independently.

Suppose an encoder sees an unwanted sinusoidal displacement of amplitude \(0.50\,\mathrm{mm}\) at 92 Hz, while feedback must track motion through 8 Hz. A fourth-order analogue Butterworth low-pass filter with cutoff 20 Hz has magnitude \(|H(f)|=[1+(f/20\,\mathrm{Hz})^8]^{-1/2}\). At 92 Hz the ratio is 0.00223, or approximately \(-53.0\,\mathrm{dB}\): the folded displacement amplitude is only \(1.12\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). At 8 Hz the ratio is 0.99967, so a \(0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) desired component retains about \(99.97\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). These are separate amplitude tests on the analogue filter. If both vibrations persist, the sampled 8 Hz component can vary with their relative phase.

A passband magnitude close to one does not bound the filter's phase lag. A controller designed with substantial gain near 8 Hz must also include the filter's complex frequency response in its phase-margin calculation. Aliasing, sample-and-hold delay and computational delay are three different contributions to the observed loop dynamics.

Impulse response and finite-duration loading

Machine dynamics

Construct the response to a short pulse and determine when an impulse approximation preserves the peak motion.

From first principles

Build a transient response by accumulating elementary impulses

An impulse \(J\) is the time integral of applied force. Across an ideal instantaneous impulse the displacement of mass \(m\) stays continuous, while integrating Newton's law gives a velocity jump \(J/m\). Finite spring and viscous forces have vanishing impulse over that ideal interval. For an initially stationary undamped mount with stiffness \(k\), the following motion is free vibration with angular frequency \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and the new initial velocity.

\[x(0^+)=0,\]
\[\dot x(0^+)=J/m,\]
\[x(t)=\frac{J}{m\omega_n}\sin(\omega_nt).\]

Now divide a finite force trace into short intervals. The interval starting at \(s\) contributes impulse \(F(s)\,ds\). For a linear time-invariant system its later displacement is the impulse solution delayed by \(s\) and scaled by that impulse. Summing over all earlier intervals yields

\[x(t)=\frac{1}{m\omega_n}\int_0^t F(s)\sin[\omega_n(t-s)]\,ds .\]

An existing initial displacement or velocity contributes a separate free response by superposition. If the pulse is very short relative to the vibration period, the sine factor changes little during it and the area of the pulse controls the subsequent motion.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the residual vibration after a ramped force is removed

The time integral of a force sets momentum change only when the support forces have little time to act. For longer pulses a linear undamped oscillator retains information about the shape and timing of the load. Consider mass \(m\), stiffness \(k\), natural frequency \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\) and zero initial motion. For a force acting only from zero to \(T\), the post-pulse displacement follows the convolution with the impulse response.

\[x(t)=\frac{1}{m\omega}\int_0^T F(s)\sin[\omega(t-s)]ds,\]
\[t\geq T.\]

Expand the sine of the difference into sine and cosine of \(t\). The two integrals multiply quadrature components of free vibration. Their resultant magnitude is the modulus of a single Fourier integral at the natural frequency. For a symmetric triangular pulse centred at \(T/2\), its area is \(J=F_pT/2\) and its transform factorises because the triangle is a convolution of two equal rectangles of duration \(T/2\).

\[A=\frac{1}{m\omega}\left|\int_0^T F(s)e^{-i\omega s}ds\right|,\]
\[\int_0^T F(s)e^{-i\omega s}ds=J e^{-i\omega T/2}\left[\frac{\sin(\omega T/4)}{\omega T/4}\right]^2.\]

Each rectangle contributes the average complex phase over its duration; the product of these averages produces the squared factor. The impulse estimate is recovered for a very short triangle, while certain longer pulses leave no residual motion in the ideal model.

\[\frac{A}{J/(m\omega)}=\left[\frac{\sin(\omega T/4)}{\omega T/4}\right]^2,\]
\[T_{\rm first\ zero}=\frac{4\pi}{\omega}.\]

Worked example

Compare a triangular pulse with its impulse limit

For \(m=2.00\) kg and \(\omega=20.0\) rad/s choose \(T=\pi/10\) s and \(F_p=4.00\) N. Pulse area is 0.628 N s and the impulse estimate is 15.7 mm. Here \(\omega T/4=\pi/2\) and the squared factor is \(4/\pi^2=0.405\). Actual residual amplitude is 6.37 mm. The cancellation occurs because different increments of force reach different phases of the oscillator.

Second application

Design a pulse with zero residual motion

Another lightly damped test rig is idealised as undamped at 5.00 Hz. The first zero requires a symmetric triangular pulse lasting two periods, \(T=0.400\) s. To deliver impulse 0.300 N s, choose \(F_p=2J/T=1.50\) N. The mass can move during the pulse, but its final displacement and velocity are both zero in the ideal model. A measured ring-down identifies sensitivity to frequency error, damping or a distorted force shape.

A short event and a long response

A latch strikes a mounted instrument for only a few milliseconds, but its motion persists after contact has ended. Peak force alone gives an incomplete description of that motion. Duration, impulse and the system’s natural time scale all matter. Define impulse \(J=\int F(t)\,dt\), with units N·s. Integrating \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F\) over an ideal instantaneous event leaves \(m[\dot x(0^+)-\dot x(0^-)]=J\). The position remains continuous since no finite mass can jump a finite distance in zero time under a finite impulse.

For an initially resting underdamped oscillator, the subsequent response follows from the two initial conditions \(x(0^+)=0\) and \(\dot x(0^+)=J/m\). With \(\alpha=c/(2m)\) and \(\omega_d=\sqrt{k/m-\alpha^2}\), the displacement is \(x=Je^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega_dt)/(m\omega_d)\) for \(t>0\). The response to a unit impulse is the same expression with \(J=1\). Its units are displacement per unit impulse.

Build a solution from elementary impulses

Divide a general forcing record into short intervals at times \(s\). Each interval contributes impulse \(F(s)\,ds\) and creates a response delayed by \(s\). Adding all contributions in a linear time-invariant system with zero initial state produces the convolution

\[x(t)=\int_0^t F(s)\,\frac{e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\sin[\omega_d(t-s)]}{m\omega_d}\,ds.\]

This construction makes causality explicit: later parts of the force record cannot influence the current response. Nonzero initial conditions add the free solution. In a multi-mode system each mode has its own impulse response, and the load’s location determines the force projection into it. Replacing the structure by its first mode may describe long-wave displacement well while missing a short, local acceleration peak.

Work a pulse of known duration

Neglect damping and apply constant force \(F_0\) for \(0<t<t_p\). During the pulse integration gives \(x=(F_0/k)[1-\cos(\omega_n t)]\) and \(\dot x=F_0\omega_n\sin(\omega_n t)/k\). After unloading the mass oscillates freely. Compute its amplitude from the end displacement and velocity:

\[A=\sqrt{x(t_p)^2+\big[\dot x(t_p)/\omega_n\big]^2}=\frac{2F_0}{k}\left|\sin(\omega_n t_p/2)\right|.\]

Let \(m=4.0\) kg, \(k=1600\) N/m, \(F_0=20\) N and \(t_p=0.050\) s. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and the impulse is 1.0 N·s. The formula predicts \(A=25\sin(0.50)=12.0\) mm after unloading. Replacing the whole pulse by an instantaneous impulse gives \(A_{\rm imp}=J/(m\omega_n)=12.5\) mm. Some restoring force developed during the real pulse, so the impulse approximation is slightly high.

Find the criterion and its limitations

Divide the finite-pulse amplitude by the impulse approximation to obtain \(|\sin z/z|\) with \(z=\omega_nt_p/2\). Its small-\(z\) expansion is \(1-z^2/6+\cdots\). At fixed impulse, a sufficiently short pulse therefore gives about the same low-mode amplitude regardless of duration. A shorter event has a broader frequency content, potentially increasing the importance of higher modes.

A pulse lasting exactly one natural period gives zero residual amplitude in the ideal undamped oscillator, although the displacement during the pulse peaks at \(2F_0/k\). Checking only the post-event motion would miss that maximum. In experiments record the force and response during contact as well as after it. Damping, uncertain pulse shape or nearby modes make exact cancellation unlikely, but the example shows why pulse duration must accompany a quoted peak force.

Compare pulses of equal impulse

A force transducer may report identical total impulse for a sharp tap and a broader pulse. For an undamped mode, the amplitude after the force has ended is the modulus of its frequency-weighted impulse divided by \(m\omega_n\): \(A=|\int F(t)e^{-i\omega_nt}dt|/(m\omega_n)\). This follows by expanding the sine kernel of the convolution into two quadratures. For a rectangular pulse the ratio to the ideal instantaneous-impulse amplitude is \(|\sin(\omega_nt_p/2)/(\omega_nt_p/2)|\). At a duration equal to half the natural period that factor is \(2/\pi=0.637\). Two force records with equal signed area can therefore have visibly different post-event amplitudes at one sensor if their durations differ. A short force pulse can consequently calibrate a low-frequency mode while underexciting a faster one in the same structure.

Derive an energy-consistent time update for a force record

A measured force history may be irregular enough that a closed-form convolution is inconvenient. The constant-average-acceleration Newmark update advances a linear oscillator one time step at a time. Let \(x_n,v_n,a_n\) be displacement, velocity and acceleration at \(t_n\). Approximate the acceleration over the step through its endpoint average in the velocity update, and use the corresponding trapezoidal velocity update for displacement. With step \(h\) the relations are

\[v_{n+1}=v_n+\tfrac h2(a_n+a_{n+1}),\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n+\tfrac{h^2}4(a_n+a_{n+1}).\]

Enforce the actual equation \(ma_{n+1}+cv_{n+1}+kx_{n+1}=F_{n+1}\) at the new time. Gathering unknown acceleration terms gives effective mass \(m+ch/2+kh^2/4\). The right side is \(F_{n+1}-c(v_n+ha_n/2)-k(x_n+hv_n+h^2a_n/4)\). Solve that scalar equation and back-substitute into both kinematic updates. For several coupled linear coordinates the same derivation gives a linear system with an effective matrix.

For undamped unforced systems, multiply the average endpoint equilibrium by \(x_{n+1}-x_n=h(v_{n+1}+v_n)/2\). Differences of squares show that the discrete change in kinetic energy plus potential energy vanishes exactly. This property detects sign or unit errors in an implementation. It does not guarantee accurate phase at a coarse time step.

Check one step and distinguish stability from temporal resolution

Take \(m=2\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(c=0\), \(k=200\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Begin from \(x_0=0.010\,\mathrm m,\ v_0=0\) and release without applied force. Initial acceleration is \(-kx_0/m=-1.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). With \(h=0.10\,\mathrm s\), effective mass is \(2+200(0.10)^2/4=2.50\,\mathrm{kg}\). Its right side is \(-200[0.010+0.10^2(-1)/4]=-1.50\,\mathrm N\), giving \(a_1=-0.600\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). The updates give \(v_1=-0.0800\,\mathrm{m/s}, x_1=0.00600\,\mathrm m\).

The initial energy is \(200(0.010)^2/2=0.0100\,\mathrm J\); after one step it is \(2(0.080)^2/2+200(0.006)^2/2=0.0100\,\mathrm J\). The exact displacement at 0.10 s is nevertheless \(0.010\cos(1)=0.00540\,\mathrm m\). The numerical oscillator advances through angle \(2\arctan(\omega_nh/2)=0.9273\) rad per step instead of 1.00 rad. Over many cycles this small period error accumulates. Halving the step is a direct temporal convergence test. For a measured force pulse, a second check is that its sampled impulse and timing converge as well; an accurate oscillator update cannot recover a force feature missed by the acquisition system.

It is possible to predict the amount of step refinement needed for a phase-sensitive comparison. For small \(z=\omega_n h/2\), expand \(2\arctan z=2z-2z^3/3+\cdots\). Dividing the numerical angular advance by the true advance gives \(1-(\omega_n h)^2/12+\cdots\). The leading relative frequency error therefore falls by a factor of four when the step is halved. A one-percent target suggests \(\omega_nh\lesssim\sqrt{0.12}=0.346\) for this single undamped mode. At 10 rad/s that means roughly 0.035 s or less, before checking the sampling of the force itself. This criterion addresses the fundamental frequency, not the fastest contributing mode in a multi-degree model. If a high-frequency mode contributes significantly at the measurement station, use its frequency to assess temporal phase resolution or test convergence of the complete measured signal directly.

Once these conditions are met, comparing the computed displacement and velocity at the end of loading also tests the predicted initial condition for the subsequent free ring-down.

Finite-amplitude oscillations and model validity

Machine dynamics

Use conservation of energy and a frequency approximation to quantify when a small-angle pendulum model loses timing accuracy.

From first principles

Derive the pendulum period and identify the small-angle assumption

A point mass \(m\) suspended on a massless rigid link of length \(\ell\) feels a gravitational torque \(-mg\ell\sin\theta\) about the hinge. Assume a stationary pivot and negligible drag. Angular momentum balance gives an exact nonlinear equation. Replacing \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) only for small angles in radians gives a linear oscillator and its predicted period.

\[m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta,\]
\[T_{\rm small}=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}.\]

To track finite amplitude, release from rest at angle \(A<\pi\) and use mechanical energy conservation. At any intermediate angle, loss of potential energy equals gained rotational kinetic energy.

\[\tfrac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2=mg\ell(\cos\theta-\cos A),\]
\[T=4\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2g}}\int_0^A\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos A}} .\]

The factor four uses the symmetry of the four quarter cycles. The mass cancels even at large amplitude. The exact restoring torque grows more slowly with deflection than its small-angle approximation, so finite-amplitude swings take longer. Measured variation of period with release angle directly tests the linearisation.

Derivation and applications

Derive the period of an oscillator with purely cubic stiffness

A spring with almost no linear stiffness near equilibrium can have a period set primarily by release amplitude. Consider mass \(m\) on a horizontal guide with restoring force \(-\beta x^3\) for \(\beta>0\). Ignore damping and assume the fitted force law holds between \(-A\) and \(+A\). Release from rest at \(x=A\). Integrating the force law gives a quartic potential, and conservation of energy provides the speed anywhere on the inward leg.

\[\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac14\beta x^4=\tfrac14\beta A^4,\]
\[|\dot x|=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2m}}\sqrt{A^4-x^4}.\]

By symmetry the time from an extreme position to the centre is one quarter of a full cycle. Write \(dt=|dx|/|\dot x|\) and substitute \(u=x/A\) in the integral. This isolates the amplitude dependence without guessing a sinusoidal shape.

\[T=\frac{4}{A}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\beta}}\int_0^1\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^4}}.\]

The dimensionless integral evaluates numerically to 1.31103. Its endpoint singularity is integrable because the speed near the turning point vanishes as the square root of the distance remaining. The resulting period and angular frequency are

\[T=\frac{7.4163}{A}\sqrt{\frac{m}{\beta}},\]
\[\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}=0.8472\,A\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{m}} .\]

The positive cubic restores the mass for displacements of either sign, but its tangent stiffness at zero is zero. The full nonlinear dynamics therefore predicts a period diverging with decreasing amplitude. Even a weak residual linear spring would eventually dominate in that limit.

Worked example

Predict the timing of two releases

Take \(m=0.500\) kg, \(\beta=2.00\times10^5\) N/m³ and \(A=20.0\) mm. The predicted period is \(7.4163\sqrt{0.500/(2.00\times10^5)}/0.0200=0.586\) s. Reducing the release to 10.0 mm doubles the predicted period to 1.17 s. At the larger amplitude the centre-crossing speed is \(A^2\sqrt{\beta/(2m)}=0.179\) m/s.

Second application

Infer stiffness from a measured period

A second fixture of mass 0.800 kg completes a cycle in 0.750 s after release at 15.0 mm. The implied coefficient is \(\beta=m[7.4163/(AT)]^2=3.48\times10^5\) N/m³. Its predicted restoring-force magnitude at release is \(\beta A^3=1.17\) N. An independent static force measurement there tests the dynamic estimate without assuming a sinusoidal motion.

Retain the actual restoring moment

A pendulum gives a practical test of a linear model because its length and release angle are easy to set while its period can be measured over many cycles. Approximate the moving body first by a point mass \(m\) at distance \(l\) from a frictionless pivot. With \(\theta\) measured from vertically downwards, gravity produces torque \(-mgl\sin\theta\). Dividing the rotational balance by \(ml^2\) leaves \(\ddot\theta+(g/l)\sin\theta=0\). The linear small-angle frequency \(\omega_0=\sqrt{g/l}\) follows only after replacing \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) in radians.

The leading correction has a definite sign: \(\sin\theta\simeq\theta-\theta^3/6\). At a large positive angle the restoring force magnitude is smaller than the linear extrapolation. The pendulum should take longer to complete its swing. A sign test like this is useful before calculating a period correction.

Derive an integral valid at finite amplitude

For release from rest at amplitude \(A<\pi\), conservation of mechanical energy gives \(\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl(1-\cos\theta)=mgl(1-\cos A)\). During one quarter of the orbit the angle travels between zero and \(A\). Substitute \(\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(A/2)\sin\psi\) into the time integral. Cancelling common square roots gives

\[T=4\sqrt{l/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\psi}},\qquad k=\sin(A/2).\]

For modest amplitudes expand the integrand as \(1+\tfrac12 k^2\sin^2\psi+\cdots\). The average of \(\sin^2\psi\) over the quarter interval is one half. Therefore \(T/T_0\simeq1+k^2/4\simeq1+A^2/16\) through second order in amplitude. The oscillation period depends on energy, while in the linear spring model all amplitudes share the same period.

Estimate a measurable timing error

Take \(l=0.80\,\mathrm m\), \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) and release at \(A=30^\circ=0.5236\,\mathrm{rad}\). The small-angle period is \(T_0=2\pi\sqrt{0.80/9.81}=1.794\,\mathrm s\). The leading correction is 1.71%, giving about 1.825 s. Over ten full oscillations the predicted delay relative to the small-angle model is roughly 0.31 s. Reducing the release angle to 15° reduces the leading relative correction by a factor of four.

A timing series from a damped experimental pendulum may show the period drifting towards \(T_0\) as its amplitude decays. Measuring only the first release angle while averaging many later periods mixes different amplitudes. To test the finite-amplitude law, use consecutive same-direction crossings to measure each local period and estimate its associated amplitude from neighbouring turning points. The correction above is an expansion around zero amplitude. As \(A\) approaches the inverted equilibrium, the exact integral grows without bound and a low-order polynomial is no longer an adequate approximation.

Check the scale of the neglected term

Retaining one further term in the integrand gives \(1+\tfrac12 k^2\sin^2\psi+\tfrac38 k^4\sin^4\psi\). The quarter-interval average of the fourth power of sine is \(3/8\), so \(T/T_0\simeq1+k^2/4+9k^4/64\). At 30° this gives 1.01738, while the leading expansion in amplitude gave 1.01713. The small difference is measurable only if the uncertainty in pendulum length, release angle and crossing times supports the comparison.

Derive an amplitude-dependent resonance relation

A pendulum softens as its amplitude grows because the sine restoring law rises more slowly than its linear tangent. A mechanical flexure can instead harden through geometry. A simple modal model is \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx+\beta x^3=F\cos\omega t\). For positive \(\beta\) the restoring stiffness increases with excursion. Assume one dominant harmonic and take \(x\approx A\cos(\omega t-\phi)\). Expanding the cubic gives \(x^3=A^3[3\cos(\omega t-\phi)+\cos(3\omega t-3\phi)]/4\). Keeping only the fundamental turns the cubic term into an amplitude-dependent in-phase force.

Balance in-phase and quadrature components against the input, square and add to eliminate \(\phi\). The first-harmonic amplitude estimate satisfies

\[F^2=A^2\left[\left(k+\tfrac34\beta A^2-m\omega^2\right)^2+(c\omega)^2\right].\]

In a lightly damped free motion the same approximation gives a backbone frequency \(\omega_b^2\approx[k+(3/4)\beta A^2]/m\). This is an amplitude-dependent estimate of conservative oscillation frequency, not a new eigenvalue of the linearised equilibrium. At very small amplitude it recovers \(\sqrt{k/m}\), while a negative cubic coefficient tilts the relation towards lower frequencies. If the cubic approximation is only a local expansion, do not extrapolate it until it predicts an unphysical loss of global stiffness.

Identify a hardening spring from two release amplitudes

A test component has moving mass 1.0 kg and small-amplitude stiffness 400 N/m. Low-level ring-down gives \(\omega_0=20\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). At measured oscillation amplitude 20 mm the dominant free frequency is approximately 22 rad/s. Under the weakly nonlinear one-harmonic model, \(\beta\simeq4m(22^2-20^2)/[3(0.020)^2]=2.80\times10^5\,\mathrm{N/m^3}\). At 10 mm the inferred backbone is \(\sqrt{400+0.75(2.8\times10^5)(0.010)^2}=20.52\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), a direct third reading against which to test the fit.

Now drive near 22 rad/s with viscous coefficient \(c=0.80\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). At the 20 mm backbone amplitude the spring and inertia fundamentals cancel, so maintaining this motion takes forcing \(F=c\omega A=0.352\,\mathrm N\) in quadrature with displacement. The neglected cubic third-harmonic force amplitude is \(\beta A^3/4=0.56\,\mathrm N\); its displacement effect can nevertheless be small because the oscillator is driven far from its fundamental resonance at 66 rad/s. A separate mode near that frequency would invalidate this simplification. In a forced frequency sweep the amplitude equation can admit several positive roots near resonance. Its algebra does not establish their stability; slow upward and downward sweeps, each with sufficient settling time, can reveal different stable responses and jump transitions.

An initial static measurement tests the sign of the fitted coefficient independently. At a displacement of 20 mm the linear restoring force is \(400(0.020)=8.0\,\mathrm N\); the cubic contribution is \(2.8\times10^5(0.020)^3=2.24\,\mathrm N\). The predicted static force is therefore 10.24 N, and the tangent stiffness is \(400+3(2.8\times10^5)(0.020)^2=736\,\mathrm{N/m}\). This tangent stiffness applies to small vibrations around a held biased equilibrium. Using it directly for an oscillation centred at zero would overestimate the measured amplitude-dependent frequency because the instantaneous stiffness changes during each cycle. Static bias and free oscillation amplitude are physically different tests of the same nonlinear restoring law.

Repeating a decay test around a deliberately shifted equilibrium distinguishes the two experimentally.

Set an amplitude limit for a pendulum-based timing model

How a magnetic field relaxes into a finite conductor

Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

A conducting sheet initially shields its interior from an applied magnetic step. Given time, a normal resistive metal admits a static tangential magnetic field. The same equations give a finite slab a discrete relaxation spectrum and a periodic drive a complex penetration depth. These are different initial and boundary conditions for one diffusion operator.

Identify the time derivative lost in the approximation

Let the conductor be stationary, Ohmic, uniform and isotropic with conductivity \(\sigma\), permeability \(\mu\) and permittivity \(\epsilon\). In the full Maxwell equations both \(\sigma\boldsymbol E\) and \(\epsilon\partial_t\boldsymbol E\) contribute to the curl of \(\boldsymbol H\). At a characteristic angular frequency \(\omega\), the ratio of the latter to the former is \(\omega\epsilon/\sigma\). For a good conductor at low frequencies this is very small. Neglect displacement current only in that constitutive region; a vacuum gap or a capacitor elsewhere can still carry significant displacement current. Ampère then gives \(\boldsymbol E=(\nabla\times\boldsymbol B)/(\mu\sigma)\). Substitution into Faraday and use of \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0\) yield \(\partial_t\boldsymbol B=D\nabla^2\boldsymbol B\), \(D=1/(\mu\sigma)\).

The diffusion time for scale \(a\) is of order \(a^2/D\). Increasing conductivity reduces \(D\), because smaller electric fields can sustain stronger opposing currents. In an ideal mathematical conductor with \(\sigma\to\infty\), an initial flux can be frozen in this model. That is a different preparation from a superconductor expelling a field in equilibrium. Magnetic diffusion alone contains neither a thermodynamic superconducting transition nor London screening. At sufficiently short times the dropped displacement current also matters: an ideal step is an approximation to a finite-risetime drive, and the diffusion equation's nonzero Gaussian tails at arbitrarily large distances should not be used to infer a superluminal signal.

Obtain a transient similarity profile

Take a half-space \(x>0\) with field \(\boldsymbol B=B_z(x,t)\hat{\boldsymbol z}\); the surface is driven to \(B_s\) at \(t=0^+\), while initially the interior has zero field. Scaling \(x\) by \(\sqrt{Dt}\) suggests \(B_z/B_s=F(\eta)\), \(\eta=x/(2\sqrt{Dt})\). Compute \(\partial_tF=-\eta F'/(2t)\) and \(D\partial_x^2F=F''/(4t)\); hence \(F''+2\eta F'=0\). Its first integral is \(F'=Ce^{-\eta^2}\). Set \(F(0)=1,F(\infty)=0\) and use the Gaussian integral to obtain \(F(\eta)=\operatorname{erfc}\eta\). The associated current \(J_y=-(1/\mu)\partial_xB_z\) is positive for this sign of surface field and has a Gaussian depth profile.

\[J_y(x,t)=\frac{B_s}{\mu\sqrt{\pi Dt}}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)},\]
\[\int_0^\infty J_y\,dx=\frac{B_s}{\mu}.\]

The current integral remains fixed as the profile spreads, even though the surface current density falls as \(t^{-1/2}\). A finite conductivity does not imply uniform current at every instant. For \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\,\mathrm{S/m}\) and \(\mu=\mu_0\), \(D=0.01372\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). The point reaching half the surface field is \(x=2\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(1/2)\sqrt{Dt}=0.9539\sqrt{Dt}\). At \(0.10\,\mathrm{ms}\) it is \(1.12\,\mathrm{mm}\) deep. At \(0.40\,\mathrm{ms}\), four times the time, it is twice as deep. Measuring several half-amplitude positions against time lets one fit \(D\) without calibrating the absolute magnetic field strength.

Satisfy both faces of a finite slab

Let the conductor occupy \(-a<x<a\). Impose the same sudden tangential field \(B_s\) at both faces. At long time the unique steady solution with these equal boundaries is uniform \(B_s\). Subtract this final value by defining the deficit \(u=1-B_z/B_s\). It satisfies homogeneous boundary values \(u(\pm a,t)=0\) and begins at one inside. Even symmetry permits cosine modes \(\cos(k_mx)\) with \(k_ma=(m+1/2)\pi\). Separating time gives the multiplier \(e^{-Dk_m^2t}\). Orthogonality on \((-a,a)\) gives squared norm \(a\) for each cosine and projection of the constant equal to \(2\sin(k_ma)/(ak_m)\). Thus

\[\frac{B_z(x,t)}{B_s}=1-\frac4\pi\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{2m+1}\cos\!\frac{(2m+1)\pi x}{2a}\,\exp\!\left[-(2m+1)^2t/\tau_0\right],\]
\[\tau_0=\frac{4a^2}{\pi^2D}.\]

The initial boundary jump is real in the idealisation; the series reproduces the interior initial value in the usual Fourier sense, while the imposed boundary value applies for \(t>0\). Later times damp the higher spatial harmonics most rapidly. At the centre and \(t=\tau_0\), the first term predicts \(B/B_s=1-(4/\pi)e^{-1}=0.5316\). The next correction is \(4e^{-9}/(3\pi)=0.0000524\), so this is already accurate to within \(6\times10^{-5}\). At very early times many terms are needed because their cancellations encode a nearly field-free interior. A half-space similarity expression near each face can be more efficient there.

For a \(4.0\,\mathrm{mm}\)-thick copper slab \(a=2.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), giving \(\tau_0=0.118\,\mathrm{ms}\). At late times the centre deficit \(1-B/B_s\) approaches a single exponential with log slope \(-1/\tau_0\). Reaching 90 percent at the centre requires \(t\simeq\tau_0\ln(4/(0.1\pi))=2.54\tau_0=0.300\,\mathrm{ms}\). The initial square-root penetration law cannot simply be extrapolated into this final regime, since the penetration fronts from both sides interact. Doubling full thickness at constant material quadruples the long relaxation time.

Compare a periodic solution and a surface impedance

Instead drive the half-space sinusoidally and adopt \(e^{i\omega t}\) peak phasors. Solving \(i\omega\widetilde B=D\,d^2\widetilde B/dx^2\) gives \(\widetilde B=B_se^{-(1+i)x/\delta}\) with the root \(\delta=\sqrt{2D/\omega}\) that decays towards infinity. The envelope falls as \(e^{-x/\delta}\), while phase lags the boundary by \(x/\delta\). The electrical surface field follows by differentiating, yielding \(\widetilde E_y=(1+i)\widetilde H_z/(\sigma\delta)\). The surface impedance in these orientations is \(Z_s=(1+i)/(\sigma\delta)\). Its positive real part measures dissipation, and the equal quadrature term records energy storage with this convention.

At \(f=1.0\,\mathrm{kHz}\) in the copper model, \(\delta=2.09\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(\operatorname{Re}Z_s=8.25\,\mu\Omega\). A tangential surface peak field \(H_s=1000\,\mathrm{A/m}\) gives mean incoming power density \(\operatorname{Re}Z_s|H_s|^2/2=4.13\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\). The same number follows from volume Joule heating. Integrate \(\sigma|\widetilde E_y|^2/2\) over depth; the squared envelope \(e^{-2x/\delta}\) integrates to \(\delta/2\). An experiment monitoring just the in-phase field can find a zero at \(x=\pi\delta/2\) while the full harmonic magnitude is still \(e^{-\pi/2}=0.208\) of the surface amplitude.

Compare a half-space step and a sinusoidal depth profile using the same material

Use the solution to design a convergence test

A mesh across the finite slab can step \(B_j^{n+1}=B_j^n+r(B_{j+1}^n-2B_j^n+B_{j-1}^n)\), with \(r=D\Delta t/h^2\). A Fourier mode \(e^{ikjh}\) is multiplied by \(1-4r\sin^2(kh/2)\), so forward Euler is stable for \(0\le r\le1/2\). Above that bound a high-frequency grid mode can grow despite the physical diffusion being dissipative. For \(h=0.20\,\mathrm{mm}\) in the copper model, \(\Delta t\le1.46\,\mu\mathrm s\). Halving the mesh spacing demands quartering this explicit time step. An implicit scheme can overcome the same stability restriction but still needs small enough steps to resolve the measured transient.

Set both face values, initialise the interior once and record the centre at \(t=\tau_0\). Compare first against the series, then against a second mesh and time step. A mistaken use of full thickness in \(\tau_0\) would shift the inferred time by a factor four even if the code has converged perfectly. Independent checks include reflection symmetry about the slab centre, zero gradient at that centre, a bounded response between zero and \(B_s\) for the continuous problem, and ohmic heating that is nonnegative at every point. The latter can catch sign mistakes in an energy budget even though squaring \(J\) hides the sign of a local measured current.

Compare surface and centre in a periodically driven finite slab

At a fixed angular frequency a finite slab with equal tangential field on both faces has a different profile from a half-space. Write the phasor equation \(d^2\widetilde B/dx^2=k^2\widetilde B\), \(k=(1+i)/\delta\) for temporal dependence \(e^{i\omega t}\), and impose \(\widetilde B(\pm a)=B_s\). Even symmetry eliminates \(\sinh kx\), giving \(\widetilde B(x)=B_s\cosh(kx)/\cosh(ka)\). The centre amplitude is \(1/|\cosh[(1+i)a/\delta]|\) of the applied field, and its phase lag is the argument of that denominator. Both incoming penetration profiles must be combined as complex amplitudes satisfying boundary conditions; adding two decaying real envelopes is not a valid phasor solution.

The low-frequency limit has \(ka\ll1\), yielding \(B(0)/B_s\simeq1-(ka)^2/2=1-i(a/\delta)^2\). The leading correction is a phase lag, while the magnitude change starts at higher order. Thus a sensitive phase detector can pick up eddy-current effects before a large change of amplitude is visible. At \(a/\delta=1\), \(\cosh(1+i)=\cosh1\cos1+i\sinh1\sin1\simeq0.834+0.989i\), giving centre amplitude 0.773 and lag \(0.870\) radians. An isolated half-space viewed one skin depth in from a single face would have amplitude 0.368 and lag 1.00 radian. These are genuinely different field boundary conditions, not two definitions of skin depth.

The phasor of the current varies as \(\sinh(kx)\) and is odd. Current on one half of the slab runs opposite to that on the other; the net integrated current across both halves vanishes for equal exterior fields. That cancellation does not suppress losses, because local Joule heating is proportional to \(|J|^2\). In the thin-slab limit expand Faraday directly. If \(B_z(t)\) is nearly uniform, \(\partial_x E_y=-\dot B_z\). Symmetry about the centre gives \(E_y\simeq-x\dot B_z\). For a sinusoidal peak field \(B_s\), the mean volumetric loss averaged across thickness is \(\sigma\omega^2 B_s^2 a^2/6\). The \(a^2\) scaling at fixed total magnetic volume explains why dividing a core into electrically insulated thin laminations can reduce classical eddy losses when the applied field lies along the lamination faces. The thin-slab approximation, isolation between laminae and field orientation are essential parts of that conclusion.

For a material with \(\sigma=2.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{S/m}\), half-thickness \(0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\), sinusoidal peak field 0.10 T and frequency 50 Hz, the formula gives \(3.29\,\mathrm{W/m^3}\). If effective permeability were \(100\mu_0\), the skin depth at that frequency would be about \(5.03\,\mathrm{mm}\), so the thin-slab ordering is internally consistent for these model numbers. Hysteresis or anomalous dynamic losses can still dominate a measured sample. Halving full lamination thickness at fixed material volume should quarter only this classical contribution, not every loss mechanism in the test fixture.

A thin conducting cylindrical shell gives a single time constant

A different finite system exposes how geometry changes a diffusion transient. Consider a long cylindrical shell of radius \(a\) and wall thickness \(w\ll a\) surrounding vacuum. Assume the skin depth in the wall exceeds \(w\) on the important time scales, so current is approximately uniform across the wall, and the solenoid producing an approximately uniform external axial field is sufficiently long. An azimuthal sheet current \(K_\varphi\) produces an additional axial field inside and negligible external field in the long-cylinder approximation. The boundary condition is \(B_{\rm in}=B_{\rm ext}+\mu_0K_\varphi\). Faraday's law around the shell gives \(2\pi a E_\varphi=-\pi a^2\dot B_{\rm in}\). The sheet obeys \(K_\varphi=\sigma w E_\varphi\). Combining the three relations gives

\[B_{\rm in}+\tau_s\dot B_{\rm in}=B_{\rm ext},\]
\[\tau_s=\mu_0\sigma w a/2 .\]

For a step from zero to \(B_s\) the field inside grows as \(B_s(1-e^{-t/\tau_s})\). For a sinusoid the interior phasor transfer is \(1/(1+i\omega\tau_s)\). This model has a single time constant instead of a slab's infinite relaxation spectrum because the wall thickness has been collapsed into one azimuthal sheet-current degree of freedom; magnetic energy is stored mainly throughout the bore. Take copper with \(a=0.030\,\mathrm m,w=0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) to obtain \(\tau_s=0.109\,\mathrm{ms}\). At its half-power frequency \(f=1/(2\pi\tau_s)\simeq1.46\,\mathrm{kHz}\), the copper skin depth is about 1.73 mm, comfortably larger than the specified wall. At 100 times this frequency the uniform-wall assumption is much weaker.

This result also suggests a discriminating experiment. Compare shells made of the same material with fixed wall thickness and different bore radius. The leading screening time grows linearly with radius. Compare solid sheets of different normal penetration thickness in the earlier slab experiment and their time grows with thickness squared. Either system can display a late exponential, so an observed straight tail in a semilog trace does not alone reveal which diffusion path limits the response. The thickness and radius dependences, together with the phase response and the known current paths, provide independent evidence. A longitudinal insulating slit in the cylindrical shell interrupts its closed eddy-current path, substantially altering the ideal shielding despite leaving most of the metal present.

Account for every joule admitted by a magnetic step

A conducting half-space initially has zero internal field. At \(t=0\) a source clamps the surface induction to \(B_s\hat z\). Let \(x>0\) measure inward distance and \(D=1/(\mu\sigma)\). The previously derived field profile is \(B_z=B_s\,{\rm erfc}[x/(2\sqrt{Dt})]\). An observer sees an ever-deepening field front even though the surface induction subsequently remains constant. Someone must provide both the magnetic field energy and the Joule heat generated by the induced current. The appropriate bookkeeping is Poynting's theorem, with positive surface power defined into the conductor.

In this geometry Ampère's law gives \(J_y=-(1/\mu)\partial_x B_z\). Differentiating the error function produces a Gaussian current density \(J_y=B_s[\mu\sqrt{\pi Dt}]^{-1}\exp[-x^2/(4Dt)]\). Ohm's law sets \(E_y=J_y/\sigma\). At the surface the Poynting vector \(E_yH_z\hat x\) points into the material and has magnitude

\[\frac{P_{\rm in}}{A}=\frac{B_s^2}{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{D}{\pi t}}.\]

The initial mathematical step creates an integrable square-root singularity in the surface power. Holding \(B_s\) fixed still requires the source to deliver power for \(t>0\). Faraday's law relates the surface electric field to the changing flux below the surface. Its value falls with time because the depth derivative of the induction spreads and weakens. A physical source with a finite rise time smooths the initial singularity and sets the earliest time for which the ideal step is a useful approximation.

To calculate heat production, square the full depth-dependent current and integrate \(J_y^2/\sigma\) from zero to infinity. The squared Gaussian has exponent \(-x^2/(2Dt)\), and its integral on the half-line is \(\sqrt{\pi Dt/2}\). The result is \(P_{\rm heat}/A=(B_s^2/\mu)\sqrt{D/(2\pi t)}\). Consequently the heat fraction of instantaneous input is \(1/\sqrt{2}\), about \(70.7\%\). The rest, \(1-1/\sqrt{2}\), supplies an increasing store of magnetic field energy. Both powers decay as \(t^{-1/2}\); their integrals up to a finite time increase as \(\sqrt t\). These fractions belong to the step-driven semi-infinite problem. They do not describe steady sinusoidal loss or a finite specimen after the diffusion front traverses it.

With \(B_s=10\,{\rm mT}\), copper conductivity \(5.8\times10^7\,{\rm S/m}\), and permeability close to \(\mu_0\), the diffusion coefficient is \(0.0137\,{\rm m^2/s}\). At \(100\,\mu{\rm s}\), the incoming power is about \(526\,{\rm W/m^2}\). Joule heating accounts for \(372\,{\rm W/m^2}\), and field energy is increasing at about \(154\,{\rm W/m^2}\). Integration from the ideal step to this time gives \(2tP_{\rm in}(t)/A=0.105\,{\rm J/m^2}\), split into approximately \(0.0744\,{\rm J/m^2}\) of heat and \(0.0308\,{\rm J/m^2}\) of stored field energy. At that time \(2\sqrt{Dt}\) is about \(2.34\,{\rm mm}\). Applying these numbers to a foil much thinner than a millimetre would use the half-space solution beyond its range.

A finite slab of thickness \(d\) is driven from both faces by the same step. How much magnetic energy is eventually stored, and why must a decaying input replace the half-space power law?

Work through the solution

At long times, Ampère's law allows a uniform induction \(B_s\) with zero current and the prescribed values on both faces. Its stored magnetic energy is \(dB_s^2/(2\mu)\) per unit footprint, independent of conductivity. With \(d=5.0\,{\rm mm}\) and the parameters above this is about \(0.199\,{\rm J/m^2}\). At early times each face behaves approximately as an independent half-space. Once their diffusion profiles overlap, extended use of that approximation would predict unlimited energy accumulation in a finite volume with bounded field magnitude. Work through the solution

Integrate the inward surface Poynting power over all time. With symmetry about the midplane, Faraday's law on the upper half relates the magnitude of the surface electric field to \(d[\int_0^{d/2}B\,dx]/dt\). The surface magnetic field after the step is the constant \(B_s/\mu\). Including both faces gives total input energy per area \(B_s^2d/\mu\). Half remains stored, so half has been dissipated as heat, or about \(0.199\,{\rm J/m^2}\) in the example. The integrated fraction one half over the complete finite transient differs from the early-time half-space fraction. Posing an electromagnetic evolution problem

Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

A solver cannot evolve 'a pulse in a box' from geometry and a time step alone. It needs material laws, compatible initial fields, a charge-conserving source, and enough boundary data to specify what enters the domain. The same bookkeeping is required when interpreting a real transient measurement.

Separate the evolution equations from the constraints

In vacuum with prescribed sources the evolution system can be written \(\partial_t\boldsymbol B=-\nabla\times\boldsymbol E\) and \(\partial_t\boldsymbol E=c^2\nabla\times\boldsymbol B-\boldsymbol J/\epsilon_0\). The divergence conditions \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0\) and \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol E=\rho/\epsilon_0\) constrain the initial data. Differentiating the first in time gives zero from the divergence of a curl. Differentiating the second constraint residual gives \(-[\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol J+\partial_t\rho]/\epsilon_0\). Compatible charge evolution therefore preserves an initially satisfied Gauss constraint. An arbitrarily chosen charge movie and unrelated current movie can violate it before a single boundary reflection occurs.

A numerical solver based only on the two curl equations does not automatically repair bad initial data. In an initial periodic vacuum box a uniform charge density of one sign is inadmissible because the integral of a periodic divergence is zero. One can prescribe a neutralising background as part of a separately justified model, choose net-neutral initial sources, or choose open boundaries that permit net electric flux. Silently discarding the zero Fourier mode of the charge would solve a different problem. Similarly, preserving a constant divergence error exactly during time integration shows consistency of the discrete curls with the chosen derivative, not correctness of the initial physical state.

Construct a right-running pulse from two initial fields

In one-dimensional vacuum choose \(\boldsymbol E=E_y(x,t)\hat{\boldsymbol y},\ \boldsymbol B=B_z(x,t)\hat{\boldsymbol z}\). Then \(\partial_tE_y=-c^2\partial_x B_z,\ \partial_tB_z=-\partial_xE_y\). Define \(w_+=E_y+cB_z,\ w_-=E_y-cB_z\). Addition and subtraction give

\[\partial_tw_++c\partial_xw_+=0,\]
\[\partial_tw_- -c\partial_xw_-=0 .\]

Thus \(w_+\) travels towards \(+x\) and \(w_-\) towards \(-x\). The initial conditions \(E_y=E_0e^{-(x-x_0)^2/(2s^2)}\), \(B_z=E_y/c\) specify a right-running packet. Prescribing the same electric shape but \(B_z=0\) launches two oppositely directed electric pulses each of half the original height. Measuring only the initial electric waveform would fail to distinguish the two preparations. At \(E_0=60\,\mathrm{V/m}\), the peak magnetic field for the first preparation is \(0.200\,\mu\mathrm T\). An observer \(0.90\) m farther right sees its peak \(3.00\) ns later in vacuum.

The component energies provide another independent comparison. Initialising only \(E_y\) contains half as much total energy as initialising \(E_y\) and its matched \(B_z\). At late times when the two pulses no longer overlap, each half-amplitude pulse has one quarter of the energy of the full matched pulse; the sum is half, as required. In the overlap region it is better to use the characteristic combinations themselves. In these units \(u=\epsilon_0(w_+^2+w_-^2)/4\) and \(S_x=\epsilon_0c(w_+^2-w_-^2)/4\) for the transverse fields. Equal oppositely running amplitudes can store energy with zero net flux.

Specify only incoming characteristic information at an open end

In \(0<x<L\), \(w_+\) enters at the left end and \(w_-\) at the right. Values travelling out are determined by the interior solution. For a pure outgoing plane wave at the right boundary prescribe \(w_-=0\), equivalently \(E_y-cB_z=0\), while allowing \(w_+\) to exit. At a perfectly conducting fixed wall the tangential electric field vanishes, so there \(w_-=-w_+\), reflecting the electric amplitude with a sign reversal. A boundary with a real voltage-like reflection coefficient \(r\) gives \(w_-=r w_+\) in this one-dimensional equal-medium case. The time-integrated returned energy fraction is \(r^2\). Specifying both \(E_y\) and \(B_z\) independently at all times generally overdetermines a wave boundary and can amount to fixing both incident and reflected waves inconsistently.

Suppose \(L=1.20\,\mathrm m,\ x_0=0.30\,\mathrm m\). The centre of a right-running pulse reaches the conducting end at \(3.00\,\mathrm{ns}\). A detector at \(x=0.60\,\mathrm m\) first sees the incident centre after \(1.00\,\mathrm{ns}\), then the reflected centre after \((0.90+0.60)\mathrm m/c=5.00\,\mathrm{ns}\). The two electric traces have opposite signs, while their magnetic contributions at the wall add. Swapping the conducting end for a matched boundary removes the reflected peak within this exact one-dimensional model. A local planar absorbing condition on a curved three-dimensional boundary may still reflect oblique waves; the exact statement depended on one normal mode and its wave impedance.

Derive uniqueness from energy

Take two solutions with the same sources, initial data and perfectly conducting closed boundaries in vacuum. Their difference satisfies homogeneous Maxwell equations with zero initial fields and zero tangential electric field at the wall. Poynting's theorem for the difference says \(dU_\Delta/dt=-\oint(\boldsymbol E_\Delta\times\boldsymbol H_\Delta)\cdot\boldsymbol n\,dA=0\) because the flux normal to a conducting wall vanishes. Since \(U_\Delta\) is a sum of nonnegative squared fields and initially zero, it stays zero. Thus the interior fields agree. A passive outgoing boundary can only remove this difference energy, so the same conclusion holds given no incoming difference signal. This proof shows why specifying incoming boundary data and initial fields correctly is more fundamental than choosing a particular potential gauge.

The argument also supplies a stability target for computation. With no drive and a perfectly reflecting cavity the discrete total energy should remain bounded, and with an absorbing passive end the loss of field energy should match outgoing flux. An unphysical sign in the wall update can inject energy, producing a growing solution even when the interior scheme is stable. Fixing the numerical grid spacing alone cannot cure an active boundary condition. At a material interface Maxwell jump conditions must be applied to the appropriate tangential or normal fields, since setting all Cartesian components continuous would generally violate flux or polarisation physics.

Discretise space and time without destroying wave kinematics

For the one-dimensional system use \(E_j^n\) at integer time and integer position, with \(B_{j+1/2}^{n+1/2}\) at half steps in both. Integrating Faraday on a small rectangular loop gives \(B_{j+1/2}^{n+1/2}=B_{j+1/2}^{n-1/2}-(\Delta t/h)(E_{j+1}^{n}-E_j^n)\). Ampère then gives \(E_j^{n+1}=E_j^n-c^2(\Delta t/h)(B_{j+1/2}^{n+1/2}-B_{j-1/2}^{n+1/2})\) without current. For material sources include the appropriate time-centred current. The staggered layout places discrete curls where the updated component lives. In several dimensions a compatible divergence of the curl vanishes by cancellation of oriented face-edge contributions.

Substitution of waves \(e^{i(kjh-\omega n\Delta t)}\) with the matching half-step phases yields \(\sin(\omega\Delta t/2)=(c\Delta t/h)\sin(kh/2)\) on the positive-frequency branch. Stability on a one-dimensional uniform grid requires \(c\Delta t/h\le1\). For \(h=5.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), the upper vacuum time step is \(16.7\,\mathrm{ps}\). A cubic three-dimensional mesh imposes the more restrictive \(c\Delta t/h\le1/\sqrt3\), about \(9.63\,\mathrm{ps}\). In one dimension the equality case transports compatible resolved plane waves at the correct speed on this grid. Numerical dispersion generally returns at smaller Courant number or for diagonal propagation in multiple dimensions. Sample a pulse across enough sites that phase and dispersion errors lie below the intended time-delay uncertainty.

A careful initialisation also respects the half time step. Assign \(B^{1/2}\) from the analytic field at that shifted time or use a consistent half-step update. Assigning \(B^0\) directly where \(B^{1/2}\) is required can create a spurious left-running component in what should have been a one-way pulse. Examine \(w_-\) reconstructed at common space and time before attributing a tiny backward peak to the material. For a real calculation, refine both space and time, compare detector arrival times as well as peak amplitudes, and integrate energy flux over complete transit windows. Solver convergence, charge compatibility, and a pulse moving in the intended direction are independent tests.

Match a travelling pulse at a material boundary

A single homogeneous characteristic impedance was built into the one-dimensional end conditions. Replace the boundary by an interface between lossless homogeneous nondispersive media with positive real impedances \(Z_1,Z_2\). At normal incidence from the left, write electric amplitudes \(E_i,E_r,E_t\) and choose the magnetic sign so a right-running wave has \(H=E/Z\) and a left-running one \(H=-E/Z\). With no free sheet current and no singular electric field at the interface, tangential continuity gives \(E_i+E_r=E_t\) and \((E_i-E_r)/Z_1=E_t/Z_2\). Solving these two equations yields

\[r=\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1},\]
\[t=\frac{2Z_2}{Z_2+Z_1},\]
\[\mathcal R=|r|^2,\]
\[\mathcal T=\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}|t|^2 .\]

The impedance factor in transmitted power is required even when electric continuity makes \(t=1+r\). With \(Z_1=300\,\Omega,Z_2=100\,\Omega\), the electric reflection is \(-1/2\) and transmission \(+1/2\). The reflected energy is one quarter, while transmitted energy is three quarters. Mistaking both energy fractions for one quarter merely because the two electric amplitudes have equal magnitudes leaves half the input pulse energy unexplained. At \(Z_2\to0\) the coefficients approach an electric short: the tangential electric field vanishes, even though the magnetic field doubles in the incident medium. Matching the impedances gives zero reflection even if material wave speeds differ, since those speeds depend on the product \(\mu\epsilon\) whereas impedance depends on their ratio.

Inject a short pulse with known area and width a sufficient distance to the left that it initially lies entirely in medium one. If the media are nondispersive and impedance independent of frequency across the pulse band, each electric waveform copies the incident temporal shape multiplied by its amplitude coefficient. A transmitted wave travelling at a different speed has a different spatial width for the same temporal width, so comparing stored energy by squaring peak fields and comparing equal geometrical intervals can easily mislead. A fixed detector measuring \(\int(E H)\,dt\) across a complete pulse directly measures transmitted energy per area. In a simulation integrate forward and backward flux at separate planes, include any energy still between the planes, and check that the field boundary gives the same answer when the detector positions are changed.

Manufacture a source that obeys charge continuity exactly

A verification case with prescribed charge and current can test different components from a source-free travelling wave. Consider a one-dimensional periodic interval with period \(2\pi/k\). Choose a longitudinal electric field \(E_x(x,t)=E_a \sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)\) and zero magnetic field. Gauss's law fixes \(\rho=\epsilon_0 kE_a\cos(kx)\cos(\omega t)\). Ampère–Maxwell requires \(J_x=-\epsilon_0\partial_tE_x=\epsilon_0\omega E_a\sin(kx)\sin(\omega t)\). Faraday gives no magnetic-field change because a purely \(x\)-directed function of \(x\) has zero curl. Differentiating the two source expressions verifies \(\partial_t\rho+\partial_xJ_x=0\), and the periodic spatial mean of the charge vanishes. This is a prescribed-source test of the equations, not a claim that an unspecified material spontaneously supplies those currents.

For period \(0.20\,\mathrm m\), \(E_a=100\,\mathrm{V/m}\), and \(f=2.0\,\mathrm{MHz}\), \(k=31.42\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\). The charge-density amplitude is \(27.8\,\mathrm{nC/m^3}\) and the free-current-density amplitude \(0.0111\,\mathrm{A/m^2}\). At \(t=0\) the longitudinal electric energy is maximal and current vanishes. One quarter period later the electric field and charge are zero while the source current is maximal at the spatial electric-field antinodes. At every point where \(E,J\) are nonzero their product is \(-\partial_t(\epsilon_0E^2/2)\); the prescribed external charges exchange energy with the field without a magnetic Poynting flux. A discrete divergence residual and an independently measured field-energy change can therefore check the signs and amplitudes of the source implementation.

Prescribing those continuous formulas sampled separately on a staggered mesh does not necessarily produce exact *discrete* continuity. If the numerical current is sampled at half time and half space, integrate the cell continuity equation to update \(\rho\) from numerical boundary currents. Alternatively use the corresponding discrete sinusoidal differentiation factors \(2\sin(kh/2)/h\) and \(2\sin(\omega\Delta t/2)/\Delta t\) when manufacturing exact discrete data. Comparing the continuous and discrete constructions separates a model truncation error that converges with mesh refinement from a genuinely nonconserving source injection. A code can look excellent on transverse vacuum pulses and still fail this test because those pulses never challenge its charge handling.

Decide what a late echo means

A narrow pulse incident on a material slab can return signals from both interfaces. Separate the two in time when possible. If the slab thickness is \(d\) and its propagation speed \(v_2\), the round-trip delay inside it is \(2d/v_2\). Later echoes spaced by that delay suggest repeated internal reflections. Changing the outer computational boundary location while keeping the material and detector fixed provides a different test: an artificial wall echo moves in time by twice the boundary displacement divided by exterior speed. A genuine material echo keeps its material-defined delay. A calibrated single detector trace and these two perturbations can thus identify which part of the model causes a late pulse.

For \(d=0.30\,\mathrm m\) and \(v_2=1.5\times10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}\) the material echo spacing is \(4.0\,\mathrm{ns}\). Moving an external vacuum boundary outward by \(0.45\,\mathrm m\) would delay a returning boundary echo by \(3.0\,\mathrm{ns}\). If the incident pulse lasts 8 ns the echoes overlap strongly and individual peaks are difficult to assign. Broadening the bandwidth with a shorter source or fitting the full complex frequency response is then more informative than selecting the largest local maximum. Any comparison must include the source bandwidth and detector response because a perfectly converged field solution can still predict a different voltage from a finite-bandwidth probe.

Set a cavity ringing from specified initial fields

A one-dimensional lossless cavity consists of perfectly conducting parallel planes at \(x=0\) and \(x=L\). Use a polarisation \(E_y,B_z\) and ignore lateral edges. With no sources inside, the incoming characteristic at each wall is set by the condition \(E_y=0\). Instead of injecting a pulse during the calculation, prescribe an initial field \(E_y(x,0)=E_0\sin(\pi x/L)\) and \(B_z(x,0)=0\). These data obey the tangential electric wall condition. They also obey the divergence constraints because the spatial variation is along \(x\) while the fields are transverse. Initial data that satisfy a wave equation but violate either of these checks would model a different physical preparation.

Let \(k=\pi/L\) and try \(E_y(x,t)=E_0\sin(kx)F(t)\). The wave equation gives \(F''+c^2k^2F=0\). The initial magnetic field has no spatial gradient and the source-free Maxwell–Ampère law therefore sets \(F'(0)=0\). It follows that \(F=\cos\omega t\), with \(\omega=ck\). Use Faraday's first-order equation to find the sign and phase of \(B_z\), obtaining

\[E_y=E_0\sin(kx)\cos\omega t,\]
\[B_z=-\frac{E_0}{c}\cos(kx)\sin\omega t.\]

For a first-order time solver this pair gives a stronger check than the electric standing-wave frequency alone. A code with an incorrect sign in one curl can reproduce an electric snapshot but will transfer energy incorrectly on the next advance. The signs of the magnetic fields at the two ends are opposite. Their amplitudes peak one quarter period after the initial electric maximum. Perfectly conducting walls carry the necessary surface currents. The standing solution has no Poynting flux through them because the tangential electric field at each wall vanishes.

Per unit transverse area the total initial energy is \(\epsilon_0E_0^2L/4\), since the integral of \(\sin^2(kx)\) over the cavity is \(L/2\). At a general time the electric share is that total multiplied by \(\cos^2\omega t\), and the magnetic share is the same total multiplied by \(\sin^2\omega t\). The integral of their sum is constant, although energy continually flows within the cavity. For \(L=0.30\,{\rm m}\) the fundamental frequency is \(c/(2L)=499.65\,{\rm MHz}\) and its quarter period is about \(0.500\,{\rm ns}\). If \(E_0=100\,{\rm V/m}\), the stored energy is \(6.64\,{\rm nJ/m^2}\) and the peak induction is \(0.334\,\mu{\rm T}\).

The experiment can also reveal the discrete wave speed. A staggered one-dimensional mesh with spacing \(h=L/10\) and Courant number \(C=c\Delta t/h=0.5\) obeys \(\sin(\omega_{\rm num}\Delta t/2)=C\sin(kh/2)\). The predicted frequency ratio is \(2\arcsin[C\sin(kh/2)]/(Ckh)\), approximately \(0.9969\) here. Measuring too short a time interval to distinguish this phase lag from sampling uncertainty would give little information about spatial accuracy. Track many periods, use consistent time-centred field estimates, and halve \(h\) at fixed \(C\). At these small \(kh\), the leading phase error should decrease by roughly a factor of four.

A moving-coil galvanometer as an electrical and mechanical system

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A scale division records an angle, yet the quantity marked on the dial is electric current. A spring, a magnetic field and the resistance of the external measuring circuit connect the two. Derive the calibration, predict how a pointer approaches its reading, and find which experiment measures the charge of a short pulse.

Convert the force on two straight sides into a torque

A light rectangular coil has \(N\) turns, active length \(l\), width \(w\), and area per turn \(A=lw\). It pivots about an axis parallel to its long sides. Shaped pole pieces confine an approximately radial magnetic field of magnitude \(B\) to an air gap. Over the working angular range the magnetic field is perpendicular to the active wire and lies approximately in the plane of the coil. An elementary length carrying conventional current \(i\) experiences \(d\mathbf F=i\,d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B\). The two long sides carry current in opposite directions and experience forces of equal magnitude \(N ilB\). Acting with lever arms \(w/2\), the forces make a couple whose torque is \(NiAB\). Their net force is approximately zero. The short ends supply negligible torque about the pivot under the ideal geometry.

Write \(K=NAB\), and choose the positive sense of current to give positive torque. Its units are \({\rm N\,m/A}\). In a uniform Cartesian field an ordinary rigid loop instead gives torque \(NiAB\sin\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the angle between its normal and the field. The radial gap maintains the factor equivalent to \(\sin\alpha\) close to unity as the pointer turns. This is a design choice that produces a nearly linear scale. If a measured calibration curve flattens at a large angle, field nonuniformity and a nonlinear torsion spring are candidate causes that can be tested separately.

For a soft torsion spring with stiffness \(\kappa\), the return torque is \(-\kappa\theta\) at small deflections from the zero-current setting. At static equilibrium \(\theta=Ki/\kappa\). With \(N=200\), \(A=1.00\,{\rm cm^2}\), \(B=0.250\,{\rm T}\) and \(\kappa=8.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm N\,m/rad}\), the torque constant is \(0.0050\,{\rm N\,m/A}\) and the current sensitivity is \(6.25\,{\rm rad/A}\). A \(5.0\,{\rm mA}\) current turns the pointer through \(0.03125\,{\rm rad}\), about \(1.79^\circ\). A mirror mounted on the same axle deflects a reflected laser through twice this angle. At a screen distance of \(0.50\,{\rm m}\) the spot shifts \(0.50\tan(2\theta)=31.3\,{\rm mm}\), making the small mechanical rotation easy to resolve.

Recover the reciprocal voltage from work

Now rotate the coil through a small positive angle while it carries positive current. The electromagnetic torque does mechanical work \(Ki\,d\theta\). The voltage associated with the motion must therefore draw electrical work \(i e_{\rm back}\,dt\) of equal magnitude when the motor torque assists that motion. This gives the back-emf relation \(e_{\rm back}=K\dot\theta\), measured as a positive drop in the motor sign convention. Its coefficient has units \({\rm V\,s/rad}\), numerically the same SI coefficient as the torque constant. One can also derive it from the motional emf on the two active sides. Each moves at speed \((w/2)\dot\theta\), crosses field \(B\), and generates a voltage \(Bl(w/2)\dot\theta\) along its wire. The two contributions add over every turn.

Let \(J\) denote the rotational inertia, \(b\) the mechanical viscous damping coefficient, \(L\) the coil inductance and \(R\) the sum of winding plus external series resistance seen by a voltage source \(v(t)\). The electromechanical equations are

\[J\ddot\theta+b\dot\theta+\kappa\theta=Ki,\]
\[L\dot i+Ri+K\dot\theta=v(t).\]

Multiplying the first equation by \(\dot\theta\) and the second by \(i\) eliminates the coupling power \(Ki\dot\theta\). The result says that source power \(vi\) equals the rate of increase of \(J\dot\theta^2/2+\kappa\theta^2/2+Li^2/2\), plus \(b\dot\theta^2+Ri^2\). This identity fixes the relative signs of the torque and back emf. If a sign mistake turns a passive closed coil into a self-accelerating pointer, the same mistake produces a residual coupling term in this power balance. Mechanical friction and winding heat represent distinct sinks even though both can damp the observed displacement.

Predict overshoot and find a critical external resistance

If the electrical relaxation time \(L/R\) is short compared with the oscillation time \(1/\sqrt{\kappa/J}\), set \(i\simeq(v-K\dot\theta)/R\). Substituting into the mechanical equation increases its effective damping to \(b_{\rm eff}=b+K^2/R\). Set \(v=0\) when discussing free relaxation with the electrical terminals connected through a resistor. A moving shorted coil generates a current that opposes its motion. Opening the circuit instead suppresses that current and leaves the mechanical damping. An ideal current source holding specified current through all pointer motions also changes the back-emf response because its output voltage adjusts to enforce that current.

The undamped angular frequency is \(\omega_0=\sqrt{\kappa/J}\) and the damping ratio is \(\zeta=b_{\rm eff}/(2\sqrt{\kappa J})\). Take \(J=2.0\times10^{-6}\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(b=1.6\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), and the preceding \(K,\kappa\). Then \(\omega_0=20.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(2\sqrt{\kappa J}=8.0\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), and an open circuit gives \(\zeta=0.20\). With \(R=1.00\,\Omega\) the electric contribution to damping is \(2.50\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), giving \(\zeta=0.5125\). For a voltage step the ideal fractional overshoot in the reduced model is \(\exp[-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}]=0.153\). The first peak occurs after \(\pi/(\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})=0.183\,{\rm s}\).

Critical damping occurs when \(b+K^2/R=2\sqrt{\kappa J}\), which requires a total resistance of about \(0.391\,\Omega\). A winding resistance of \(0.300\,\Omega\) therefore leaves \(0.091\,\Omega\) for external source and leads. If the winding alone exceeds the target resistance, a simple passive external series resistor cannot reach this damping in the reduced model. A finite \(L\) must also be checked: with \(L=5.0\,{\rm mH}\) and \(R=1.0\,\Omega\), \(\omega_0L/R=0.10\), so the approximation provides a useful starting estimate. Reducing \(R\) indefinitely eventually invalidates the elimination of current. The full pair of equations then predicts the damping and frequency dependence.

Distinguish a charge impulse from a slow current reading

Suppose a brief imposed current pulse ends long before the pointer has turned appreciably. Integrating the torque equation over that interval gives \(J\Delta\dot\theta\simeq K\int i\,dt=Kq\). The initial signal is now angular velocity proportional to total transmitted charge. For weak subsequent damping, the first excursion is approximately \(Kq/(J\omega_0)\). A dose of \(20\,\mu{\rm C}\) gives \(2.50\,{\rm mrad}\) with the instrument above, corresponding to about \(2.50\,{\rm mm}\) on the mirror scale. Two current pulses of identical charge but different shapes give nearly the same reading if both are short on the mechanical time scale. Their peak currents can be entirely different.

For appreciable damping, use the impulse solution \(\theta(t)=Kq\,e^{-\zeta\omega_0t}\sin(\omega_dt)/(J\omega_d)\), with \(\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) for an underdamped pointer. Its maximum obeys \(\tan(\omega_dt_p)=\omega_d/(\zeta\omega_0)\) in the first quarter cycle. Substitution corrects the ballistic calibration instead of treating the damped peak as an undamped amplitude. The state of the external circuit after the pulse matters because it determines \(\zeta\) during the throw. A calibration pulse should leave the measuring circuit connected in the same way as the unknown pulse.

Measure the torque constant without assuming the magnetic field

The preceding design calculation multiplies turn count, active area and a nominal air-gap induction. An independent calibration can measure the same constant without knowing the field distribution along every wire. Move the pointer through a small controlled angle while measuring its open-circuit voltage with a high-impedance instrument. In the same working interval the voltage is \(K(\theta)\dot\theta\) up to a polarity choice. Integrating the voltage over the sweep gives \(\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}K(\theta)\,d\theta\). If \(K\) is constant this is simply \(K\Delta\theta\), independent of sweep speed. It is often easier to integrate a recorded voltage pulse accurately than to differentiate the angle trace to obtain instantaneous speed.

For a sweep through \(0.0800\,{\rm rad}\), suppose the measured voltage integral is \(0.401\,{\rm mV\,s}\). The generator constant is \(5.01\times10^{-3}\,{\rm V\,s/rad}\). Independently add a known small torque to the spring and measure its static angle. A torque \(32.0\,\mu{\rm N\,m}\) rotates the pointer by \(0.0400\,{\rm rad}\), giving \(\kappa=8.00\times10^{-4}\,{\rm N\,m/rad}\). Combining the two measurements predicts a current sensitivity of \(6.27\,{\rm rad/A}\). Splitting the sweep into smaller equal angular intervals tests whether the measured \(K\) depends on angle. Sweeping backwards should reverse the integrated voltage. A voltage offset instead contributes its offset times duration, so repeating at two sweep speeds helps separate it.

A dynamic cross-check uses free decay under two load conditions. Measure the exponential decay rate \(\alpha_{\rm open}\) with a high-impedance voltage probe and \(\alpha_R\) with a known total resistance in the closed circuit. In the short-electrical-time limit, subtraction of the two damping laws yields \(K^2=2JR(\alpha_R-\alpha_{\rm open})\). With \(J=2.0\times10^{-6}\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(R=1.00\,\Omega\), and decay rates \(4.0\) and \(10.25\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), this gives \(K=0.0050\,{\rm N\,m/A}\). The method needs two identifiable decay rates and independent inertia. If the loaded response is overdamped, the envelope of two oscillation peaks is no longer available; fit the full two-real-pole response to the measured displacement.

Keep the third dynamical variable in a frequency-response experiment

For a sinusoidal voltage at angular frequency \(\omega\) the exact linear transduction law follows by eliminating the complex current after transforming both differential equations. The mechanical impedance in angular-displacement form is \(\kappa-J\omega^2+i\omega b\); the winding impedance is \(R+i\omega L\). Using phasors with time dependence \(e^{i\omega t}\) gives

\[\frac{\Theta}{V}=\frac{K}{(R+i\omega L)(\kappa-J\omega^2+i\omega b)+i\omega K^2}.\]

At zero frequency the gain is \(K/(R\kappa)\) and the phase tends to zero, as the static calibration requires. At a sufficiently high frequency both the electrical and inertial impedances grow. For \(L>0\) the magnitude approaches \(K/(LJ\omega^3)\). The reduced model with the current eliminated algebraically falls only as \(1/\omega^2\). The change of the asymptotic slope provides an experimental way to distinguish finite inductance from an incorrectly adjusted viscous damping constant. Beyond the working band of a real instrument, elastic modes of the pointer or parasitic capacitance can enter before that asymptote is resolved.

At the nominal undamped frequency \(20\,{\rm rad/s}\), with \(R=1.0\,\Omega\) and \(L=5.0\,{\rm mH}\), the stiffness and inertia terms cancel. The denominator becomes \(-\omega^2 L b+i\omega(Rb+K^2)\), or approximately \(-0.000032+i\,0.000820\) in consistent SI units. Its magnitude gives about \(6.09\,{\rm rad/V}\). A sinusoid of \(1.0\,{\rm mV}\) peak produces about \(6.09\,{\rm mrad}\) peak, whereas the static response to \(1.0\,{\rm mV}\) is \(6.25\,{\rm mrad}\). The phases differ radically despite their similar amplitudes: near this frequency the pointer lags by about \(92.2^\circ\). A gain-only calibration at a single frequency would miss the phase lag.

That convention keeps the final angle common to all curves and isolates the transient response of the load. The full model retains \(i,\theta,\dot\theta\); the second curve sets \(L\dot i=0\). Eddy-current braking, magnetic penetration and the heat left behind

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A conducting loop moves above a row of magnetic poles. The drag first increases with speed, but a rapid crossing need not generate proportionally greater braking. Trace the work done against the Lorentz force into circuit energy, heat and a measurable temperature change. Then decide which features of a solid plate the single-loop model can capture.

Generate a voltage from a spatially periodic field

Let a rectangular loop of height \(h\) along \(y\) and width \(w\) along \(x\) translate to the right above a stationary magnetic pattern. At the loop let the normal induction be \(B_z(x)=B_0\cos(kx)\), with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). Its two active sides are centred symmetrically about the coordinate \(x_c\). Assume the field varies negligibly along \(y\) over this height. Integrating through the loop gives \(\Phi=h\int_{x_c-w/2}^{x_c+w/2}B_0\cos(kx)\,dx=[2hB_0\sin(kw/2)/k]\cos(kx_c)\). A uniform translation \(x_c=ut\) therefore produces an external emf with peak amplitude \(E_{\rm pk}=K u\), where \(K=2hB_0|\sin(kw/2)|\).

This spatial averaging explains an initially surprising design feature. A loop spanning precisely one full spatial period always links zero net flux from the sinusoidal field and therefore generates no emf in this ideal geometry. A loop of half a period has maximum coupling at a specified height. If \(w\) is much smaller than the period, \(\sin(kw/2)\simeq kw/2\) and \(K\simeq h w B_0 k\). This is exactly the small-loop result from using its area times the local gradient of induction. Reversing velocity reverses the emf at a fixed position. At a fixed positive speed the temporal frequency is \(f=u/\lambda\), so the same conductor experiences different electrical frequencies at different mechanical speeds.

Include finite inductance and derive the drag curve

Model the current path by a constant resistance \(R\) and self-inductance \(L\). Its steady periodic current solves \(L\dot i+Ri=\mathcal E(t)\). In phasor notation its magnitude is \(E_{\rm pk}/\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L)^2}\), lagging the emf by \(\arctan(\omega L/R)\), where \(\omega=ku\). The current develops field energy \(Li^2/2\) and dissipates \(Ri^2\). Multiplying the circuit equation by \(i\) and averaging over a period eliminates the derivative of stored energy. The average mechanical power delivered against the drag is therefore the mean Joule heating \(P=E_{\rm pk}^2R/[2(R^2+\omega^2L^2)]\). Dividing by the speed gives the drag magnitude

\[F(u)=\frac{K^2u}{2R[1+(u/u_c)^2]},\]
\[u_c=\frac{R}{kL}.\]

At low speed the drag is proportional to velocity. At the crossover \(u=u_c\) it reaches \(K^2u_c/(4R)\), and at much larger speeds it falls approximately as \(1/u\) within the assumed lumped model. The incoming mechanical power then tends to a constant rather than growing without bound. Why is there a maximum? Increasing speed raises the emf, but it also raises the reversal frequency. An inductive current increasingly lags the driving emf, and its component in phase with the emf does the net dissipative work. A measured turnover therefore tests both an electrical time scale and the spatial scale of the magnet pattern.

Use \(h=50\,{\rm mm}\), \(w=25\,{\rm mm}\), \(\lambda=0.100\,{\rm m}\), \(B_0=0.20\,{\rm T}\), \(R=0.050\,\Omega\) and \(L=0.150\,{\rm mH}\). Then \(K=0.01414\,{\rm T\,m}\), \(u_c=5.31\,{\rm m/s}\), and maximum mean drag is \(5.31\,{\rm mN}\). At \(u=2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) the field alternates at \(20\,{\rm Hz}\), giving an emf of \(28.3\,{\rm mV}\) peak. The current is \(0.529\,{\rm A}\) peak and its phase lag \(20.7^\circ\). The resistor receives approximately \(7.00\,{\rm mW}\), and the motor maintaining translation supplies a mean force of \(3.50\,{\rm mN}\). Using peak current in \(I^2R\) without its factor one half would overstate the mechanical force by a factor two.

Determine whether the current penetrates a real plate

A continuous metal plate contains many possible current paths, unlike a preformed loop. Their resistances, inductances and mutual couplings depend on the plate boundary, magnet geometry and frequency. For a good homogeneous conductor the magnetic diffusion equation gives a skin depth \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\) under sinusoidal excitation. At a depth \(\delta\) a field entering a locally planar half-space has lost a factor \(e\) in amplitude and acquired a phase delay of one radian. The frequency to use is the frequency experienced by the moving material; the fact that the magnets are stationary in the laboratory does not make this frequency zero.

For copper with \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\,{\rm S/m}\) and permeability close to \(\mu_0\), the skin depth is about \(14.8\,{\rm mm}\) at \(20\,{\rm Hz}\) and \(1.48\,{\rm mm}\) at \(2.0\,{\rm kHz}\). A \(2.0\,{\rm mm}\) plate therefore sees little through-thickness attenuation in the first experiment but may need a diffusion calculation in the second. The plate's lateral current pattern can still matter when the through-thickness current density is nearly uniform. Slots interrupt wide current loops and usually reduce drag even when the mass and the bulk conductivity change little. Comparing a slotted and an unslotted plate directly tests the importance of current closure.

If a plate experiences sharply separated poles, its applied field in the moving frame contains a fundamental and higher harmonics. Harmonic \(n\) has a skin depth smaller by \(\sqrt n\), so checking only the fundamental can miss surface concentration associated with narrow features. A Hall scan along the track supplies the actual spatial field spectrum. Multiplying each spatial wavenumber by the speed converts it into a temporal angular frequency for a constant-speed pass. Once the speed changes significantly over one pole spacing, a time-domain calculation using the measured position trace is preferable to this fixed-frequency conversion.

Close a mechanical and thermal energy budget

Take a rotating brake as a second experiment. A flywheel of known inertia \(J_{\rm rot}=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) slows from \(120\) to \(80\,{\rm rad/s}\) while no drive supplies power. The released rotational kinetic energy is \(J_{\rm rot}(120^2-80^2)/2=80.0\,{\rm J}\). A separate run with the magnet removed measures bearing and air losses. Suppose the corresponding speed-dependent losses integrated over the magnetic braking trajectory total \(8.0\,{\rm J}\). Then the electromagnetic channel receives \(72.0\,{\rm J}\). Using the reference run's total energy over an arbitrary equal time could give a different answer, since the two speed histories affect the parasitic losses.

A calorimeter enclosing the conducting rotor has effective heat capacity \(C=60\,{\rm J/K}\) and a thermal link of conductance \(G=0.12\,{\rm W/K}\) to an environment at constant temperature \(T_a\). Its balance is \(C\,dT/dt=P_{\rm deposited}-G(T-T_a)\). During a \(20\,{\rm s}\) run, suppose the time-averaged rise above ambient is \(0.50\,{\rm K}\). Then \(1.20\,{\rm J}\) flowed through the thermal link. If all electromagnetic heat entered the calorimeter, its final rise from an ambient start is \((72.0-1.20)/60=1.18\,{\rm K}\). A measured rise near \(0.80\,{\rm K}\) would leave an energy discrepancy of approximately \(23\,{\rm J}\). Check conduction into a shaft, heat stored outside the instrumented volume, and the estimate of parasitic torque before adjusting the electromagnetic theory.

Find the spatial features that dominate a measured braking signal

A single Fourier component of field already produces an informative turnover. In a tracked experiment the field often has a measurable third harmonic. Let \(B(x)=B_1\cos kx+B_3\cos(3kx+\varphi)\), with constant translation speed. Flux integration across the finite loop multiplies each harmonic by its own width factor. The corresponding emf peak is \(2h|B_n|u|\sin(nkw/2)|\), and its temporal angular frequency is \(nku\). The linear \(RL\) equation treats the two drives independently. Orthogonality over a complete fundamental period makes the mean of their product vanish, so total mean heat can be found by adding the individual resistive powers. Within one period the interference terms do affect instantaneous torque and power.

Suppose \(B_3/B_1=0.20\) and keep the preceding \(w=\lambda/4\). Here \(\sin(kw/2)\) and \(\sin(3kw/2)\) both have magnitude \(1/\sqrt2\). At low speed the third harmonic therefore adds about four percent to mean drag. At \(u=u_c\), however, its angular frequency sees inductive impedance \(3R\) while the fundamental sees \(R\). The third-harmonic share relative to the fundamental becomes \(0.20^2(1+1)/(1+9)=0.008\), only \(0.8\%\). A peak-to-peak field reading can change visibly without a comparable change in mean drag. Conversely, if the loop width happens to null one spatial harmonic, another smaller harmonic can supply most of the signal. A field map and the loop geometry together determine what source drives the circuit.

In a solid plate, predicting force from field and speed needs a field solution that enforces current closure. In the slowly moving conductor, \(\mathbf J=\sigma[-\nabla\phi+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B]\) when the imposed magnetic pattern is effectively time independent in the laboratory and the induced magnetic field can be neglected. Charge conservation then gives \(\nabla\cdot[\sigma(-\nabla\phi+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)]=0\). On an electrically insulated plate edge, enforce \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J=0\). Solving this potential problem accounts for charge accumulation that redirects currents at a boundary. Simply setting \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf u\times\mathbf B\) can predict current flowing straight out of an insulating edge. The force follows from integrating \(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B\) through the conducting volume, and Joule heat follows from \(\int J^2/\sigma\,dV\).

A useful check on such a field calculation is a finite plate translating wholly inside an exactly uniform static magnetic field. Charges can rearrange until a uniform electrostatic field cancels \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B\). An isolated plate in this steady limit then has zero transport current and zero drag. Motion alone does not guarantee braking. At an entry edge, the moving conductor spans a spatial change of induction, and one scalar potential generally cannot cancel the motional drive around every closed path. Slits, thickness variations, a conducting contact at a boundary, and the induced field at higher speeds each change this test problem in an identifiable way.

Interpret a thermal transient without assuming it is instantaneous

If the brake runs at a stable speed and equilibrium electrical conditions, a local single-temperature model sees a nearly constant power. Its step response from ambient is \(\Delta T=(P/G)[1-\exp(-Gt/C)]\). Removing the drive gives an exponential decay with the same time constant \(C/G\). Measuring that cooling tail fixes the ratio of heat capacity to conductance but does not set either one separately. A known heater power or an independent heat-capacity measurement supplies the missing absolute scale. A slope evaluated over a long interval without correcting leakage will underestimate the input power as the sample warms.

Take a small bench calorimeter enclosing the single test loop with \(C=1.20\,{\rm J/K}\) and \(G=0.020\,{\rm W/K}\). The \(7.00\,{\rm mW}\) low-speed run gives a \(0.350\,{\rm K}\) eventual rise and a \(60\,{\rm s}\) time constant. After \(30\,{\rm s}\) the rise is about \(0.138\,{\rm K}\), whereas a thermally isolated calculation would give \(0.175\,{\rm K}\). Its stored heat \(0.166\,{\rm J}\) is less than the \(0.210\,{\rm J}\) mechanical work over that interval; the thermal link accounts for the remainder. Loading the conductor thermally with an additional copper holder increases the response time and changes the initial slope without changing the final rise if conductance to the bath remains the same.

If an independent steady \(10.0\,{\rm mW}\) heater then produces a \(0.500\,{\rm K}\) temperature rise, \(G=0.0200\,{\rm W/K}\) and \(C=1.21\,{\rm J/K}\). A magnetic run that settles at \(0.350\,{\rm K}\) now corresponds to \(7.00\,{\rm mW}\), provided the thermal boundary and deposition pattern are sufficiently similar to the calibration.

Check the sign of instantaneous electromagnetic work

At a phase where the applied emf has just crossed zero, a finite-inductance loop may still carry considerable current. During part of a cycle the emf and current can have opposite signs. The product \(\mathcal E i\) is then negative even though \(Ri^2\) remains positive. The difference is a negative derivative of the magnetic store \(Li^2/2\). The loop briefly returns energy mechanically while its stored field decreases, and some of that release also becomes heat. A force transducer resolving separate pole passages can therefore see instantaneous variations that a mean braking-force estimate suppresses.

For the numerical \(2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) case the peak field energy is \(L I_{\rm pk}^2/2\approx21.0\,\mu{\rm J}\). The dissipated energy per full pole period is \(7.00\,{\rm mW}/20\,{\rm Hz}=350\,\mu{\rm J}\). These quantities serve different purposes. The first is a reversible instantaneous store; the second is net irreversible energy delivered each time the conductor moves one spatial period. Dividing the second by the spatial period of \(0.100\,{\rm m}\) recovers the mean drag of \(3.50\,{\rm mN}\) without using the translational speed explicitly.

Estimate a cavity linewidth from a finite, biased record

Connect the time and frequency descriptions of one damped mode, then diagnose why a weak detector background systematically biases the inferred quality factor.

Know whether the detector reads field or energy

After a drive switches off, let a single cavity mode have complex analytic amplitude \(a(t)=a_0e^{-\kappa t/2}e^{-i\omega_0t}\) for \(t\ge0\). The energy is proportional to \(|a|^2\), giving \(U=U_0e^{-\kappa t}\). Its instantaneous decay rate \(-U'/U=\kappa\) implies \(Q=\omega_0 U/P_\mathrm{loss}=\omega_0/\kappa\). A phase-sensitive receiver measuring field amplitude sees a time constant twice as long as the energy lifetime. Before fitting the data, ask how the measurement transducer converts the field into a reported number.

Fourier transforming the causal decaying amplitude gives an integral proportional to \(1/[\kappa/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)]\), up to the time convention. Its squared magnitude has half-power points at \(|\omega-\omega_0|=\kappa/2\). Therefore the full power linewidth in hertz is \(\Delta f=\kappa/(2\pi)\). A measurement of either the energy decay time \(\tau_U=1/\kappa\) or the spectral width can test the other without having to calibrate the absolute detector gain.

At \(f_0=2.0\) GHz and \(\tau_U=0.50\ \mu\mathrm s\), we obtain \(\kappa=2.0\times10^6\) s⁻¹, \(\Delta f\simeq318.3\) kHz and \(Q\simeq6283\). A field receiver would report an amplitude time constant of 1.0 μs. Substituting that time directly into \(Q=2\pi f_0\tau_U\) would double the apparent quality.

Derive the two-point fit with a detector offset

Let a power detector report \(S(t)=A e^{-\kappa t}+B\), with a constant background \(B>0\). In the absence of background two samples separated by \(\Delta t\) give \(\widehat\kappa=\log[S(t)/S(t+\Delta t)]/\Delta t\). With background, the logarithmic derivative is

\[-\frac{d}{dt}\log S=\kappa\frac{Ae^{-\kappa t}}{Ae^{-\kappa t}+B}.\]

It starts below the true rate and tends to zero in the tail. A straight line fitted to late log data can therefore look impressively precise while overestimating the lifetime. Increasing the acquisition duration can worsen this bias if the entire record is forced into a straight-line model.

In the experiment set \(b=B/A=0.04\). A two-point fit from one true lifetime to two has dimensionless rate \(\log[(e^{-1}+0.04)/(e^{-2}+0.04)]\), approximately 0.844. Its apparent lifetime is about 18.5% high. The source, walls and coupling have not changed. Only the interpretation of detector output has changed. Move the start of the fit towards three lifetimes and inspect the increasing gap from the ideal log line.

Design a second independent inference

With three equally spaced, noiseless readings \(S_0,S_1,S_2\) of one exponential plus a constant, subtract consecutive values before taking a logarithm. If \(a=e^{-\kappa\Delta t}\), the differences are \(A'(1-a)\) and \(A'a(1-a)\), giving \(a=(S_1-S_2)/(S_0-S_1)\). This cancels the constant background. At an interval of one lifetime, a first signal of unit energy and background 0.04 gives approximately \(1.04,0.407879,0.175335\). Their ratio of differences recovers \(e^{-1}\). In a real noise floor two nearly equal readings make this division unreliable. Use multiple windows or fit the raw signal with a measured noise model.

Finite spectral resolution creates a separate challenge. Multiplying the measured field by a rectangular window of duration \(T\) convolves its spectrum with a sinc-like response of width of order \(1/T\). For the 318 kHz linewidth in the example, \(T=1\) μs gives frequency bins spaced by 1 MHz in a basic Fourier transform. Zero padding can interpolate its curve, but cannot restore the unmeasured late-time signal. A parametric fit may extract a sub-bin frequency or decay rate by using its model assumptions; its uncertainty then depends on signal-to-noise ratio and on whether the one-mode theory is adequate.

Check the single-mode model before refining its fit

If two nearby modes reach the same detector, their fields add before squaring. The result contains an interference term oscillating at their frequency difference on top of a decaying envelope. A scalar positive background cannot explain that alternating residual. Test by narrowing the excitation band, moving the pickup to a symmetry node of one mode, or examining a complex receiver's quadratures. A model with two exponentials has more degrees of freedom and can fit noise as well as signal; require consistency across independent records.

An honest linewidth report specifies the measured quantity, acquisition window, background treatment, coupling state, reference frequency and uncertainty. A fit to stored energy measures total loss. Inferring internal absorption alone additionally requires a known port coupling or a measurement that changes it separately, as in the course's cavity project.

Numerical field laboratory

Design a small-signal experiment to estimate resistance and inductance, propagate amplitude and phase uncertainty and decide when a distributed field model is needed.

A measured impedance is one complex number

A laboratory sensing coil is driven by a small sinusoidal current at angular frequency \(\omega\). In its simplest usable range, a series model predicts \(Z=R+i\omega L\). The voltage across a port defines the sign of current entering the positive terminal. With peak phasors, complex power is \(S=VI^*/2=|I|^2(R+i\omega L)/2\). Thus the real part measures average irreversible loss. Magnetic energy at peak current is \(L|I|^2/2\); reactive power equals \(\omega\) times this peak energy. These equalities supply two routes to the same parameters provided parasitic capacitance and frequency-dependent losses remain negligible.

At 100 Hz, a coil with \(R=20\ \Omega\) and \(L=40\) mH has reactance \(2\pi(100)(0.040)=25.13\ \Omega\), magnitude 32.12 Ω and phase about 51.49 degrees. A 10 mA peak current produces about 0.321 V peak and 1 mW average resistive loss. The peak magnetic energy is only \(2\,\mu\mathrm J\). Check that the reactive power obtained from the phase and current is about 1.26 mvar. Confusion between RMS and peak phasors easily introduces a factor two while still leaving the impedance ratio correct.

Derive what an amplitude error and a timing error do

Suppose the readout gives magnitude \(M\) and phase \(\phi\). Then \(R=M\cos\phi\), \(L=M\sin\phi/\omega\). For small independent uncertainties and known \(\omega\), differentiate both expressions. The relative first-order perturbations are

\[\frac{\delta R}{R}=\frac{\delta M}{M}-\tan\phi\,\delta\phi,\qquad\]

\frac{\delta L}{L}=\frac{\delta M}{M}+\cot\phi\,\delta\phi .

The phase uncertainty must be expressed in radians. For independent random errors their variances add with the squared coefficients; the signed equations above describe individual perturbations. Near zero phase even a very accurate amplitude tells little about inductance, while near 90 degrees inferring resistance requires exceptional phase accuracy. A near-45-degree measurement balances these two sensitivities when the uncertainty budget treats them similarly.

If the phase uncertainty is 0.5° and relative magnitude uncertainty is 1%, the 45-degree standard deviation in either recovered parameter is approximately \(\sqrt{0.01^2+0.00873^2}=0.0133\), or 1.33%. At five degrees the relative inductance contribution from phase alone is \(\cot5^\circ \times0.00873\simeq10\%\). Taking data at more digits does not improve phase calibration.

Measure at two or more separated frequencies and reconstruct \(R\) and \(L\) independently at each. A frequency-dependent apparent \(R\) could be caused by skin effect or core loss. A falling apparent inductance near a self-resonance can signal stray capacitance. Simply allowing \(R,L\) to take arbitrary values at each frequency fits all data without explaining the mechanism. Add a candidate physical effect and check an independent observable such as loss under a different current amplitude, ring-down frequency or a change in lead geometry.

Close the loop from a fitted parameter to a field calculation

The lumped magnetic energy \(LI^2/2\) should match the field integral \(\int \mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H\,dV/2\) for a linear reversible system that includes the relevant surrounding field. This is why the exterior air region and its imposed boundary can affect a numerical estimate even when the measured coil is compact. Increase the domain and refine the mesh separately. A converged mesh in a small box can deliver a very repeatable value for the wrong boundary problem. The earlier chapter on mutual-inductance matrices generalises this check to cross terms between currents.

Another independent measurement uses a slowly executed current ramp with a known winding resistance. Subtract the measured resistive voltage from terminal voltage and integrate over time. Under the linear model, \(\int(V-RI)\,dt=L\Delta I\). This test weights offsets very differently from sinusoidal phase detection: a constant voltmeter offset integrates into a bias proportional to record duration, while a high-frequency phase error drops out. Agreement of the two methods is stronger evidence than repeating a single instrument setup at the same frequency.
