Chapter 1 · Initial values and propagated error

Derive an error bound for forward Euler and distinguish a poor approximation of a well-posed trajectory from uncertainty in the starting state.

From first principles

Follow one local error through the remaining steps

An initial-value problem specifies a rate \(y'=f(t,y)\) and a starting value. Integrating the rate over a short interval gives the exact change. Approximating the integrand by the rate at the left endpoint is forward Euler. Suppose the solution is twice differentiable with \(\lVert y''\rVert\le M\). Taylor’s theorem bounds the defect made by a single step starting at the exact value:

\[y(t_{n+1})=y(t_n)+h f(t_n,y(t_n))+d_n,\]
\[\lVert d_n\rVert\le Mh^2/2.\]

The next step generally starts at a computed value \(y_n\). If changing the input state changes the rate by at most \(L\) times that change in norm, then for \(e_n=y(t_n)-y_n\) subtraction gives

\[\lVert e_{n+1}\rVert\le(1+hL)\lVert e_n\rVert+Mh^2/2.\]

Each earlier defect can therefore be amplified by every later step. With exact initial data, sum the resulting geometric series up to fixed time \(T=nh\), for \(L>0\):

\[\lVert e_n\rVert\le \frac{Mh}{2L}\big[(1+hL)^n-1\big]\le\frac{Mh}{2L}(e^{LT}-1).\]

The local \(h^2\) becomes global \(h\) because the number of terms grows on refinement. When \(L=0\), their sum is \(MTh/2\). An uncertain initial value contributes a separate amplified initial-error term.

Derivation and applications

Estimate an endpoint error from two grids that share the same endpoint

A local defect need not equal the error in a reported final state. A practical test for a smooth nonstiff initial-value problem is to integrate to the identical physical time on two or more nested time grids. Suppose the method has global order \(p\) and its leading error varies smoothly with step size. Write \(Y_h\) for its value at the final time and \(y\) for the exact value there. The asymptotic expansion and its version on the refined grid are

\[Y_h=y+Ch^p+O(h^{p+1}),\]
\[Y_{h/2}=y+2^{-p}Ch^p+O(h^{p+1}).\]

Here \(C\) depends on the problem and final time but is common to these two runs. Subtract to estimate the error of the fine result. The sign convention below is correction to the fine result, not numerical value minus exact value:

\[y-Y_{h/2}\simeq\frac{Y_{h/2}-Y_h}{2^p-1},\]
\[Y_{\rm ext}=Y_{h/2}+\frac{Y_{h/2}-Y_h}{2^p-1}.\]

A third grid can test whether the nominal \(p\) applies. The unknown coefficient cancels in the ratio of successive differences:

\[p_{\rm obs}=\log_2\left|\frac{Y_h-Y_{h/2}}{Y_{h/2}-Y_{h/4}}\right|.\]

If two differences have incompatible signs or are close to roundoff, this diagnostic becomes unreliable. A nonsmooth switch, an approximate event location or comparing different physical final times can also destroy the shared expansion. The estimate concerns the specific endpoint output; a zero leading error there need not indicate higher order throughout the trajectory.

Worked example

Extrapolate a smooth decay calculation

For \(y'=-y\), \(y(0)=1\), integrate to \(t=1\) with Euler steps \(h=1/4\) and \(1/8\). The endpoint values are \((3/4)^4=0.316406\) and \((7/8)^8=0.343609\). For \(p=1\) the fine estimate needs a positive correction of \(0.027203\), giving \(Y_{\rm ext}=0.370812\). The exact value \(e^{-1}=0.367879\) lies much closer to the extrapolation than either raw value. Extrapolation removes the leading bias but does not make an exact solution.

Second application

Check the order before trusting an error bar

A different endpoint measurement returns \(1.2480\), \(1.2120\) and \(1.2030\) on successive halvings. Their differences are \(0.0360\) and \(0.0090\), yielding \(p_{\rm obs}=2.00\). The finest correction is \(-0.0090/(4-1)=-0.0030\), so the extrapolated endpoint is \(1.2000\). Using a first-order denominator would triple the estimated correction.

OneSimulation laboratory · Compare time-step error with conservation

Specify what the trajectory represents

In an initial-value problem \(y'=f(t,y)\), \(y(t_0)=y_0\), the time history follows from both the law and the initial state. A measurement made up to an absolute uncertainty gives a family of possible trajectories even before selecting a method. A numerical calculation adds further deviations each time it replaces the continuous flow over a short interval by an algebraic update. To estimate the final error, track how deviations arising early in a run are transported through later steps.

If \(f\) is continuous in time and locally Lipschitz in the state, the initial-value problem has a unique local solution. Lipschitz continuity is more than a technical detail. For \(y'=2\sqrt{|y|}\) with \(y(0)=0\), solutions may remain zero up to any chosen nonnegative time \(c\) and then follow \((t-c)^2\). Continuity alone gives no unique departure time. A code that keeps returning zero is computing one admissible trajectory, but there is no single exact departure trajectory against which to grade it without further modelling information.

Accumulate one-step errors

Write the exact integral from \(t_n\) to \(t_n+h\). Approximating the integrand by its value at the start gives \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n)\). Inserting an exact smooth solution in this update produces a one-step defect bounded by \(Mh^2/2\), where \(M\) bounds \(|y''|\) over the interval. This defect starts a step from the exact state. Actual steps generally start from an already perturbed state.

Let \(e_n=|y(t_n)-y_n|\), with state Lipschitz bound \(L\). Adding and subtracting a step started at the exact value gives \(e_{n+1}\leq(1+hL)e_n+Mh^2/2\). Iterate this inequality as a geometric series. For \(L>0\) and elapsed time \(T=nh\), use \((1+hL)^n\leq e^{LT}\) to conclude

\[e_n\leq e^{LT}e_0+\frac{Mh}{2L}(e^{LT}-1).\]

For \(L=0\) the second term becomes \(MTh/2\). A local error of order two gives order one over fixed time, while the initial uncertainty contributes separately. The bound treats every perturbation as if amplified. A dissipative system may damp them strongly, so a more problem-specific calculation can be much sharper.

Test the prediction against exact exponential growth

Take \(y'=y,\ y(0)=1\) over one time unit. Forward Euler with two steps gives \(1.5^2=2.25\); with four it gives \(1.25^4=2.44141\). The exact result is \(e=2.71828\). The errors 0.46828 and 0.27688 decrease by a factor about 1.69. The limiting factor of two requires a finer asymptotic regime. Expanding \(N\log(1+1/N)=1-1/(2N)+O(N^{-2})\) predicts \(e-(1+1/N)^N=e/(2N)+O(N^{-2})\).

If the initial value is actually 1.01, its exact trajectory at time one differs from the nominal one by \(0.01e\). Mesh refinement of the nominal solver cannot decide which initial reading is correct. To evaluate an integration scheme experimentally, fix the model and initial data, compare at a common endpoint and record the norm used. Resampling a coarse trajectory at extra plot positions changes the reported curve but does not alter the integration error accumulated at its original nodes.

Use a manufactured trajectory to test a solver

Instead of relying on a familiar unforced exponential, choose an exact function and derive the source that produces it. For \(y'+2y=s(t)\), selecting \(y_\star=1+\sin t\) requires \(s=\cos t+2(1+\sin t)\) and \(y(0)=1\). The forcing and damping are both active. A solver that silently evaluates the source at the wrong stage time can pass constant-source tests but fail this one.

Over one Euler step of 0.2 the initial derivative is one, so \(y_1=1.2\) versus the exact \(1+\sin(0.2)=1.19867\). This small first-step discrepancy is unusually favourable because \(y_\star''(0)=0\). It does not imply the global method has become second order. Report an error over a fixed time interval with multiple nontrivial phases of the forcing. A single specially located sample can suppress the leading contribution and give a misleading estimate.

Measure the defect of an entire reconstructed trajectory

Suppose a time integrator supplies a differentiable curve \(U(t)\) between its stored nodes. Its continuous defect is \(d(t)=U'(t)-f(t,U(t))\), the forcing that would make the reconstructed curve satisfy a perturbed differential equation exactly. For a scalar linear equation \(y'=a(t)y+b(t)\), the state error \(e=U-y\) satisfies \(e'=a(t)e+d(t)\). Multiplication by an integrating factor gives the exact identity

\[e(T)=\exp\!\left(\int_0^T a\right)e(0)+\int_0^T\exp\!\left(\int_s^T a(\xi)\,d\xi\right)d(s)\,ds.\]

This equation distinguishes defects by when they occur. In a dissipative equation with \(a\le-\alpha<0\), older defects receive exponentially smaller weights. If \(|d|\le D\), the resulting bound is \(e^{-\alpha T}|e(0)|+D(1-e^{-\alpha T})/\alpha\). Simply multiplying the worst defect by the duration misses this damping. For a growing mode the weighting reverses: even a modest defect introduced early can dominate the final state. A defect must be examined between nodes; sampling only locations where a collocation method enforces the equation can return zero by construction.

Check a polynomial approximation to a driven relaxation

Take \(y'=-2y+t,\ y(0)=0\), and on \([0,1]\) use the approximate curve \(U(t)=t^2/2\), obtained by integrating the input while ignoring damping. Then \(U'=t\), while the actual right-hand side at \(U\) is \(t-t^2\). The defect is \(d(t)=t^2\), zero initially and largest at the final time. Its sign shows that the approximate curve is receiving an artificial positive input. The crude bound with \(D=1,\alpha=2\) gives final error at most \((1-e^{-2})/2\approx0.4323\), already tighter than the undamped bound of one.

Solve the true equation with an integrating factor: \(y(t)=t/2-1/4+e^{-2t}/4\). At the endpoint the error is \(0.5-(0.25+e^{-2}/4)=(1-e^{-2})/4\approx0.2162\). Independently integrating the defect with the correct damping weight gives \(\int_0^1 e^{-2(1-s)}s^2ds=(1-e^{-2})/4\), exactly reproducing it. The difference between the two estimates has an interpretation: the simpler bound assumed the worst defect was present throughout, while the actual curve develops most of its error only near the end.

One can even use the defect to form a correction by solving the linear error equation and subtracting the estimated error from \(U\). For a nonlinear problem linearising around the reconstructed path introduces a new remainder and the correction is no longer exact. Still, it targets the right mechanism: identify where the reconstructed curve fails the original equation and propagate that imbalance to the particular observation time instead of judging a trajectory only by its nodal appearance.

The same forcing identity can distinguish two curves passing through identical initial and final data. One curve may solve the differential equation nearly everywhere, while another contains positive and negative defects whose weighted integrals cancel exactly at the final time. If the experiment also observes a midpoint, that cancellation no longer guarantees agreement there. A dense-output validation should therefore compare defect over the full interval and state error at the actual observation times, especially when a numerical interpolant is used for later event finding.

Chapter 2 · Runge–Kutta stages and order

Construct a two-stage second-order integrator from Taylor conditions and calculate how different stage choices affect autonomous and time-dependent problems.

From first principles

Choose a second slope so that the curvature term is correct

A rate at the start of a step gives a tangent. To see what a single tangent misses, differentiate \(y'=f(t,y)\) along the solution. Time changes the slope directly and the moving state changes it through the Jacobian \(f_y\). For a smooth right-hand side the exact increment begins

\[y(t+h)-y(t)=hf+\frac{h^2}{2}(f_t+f_y f)+O(h^3).\]

Construct a second slope at a fraction \(c\) of the interval. The exact intermediate state is unknown, so predict it with the first slope \(k_1=f(t,y)\). Expand the new evaluation:

\[k_2=f(t+ch,y+ch k_1)=f+ch(f_t+f_y f)+O(h^2).\]

Take a weighted average of both slopes, \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h(b_1 k_1+b_2 k_2)\). Matching the exact coefficients of \(h\) and \(h^2\) imposes two conditions:

\[b_1+b_2=1,\]
\[b_2c=\tfrac12 .\]

Choose \(c=1/2\) and the first slope guides the midpoint location while the second supplies the whole update. Choose \(c=1\) and the two slopes receive equal weights. Both constructions have local defect \(O(h^3)\); they can disagree at the next order because slope matching has imposed no condition on cubic terms.

Derivation and applications

Why two second-order Runge–Kutta formulas diverge on a nonlinear system

A linear decay test cannot distinguish every pair of Runge–Kutta methods. Compare a family of explicit two-stage schemes on a smooth autonomous scalar equation \(y'=f(y)\). Starting from the same \(y\), choose a nonzero stage location \(a\), compute \(k_1=f(y)\) and \(k_2=f(y+ahk_1)\), and update with weights \(b_1,b_2\). Expand the internal slope at the unperturbed state:

\[k_2=f+ah f'f+\tfrac12a^2h^2 f''f^2+O(h^3).\]

Insert this expansion into \(y_+=y+h(b_1k_1+b_2k_2)\). Matching the coefficients of \(h\) and \(h^2\) with the exact Taylor series requires \(b_1+b_2=1\) and \(ab_2=1/2\). The update for every such method then reads

\[y_+=y+hf+\tfrac12h^2f'f+\tfrac14ah^3f''f^2+O(h^4).\]

Differentiate the differential equation twice to obtain the exact cubic term. This isolates two distinct effects of nonlinearity and slope propagation:

\[y'''=f''f^2+(f')^2 f,\]
\[y(t+h)=y+hf+\tfrac12h^2f'f+\tfrac16h^3[f''f^2+(f')^2f]+O(h^4).\]

Changing \(a\) can adjust the coefficient of \(f''f^2\). No choice supplies the missing \((f')^2f\) term for arbitrary \(f\), explaining why a third independent stage is needed for a general third-order explicit method. When \(f\) is linear, \(f''=0\), so this two-stage family gives the same output regardless of \(a\).

Worked example

Compare one nonlinear growth step

For \(y'=y^2\), \(y(0)=1\) and \(h=0.100\), explicit midpoint uses \(a=1/2\) and \(y_+=1+0.1(1.05)^2=1.11025\). Heun uses \(a=1\), averages \(1\) and \((1.10)^2\), and gives \(1.11050\). The exact solution \(1/(1-t)\) yields \(1.11111\). Both underpredict; Heun happens to be closer on this step. That comparison alone does not establish a generally higher order.

Second application

Construct a test unable to separate the methods

With \(y'=-2y\), \(y(0)=3\) and \(h=0.100\), every second-order member has amplification \(1+z+z^2/2\) at \(z=-0.200\). The output is \(3(0.820)=2.46\). The exact result is \(3e^{-0.2}=2.45619\). Matching between midpoint and Heun in this test checks their shared polynomial but cannot test the implementation of their nonlinear stage terms.

Sample slopes without solving a new differential equation

Euler uses a single slope at the start of a step. If the direction changes significantly over the interval, a second evaluation at a predicted point can estimate that change. Consider \(k_1=f(t,y)\), \(k_2=f(t+ch,y+ahk_1)\) and \(y_{new}=y+h(b_1k_1+b_2k_2)\). The time and state arguments of a stage must both be specified. In a forced system, evaluating a source at the old time while using a midpoint state can remove the expected order.

Expand around the start of the step. The second stage is \(f+h(cf_t+af_y f)+O(h^2)\). The exact trajectory has \(y(t+h)=y+hf+h^2(f_t+f_y f)/2+O(h^3)\). Comparing coefficients gives \(b_1+b_2=1,\ b_2c=1/2,\ b_2a=1/2\). For the usual internally consistent stages \(a=c\). The explicit midpoint rule takes \(a=c=1/2,\ b_2=1\); the explicit trapezoidal or Heun rule takes \(a=c=1,\ b_1=b_2=1/2\). Both have order two globally under regularity and stable error propagation.

Compute two different approximations of equal order

For \(y'=y^2,\ y(0)=1\), use \(h=0.1\). The exact solution \(1/(1-t)\) gives 1.111111 at \(t=0.1\). Explicit midpoint predicts a midpoint value 1.05, evaluates slope \(1.05^2=1.1025\) there and returns \(1+0.1(1.1025)=1.11025\). Heun predicts an endpoint value 1.1, averages endpoint and initial slopes to obtain 1.105 and returns 1.11050. Both improve on Euler’s 1.1. Equal formal order allows different error constants, even for the same step size.

The exact solution develops a pole at \(t=1\). Approaching that time its derivatives grow and a step that was satisfactory at the start can perform poorly. This illustrates the dependence of all Taylor estimates on bounds over the interval of interest. A finite numerical value beyond the exact blow-up time is not automatically a valid continuation of the continuous solution. The physical or mathematical existence interval is part of any convergence claim.

Check what a higher-order stage sequence buys

Classical RK4 evaluates one initial slope, two midpoint slopes based on successive predictions and one endpoint slope. It averages them with weights \(1,2,2,1\) divided by six. For a constant linear scalar mode \(y'=\lambda y\), substitution gives \(R(z)=1+z+z^2/2+z^3/6+z^4/24\), \(z=h\lambda\). Its first missing exponential term has degree five. This test explains fourth order on that mode. General nonlinear fourth order requires additional stage conditions because derivatives such as \(f_{yy}(f,f)\) and \(f_y f_y f\) are independent.

Take \(\lambda=-1,\ h=1\) and \(y_0=1\). The four slopes are \(-1,-0.5,-0.75,-0.25\), yielding \(y_1=0.375\) against \(e^{-1}=0.36788\). The two-stage midpoint rule gives 0.5 at the same endpoint. Cost matters: a comparison at equal steps lets RK4 use twice as many right-hand-side evaluations as a two-stage scheme. At equal evaluation budgets one can instead compare one RK4 step with two midpoint steps. Over a complex trajectory it is the measured work for an agreed error tolerance that answers which method is economical.

Check a linear invariant at the internal stages

Suppose \(y=(A,B)\) and the right-hand side obeys \(f_A+f_B=0\) at every admissible state. At any explicitly constructed stage the vector of slopes still has zero component sum. In the Runge–Kutta update \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h\sum_j b_j k_j\), the sum of the increments therefore vanishes for any weights. Every method of this form preserves the linear invariant \(A+B\) in exact arithmetic, regardless of its order.

A protection against negative concentrations is a different requirement. With \(A'=-kA\) and \(kh=3\), explicit midpoint forms the internal value \(A_{\rm mid}=A_n(1-3/2)=-0.5A_n\) even though the final polynomial \(1-3+9/2\) is positive. A nonlinear rate law involving a logarithm of concentration would be undefined at this intermediate evaluation. Checking final stored states alone misses the problem. This is why a useful stage test examines both admissibility of intermediate evaluations and the conservation of the accepted update.

Compare work at a fixed endpoint

Comparing two Runge–Kutta schemes on the same step size need not compare the same computational budget. Record the number of right-hand-side evaluations and the actual endpoint error. When evaluating the model dominates all other costs, four one-stage steps and one four-stage step cost roughly the same, yet respond quite differently to curvature of the solution. Some implementations reuse a stage across accepted steps; count evaluations actually made.

Derive implicit stages from polynomial collocation

Let \(U(\theta)\) denote a polynomial approximation to \(y(t_n+h\theta)\) on one time slab, with \(U(0)=y_n\). At \(s\) distinct nodes \(c_i\), require \(U'(c_i)=h f(t_n+hc_i,U(c_i))=hK_i\). A polynomial \(U\) of degree at most \(s\) has a derivative determined by those \(s\) values. With cardinal polynomials \(\ell_j\) through the nodes, integration gives \(U(\theta)=y_n+h\sum_j K_j\int_0^\theta\ell_j(r)\,dr\). Evaluating at the nodes and at the endpoint turns a polynomial matching problem into a Runge–Kutta scheme:

\[a_{ij}=\int_0^{c_i}\ell_j(r)\,dr,\qquad b_j=\int_0^1\ell_j(r)\,dr,\qquad K_i=f(t_n+hc_i,y_n+h\sum_j a_{ij}K_j).\]

The stage values generally depend on one another and must be solved simultaneously. With the single midpoint node this gives the implicit midpoint step, since \(\ell_1=1\), \(a_{11}=1/2\) and \(b_1=1\). With two Gaussian nodes \(c_{1,2}=1/2\mp\sqrt3/6\), integrating their linear cardinal functions gives weights \(1/2,1/2\), diagonal entries \(1/4\), and off-diagonal entries \(1/4-\sqrt3/6,\ 1/4+\sqrt3/6\) in row order.

For \(y'=\lambda y\), write \(z=h\lambda\) and \(Y={\bf1}y_n+z A Y\) for the stage states. Solving this two-by-two system and substituting in \(y_{n+1}=y_n+z(Y_1+Y_2)/2\) gives amplification \((1+z/2+z^2/12)/(1-z/2+z^2/12)\). For \(y_0=1,\lambda=-3,h=1\), the result is \(1/13\approx0.07692\), against \(e^{-3}\approx0.04979\). The endpoint formula is fourth order as \(h\to0\). For arbitrarily large negative \(z\) this ratio tends to one. Accurate integration of smooth resolved trajectories and rapid removal of unresolved stiff modes remain distinct design goals.

Match the two independent third-order terms

For an autonomous smooth vector field write \(J=f_y\) and \(f''\) for its second derivative. Differentiating \(y'=f(y)\) twice gives \(y''=Jf\) and \(y'''=f''(f,f)+J(Jf)\). A third-order step has to reproduce both distinct vector expressions. For Runge–Kutta stages \(k_i=f(y+h\sum_j a_{ij}k_j)\), put \(c_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\). Stage expansion gives \(k_i=f+h c_iJf+h^2[\sum_j a_{ij}c_j J(Jf)+(c_i^2/2) f''(f,f)]+O(h^3)\). Multiplying by the output weights \(hb_i\) and comparing independent Taylor terms gives the conditions.

Besides \(\sum b_i=1\) and \(\sum b_ic_i=1/2\), they are \(\sum b_i c_i^2=1/3\) and \(\sum_{ij}b_i a_{ij}c_j=1/6\). The first additional condition concerns curvature of the vector field along a displacement; the second concerns propagation of one derivative through another stage. A single test equation with linear \(f\) makes the curvature term vanish, so matching an exponential stability polynomial cannot alone certify third order on general nonlinear systems. Smoothness over the region visited by all stages underlies the Taylor argument.

Verify a three-stage method on nonlinear growth

Use the stages \(k_1=f(y)\), \(k_2=f(y+hk_1/2)\), \(k_3=f(y-hk_1+2hk_2)\) and output \(y+h(k_1+4k_2+k_3)/6\). The stage abscissae are \(0,1/2,1\). The squared-abscissa sum is \((4/6)(1/4)+(1/6)=1/3\). For the nested derivative only the third stage contributes: \(b_3 a_{32}c_2=(1/6)(2)(1/2)=1/6\). The two lower-order conditions are checked by summing the weights and their first moments. Together these calculations show why the intermediate stages are arranged this way.

Take \(y'=y^2,\ y(0)=1,\ h=0.1\). The exact value is \(1/(1-h)=1.111111\ldots\). The stages are \(k_1=1,\ k_2=1.05^2=1.1025\), and \(k_3=(1-0.1+0.2\cdot1.1025)^2=1.1205^2=1.25552025\). Their weighted combination gives \(y_1=1+0.1(1+4.41+1.25552025)/6\approx1.11109200\). The local error is about \(-1.91\times10^{-5}\), small despite appreciable curvature in the vector field. The stages themselves are sampling states for evaluating slopes; their individual values are not third-order predictions at those times.

Chapter 3 · Absolute stability and stiffness

Determine the step sizes that damp a decaying mode and explain why fast, physically uninteresting modes can dominate the cost of explicit integration.

From first principles

Find a stability limit directly from repeated multiplication

A small perturbation around an equilibrium often satisfies a linear equation \(y'=\lambda y\). Begin with real \(\lambda<0\), for which the continuous value is multiplied by \(e^{h\lambda}\), strictly between zero and one. Apply Euler’s left- and right-endpoint approximations to the integral of this same rate. Let \(z=h\lambda\):

\[y_{n+1}=y_n+z y_n,\]
\[y_{n+1}=y_n+z y_{n+1}.\]

Solve each update for its new value and iterate \(n\) steps. Persistence of roundoff or initial error is governed by the same powers:

\[y_n=R(z)^n y_0,\]
\[R_{\rm forward}=1+z,\]
\[R_{\rm backward}=(1-z)^{-1}.\]

For strict decay, \(|R|<1\) is necessary. Forward Euler on this real mode requires \(-2<z<0\), and avoiding sign alternation further requires \(z\ge-1\). Backward Euler damps every negative real \(z\), even when the step is too large to approximate a transient accurately. With modes at \(-1\) and \(-1000\) s⁻¹, the fastest mode restricts a forward step to less than 0.002 s even after that transient has become tiny. This mismatch between stability and the resolution needed for the slow observable is the source of stiffness in this example.

Derivation and applications

Separate unconditional stability from damping of an unresolved mode

A stiff dissipative mode may have decayed almost completely during a single time step. Merely keeping its computed amplitude bounded is then a weak requirement. Examine the family of one-step formulas that weights the old and new slopes with \(1-\theta\) and \(\theta\). On the scalar test equation \(y'=-\lambda y\), \(\lambda>0\), define the dimensionless decay over a step as \(r=h\lambda\). Collecting the new value on the left gives

\[y_{n+1}=y_n-r[(1-\theta)y_n+\theta y_{n+1}],\]
\[G(r)=\frac{y_{n+1}}{y_n}=\frac{1-(1-\theta)r}{1+\theta r}.\]

The denominator is positive for \(\theta\ge0\). The amplification never exceeds one from above; enforcing \(G\ge-1\) supplies the other half of the real-axis stability condition:

\[|G|\le1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2+(2\theta-1)r\ge0 .\]

Thus all positive step sizes are stable along the negative real eigenvalue axis for \(\theta\ge1/2\). Now investigate what actually appears in the stored solution when the mode is unresolved:

\[\lim_{r\to\infty}G(r)=-\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}\quad(\theta>0),\]
\[\lim_{r\to\infty}e^{-r}=0 .\]

The trapezoidal choice \(\theta=1/2\) approaches a sign-reversing factor of unit magnitude. Backward Euler \(\theta=1\) suppresses the fast mode. These properties govern the handling of transients; the order of approximation for a smooth, resolved mode is a separate question.

Worked example

Check a relaxation after one coarse step

Take \(\lambda=1000\) s⁻¹, \(h=0.100\) s and an initial perturbation of \(1.00\) K. The exact remnant is \(e^{-100}\) K, negligible. Trapezoidal stepping leaves \((1-50)/(1+50)=-0.961\) K, while backward Euler leaves \(1/101=0.00990\) K. After two trapezoidal steps the perturbation becomes positive again at \(0.923\) K. That oscillatory persistence is numerical, even though the amplification satisfies the stability condition.

Second application

Choose a step for monotonically decreasing transients

Suppose a solver uses \(\theta=0.750\) and should preserve positivity for a spectrum of decay rates up to \(400\) s⁻¹. The numerator of \(G\) requires \(1-0.250h\lambda\ge0\), hence \(h\le0.0100\) s. A \(0.0200\) s step would remain stable on every mode but gives \(G=(1-2)/(1+6)=-1/7\) for the fastest one. A positivity requirement can impose a finite time step even when boundedness does not.

Examine a single amplification factor

For the real decay problem \(y'=-\lambda y\), \(\lambda>0\), the physical solution multiplies by \(e^{-h\lambda}\) over a step. A linear one-step method replaces this by \(R(z)\), with \(z=-h\lambda\). Absolute stability at \(z\) means repeated multiplication does not amplify magnitude, conventionally \(|R(z)|\leq1\). A truly decaying mode requires a strict inequality for numerical damping. Positivity and phase fidelity impose additional requirements beyond this magnitude test.

Forward Euler has \(R=1+z\). In the complex plane the stability region is the disk of radius one centred at \(-1\). On the real negative axis, decay requires \(0<h\lambda<2\); nonnegative decay requires \(h\lambda\leq1\). Backward Euler has \(R=1/(1-z)\), and the trapezoidal method has \(R=(1+z/2)/(1-z/2)\). Both contain the entire left half-plane in their stability regions. As \(z\) becomes large and negative, backward Euler tends to zero while trapezoidal tends to minus one. The former strongly damps unresolved fast components; the latter can keep them as alternating numerical transients.

Follow a slow and a fast component together

Let \(u'=-u,\ v'=-1000v\), with \(u(0)=v(0)=1\). At \(t=0.1\) the exact slow state is 0.90484, while the fast state is \(e^{-100}\), effectively zero on a plot at unit scale. Suppose the slow state is the desired observable. One forward Euler step of 0.1 s gives \(u=0.9\) but \(v=-99\). The method becomes unstable even though the desired slow response is well resolved. To damp the fast mode explicitly, \(h\) must stay below 0.002 s whenever that mode can be excited.

Backward Euler with \(h=0.1\) gives \(u=1/1.1=0.90909\) and \(v=1/101=0.00990\). Trapezoidal gives \(u=0.95/1.05=0.90476\) and \(v=-49/51=-0.96078\). The second-order rule is especially accurate on the slow mode but retains a conspicuous oscillatory fast component. Neither the maximum order nor a broad stability region alone identifies the right integrator. The observable, acceptable transient error and stiffness all enter the decision.

Trace the cost back to the model

A diffusion equation discretised on a spatial mesh produces modes with rates up to order \(D/h_x^2\). Halving spatial spacing roughly quadruples the magnitude of the fastest eigenvalue. An explicit time step chosen for a coarse grid can therefore fail after spatial refinement, even if the initial physical signal looks smooth. Small high-frequency components from boundary changes, rounding or nonlinear interactions may excite modes absent from an idealised initial state.

Implicit stability shifts work into linear or nonlinear systems inside each time step. An oversized implicit step can miss a forcing pulse altogether, or produce a poor slow-phase estimate despite decaying every homogeneous mode. Compare the output on two time grids, distinguish the time required to resolve a source from the time required merely for stability, and inspect at least one fast component when interpreting unexpected oscillations. The distinction is visible in a reaction where exact species totals coexist with substantial errors in the individual states. Compare reaction histories under Euler and RK4.

Explain why a tiny initial fast component can still matter

Take the same two-rate model but start with \(v_0=10^{-12}\) and apply forward Euler with \(h=0.01\). The physical fast mode should disappear almost immediately. The numerical factor is \(1-10=-9\), so after twelve steps its magnitude is \(9^{12}10^{-12}\simeq0.282\). An initial disturbance that was invisible in the output has become comparable with the desired slow signal in little physical time.

If the fast variable started exactly at zero in a perfectly decoupled model, exact arithmetic would keep it zero. That special trajectory would give a misleading sense of stability. Roundoff, forcing or a weak coupling can generate a nonzero fast component at later stages. A robust experiment deliberately perturbs fast directions and examines whether numerical damping agrees with the model under those perturbations. This is especially relevant when a mesh refinement adds high-frequency modes that were absent from the coarser spectrum.

Separate damping of a perturbation from tracking a moving state

A useful stiff test has an exact slow path \(g(t)\) embedded in \(y'=\lambda(y-g(t))+g'(t)\), with \(\lambda<0\). Subtracting \(g\) gives \((y-g)'=\lambda(y-g)\). An initial condition on the path stays on it, whereas an off-path disturbance decays exponentially. A numerical method must therefore address two different tasks: damp erroneous components perpendicular to the slow path and follow the path as it moves. A homogeneous stability test measures only the first.

Apply backward Euler and define \(e_n=y_n-g(t_n)\). Subtract \(g(t_{n+1})\) after the implicit update. The error satisfies \((1-h\lambda)e_{n+1}=e_n+h g'(t_{n+1})-[g(t_{n+1})-g(t_n)]\). The last two terms are the error in approximating the change of \(g\) using its new endpoint derivative. Even with \(e_n=0\), this forcing can create error at the next time. The factor \(1/(1-h\lambda)\) damps both old error and newly created defect. Increasing stiffness changes their balance, while changing the time step alters both the source and its propagation.

Track a quadratic path with a fast relaxation

Take \(g=t^2,\ \lambda=-100\) per second, \(h=0.1\) s, \(e_0=0\). Since \(h(2t_{n+1})-(t_{n+1}^2-t_n^2)=h^2\), the recurrence is \(e_{n+1}=(e_n+0.01)/11\). The first error is \(0.00090909\), so the first computed state is 0.01090909 when the exact value is 0.01. The second error is approximately 0.00099174. Solving the scalar recurrence shows that the error tends to 0.001, the fixed point of \(11e=e+0.01\). Stability does not force it to vanish because each step creates a new tracking defect.

For comparison, trapezoidal integration of the slow-path derivative integrates the linear function \(g'=2t\) exactly over each interval. Substituting the exact nodal values \(g(t_n)\) therefore satisfies its full discrete equations, so an exactly initialized trajectory stays on this particular path. However, a small off-path perturbation is multiplied per step by \((1-5)/(1+5)=-2/3\), whereas backward Euler multiplies it by \(1/11\). Thus one method is exact on the specified moving path and the other damps unrelated fast perturbations more rapidly.

A numerical test that initialises precisely on \(g\) and uses only this quadratic path could make the trapezoidal method appear flawless. Add a known small perturbation and measure its sign and amplitude separately. Conversely a test with \(g=0\) detects damping but cannot expose a tracking defect. Both controlled initialisations are needed to tell whether an observed error arises from resolving the moving forcing or dissipating the artificially fast component.

A final scaling experiment holds the stiffness fixed and halves the step. The long-time tracking error in this quadratic case is \(h/|\lambda|\), obtained by cancelling the fixed-point terms in the recurrence. With \(h=0.05\) and the same relaxation rate it becomes 0.0005. A one-step error alone, evaluated from the exact state on each grid, would not reveal the same accumulation mechanism. Chapter 4 · Multistep formulas and parasitic modes

Derive Adams–Bashforth and BDF formulas from interpolation, test a characteristic polynomial and determine whether starting errors survive refinement.

From first principles

Integrate the straight line through two past slopes

A one-step method evaluates new slopes inside its current interval. A multistep method can instead reuse the recorded rates \(f_n=f(t_n,y_n)\) and \(f_{n-1}\). On a uniform mesh, approximate the rate by the straight line through these two values. At a future offset \(s\) from \(t_n\), extrapolation gives

\[p(s)=f_n+\frac{s}{h}(f_n-f_{n-1}),\]
\[0\le s\le h.\]

Integrating this line gives one area \(hf_n\) from the constant part and a triangle from its change. The two-step Adams–Bashforth update follows:

\[y_{n+1}=y_n+\int_0^h p(s)\,ds=y_n+\frac h2(3f_n-f_{n-1}).\]

For smooth slopes the pointwise interpolation error is \(O(h^2)\) here, so integration leaves \(O(h^3)\) local defect with exact previous data. Reusing history also means more ways for an error to propagate. Test the recurrence at zero rate by inserting \(y_n=\xi^n\):

\[\xi^2-\xi=0,\]
\[\xi=1,\ 0 .\]

One mode carries a constant initial offset and the other vanishes. More general multistep formulas require all such roots inside or on the unit circle and simple roots on the circle for zero stability. A starting method must still supply a sufficiently accurate \(y_1\).

Derivation and applications

Identify the physical and parasitic components of a two-step calculation

A two-step formula needs an extra starting value. Even a zero differential equation can reveal how an inaccurate start persists. Begin with a uniform grid and the second-order backward differentiation formula. Compute the unique quadratic interpolant through the most recent three values, differentiate at the newest point, and equate this derivative to the new slope. On the constant equation \(y'=0\) this gives

\[(3y_{n+1}-4y_n+y_{n-1})/(2h)=0.\]

To determine which patterns in the history survive, insert \(y_n=r^n\) and cancel \(r^{n-1}\). The polynomial and its two independent modes are

\[3r^2-4r+1=(r-1)(3r-1)=0,\]
\[y_n=A+B(1/3)^n .\]

The unit root represents a constant exact solution; the second root is an unrelated decaying numerical mode. Suppose the exact initial value is \(Y\), but the supplied second value is \(Y+\delta\). At indices zero and one, solve for both constants:

\[A+B=Y,\]
\[A+B/3=Y+\delta,\]
\[y_n=Y+\tfrac32\delta[1-(1/3)^n].\]

The transient numerical component dies out, but the startup error changes the persistent constant. This illustrates the root condition for zero stability: all roots must have magnitude at most one and those on the unit circle must be simple. It does not guarantee that an arbitrarily inaccurate initializer has the intended accuracy.

Worked example

Track the effect of a faulty starting value

For an exact constant signal \(Y=2.000\) and a second reading \(2.006\), \(\delta=0.006\). The next value is \((4\times2.006-2.000)/3=2.008\). The limiting constant is \(2.009\). Reducing the time step while keeping the same fixed initializer bias leaves this limiting offset unchanged, regardless of the formal second-order truncation error.

Second application

Compare an undamped parasitic root

The centred two-step formula \((y_{n+1}-y_{n-1})/(2h)=0\) has roots \(+1\) and \(-1\). With the same initial pair, even values stay \(2.000\) and odd values \(2.006\). The sequence is bounded but alternates indefinitely. Averaging adjacent values gives \(2.003\) and conceals the oscillation in a plot. Checking the even and odd subsequences distinguishes a persistent computational mode from a physically varying solution.

Reuse information from earlier steps

A Runge–Kutta method constructs within-step stage values and can usually start from a single known initial state. A multistep method instead keeps a short history of states or slopes. Once the history exists, it can attain higher order with fewer new function evaluations per step. Startup, changes of step size and discontinuities need special care because the coefficients embody assumptions about where past data lie.

For a uniform step \(h\), approximate \(f\) on \([t_n,t_{n+1}]\) by the line through \(f_{n-1}\) and \(f_n\). In the forward interval the line is \(f_n+s(f_n-f_{n-1})/h\), with \(s=t-t_n\). Integrating from zero to \(h\) gives \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h(3f_n-f_{n-1})/2\), the two-step Adams–Bashforth method. The negative weight results from extrapolating a straight line beyond its data. The formula integrates a linearly varying right-hand side exactly if the previous states and slopes are exact.

Differentiate a polynomial at the new point

A backward differentiation formula instead interpolates the recent state values. Differentiating the quadratic through \(y_{n-1},y_n,y_{n+1}\) at \(t_{n+1}\) gives \((3y_{n+1}-4y_n+y_{n-1})/(2h)\). Equating this to \(f(t_{n+1},y_{n+1})\) defines BDF2. To check the coefficients without constructing the full polynomial, substitute the constants, linear and quadratic monomials centred at \(t_{n+1}\). Their derivatives at the evaluation point are \(0,1,0\), respectively, which gives three linear conditions for the three weights.

For \(y'=-y\) with step 0.2 and exact starts \(y_0=1,y_1=e^{-0.2}=0.81873\), BDF2 gives \(y_2=(4y_1-y_0)/(3+0.4)=0.66910\). Adams–Bashforth gives \(y_2=y_1+0.1(-3y_1+1)=0.67311\). The exact value at 0.4 is 0.67032. Exact starting data isolate the behaviour of the multistep formula. In a practical code, a single-step method of adequate order creates the first value. The starting error becomes an input disturbance for the recurrence.

Inspect zero stability before trusting the order

For \(y'=0\) with no new forcing, BDF2 obeys \(3y_{n+1}-4y_n+y_{n-1}=0\). Try a sequence \(y_n=\zeta^n\); the characteristic roots are \(1,1/3\). One root represents the constant physical solution, and the other is a decaying parasitic component. In a general constant-step linear multistep formula all characteristic roots must satisfy \(|\zeta|\leq1\) and those on the unit circle must be simple for zero stability. A repeated root at one would admit a term proportional to \(n\) and hence unbounded amplification of a fixed initial mismatch as \(h\) decreases at fixed physical time.

Zero stability controls propagation over many small steps near \(h=0\). Absolute stability addresses a given finite \(h\lambda\) for a dynamical test mode. A formula may satisfy the first and still require a restrictive explicit step for the second. With consistency and a Lipschitz right-hand side, zero stability is the condition that converts small local defects and sufficiently accurate starts into convergence on fixed intervals. After a state reset or a discontinuity, reconstruct the history from the new side rather than extrapolating slopes belonging to a different evolution law.

Change the step without silently changing the order

If the preceding step had length \(h_p\) and the proposed step has length \(h\), the slope of the line through the two old right-hand sides is \((f_n-f_{n-1})/h_p\). Integrating that line over the proposed interval gives \(h f_n+h^2(f_n-f_{n-1})/(2h_p)\). The constant-step coefficients \(3/2,-1/2\) are recovered only when \(h=h_p\). For \(h=h_p/2\), the new weights multiplying \(h\) are \(5/4,-1/4\).

As a numerical check, integrate \(y'=t\) starting at \(t_n=1\) with \(h_p=0.2,\ h=0.1\). Slopes are 1 and 0.8, so the variable-step formula predicts an increment \(0.1(1.25-0.25\cdot0.8)=0.105\), exactly \(\int_1^{1.1}t\,dt\). Reusing constant-step coefficients would give 0.11. Adjusting only the time interval in the integrator while leaving coefficients fixed can therefore introduce a measurable error even on linear forcing.

After an abrupt change of forcing

Suppose an input is switched from zero to one exactly at a recorded time. A two-step formula mixing a pre-switch derivative with the new value is extrapolating across a discontinuity. On the first step wholly after the switch, the true integral of the input is exactly \(h\), whereas the old and new samples in Adams–Bashforth give \(3h/2\). Restarting with a suitable one-step update reconstructs a history belonging to the new smooth segment.

An implicit formula is only as accurate as its correction solve

An implicit linear multistep method has an equation \(y_{n+1}=a+h\beta f(t_{n+1},y_{n+1})\), where \(a\) includes old states and old slopes. A predictor supplies a first iterate \(v_0\). Functional correction uses \(v_{j+1}=a+h\beta f(t_{n+1},v_j)\). If \(f\) is Lipschitz in its state with constant \(L\), differences contract whenever \(h|\beta|L<1\). Under that condition \(j\) corrections reduce an initial error by at least the corresponding geometric factor. This is a restriction on a particular inner solver and can be much more severe than the stability condition for the time formula itself.

For \(f=\lambda y\), the fixed-point correction has error multiplier \(h\beta\lambda\), even though solving the implicit time equation exactly produces a rational amplification factor. A single correction from an explicit predictor replaces that rational response by a polynomial. Accordingly, tables of stability regions for exactly solved formulas cannot be quoted unchanged when an implementation uses a fixed small number of functional corrections. Newton or a direct solve can restore the intended implicit update, provided its own arithmetic error is sufficiently controlled.

Compare a predictor, one correction and an exact implicit step

Consider \(y'=-4y,\ y_n=1,\ h=1\). The trapezoidal implicit equation is \(y_{n+1}=1-2(1+y_{n+1})\), giving \(3y_{n+1}=-1\) and \(y_{n+1}=-1/3\). For comparison the true value after the interval is \(e^{-4}\approx0.0183\). The exactly solved trapezoidal update remains bounded but oscillatory. Forward Euler prediction gives \(v_0=1-4=-3\). One functional trapezoidal correction yields \(v_1=-1-2(-3)=5\), and another gives \(v_2=-1-10=-11\).

Relative to the implicit solution the correction errors are \(-8/3,\ 16/3,\ -32/3\). Their absolute magnitudes double after every correction, precisely as predicted by \(|h\lambda|/2=2\). The linear residual of the intended equation at the once-corrected value five is \(3(5)+1=16\). Reporting this as the result of an implicit stable solver would hide a far larger failure than the known phase error of the exactly solved trapezoidal rule. The inner iteration has failed to find the state required by the time method.

At \(h=0.1\) the correction multiplier instead becomes −0.2. The implicit update is \(2/3\), while the explicit predictor is 0.6. One correction gives \(0.8-0.2(0.6)=0.68\), and another gives 0.664. Monitoring the actual implicit-equation residual now documents successful contraction. For nonlinear multistep implementations use the same principle: record whether each implicit equation was solved to accuracy compatible with the local time error before interpreting convergence under time refinement.

One can also solve the scalar implicit equation by Newton iteration. Here its derivative with respect to the unknown is the constant three at \(h=1\), so one Newton correction from any starting guess recovers \(-1/3\) exactly. The comparison demonstrates a failure of a particular nonlinear iteration, while the mathematical time formula itself is well defined and has a unique implicit solution. More generally stiffness makes the choice of inner solver central to total runtime and reliability.

Problem sheet 2 · Damping and multistep memory

Locate a stability region, compare two implicit formulas in the stiff limit and test whether a recurrence transports initial errors consistently.

Separate positivity from decay

A mode obeys \(y'=-4y\). Starting at one, find the first two forward Euler values for steps 0.2, 0.35 and 0.55. Classify the histories by sign and decay, and find the exact positive-step threshold for each property.

Worked solution

The three factors \(1-4h\) are 0.2, −0.4 and −1.2. The first two values are respectively \((0.2,0.04)\), \((-0.4,0.16)\) and \((-1.2,1.44)\). The first sequence is nonnegative and decreasing, the second sign-changing with decreasing magnitude, and the third sign-changing with growing magnitude.

For positive steps, nonnegative propagation requires \(h\leq0.25\); decay of magnitudes requires \(h<0.5\). At 0.5 the amplitude remains one while the sign alternates.

Switch off a stiff transient

At a proposed step, a fast mode has \(z=-50\). Compute its factors under backward Euler and the trapezoidal method, and the magnitude after two steps. Which would you use when the desired late-time response should no longer contain the fast mode?

Worked solution

Backward Euler gives \(1/51=0.01961\), hence \(1/2601=0.0003845\) after two steps. Trapezoidal gives \((1-25)/(1+25)=-12/13\), hence \(144/169=0.8521\) in magnitude after two steps. The physical magnitude is \(e^{-100}\).

Backward Euler suppresses this unresolved transient much more strongly. If the early transient itself were the observable, reducing the step would remain necessary even with the more strongly damping formula.

Diagnose a parasitic solution

For \(y'=0\), a proposed two-step recurrence gives \(y_{n+2}-2y_{n+1}+y_n=0\). Start at \(y_0=1,y_1=1+\epsilon\). Obtain \(y_n\) and compare with BDF2 from the same values. Which can bound the propagated starting error independently of step count?

Worked solution

The characteristic polynomial is \((z-1)^2\). Solving for the constants in \(a+bn\) gives \(y_n=1+n\epsilon\); the starting error grows linearly with step count.

BDF2 gives \(y_n=a+b(1/3)^n\). The initial conditions yield \(b=-3\epsilon/2\), \(a=1+3\epsilon/2\). Its departure from one stays bounded by \(1.5|\epsilon|\) for all \(n\). Two simple roots behave differently from a repeated unit root even when all root magnitudes are at most one.

Chapter 5 · Adaptive steps and event locations

Estimate local integration error with a low–high order pair, choose a dimensionally consistent tolerance and bracket a zero of an event function in time.

From first principles

Obtain a step controller from the measured error of two approximations

Take a proposed step \(h\) twice from the same current state, once using an order-\(p\) formula and once using a more accurate formula whose leading defect is one order smaller. For smooth data and sufficiently short steps the two values have a common exact target \(y_*\). Their expansion gives

\[y_{\rm low}=y_*+Ch^{p+1}+O(h^{p+2}),\]
\[y_{\rm high}=y_*+O(h^{p+2}),\]
\[d=y_{\rm low}-y_{\rm high}.\]

The difference estimates the leading local error of the lower-order formula. It has the units of the state. Scale each component by an absolute tolerance with those units plus a dimensionless relative tolerance times the current magnitude:

\[r=\max_i\frac{|d_i|}{{\rm atol}_i+{\rm rtol}\max(|y_{n,i}|,|y_{{\rm high},i}|)} .\]

If \(r\le1\) the proposed step passes this error estimate. Provided its leading coefficient varies slowly, a new step should change the estimated error by the ratio of step sizes raised to \(p+1\). Targeting unit scaled error gives

\[h_{\rm new}=s\,h\,r^{-1/(p+1)},\]
\[0<s<1 .\]

The safety factor leaves room for variation in the neglected terms. A real implementation bounds growth and handles \(r=0\) separately. A discontinuity or an event between endpoints can violate the smooth-error assumption even when the endpoints appear accurate.

Derivation and applications

How component tolerances become a single step decision

A time step acceptable for one component can be inaccurate for another whose magnitude or units differ. Consider an embedded pair of methods sharing their internal stages and producing two endpoint estimates \(y_i^{H},y_i^{L}\). Let \(d_i=y_i^H-y_i^L\). For a smooth right-hand side and a pair whose lower formula has order \(p\), this difference is normally dominated by an \(h^{p+1}\) local term. Assume all components use the same step but have physically appropriate absolute scales \(a_i>0\) and relative tolerances \(r_i\).

Convert each difference to a dimensionless ratio using the larger recent endpoint magnitude. An infinity norm requires every component to meet its own allowance:

\[s_i=a_i+r_i\max(|y_{i,n}|,|y^H_i|),\]
\[E=\max_i |d_i|/s_i.\]

With approximately constant leading error coefficient and scale over the proposed retry, the new norm is predicted from the power law. Solving for a target near one and including a safety factor \(\nu<1\) gives

\[E_{\rm new}\simeq E(h_{\rm new}/h)^{p+1},\]
\[h_{\rm new}=\nu h E^{-1/(p+1)} .\]

Accept and advance with the higher-order estimate only when \(E\le1\) under this policy. If a component is converted to another unit by a factor \(c\), both its measured difference and absolute tolerance must scale:

\[|c d_i|/(|c|a_i+r_i|c|\max|y_i|)=|d_i|/s_i.\]

The decision is therefore invariant under consistent unit conversion. A bounded growth factor handles zero estimated differences and prevents a single unusually small defect from triggering a huge next step. This controller targets local agreement; its acceptance is not a proof of a global error bound.

Worked example

Decide a two-component retry

Let an order-two embedded lower estimate at \(h=0.100\) s produce differences \(0.000800\) m and \(0.00600\) m/s. The chosen component scales are \(0.00100\) m and \(0.00200\) m/s. Their ratios are \(0.800\) and \(3.00\). Reject the step and take \(\nu=0.9\), giving \(h_{\rm new}=0.9(0.100)3^{-1/3}=0.0624\) s. The leading-error model predicts a new norm \(0.9^3=0.729\).

Second application

Compare a componentwise limit with an averaged one

Suppose a system has 100 components, with 99 normalized errors zero and one equal to \(3.00\). Its root-mean-square norm is \(\sqrt{9/100}=0.300\), so an RMS policy would accept. The infinity policy rejects because one variable missed its tolerance threefold. An RMS policy can be appropriate when errors are distributed, while a strict component requirement demands the maximum or a separate check for a critical variable.

Ask different parts of a trajectory for different resolutions

A cooling transient changes rapidly at first and slowly near equilibrium. A uniform step small enough for the beginning can waste effort later. An adaptive controller proposes a step, estimates its error and accepts or rejects it. To estimate the error without the exact solution, two formulas can share function evaluations. With Euler and Heun, compute \(k_1=f(t_n,y_n)\), predict \(y_E=y_n+hk_1\), then evaluate \(k_2=f(t_n+h,y_E)\) and \(y_H=y_n+h(k_1+k_2)/2\).

Their difference \(y_H-y_E=h(k_2-k_1)/2\) estimates the leading local error of the lower-order Euler step. It normally scales as \(h^2\) in smooth regions. An embedded difference does not equal the local error of the higher-order accepted value; the higher-order value generally has a smaller error starting from accurate data. Nor does a local tolerance directly bound the error at the end of the whole run. Previous accepted steps already carry accumulated trajectory error.

Scale the estimate and choose a step

For a scalar use \(w=\mathrm{atol}+\mathrm{rtol}\max(|y_n|,|y_H|)\) and the dimensionless estimate \(E=|y_H-y_E|/w\). Accept when \(E\leq1\). If the next difference scales as \(h^2\), a proposed change is \(h_{new}=0.9h E^{-1/2}\). The factor 0.9 leaves a margin for changes in curvature. Practical implementations limit the ratio between successive steps and handle a zero difference by bounded growth. A rejected step is retried from the same original state and time.

For \(y'=-2y,\ y_n=1,\ h=0.1\), Euler gives 0.8 and Heun gives 0.82, a difference 0.02. With absolute tolerance 0.005 and zero relative tolerance for this demonstration, \(E=4\). The step is rejected and a revised step is \(0.9(0.1)/2=0.045\). Re-evaluation from the original state gives \(y_E=0.91,\ y_H=0.91405\), with difference 0.00405 and \(E=0.81\). The accepted state corresponds to time 0.045, not 0.145. Advancing time on rejection would skip the unresolved portion.

Estimate error using one full step and two half steps

A solver without an embedded pair can estimate its local error by applying the same order-\(p\) stepper twice from a common starting state: take one full step for \(Y_c\), and separately two successive half steps for \(Y_f\). Suppose the local error for one smooth step, numerical minus exact, is \(C h^{p+1}+O(h^{p+2})\). The two half-step errors accumulate to \(2C(h/2)^{p+1}+O(h^{p+2})\) because propagating the first over the short second half changes its leading coefficient only at higher order. Thus \(Y_f\) carries approximately \(2^{-p}\) of the full-step error.

Subtracting the two approximations gives an estimate of the correction to \(Y_f\): \((Y_f-Y_c)/(2^p-1)\). Adding it gives a Richardson-extrapolated value. The exponent in a local step controller remains \(1/(p+1)\) because the correction estimate scales as \(h^{p+1}\). The denominator \(2^p-1\) comes from taking two shorter steps to the same endpoint. Confusing that denominator with a single half-step local error factor introduces a systematic error in the estimate.

Take Heun's second-order method on \(y'=-y\), starting from one with \(h=0.4\). The full-step amplification is \(1-0.4+0.4^2/2=0.68\). Each half step has amplification \(0.82\), so \(Y_f=0.6724\). The fine correction is \((0.6724-0.68)/3\approx-0.0025333\). The extrapolated value is 0.6698667. The exact endpoint \(e^{-0.4}\approx0.6703200\) requires correction \(-0.0020800\) to \(Y_f\). The estimated sign and scale are correct while higher-order terms are still visible. Discontinuous forcing, a skipped event or inconsistent internal solver tolerances can spoil this comparison even when the two output times agree.

Locate events between stored states

An event is a zero of \(g(t,y(t))\), such as crossing a chosen temperature threshold. When its endpoint values have opposite signs, continuity brackets at least one event. Use a dense approximation to the trajectory or refine the time interval to locate the crossing. A linear interpolation between endpoints may be adequate for a smooth signal away from a turning point. If a value changes by a small amount near a flat crossing, a small field error can produce a much larger error in event time.

For \(y=e^{-t}\), readings at 0.6 and 0.8 are 0.54881 and 0.44933. A straight interpolation to 0.5 gives \(t\simeq0.69813\), while the exact time is \(\ln2=0.69315\). The discrepancy arises from the curved trajectory even if the endpoints are exact. A tangential event such as \((t-1)^2=0\) never changes sign, and two crossings in one step can also be missed. Declare the crossing direction, the required timing accuracy and the physical effect of reaching it. After an event that changes the model, start the next segment using the event state and preserve the time at which the transition actually occurred.

Test the event interpolant with a known direction

At a crossing where a scalar signal has nonzero derivative, an approximate amplitude error \(\delta y\) produces a first-order timing error \(\delta t\simeq-\delta y/y'\). For \(e^{-t}=0.5\), the derivative is \(-0.5\) per second. A signal that is 0.002 too high near the crossing therefore appears to reach the threshold about 0.004 s late. This local estimate converts a solver tolerance on a state into a physically interpretable timing requirement.

A source that switches precisely at the threshold makes the event time part of the subsequent model. A timing error then affects all later states even if ordinary integration error within each regime is small. When comparing grids, compare both the located event times and the post-event states at shared physical times. Two traces that agree before a switch can diverge later purely because one entered the new law sooner.

Relate state accuracy to event-time accuracy

Let an event be defined by \(G(t,y(t))=0\) and write the scalar event signal as \(g(t)\). Its derivative along a smooth trajectory is \(g'=G_t+G_y f\). At a simple crossing \(g'(t_\star)\ne0\), an error \(\delta g\) in evaluating the signal displaces the reported time by approximately \(-\delta g/g'(t_\star)\). More rigorously, if the true signal derivative has magnitude at least \(m\) throughout the final bracket, the mean value theorem bounds time error by the event-signal error divided by \(m\), in addition to any error left by the scalar root search.

Thus an event locator can solve its interpolated scalar equation to a tiny tolerance while returning a poor physical time. Errors in the interpolated state, uncertain event threshold and flat slope all propagate into the time estimate. At a tangential contact the derivative vanishes and the first-order estimate ceases to apply. Two simple events can merge into one contact or disappear altogether under a perturbation that would merely shift a steep isolated crossing by a small amount.

Time two crossings around the top of a trajectory

Take vertical position \(y(t)=10t-5t^2\) metres, with time in seconds. Its maximum is 5 m at one second. A detector at 4.8 m records two crossings, since \(5-5(t-1)^2=4.8\) gives \(t=1\pm0.2\), namely 0.8 and 1.2 s. The respective vertical velocities are \(+2\) and \(-2\) m/s. A small threshold increase of 0.01 m therefore moves the rising crossing about 0.005 s later and the falling crossing about 0.005 s earlier.

Check the exact effect at 4.81 m. The half-separation becomes \(\sqrt{0.19/5}=\sqrt{0.038}\approx0.194936\) s. The actual time shifts have magnitude approximately 0.005064 s, so the local slope estimate is already informative. At a threshold of 4.999 m the speed is instead only \(10\sqrt{0.001/5}\approx0.14142\) m/s. Another increase of 0.01 m moves the threshold above the maximum and removes both crossings. Applying the previous linear timing estimate at this near-tangent setting would predict a displacement for an event that no longer exists.

A direction flag distinguishes the rising and falling events if each is bracketed, but it cannot recover two crossings hidden inside one large step with endpoint signals of the same sign. In this model the velocity event \(y'=0\) identifies the turning point and splits the time interval into monotone pieces. On each piece check the threshold against its attainable position range before starting a time root search. This strategy uses knowledge of the dynamics to ensure the event actually exists.

For example, a single step from 0.7 to 1.3 s gives position 4.55 m at both endpoints and therefore no sign difference relative to the 4.8 m detector. Inserting the peak at 1 s gives a positive signal there and immediately produces two valid brackets. Decreasing only the scalar root tolerance would have found neither event on the original unsplit step.

Chapter 6 · Energy and geometric integration

Compare three time updates on a conservative oscillator and explain why a method can have small short-term state error yet distort an orbit over many periods.

From first principles

Build an oscillator update out of two area-preserving shears

Take one undamped oscillator of mass \(m>0\) and stiffness \(k>0\). Its canonical position \(q\) and momentum \(p\) satisfy \(\dot q=p/m\) and \(\dot p=-kq\). Exact trajectories run along energy ellipses and preserve phase-plane area. If both slopes are taken at the left endpoint, the update and its area factor are

\[\begin{pmatrix}q_{n+1}\\p_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&h/m\\-hk&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}q_n\\p_n\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\det=1+h^2k/m .\]

A positive factor greater than one at each step makes the numerical orbit spiral outward. Instead update the momentum at fixed position, then move the position using that new momentum. Both operations are shears:

\[p_{n+1}=p_n-hkq_n,\]
\[q_{n+1}=q_n+h p_{n+1}/m.\]

The product matrix has diagonal entries \(1-h^2k/m\) and \(1\), with off-diagonal product \(-h^2k/m\), giving determinant one. Its eigenvalues form a conjugate pair on the unit circle when \(|2-h^2k/m|<2\). This calculation explains the bounded oscillatory behaviour for \(0<h\sqrt{k/m}<2\). Preserving area alone would also allow one real mode to grow while another shrinks. It does not establish either stability or exact conservation of the original energy.

Derivation and applications

Which quadratic energy does symplectic Euler preserve?

An undamped linear oscillator conserves the physical sum of kinetic and potential energy. A geometric time integrator may conserve a nearby quadratic form instead. Take \(q''=-\omega^2q\) with constant \(\omega\) and unit mass. Scale velocity by setting \(u=\dot q/\omega\) so that both variables have units of length, and write \(z=h\omega\). Use the kick-then-drift version of symplectic Euler. Its step is two triangular shears:

\[u'=u-zq,\]
\[q'=q+zu'=(1-z^2)q+zu .\]

To find a preserved quantity, test a quadratic form \(I=q^2+u^2+\alpha qu\). The changes of the ordinary sum and the product follow directly by multiplication and collection:

\[q'^2+u'^2-q^2-u^2=z(q'u'-qu),\]
\[I'-I=(z+\alpha)(q'u'-qu).\]

Choosing \(\alpha=-z\) cancels the entire change at every step. Completing the square identifies the range where the invariant confines trajectories to closed ellipses:

\[I=q^2+u^2-zqu=(u-zq/2)^2+(1-z^2/4)q^2.\]

For \(|z|<2\) the form is positive definite. The physical energy is proportional to \(q^2+u^2\) and can oscillate, but the invariant bounds it. As \(h\to0\) the tilted ellipse approaches the exact energy circle. At the endpoint \(|z|=2\) the quadratic form ceases to bound both variables; preservation of an indefinite form alone does not guarantee stable trajectories.

Worked example

Follow a moderate step exactly

Take \(\omega=2.00\) s⁻¹, \(h=0.250\) s, \(q=1.00\) m and \(u=0\). Then \(z=0.500\), \(u'=-0.500\) m and \(q'=0.750\) m. The ordinary sum falls from \(1.00\) to \(0.8125\) m². But \(I'=0.8125-0.500(0.750)(-0.500)=1.00\) m². A plot restricted to physical energy at one time could mistake the discrepancy for damping.

Second application

Diagnose a seemingly exact conservation test outside stability

Choose \(z=2.50\) and the same initial \((q,u)=(1,0)\). One step gives \((-5.25,-2.50)\). The ordinary sum is \(33.8125\), whereas the cross term is \(2.50(13.125)=32.8125\), so \(I'=1\). The invariant remains exact while the amplitude grows. Testing positivity of the conserved quadratic form therefore supplies information absent from a conservation-only check.

Choose the diagnostic before choosing the method

A mass on an ideal spring with unit mass and stiffness obeys \(\dot q=p,\ \dot p=-q\). The state moves on circles with conserved energy \(H=(q^2+p^2)/2\). At short times a state norm measures trajectory error, but over many periods there are independent questions about amplitude, phase and energy. A time-stepping method that systematically increases the orbit radius can produce an artificial runaway even on this simplest stable physical system.

Forward Euler updates \(q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_n,\ p_{n+1}=p_n-hq_n\). Square and add: the cross terms cancel, leaving \(H_{n+1}=(1+h^2)H_n\). The phase space area factor of the update matrix is its determinant \(1+h^2\), also greater than one. Backward Euler instead contracts energy by \(1/(1+h^2)\). The second method is stable in the sense of bounded states, but it removes mechanical energy from an undamped model. The sign of drift explains much more here than a solitary endpoint comparison.

Update momentum before position

The momentum-first symplectic Euler scheme sets \(p_{n+1}=p_n-hq_n\) and \(q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_{n+1}\). Its update matrix in \((q,p)\) has rows \((1-h^2,h)\) and \((-h,1)\). Its determinant is one and trace is \(2-h^2\). For \(0<h<2\), the two eigenvalues are a complex conjugate pair of unit magnitude because their product is one and the trace lies strictly between −2 and 2. The resulting numerical frequency satisfies \(2\cos\theta=2-h^2\). Thus phase error still accumulates even if the amplitudes remain bounded.

Direct substitution also verifies an exact discrete invariant \(\tilde H=(q^2+p^2-hqp)/2\). For \(h<2\) the corresponding quadratic form is positive definite, so constant \(\tilde H\) confines the state to an ellipse. The exact physical energy can fluctuate around its initial level as the point moves around that ellipse. Area preservation alone would not guarantee this behaviour: for \(h>2\) the determinant remains one but real reciprocal eigenvalues permit growth. The eigenvalue and invariant calculations supply the missing qualification.

Check a long-time forecast with numbers

Start at \((q,p)=(1,0)\) with \(h=0.1\). Forward Euler gives \((1,-0.1)\) and \(H=0.505\) after one step. After 100 steps the exact multiplicative law predicts \(H=0.5(1.01)^{100}=1.3524\). Symplectic Euler instead gives \((0.99,-0.1)\), \(H=0.49505\), but \(\tilde H=0.5\) again. The first step alone might suggest that the second scheme dissipates energy. The invariant predicts that the decrease cannot continue monotonically over many periods.

Implicit midpoint, \((y_{n+1}-y_n)/h=J(y_n+y_{n+1})/2\) with the skew-symmetric oscillator matrix \(J\), preserves the exact quadratic energy. Dot both sides with the midpoint state. The left side is proportional to \(H_{n+1}-H_n\) and the right side is zero by skew symmetry. Its rotations need not accumulate the exact physical phase, since the rotation angle per step is \(2\arctan(h/2)\). A meaningful long-time study therefore records measured period, energy range and reversibility alongside any state error after a specified number of cycles.

Test an algorithm by reversing the clock

A method is time symmetric if taking a step of length \(h\) and then a step of length \(-h\) restores the starting state, up to arithmetic and solve tolerance. For the midpoint update, exchanging the old and new values leaves their average unchanged and changes both sides of the difference equation consistently. This gives exact reversibility for an accurately solved implicit step. For forward Euler on the oscillator, the product of the forward and backward matrices is \((1+h^2)I\), so reversal actually scales the state.

At \(h=0.1\), the simple reversal from \((1,0)\) therefore returns \((1.01,0)\) for forward Euler, making the failure visible in one pair of steps. This diagnostic is useful only for a reversible underlying evolution paired with its time-reversed integration law. A dissipative or dissipatively constrained physical system has different requirements. Reversibility, energy preservation and phase accuracy probe separate mathematical properties, each with a different operational test.

Test a flow map on a small cell of phase space

For canonical coordinates \(z=(q,p)\), a differentiable Hamiltonian update \(z_+=\Psi(z)\) is symplectic when its Jacobian \(D\Psi\) preserves the matrix \(J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) through \(D\Psi^T J D\Psi=J\). Direct multiplication shows that for any real two-by-two matrix \(M\), \(M^TJM=(\det M)J\). Thus in one degree of freedom symplecticity is equivalent to preserving oriented area. A small rectangle of nearby initial conditions should map to a region of the same signed area even when its sides stretch or tilt.

For more than one degree of freedom, preserving ordinary volume is necessary but no longer sufficient. A map may stretch one canonical \((q,p)\) plane and compress another by the reciprocal factor. Its full determinant remains one while the area pairing in each plane changes. Energy is a further, independent diagnostic: an area-preserving map can move individual points among nearby energy contours. The determinant calculation tests a geometric property of the map across neighbouring trajectories, rather than just the energy at one chosen state.

Compare the phase volume of an oscillator ensemble

For \(q'=p,\ p'=-q\) with unit frequency, forward Euler gives \(M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&h\\-h&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), of determinant \(1+h^2\). Backward Euler gives \((I-hA)^{-1}\), where \(A\) has off-diagonal entries \(1,-1\), so its determinant is \(1/(1+h^2)\). A kick followed by a drift gives the symplectic Euler matrix with rows \((1-h^2,h)\) and \((-h,1)\), whose determinant is exactly one. These facts hold for every input rectangle, so they can be checked by integrating its corners rather than relying on a single solution.

Choose \(h=0.2\) and an initial ensemble occupying a rectangle of area 0.01 in normalised phase coordinates. After 25 steps forward Euler predicts area \(0.01(1.04)^{25}\approx0.02666\). Backward Euler gives \(0.01/(1.04)^{25}\approx0.00375\). Symplectic Euler preserves 0.01. Repeated steps compound even a four percent per-step area change into a substantial distortion. For the oscillator all three approximate individual paths can initially look plausible, so this ensemble diagnostic reveals information inaccessible in a short plot of one coordinate.

As a counterexample in four dimensions, scale the first pair of coordinates by \((2,1)\) and the second pair by \((1/2,1)\). The volume multiplier is one, but the first canonical area doubles while the second halves. A full symplecticity test must check \(M^TJM-J\) using the correctly ordered canonical coordinate pairs. An algorithm preserving volume in an arbitrary collection of engineering state units is not automatically qualified for long-time Hamiltonian integration.

Area preservation also survives composing individual canonical shears: the determinants multiply and every shear has determinant one. This explains why reversing the order of a kick and a drift changes the phase error but preserves the two-dimensional area test. The two compositions may therefore produce different numeric positions while agreeing precisely on one geometric invariant. A useful verification suite includes both ensemble area and a trajectory observable with a known time dependence.

Chapter 7 · Variation of constants and exponential steps

Integrate a stiff linear decay exactly while approximating only its slowly changing forcing term.

From first principles

Integrate the linear part exactly before approximating the source

Suppose \(y'=Ay+g(t,y)\), with a constant matrix \(A\). It may carry a large decay rate even though the applied source \(g\) varies smoothly. Multiply by \(e^{-At}\) and use the product rule. The derivative of the exponential cancels the linear term:

\[\frac{d}{dt}(e^{-At}y(t))=e^{-At}g(t,y(t)).\]

Integrate from \(t_n\) to \(t_n+h\) and multiply back by the final exponential. The exact formula assigns each source contribution the propagation corresponding to its remaining time in the step:

\[y(t_n+h)=e^{hA}y(t_n)+\int_0^h e^{(h-s)A}g(t_n+s,y(t_n+s))\,ds .\]

Approximate only the source by its starting value. Integrating the exponential weight yields

\[y_{n+1}=e^{hA}y_n+h\varphi_1(hA)g(t_n,y_n),\]
\[\varphi_1(z)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty z^j/(j+1)! .\]

For nonzero scalar \(z\) the series equals \((e^z-1)/z\); at zero it equals one. The series remains meaningful for a singular matrix. A constant source is integrated exactly by this step. Variation in the source, including its dependence on the moving state, determines its remaining time-discretisation error.

Derivation and applications

Evaluate exponential weights without subtracting almost equal numbers

The integrating-factor solution of \(y'=\lambda y+g(t)\) gives useful one-step formulas when \(\lambda\) is constant and the source changes slowly. Approximate the source within a step by the straight line from \(g_n\) to \(g_{n+1}\). Set \(z=h\lambda\) and use the unit interval variable \(\theta=(s-t_n)/h\). Variation of constants first yields

\[y_{n+1}=e^z y_n+h\int_0^1 e^{(1-\theta)z}[g_n+\theta(g_{n+1}-g_n)]\,d\theta .\]

Integrate the constant and linear source terms separately. For \(z\ne0\) direct evaluation gives the two weights and their connection:

\[\varphi_1(z)=\frac{e^z-1}{z},\]
\[\varphi_2(z)=\frac{e^z-1-z}{z^2},\]
\[y_{n+1}=e^z y_n+h[\varphi_1g_n+\varphi_2(g_{n+1}-g_n)].\]

The apparent poles at zero are removable. Expanding the exponential before subtracting gives stable small-argument evaluations:

\[\varphi_1=1+z/2+z^2/6+\cdots,\]
\[\varphi_2=1/2+z/6+z^2/24+\cdots .\]

An accurate routine for \(e^z-1\) helps with the first weight, but subtracting \(z\) to compute the second can still lose digits. Use its own series near zero. At \(z=0\) the formula reduces to trapezoidal integration of the source, which is exact for the chosen linear interpolant. This limit tests simulated components whose linear decay is zero.

Worked example

Check a strongly damped ramp

Set \(\lambda=-2.00\) s⁻¹, \(h=0.500\) s, \(y_n=0\) and let the source rise from zero to \(4.00\) units/s over the step. Here \(z=-1\) and \(\varphi_2(-1)=e^{-1}=0.367879\). The calculated rise is \(0.500(4.00)(0.367879)=0.73576\) units. Without decay the same ramp deposits exactly \(1.00\) unit. Both predictions can be checked directly by integrating the specified source history.

Second application

Test a nearly neutral component

Take \(z=-10^{-8}\) and evaluate \(e^z-1-z\). Its correct numerator is about \(5.00\times10^{-17}\), comparable to the rounding uncertainty from subtracting one from a number near one. The series instead gives \(\varphi_2\approx0.499999998333\). For unit-duration integration of a ramp from zero to two units/s the response is about \(0.9999999967\) units. A returned zero here signals cancellation in weight evaluation, not suppression of the source by the model.

Preserve the linear response

For \(y'=Ay+g(t,y)\) with constant \(A\), multiply by \(e^{-At}\). The derivative of the product is \(e^{-At}g(t,y(t))\), because the two terms containing \(A\) cancel. Integrating between \(t_n\) and \(t_n+h\), then multiplying back, gives the exact variation-of-constants formula

\[y(t_n+h)=e^{hA}y(t_n)+\int_0^he^{(h-s)A}g(t_n+s,y(t_n+s))\,ds.\]

It separates the free propagation of the initial state from the accumulated, propagated forcing. Freezing \(g\) at the beginning of the step gives exponential Euler. Its forcing weight is \(h\varphi_1(hA)\), where \(\varphi_1(z)=(e^z-1)/z\) has the continuous value 1 at zero. The defining integral or power series applies to a singular matrix without computing an inverse.

Derive the leading defect

Assume along the solution that the change in \(g\) over \(s\) is bounded by \(Ls\), and that the linear propagator has norm at most \(M\) during the step. Subtract the frozen approximation from the exact integral. Its norm is bounded by \(ML\int_0^h s\,ds=MLh^2/2\). This is a local error bound. To obtain a global first-order estimate one must also bound propagation of earlier state errors, using the stability of the linear propagator and the Lipschitz dependence of the remaining forcing on the state. A stiff term hidden inside \(g\) can still impose a severe practical restriction.

Heat loss with a steady input

Let \(y'=-100y+20\) with \(y(0)=1\) and time in seconds. The equilibrium is 0.2. At \(h=0.1\), exponential Euler gives \(y_1=e^{-10}+0.2(1-e^{-10})\approx0.20003632\), exactly the analytical solution at that time. Forward Euler would give \(-7\), whereas backward Euler gives \(3/11\approx0.27273\). Both the exponential step and backward Euler damp the fast mode here, but the computed amount of transient that survives differs substantially. A large step that accurately reproduces its endpoint can still leave an output trace too sparse to measure the early decay time.

Change the input to \(20+10t\). During the first step exponential Euler ignores its linear growth. The missing term is \(10\int_0^h e^{-100(h-s)}s\,ds=10[h/100-(1-e^{-100h})/10000]\). At 0.1 s it is approximately 0.00900005. Including a linear approximation to the forcing would recover this term exactly. This calculation isolates the effect of freezing a changing input while the stiff decay remains exact.

Evaluating the weights

For small \(|z|\), direct subtraction of one from \(e^z\) loses significant digits. A dedicated exponential-minus-one function or the series \(1+z/2+z^2/6+\cdots\) yields a reliable \(\varphi_1\). Large sparse systems often require only the action of an exponential on a vector. Computing this action introduces another error budget separate from time approximation; a constant-forcing test with a known equilibrium checks both parts together.

Constant states as an implementation test

If \(Ay_\ast+g_\ast=0\), an exponential step starting at \(y_\ast\) must reproduce the same state for every step length. The series identity \(h\varphi_1(hA)A=e^{hA}-I\) makes the cancellation exact mathematically. Test this condition with a matrix containing both a zero eigenvalue and a strongly negative eigenvalue: the zero mode should accumulate a constant source linearly, while the decaying mode approaches its forced equilibrium. This test distinguishes a faulty treatment of the zero limit from ordinary temporal error caused by a varying input.

Integrate a linearly varying source with a second exponential weight

For \(y'=Ay+b_0+(t-t_n)b_1\) during a step, the variation-of-constants integral contains a constant and a linear source. Expand \(e^{(h-s)A}\) in its power series inside \(\int_0^h e^{(h-s)A}s\,ds\). Integrating each term gives \(h^{j+2}A^j/(j+2)!\). Consequently the appropriate weight for the slope is \(h^2\varphi_2(hA)\) with \(\varphi_2(z)=\sum_{j\ge0}z^j/(j+2)!\). Together with the constant-source \(\varphi_1\) term this gives the exact step for a source genuinely affine in time.

The identity \(z\varphi_2(z)=\varphi_1(z)-1\) follows from the series. At zero the continuous value is one half, matching the elementary time integral of a ramp. Computing this weight by dividing two nearly equal numbers can lose digits near zero and fails as an ordinary matrix division when \(A\) is singular. The series definition and augmented-system formulations give well-defined actions in both cases. If the source depends on the unknown state, an independently known time slope is no longer enough: its total derivative includes the state sensitivity carried along the current trajectory.

Propagate a linearly increasing acceleration through a coupled system

Let \(y=(q,v)^T,\ q'=v,\ v'=2+6t\), with \(q(0)=1\) m and \(v(0)=0\). The matrix \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) is singular and satisfies \(A^2=0\). The matrix series therefore terminate: \(e^{hA}=I+hA,\ h\varphi_1=hI+h^2A/2,\ h^2\varphi_2=h^2I/2+h^3A/6\), with arguments \(hA\) understood. Multiplying \(b_0=(0,2)^T\) by the first source weight gives \((h^2,2h)^T\). Multiplying \(b_1=(0,6)^T\) by the ramp weight gives \((h^3,3h^2)^T\).

At \(h=0.5\) s, the freely propagated initial state is still \((1,0)^T\). The constant-source contribution is \((0.25,1)^T\) and the ramp contribution is \((0.125,0.75)^T\). The final state is therefore \((1.375,1.75)^T\). Independently integrating the acceleration gives \(v=2t+3t^2\) and \(q=1+t^2+t^3\), confirming both numbers. A method freezing acceleration at its initial value would report \(q=1.25,\ v=1\) despite propagating the coupling \(q'=v\) exactly.

This example shows how information travels through a seemingly empty entry of the source: the position receives a ramp contribution although the forcing acts directly only on velocity. Treating the exponential as an entrywise operation would miss it. For larger sparse systems the same action can be obtained by augmenting the state with coordinates representing the constant and linear input, then applying one matrix exponential to the augmented initial vector.

For the stated ramp, define two extra scalar coordinates \(c=1\) and \(s=t\) with \(c'=0,\ s'=c\). Then the augmented linear equation has \(y'=Ay+b_0 c+b_1 s\). Powers of this single augmented matrix transfer \(c\) successively into \(s\), acceleration, velocity and position. Its exponential is therefore a finite polynomial for the present nilpotent structure. This explicit construction supplies a second implementation that can test the individual \(\varphi\) actions without forming a matrix inverse. If the ramp is restarted at each step, initialise the auxiliary coordinate consistently with whether \(b_0\) is the original source or its value at the new time.

Chapter 8 · Operator splitting and noncommuting flows

Determine how the order of two exact substeps affects a coupled evolution and recover second order by a symmetric composition.

From first principles

Identify what goes wrong when two exact substeps are composed

Suppose two linear processes act simultaneously, \(y'=(A+B)y\), with constant matrices and readily computed separate flows. The exact joint step is \(e^{h(A+B)}\). Advancing first with \(A\) and then with \(B\) gives \(e^{hB}e^{hA}\). Multiply their Taylor expansions through second order:

\[e^{hB}e^{hA}=I+h(A+B)+h^2(A^2/2+BA+B^2/2)+O(h^3).\]

In the true joint exponential the mixed term is \((AB+BA)/2\). Subtract it, keeping the order of matrix multiplication:

\[e^{hB}e^{hA}-e^{h(A+B)}=\tfrac12 h^2(BA-AB)+O(h^3).\]

Each separate step can be exact and the composition can still have an error. If \(AB=BA\) the linear flows commute and the composition is exact for all \(h\). In the general case a symmetric schedule places half of the first process on either side of a full step of the second:

\[S_h=e^{hA/2}e^{hB}e^{hA/2}=I+h(A+B)+\tfrac12 h^2(A+B)^2+O(h^3).\]

Both mixed products now acquire the exact coefficient \(1/2\). Under smooth, stable propagation the resulting global error over a fixed interval is second order. Boundary conditions or an approximate internal subsolver can add other defects.

Derivation and applications

Measure the error created by splitting a source from a decay

Two individually exact updates need not produce the exact combined dynamics. Examine \(y'=s-\lambda y\) with constant source \(s\) and \(\lambda>0\). Both subproblems have explicit flows: source alone translates by \(sh\), and decay alone multiplies by \(e^{-\lambda h}\). The full equation instead admits a stationary state \(y_*=s/\lambda\). Subtracting this state and integrating homogeneous decay gives the exact update

\[y_{\rm exact}=e^{-z}y_n+y_*(1-e^{-z}),\]
\[z=\lambda h.\]

If the source is applied first, its entire increment is damped for duration \(h\). If decay is applied first, its entire increment escapes damping. A symmetric source-half, decay-full, source-half sequence averages these placements:

\[y_{SD}=e^{-z}y_n+sh e^{-z},\]
\[y_{DS}=e^{-z}y_n+sh,\]
\[y_{\rm sym}=e^{-z}y_n+\tfrac12 sh(1+e^{-z}).\]

The exact source increment is the integral of decay over all arrival times. The two one-sided approximations are rectangles and the symmetric one is a trapezoid. Expanding the source increments around \(h=0\) gives their local errors:

\[y_{SD}-y_{\rm exact}=-s\lambda h^2/2+O(h^3),\]
\[y_{\rm sym}-y_{\rm exact}=s\lambda^2h^3/12+O(h^4).\]

The opposite one-sided order has leading error of the opposite sign. With a fixed final time, repeated errors usually reduce the respective global orders by one. If \(\lambda=0\) or \(s=0\), all orders coincide exactly. A stationary state in each individual subproblem would be preserved by composition, whereas this full-system equilibrium arises from cancellation of two nonzero rates.

Worked example

Compare a single step at the exact equilibrium

Let \(s=2.00\) units/s, \(\lambda=2.00\) s⁻¹, \(h=0.500\) s and \(y_n=y_*=1.00\). Exact evolution remains at one. The two split orders give \(2e^{-1}=0.73576\) and \(1+e^{-1}=1.36788\). The symmetric sequence gives \(0.5+1.5e^{-1}=1.05182\). Symmetry reduces the drift markedly without eliminating it.

Second application

Predict the long-time state of the discrete map

Iterating the symmetric step eventually reaches its own fixed point \(y_h=(sh/2)(1+e^{-z})/(1-e^{-z})\). With the preceding data this is \(1.08198\), not the true value \(1.00\). Halving \(h\) gives \(0.25(1+e^{-0.5})/(1-e^{-0.5})=1.02075\). The excess falls by a factor close to four, consistent with a second-order bias in a long-running equilibrium calculation.

Two solvable subproblems

Suppose an evolution has the form \(u'=(A+B)u\), where the subproblems involving just \(A\) or just \(B\) have inexpensive exact flows. First applying \(A\) for one nominal step and then \(B\) gives \(e^{hB}e^{hA}u_n\). Each operator is advanced over the same physical interval. When \(A\) and \(B\) commute, their exponential product equals the exponential of the sum. For general matrices the missing interaction between simultaneous changes controls the error.

Expand the factors through \(h^2\). The mixed term of their product is \(h^2BA\); that of the exact exponential is \(h^2(AB+BA)/2\). The difference is \(h^2(BA-AB)/2\), the signed commutator term. Reversing the order reverses its sign. Composing a half step of \(A\), a full step of \(B\) and another half step of \(A\) instead balances both mixed terms. The local discrepancy is then order \(h^3\) for fixed bounded matrices, giving global second order over a fixed interval under stable propagation.

Work out two shears

Let \(u=(q,p)^T\), \(A(q,p)=(p,0)\) and \(B(q,p)=(0,-q)\), so the exact motion is a unit-frequency oscillator. Each separate flow is a shear. From \((1,0)\), a drift of length 0.2 followed by a kick produces \((1,-0.2)\). A kick followed by a drift produces \((0.96,-0.2)\). The exact result is \((\cos0.2,-\sin0.2)\approx(0.980067,-0.198669)\). The positions from the two first-order compositions bracket the exact one in this example. Their leading positional deviations are almost equal and opposite.

The symmetric drift–kick–drift composition first leaves the position at 1, changes the momentum to \(-0.2\), then drifts by \(-0.02\). Its final state is \((0.98,-0.2)\). The position error is about \(6.66\times10^{-5}\) while the momentum error is about \(1.33\times10^{-3}\). Halving the step in a one-step test scales the leading momentum discrepancy roughly by eight. When measuring at a fixed final time instead, the number of steps doubles and the expected asymptotic error reduction is approximately four.

What the substeps must share

Both subflows need compatible state variables and boundary data. If each independently applies an external source intended to appear once in \(A+B\), the combined model advances a different equation and cannot converge to the desired solution even with arbitrarily small steps. Conversely a source assigned to neither flow disappears. Check the combined first derivative of the composition at \(h=0\); it must reproduce the complete original right-hand side. This low-cost test catches modelling errors before comparing convergence slopes.

An exact limiting case for a code

Take two diagonal decay matrices with first-coordinate rates −1 and −3 and second-coordinate rates −2 and zero. Each separate flow rescales the coordinates. Their products commute, so starting from \((1,1)\) and integrating to \(t=0.5\) must give \((e^{-2},e^{-1})\) in either order. The equality holds for a single full step as well as any subdivision. This commuting case tests time scaling and state transfer without any expected splitting error. The shear example then tests the distinct question of whether the implementation achieves its intended order when a commutator is present.

Resolve the direction of a nonlinear splitting error

Consider logistic growth \(u'=au-bu^2\) with \(a,b>0\). The separate linear growth flow is \(G_h(u)=e^{ah}u\). The saturation flow solves \(u'=-bu^2\) and is \(D_h(u)=u/(1+bhu)\) for positive initial states. Each flow is exact, positivity preserving and inexpensive. But the saturation strength depends on the current population, which growth changes during a step. Therefore applying growth before saturation generally produces a different result from applying saturation before growth.

Write the two full-step maps explicitly. Growth then saturation yields \(e^{ah}u/[1+bh e^{ah}u]\); saturation then growth gives \(e^{ah}u/(1+bhu)\). For positive \(u,h\) and \(a\) the first denominator is larger and the final value smaller. The exact nonlinear flow has denominator \(1+(b/a)(e^{ah}-1)u\), obtained by solving the linear equation for \(1/u\). Since \(h<(e^{ah}-1)/a<h e^{ah}\), the two orderings bound the exact positive answer on opposite sides. This ordering result uses the particular signs and monotonicity of logistic growth; it is not a generic enclosure theorem for split systems.

Test a physical equilibrium that neither subflow preserves

Set \(a=b=1,\ u_0=1\). The complete model is exactly stationary because growth and saturation cancel. Choose \(h=\log2\), so an isolated growth step doubles the state. Growth followed by saturation gives \(2/(1+2\log2)\approx0.8381\). Saturation followed by growth gives \(2/(1+\log2)\approx1.1812\). Neither result is the stationary true value. Powering either map through many long intervals would move the solution toward a numerical equilibrium different from the physical one, although each individual substep is solved without approximation.

A symmetric growth–saturation–growth step gives \(u_1=2/(1+\sqrt2\log2)\approx1.0100\). Symmetry has cancelled the leading ordering discrepancy without enforcing exact preservation of the steady state. To predict the long-run drift of the first ordering, solve its nonzero fixed-point equation \(u=2u/(1+2hu)\), giving \(u=1/(2h)\approx0.7213\). For the opposite ordering the numerical equilibrium is \(1/h\approx1.4427\). These values expose a persistent bias that a one-step comparison can understate.

A steady physical reference is particularly informative when a splitting method is used to integrate dynamics near a balance of large opposing processes. Compare the numerical equilibrium and the relaxation rate toward it. An implementation can improve its order by a symmetric composition while still requiring much finer steps, or a different assignment of source terms, to maintain the equilibrium accuracy a physical application demands.

A refinement study can use the exact nonzero fixed point formulas to separate local error from accumulated drift. For arbitrary positive \(h\), the growth-first numerical fixed point is \((1-e^{-h})/h=1-h/2+O(h^2)\), while the saturation-first fixed point is \((e^h-1)/h=1+h/2+O(h^2)\). Thus their steady-state biases have opposite signs and are first order even though the individual one-step defects from the physical equilibrium start at second order. An empirical comparison based on the first step and another measured after equilibration should not be expected to return the same power of the step size.

The two steady-state formulas also converge toward the same physical value as the step tends to zero.

Chapter 9 · Störmer–Verlet and a discrete invariant

Derive the leapfrog update, identify its oscillatory stability interval and quantify the difference between bounded energy and correct phase.

From first principles

Derive a reversible position update from Newton’s law

For a force that depends only on position, \(m\ddot q=F(q)\), the acceleration can be evaluated at a stored position without an implicit solve. Expand the exact position one step forward and one step backward about the same time \(t_n\). The odd derivatives reverse sign and cancel when the expansions are added:

\[q(t_n+h)+q(t_n-h)=2q(t_n)+h^2\ddot q(t_n)+O(h^4).\]

Replace acceleration by force divided by mass and solve for the forward position. The resulting relation is unchanged if the labels \(n+1\) and \(n-1\) are exchanged:

\[q_{n+1}=2q_n-q_{n-1}+\frac{h^2}{m}F(q_n).\]

Introduce the staggered momentum \(p_{n-1/2}=m(q_n-q_{n-1})/h\). The same algebra becomes an impulse followed by a drift,

\[p_{n+1/2}=p_{n-1/2}+hF(q_n),\]
\[q_{n+1}=q_n+h p_{n+1/2}/m .\]

A specified momentum at \(t=0\) starts the first half step with \(p_{1/2}=p_0+h F(q_0)/2\). For smooth forces the method has second-order position accuracy. It is important to distinguish the staggered momentum from momentum at the stored position time when checking energy.

Derivation and applications

Why a stable Verlet trajectory can arrive at the wrong time

Long-term energy behaviour is important in reversible mechanics, but phase error can dominate a timing measurement. Consider the velocity-independent linear oscillator \(q''=-\omega^2q\). Use a uniform step \(h\), an accurately initialized first point and the position recurrence obtained by eliminating the half-step momenta in Verlet. Write \(z=h\omega\):

\[q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-z^2q_n .\]

Assume a discrete mode \(q_n=e^{in\vartheta}\). Divide by the mode value at \(n\) and combine the two exponentials. The angle advanced per step obeys

\[2\cos\vartheta-2=-z^2,\]
\[\sin(\vartheta/2)=z/2 .\]

For \(0<z<2\) choose the branch continuous with the exact oscillation at small \(h\). The discrete angular frequency and its expansion are

\[\widetilde\omega=\frac2h\arcsin(z/2)=\omega\left[1+\frac{z^2}{24}+O(z^4)\right].\]

The numerical oscillator runs slightly fast even though its amplitude can remain bounded indefinitely. Its phase lead grows approximately linearly with elapsed time at fixed step. Above \(z=2\) the dispersion relation permits no real phase angle, signaling a separate stability failure. The formula concerns time discretization alone; incorrect initial half-step velocities can add an initial phase offset.

Worked example

Estimate a ten-period timing error

For \(\omega=5.00\) rad/s and \(h=0.100\) s, \(z=0.500\) and \(\widetilde\omega=20\arcsin(0.250)=5.054\) rad/s. Ten true periods last \(12.57\) s. Over that duration the numerical phase gains about \((5.054-5.00)(12.57)\simeq0.674\) rad. The estimated time at which the numerical oscillator completes its tenth period is \(20\pi/5.054=12.43\) s, about \(0.13\) s too soon.

Second application

Select a step from a phase requirement

Suppose 100 periods should accumulate less than \(0.100\) rad of phase error. The leading estimate gives \(2\pi(100)z^2/24<0.100\), so \(z<0.0618\). For \(\omega=5.00\) rad/s this means \(h<0.0124\) s. A choice based only on the stability requirement \(h<0.400\) s would be far too coarse. Halving a small step should reduce the accumulated phase error by approximately four at fixed physical duration.

Position and momentum at staggered times

For unit mass and a time-independent potential \(V(q)\), the equations are \(q'=p,\ p'=-V'(q)\). Treat the potential force over a half step, drift with the updated momentum for a full step, then use the new position for the last half kick. This yields

\[p_{n+1/2}=p_n-\tfrac h2V'(q_n),\quad q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_{n+1/2},\quad p_{n+1}=p_{n+1/2}-\tfrac h2V'(q_{n+1}).\]

Successive half kicks at the same position share a force evaluation. Reversing the sign of \(h\) exactly undoes the steps in reverse order, in exact arithmetic. Subtracting consecutive position increments gives \(q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-h^2V'(q_n)\). The starting position and velocity must initialise the very first half step correctly; using the full velocity as the half-step velocity injects an initial phase error.

The oscillator has an exactly computable modified energy

For \(V=\omega^2q^2/2\) define \(z=h\omega\). The position coefficient of \(q_n\) after one step is \(a=1-z^2/2\); the momentum coefficient of \(q_n\) is \(-h\omega^2(1-z^2/4)\). The full map has equal diagonal entries \(a\) and off-diagonal entries \(h,-hc\) with \(c=\omega^2(1-z^2/4)\). Since \(a^2+h^2c=1\), expanding squares shows \(p_{n+1}^2+cq_{n+1}^2=p_n^2+cq_n^2\).

When \(0<z<2\), \(c>0\) and level sets of this invariant are bounded ellipses. The eigenvalues are \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) with \(\cos\theta=a\). Beyond two, one real eigenvalue has magnitude greater than one and trajectories generically grow. At exactly two the invariant loses control of position and a repeated eigenvalue can cause linear growth. An apparently bounded run starting from specially chosen data at the threshold cannot certify stability for arbitrary perturbations.

Follow a step and its timing

For \(\omega=1,h=1,q_0=1,p_0=0\), the half kick gives \(-0.5\), the drift gives \(q_1=0.5\), and the final half kick gives \(p_1=-0.75\). The physical energy decreases from 0.5 to 0.40625 in this first step. The modified energy \((p^2+0.75q^2)/2\) equals 0.375 at both points. The discrete phase is \(\theta=\pi/3\), so the computed trajectory returns after six steps. The true oscillator returns after \(2\pi\approx6.283\) seconds. A stable trajectory with a bounded energy error can therefore accumulate a large timing error over many cycles.

For small \(z\), expanding \(\theta=2\arcsin(z/2)\) gives \(\theta=z+z^3/24+O(z^5)\). At a fixed final time the accumulated phase error is therefore second order in \(h\). A period estimate and an energy trace probe different aspects of long-time integration and should be reported separately.

Why the initial velocity test matters

A test with only the initial position nonzero may conceal the borderline instability at \(h\omega=2\). At that boundary the update is \(q_{n+1}=-q_n+hp_n,\ p_{n+1}=-p_n\). For \(p_0=0\) the positions merely alternate sign forever. With \(q_0=0\) and \(p_0=1\), however, explicit iteration gives \(p_n=(-1)^n\) and \(q_n=(-1)^{n-1}nh\). The position magnitude grows linearly. A stability test must account for all independent starting directions or small perturbations of the chosen one, especially when an eigenvalue of modulus one is repeated.

Prove conservation of angular momentum by individual substeps

For a particle of constant mass in a radial potential \(V(\|q\|)\), the force is parallel to the position vector. Its angular momentum is \(L=q\times p\). During a kick of duration \(\tau\), momentum changes by \(\tau F(q)\) at fixed position, so the change in \(L\) is \(\tau q\times F(q)=0\). During a drift, position changes by \(\tau p/m\) at fixed momentum, giving change \((\tau/m)p\times p=0\). The full kick–drift–kick map therefore preserves angular momentum exactly in arithmetic without rounding, for any step length for which these evaluations are defined.

The proof uses radial force and the relationship between velocity and momentum. An external noncentral force exerts a physical torque, while a coordinate-dependent mass matrix changes the drift subflow. Under either modification the two zero cross products need rechecking. The argument also gives no bound on energy or phase. Exact conservation of angular momentum constrains orbital geometry but leaves freedom to move from the correct energy level onto a nearby one or to return to a point on the orbit at the wrong time.

Compare three diagnostics on an elliptical oscillator trajectory

Use unit mass and two-dimensional potential \(V=(x^2+y^2)/2\). Start at \(q=(1,0)\), \(p=(0,2)\), so \(L=2\) and physical energy is \(E=(1+4)/2=2.5\). At step \(h=0.5\), the first half kick gives \(p_{1/2}=(-0.25,2)\). The drift leads to \(q_1=(0.875,1)\). The last half kick subtracts \(0.25q_1\), giving \(p_1=(-0.46875,1.75)\). The resulting planar angular momentum is \(0.875(1.75)-1(-0.46875)=2\), exactly as proved.

Calculate energy separately. The squared position is \(0.875^2+1=1.765625\), and the squared momentum is \(0.46875^2+1.75^2=3.2822265625\). Hence the physical energy after this step is 2.52392578125, about 0.96 percent above the initial value. The exact continuous position at this time is \((\cos0.5,2\sin0.5)\approx(0.87758,0.95885)\), so the position error is concentrated in the second coordinate. Angular momentum, physical energy and the individual trajectory coordinates expose three distinct properties of the same update.

Because the harmonic force is separable by coordinate, the modified invariant from the scalar oscillator adds across the two directions: \([\|p\|^2+(1-h^2/4)\|q\|^2]/2\) is constant. Substitution gives 2.46875 at both steps, independently checking the arithmetic. For a general radial potential this particular quadratic modified invariant no longer follows, while the angular momentum proof remains valid. A conservation claim should identify the symmetry responsible rather than assuming every observed invariant survives a change of potential.

If there were a small constant transverse force, the physical change in angular momentum would be the time integral of \(q\times F_{\rm ext}\). A better diagnostic would subtract the accumulated applied torque from the measured change in \(L\), rather than demand that \(L\) remain fixed. This distinction also guards against a subtle implementation error: zeroing a real torque to improve a conservation plot would change the physical problem and could hide a significant trajectory bias.

Measuring the signed torque makes its direction as well as its magnitude available for this balance check.

Chapter 10 · Tangent equations and adjoint outputs

Compute how an observed final state changes when an initial state or a model parameter is perturbed.

From first principles

Differentiate the trajectory that your instrument measures

Let a scalar parameter \(\theta\) enter both a smooth rate law \(y'=f(t,y,\theta)\) and the initial data \(y(0)=g(\theta)\). Compare two solutions at the same physical time. Their difference divided by a small parameter increment tends to the sensitivity \(S=\partial y/\partial\theta\). Differentiating the rate law at fixed time supplies a term from the moving state and a direct parameter term:

\[\dot S=f_y S+f_\theta,\]
\[S(0)=g'(\theta).\]

For a scalar terminal measurement \(J=\psi(y(T),\theta)\) the chain rule gives \(J'=\psi_y S(T)+\psi_\theta\). When many parameters enter a single output, a backward equation can reuse their common output weighting. Choose \(\lambda(T)=\psi_y^T\) and \(\dot\lambda=-f_y^T\lambda\). Then the terms containing the unknown sensitivity cancel in the derivative of their scalar product:

\[\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda^T S)=\lambda^Tf_\theta,\]
\[J'=\psi_\theta+\lambda(0)^Tg'(\theta)+\int_0^T\lambda^Tf_\theta\,dt.\]

Evaluate all derivatives on the original trajectory. For \(y'=-\theta y\) with fixed \(y(0)\), direct differentiation gives \(S(T)=-Ty(T)\), the sign expected when increasing the decay rate. A parameter-dependent observation time would add an endpoint term.

Derivation and applications

Differentiate the numerical update that produced the observed output

A gradient used in fitting a parameter should agree with the discretized objective actually evaluated. Consider explicit Euler on a scalar equation \(y'=f(y,\theta,t)\) with fixed, parameter-independent time steps and initial state. A single final observation defines \(J=\Phi(y_N,\theta)\). Treat the computed states as a chain of differentiable maps rather than replacing their dependence by that of the exact differential equation.

For the step \(F_n(y,\theta)=y+h f(y,\theta,t_n)\) a small perturbation satisfies

\[\delta y_{n+1}=A_n\delta y_n+B_n\delta\theta,\]
\[A_n=1+hf_y,\]
\[B_n=hf_\theta\]

with derivatives evaluated on the stored numerical trajectory. Start from \(\delta J=\Phi_y\delta y_N+\Phi_\theta\delta\theta\) and substitute the last step. Repeating this substitution backward defines a weight on each earlier state:

\[\lambda_N=\Phi_y,\]
\[\lambda_n=A_n\lambda_{n+1}.\]

Each stage contributes its local parameter derivative weighted by the future effect of that stage. For a fixed initial condition the total derivative is

\[dJ/d\theta=\Phi_\theta+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n+1}B_n .\]

With vector states the reverse update uses the transpose Jacobian. An initial condition depending on \(\theta\) adds \(\lambda_0\,\partial_\theta y_0\). The resulting gradient is exact for the discrete map up to arithmetic precision, though it can differ from the gradient of the continuous solution. Adaptive control decisions need special care if differentiating through changes in the time grid.

Worked example

Differentiate a two-step fitted decay

Let \(y'=-\theta y\), \(y_0=2.00\), \(h=0.250\), \(\theta=1.00\), and \(J=(y_2-1)^2/2\). The Euler states are \(1.50\) and \(1.125\), giving \(\lambda_2=0.125\), \(\lambda_1=0.09375\). The local parameter derivatives are \(-0.500\) and \(-0.375\). Thus \(J'=0.09375(-0.500)+0.125(-0.375)=-0.09375\). Increasing the decay rate locally reduces the positive final residual.

Second application

Compare with the continuous sensitivity at the same time

At \(t=0.500\) the exact value is \(2e^{-0.5}=1.21306\) and its derivative is \(-0.500(1.21306)=-0.60653\). The continuous objective therefore has derivative \(-0.1292\). That difference is expected at this coarse step. A centred parameter perturbation of the two-step numerical objective should approach \(-0.09375\), providing a direct implementation check without assuming that the time integrator is already accurate.

Differentiate the evolution

Let \(y'=f(t,y,\alpha)\) with smooth \(f\) and \(y(0)=y_0(\alpha)\). Assume that the solution exists and depends differentiably on \(\alpha\) throughout the observation window. Differentiating the equation along the solution gives \(S'=f_y S+f_\alpha\) and \(S(0)=y_0'(\alpha)\), where \(S=\partial y/\partial\alpha\). The first term propagates state changes through the dynamics and the second injects the direct parameter effect. Losing the initial derivative gives the wrong sensitivity whenever a parameter also changes the initial preparation.

For an output \(Q=\phi(y(T),\alpha)\), the chain rule gives \(Q_\alpha=\phi_y S(T)+\phi_\alpha\). Each parameter requires its own tangent column. Many observations can then reuse the propagated columns. Scaling matters because a derivative with respect to milliseconds is numerically different from the derivative with respect to seconds. Multiplying by a stated parameter perturbation converts the derivative back into a predicted observation change that can be checked directly.

A rate parameter and a final measurement

Take \(y'=-\alpha y,\ y(0)=2\). Then \(S'=-\alpha S-y,\ S(0)=0\). Multiplying by \(e^{\alpha t}\) leaves \((e^{\alpha t}S)'=-2\), hence \(S=-2te^{-\alpha t}\). At \(\alpha=0.5\) per second and \(T=2\) s the sensitivity of final amplitude is \(-4/e\approx-1.47152\) amplitude units per inverse second. Raising the decay rate by 0.01 per second predicts a decrease of 0.014715. The exact change is \(2e^{-1}(e^{-0.02}-1)\approx-0.01457\); the discrepancy is quadratic in the parameter perturbation.

Testing a tangent code by differences requires controlling both the ODE integration error and subtraction error. Use several perturbation sizes. With a forward difference the truncation error normally decreases linearly at first, until numerical noise in subtracting two nearly equal outputs dominates.

Run one dual equation for a scalar output

Define a column vector \(z\) backward in time by \(-z'=f_y^Tz,\ z(T)=\nabla_y\phi\). Differentiating \(z^TS\) cancels the two terms containing \(f_y\). Integrating what remains gives

\[Q_\alpha=\phi_\alpha+z(0)^T y_0'(\alpha)+\int_0^T z^T f_\alpha\,dt.\]

For the preceding final-amplitude observation \(z=e^{-\alpha(T-t)}\). Its product with \(f_\alpha=-y\) is the constant \(-2e^{-\alpha T}\), whose integral reproduces the tangent result. One adjoint trajectory can provide gradients with respect to many parameters for this single output. Its coefficients require the forward trajectory at earlier times, so storing or reconstructing that trajectory is part of the numerical cost. If the desired derivative is of the discrete numerical update itself, differentiating its specific steps and interpolation choices can matter at finite time resolution.

A cancellation at a particular observation time

Compare observing the final amplitude with observing the lost amplitude \(Q=y(0)-y(T)\). If the rate is the only parameter, changing the observation reverses the sign of the computed rate sensitivity. If instead the unknown is the common initial amplitude, the direct derivative of \(y(0)\) is one and the propagated derivative is \(e^{-\alpha T}\). The final derivative of the loss is \(1-e^{-\alpha T}\), tending to zero as the observation window shrinks. Omitting the direct term would predict a large negative initial sensitivity for a loss that is identically zero at zero time.

Differentiate the time at which a model switches

If a trajectory switches dynamics at a threshold, the event time itself depends on the parameter. Let a pre-event solution \(y^-(t,p)\) meet \(G(t,y,p)=0\) at \(\tau(p)\). Denote by \(S^-\) its parameter derivative at fixed time, evaluated on the nominal event. Differentiating the event equation gives

\[\tau_p=-\frac{G_y S^-+G_p}{G_t+G_y f^-}.\]

The denominator is the rate at which the trajectory crosses the threshold. This formula requires a transversal crossing and a locally unchanged event sequence. At a grazing contact the derivative may diverge or cease to exist, and a perturbed trajectory can encounter a different number of events.

Assume the state itself is continuous while the right-hand side changes from \(f^-\) to \(f^+\). The total parameter change of the event state is \(S^-+f^-\tau_p\). But the post-event tangent at a fixed clock time must remove the amount of post-event evolution gained or lost by shifting the start time, giving \(S^+=S^-+(f^--f^+)\tau_p\). This jump in a derivative of a continuous state is sometimes surprising. Comparing trajectories taken at the same physical clock time is different from comparing their states at their respective event times.

Estimate the final position after a velocity switch

Let a cart start at zero and travel at constant positive speed \(p\) until it reaches position one. It then travels at fixed speed 0.5. Observe its position at \(T=2\), and examine \(p=1\), so the event occurs at \(\tau=1\). The pre-event position is \(pt\) and its tangent at fixed time is \(S^-=t=1\). Using \(G=y-1\) gives \(\tau_p=-1/p^2=-1\). Since \(f^-=1\) and \(f^+=0.5\), the tangent immediately after the switch becomes \(1+(1-0.5)(-1)=0.5\). Subsequent evolution at the fixed speed adds no further parameter source.

Check directly: as long as the event precedes the observation, \(y(T,p)=1+0.5(2-1/p)\). Its derivative at one is indeed 0.5. At \(p=1.01\) the exact final position is approximately 1.5049505 instead of 1.5. The first-order prediction is 1.505. Carrying the pre-event tangent unchanged through the switch would predict 1.51, nearly doubling the response. Resetting it to zero because the event position is fixed would miss the change entirely.

At \(p=0.5\) the switch is exactly at the observation endpoint. Parameters below this value give \(y(T)=2p\), whereas parameters above it use the switched expression. Both one-sided derivatives happen to equal two here because the two velocities match at this specific threshold value. Their second derivatives differ. Changing the post-event speed to 0.25 makes even the first derivatives disagree at the boundary. An apparent smooth result in one carefully chosen switching case cannot justify ignoring event timing throughout a parameter study.

A finite-difference sensitivity test should consequently check which events occurred on both perturbed trajectories. Agreement in total simulated duration and integrator tolerance is insufficient if the jump sequence changed. Recording event times together with the reported output makes it possible to distinguish an error in the reset law from a derivative that has ceased to exist across the selected parameter interval.

Propagate a reset depending on the event location

If a switching event resets the state by \(y^+=R(t,y^-,p)\), differentiate the reset along the moving event before returning to a fixed clock time. The result is \(S^+=R_yS^-+R_p+(R_t+R_y f^--f^+)\tau_p\). This reduces to the continuous-state formula when \(R\) is the identity. The occurrence time must still come from the pre-event trajectory, since the post-event state generally does not satisfy the original trigger condition.

Modify the cart example so the trigger position is \(H(p)=1+0.2(p-1)\), and the indicated position is reset to \(R=0.3y^-+0.2\) before it increases at speed 0.5. At \(p=1\), \(\tau=H/p=1\) and \(\tau_p=0.2-1=-0.8\). The pre-event fixed-time tangent is one. The reset gives \(S^+=0.3+(0.3-0.5)(-0.8)=0.46\). The indicated final position at time two is \(0.3H+0.2+0.5(2-H/p)\), exactly one at the nominal parameter. Differentiating this expression gives \(0.3(0.2)-0.5(0.2-1)=0.46\), independently verifying the sign and the event-time correction. Simply differentiating the reset map at the nominal crossing position would give 0.3 and lose the varying length of the post-event trajectory.

Predict a numerical period

For velocity Verlet with \(h=0.5\), \(\omega=2\), compute the stability indicator and period in time units. Starting with \(q=0,p=2\), give the first step and verify the modified energy.

Worked solution

\(z=h\omega=1\) gives \(\cos\theta=1-1/2=1/2\), hence \(\theta=\pi/3\). Six steps make a period of 3 time units, versus the exact period \(\pi\).

The first half kick is zero, the drift gives \(q=1\), and the last half kick leaves \(p=1\). With \(c=4(1-1/4)=3\), the modified energy \((p^2+3q^2)/2\) takes the value 2 at both states.

Initial data also carry the parameter

For \(y'=-\alpha y,\ y(0)=\alpha\), find \(S=\partial y/\partial\alpha\) directly and from its differential equation. At what positive observation time is the first-order sensitivity zero if \(\alpha=2\)?

Worked solution

Direct differentiation of \(y=\alpha e^{-\alpha t}\) gives \(S=e^{-\alpha t}(1-\alpha t)\). The tangent equation is \(S'=-\alpha S-y\), with \(S(0)=1\). Its integrating factor produces \(e^{\alpha t}S=1-\alpha t\).

At \(t=1/\alpha=0.5\), the initial-amplitude increase and the increased decay rate cancel to first order. Setting the initial tangent to zero would miss this cancellation completely.

An accumulated output

Let \(y'=\alpha\), \(y(0)=0\), \(Q=\int_0^T y(t)\,dt\). Compute \(dQ/d\alpha\) directly. Derive an adjoint with terminal value zero by integrating \(zS'\) by parts, and check \(T=3\).

Worked solution

The trajectory is \(\alpha t\), so \(Q=\alpha T^2/2\). The tangent satisfies \(S'=1\), \(S(0)=0\). To recover \(\int S\), choose \(-z'=1,\ z(T)=0\), giving \(z=T-t\).

Chapter 11 · Shooting for two-point boundary values

Turn a missing initial slope into a scalar root, measure the sensitivity of endpoint matching and recognise an unstable propagation direction.

From first principles

Convert a missing boundary slope into a measurable residual

Consider a twice differentiable scalar field on \(0\le x\le L\) satisfying \(y''=f(x,y,y')\), \(y(0)=a\), \(y(L)=b\). A forward initial-value solver needs both the starting value and its slope. Guess a slope \(s\), integrate the two first-order equations \(y'=v,\ v'=f(x,y,v)\) from \((a,s)\), then record the signed endpoint discrepancy:

\[R(s)=y(L;s)-b .\]

Changing the guess by a small amount changes the residual by its derivative times the change. To compute that derivative along the same trajectory define \(z=\partial_s y\), \(w=\partial_s v\). Differentiate both the equations and their initial conditions:

\[z'=w,\]
\[w'=f_y z+f_v w,\]
\[z(0)=0,\]
\[w(0)=1 .\]

Now \(R'(s)=z(L)\). Setting the linearised next residual to zero gives the Newton correction:

\[s_{\rm new}=s-\frac{y(L;s)-b}{z(L)} .\]

For \(f=0\) the propagated sensitivity is exactly \(z(x)=x\). The correction recovers the slope of the line connecting the boundary values in one step. A nearly zero \(z(L)\) signals poor slope identifiability from that endpoint. A large growing sensitivity instead makes forward propagation vulnerable to numerical contamination between the boundaries.

Derivation and applications

Recognize two shooting solutions and the fold between them

A successful endpoint residual can describe more than one solution. Consider the dimensionless steady boundary-value model \(u''+\lambda e^u=0\) on \(0<x<1\), with \(u(0)=u(1)=0\) and positive \(\lambda\). It represents a simplified nonlinear source balanced by diffusion. A shooting code would vary the unknown initial slope until the final value vanishes. To understand the possible roots, consider a symmetric trial family centred at \(x=1/2\):

\[u_\mu(x)=2\ln\!\frac{\cosh(\mu/2)}{\cosh[\mu(x-1/2)]},\]
\[\mu>0 .\]

This family automatically enforces both boundary conditions. Differentiating twice gives \(u_\mu''=-2\mu^2\operatorname{sech}^2[\mu(x-1/2)]\). The exponential source has the same spatial factor. Cancel it to determine exactly which problem parameter each profile solves:

\[\lambda(\mu)=2\mu^2\operatorname{sech}^2(\mu/2),\]
\[u'_\mu(0)=2\mu\tanh(\mu/2).\]

As \(\mu\) increases, \(\lambda\) initially grows quadratically, then decays toward zero. Differentiate its logarithm to locate the turning point that separates the two branches of initial slopes:

\[\frac{d\ln\lambda}{d\mu}=\frac2\mu-\tanh(\mu/2)=0 .\]

Numerical solution of \(\mu\tanh(\mu/2)=2\) gives \(\mu\simeq2.3994\) and \(\lambda_{\rm fold}\simeq3.5138\). Below this value and above zero the family gives two distinct symmetric positive solutions. Close to the fold a small change in \(\lambda\) can produce a large change in the solution branch. Numerical residual alone cannot tell which branch a shooting iteration found.

Worked example

Generate a known solution for checking a solver

Choose \(\mu=2\). Then \(\lambda=8/\cosh^2(1)=3.3598\), the initial slope is \(4\tanh(1)=3.0464\) and the centre value is \(2\ln\cosh(1)=0.8676\). Integrating from \((u,u')=(0,3.0464)\) should return near zero at \(x=1\) and near \(0.868\) at the midpoint. The second observation checks the shape, not only the enforced endpoint.

Second application

Identify the branch from an additional reading

At the fold the maximum centre value on the lower-\(\mu\) branch is \(2\ln\cosh(2.3994/2)=1.187\). A computed solution at \(\lambda=3.00\) with centre value \(1.98\) therefore lies on the higher-\(\mu\) branch. Solving \(\lambda(\mu)=3\) on that side gives \(\mu\approx3.29\) and centre value about \(1.98\). A different initial slope producing the same endpoint can represent another valid solution.

Replace a missing slope by a parameter

Consider \(y''=f(t,y,y')\), \(y(0)=a,\ y(T)=b\). Integrate as an initial-value problem with trial slope \(s=y'(0)\) and define \(R(s)=y(T;s)-b\). A root enforces the second boundary. If the initial-value solution is smooth in \(s\), its derivative satisfies \(v''=f_yv+f_{y'}v'\) with \(v(0)=0,\ v'(0)=1\). The derivative of the shooting residual is \(v(T)\). Newton therefore updates the missing slope by \(-R/v(T)\); a vanishing endpoint sensitivity calls for a different parameterisation or further analysis of existence and uniqueness.

Consider \(y''=-y,\ y(0)=0,\ y(T)=1\), for which \(y(t;s)=s\sin t\). At \(T=\pi/2\) the solution has \(s=1\). At \(T=\pi\) no slope reaches the required endpoint. Changing the terminal boundary to zero at the same time admits every slope. An integrator returning a tiny nonzero value for \(\sin\pi\) might suggest an enormous numerical slope, so inspect endpoint sensitivity and the mathematical boundary conditions before trusting a root.

A well-defined solution with a fragile shooting trajectory

For \(y''=k^2y,\ y(0)=1,\ y(1)=0\), direct propagation gives \(y(t)=\cosh(kt)+(s/k)\sinh(kt)\), with exact slope \(-k\coth k\). The required boundary-value solution is equivalently \(\sinh(k(1-t))/\sinh k\), a bounded positive curve. A slope perturbation \(\delta s\) produces endpoint error \(\sinh(k)\delta s/k\). For \(k=10\), this multiplier is approximately 1101.32. A slope error of \(10^{-5}\) thus creates endpoint error 0.0110 even though the exact profile stays between zero and one.

This large number describes forward amplification of slope error. The inverse sensitivity of the fitted slope to the terminal boundary value is its reciprocal. At large \(k\), direct propagation must subtract two growing numbers to recover the small endpoint. Tight initial-value tolerances can postpone the resulting loss of accuracy, while leaving the underlying direction of exponential growth intact.

Match shorter intervals

In multiple shooting introduce independent state guesses at intermediate nodes. Integrate each segment and impose matching equations equating its terminal state to the next guess. Together with the boundary conditions these equations form a sparse coupled nonlinear system. On a half interval the growing factor for \(k=10\) is \(e^5\) instead of \(e^{10}\). Requiring continuity couples the segments while reducing the range of each forward propagation. Cancelling all the intermediate states algebraically can reintroduce the original long product.

Check the final curve against both endpoint conditions, the maximum matching defect and the differential equation. Small endpoint mismatch alone says little about the interiors of independently matched segments. For highly unstable propagation a simultaneous boundary-value discretisation or collocation method can be a more suitable formulation.

Separate root iteration error from integration error

Suppose the propagated terminal state carries absolute numerical error at most \(\epsilon\). Near a simple shooting root the induced slope uncertainty is approximately \(\epsilon/|R'(s)|\). For the oscillator with \(T=\pi/6\), the sensitivity is one half, so endpoint uncertainty \(10^{-5}\) leaves slope uncertainty roughly \(2\times10^{-5}\). Solving the numerical shooting equation to a terminal residual of \(10^{-12}\) would not establish twelve accurate digits of slope. Change the propagation tolerance independently and compare the recovered slopes before interpreting the outer root solver's convergence.

Use shooting to locate an eigenvalue

For a string fixed at both ends, the eigenvalue problem \(-u''=\lambda u,\ u(0)=u(L)=0\) has the trivial solution for every parameter. A shooting formulation that merely adjusts the initial slope while allowing it to vanish can therefore converge to an uninformative zero mode. Fix the amplitude scale instead by prescribing \(u'(0)=1\). For each trial positive \(\lambda\) define the residual \(R(\lambda)=u(L;\lambda)\). A root then enforces the far boundary for a nonzero solution because the prescribed starting slope excludes the trivial state.

On the unit interval the solution is \(u(t;\lambda)=\sin(\sqrt\lambda\,t)/\sqrt\lambda\), so the residual vanishes at \(\lambda_j=j^2\pi^2,\ j=1,2,\ldots\). The limiting solution at zero is \(u=t\), which fails the far boundary. At \(\lambda<0\) the sine becomes a hyperbolic sine and again cannot vanish away from the origin. These facts locate the whole physical spectrum before considering numerical root searches. For a variable material coefficient the simple formula disappears, but amplitude normalisation and endpoint residual remain well-defined.

Bracket a mode and check its identity inside the interval

It is convenient to solve for wave number \(k=\sqrt\lambda>0\). At \(k=3\) the residual is \(\sin3/3\approx0.04704\), and at \(k=3.2\) it is \(\sin3.2/3.2\approx-0.01824\), bracketing the first eigenvalue. Its derivative is \((k\cos k-\sin k)/k^2\). Starting with \(k=\pi\) in the exact formula gives \(R'= -1/\pi\). An absolute integration error of \(10^{-5}\) in the endpoint displacement can therefore shift the fitted wave number by roughly \(3.14\times10^{-5}\). Propagating through \(\lambda=k^2\) gives eigenvalue uncertainty about \(1.97\times10^{-4}\).

A residual search on \(k\in[2,7]\) could instead overlook both \(k=\pi\) and \(2\pi\), since the residuals at the endpoints have the same positive sign. Counting interior nodes provides an independent mode label. With normalisation \(u'(0)=1\), the first eigenfunction has no internal zero and the second has exactly one at \(t=1/2\). Check these zero locations on the computed trajectory at a resolution sufficient to distinguish oscillations. A small endpoint residual alone confirms neither the desired mode index nor the absence of missed modes.

For high modes the physical wavelength decreases and the initial-value integrator must resolve phase over many cycles. A frequency estimate can drift substantially even while the trajectory's amplitude remains bounded. Refining the propagation tolerance, refining the scalar root tolerance and counting the interior nodes are three different checks. Changing all three simultaneously can hide which part of an apparent convergence failure needs attention.

A second normalisation issue appears when comparing eigenfunctions with sensor data. Multiplying any computed eigenfunction by a nonzero constant or by minus one leaves both homogeneous boundary conditions and the eigenvalue unchanged. Matching raw amplitudes or signs between two independently found modes can therefore suggest an error where only conventions differ. Normalise both functions in the same mass-weighted norm and align their signs before checking their shapes; keep the nodal count as a separate check on the mode index.

Chapter 12 · Differential–algebraic equations and consistency

Derive a reduced evolution when an algebraic variable can be eliminated and find the hidden velocity constraint in a mechanical example.

From first principles

Determine which initial states can satisfy an algebraic constraint

A differential–algebraic model may evolve \(y\) while determining \(z\) instantaneously through a constraint. In a semi-explicit model, \(y'=f(t,y,z)\) specifies a rate and \(g(t,y,z)=0\) specifies admissible states. Initial data must satisfy the latter before the first time step. A differentiable solution must remain on it, hence the time derivative along the trajectory vanishes:

\[0=\frac{d}{dt}g(t,y,z)=g_t+g_y f+g_z\dot z .\]

If the square matrix \(g_z\) is invertible near the initial state, the constraint locally determines \(z\) from \(t,y\), and the hidden rate is

\[\dot z=-g_z^{-1}(g_t+g_y f).\]

For example impose the unit circle \(y^2+z^2=1\) with \(y'=u(t)\). On its upper half \(z=\sqrt{1-y^2}\), giving \(\dot z=-yu/z\). Algebraic consistency fixes \(z(0)\) once the branch and \(y(0)\) are known. At \(z=0\) this coordinate elimination becomes singular even though the circle itself is smooth. A constrained implicit time step imposes the original algebraic equation at the new time, which prevents the numerical drift that can accumulate from integrating only its differentiated version.

Derivation and applications

Derive the constraint force of a pendulum without eliminating coordinates

A solver using Cartesian coordinates for a pendulum must satisfy geometric, velocity and acceleration constraints. Let \(\mathbf r=(x,y)\) be the displacement from a fixed pivot, \(\mathbf v=\dot{\mathbf r}\), \(m\) the bob mass, and \(L\) the fixed link length. The applied force \(\mathbf F\) excludes the constraint reaction. Write the reaction as \(-\lambda\mathbf r\), so positive \(\lambda\) corresponds to an inward force and has units of N/m. The holonomic constraint and its first derivative are

\[g=\tfrac12(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r-L^2)=0,\]
\[\dot g=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v=0.\]

Differentiating once more shows that the acceleration is generally not tangent: maintaining curvature of the trajectory requires an inward normal component. Insert Newton's law and keep the known tangential speed:

\[\ddot g=|\mathbf v|^2+\mathbf r\cdot\dot{\mathbf v}=0,\]
\[m\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf F-\lambda\mathbf r .\]

Solving for the scalar multiplier gives both the required magnitude and the tangentially projected forcing:

\[\lambda=\frac{m|\mathbf v|^2+\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf F}{L^2},\]
\[\dot{\mathbf v}=\frac{\mathbf F}{m}-\frac{\lambda}{m}\mathbf r .\]

The multiplier can be found only after compatible position and velocity are supplied. Satisfying the position constraint alone at the initial time does not remove radial velocity. For a rigid rod either sign of \(\lambda\) is admissible. A string goes slack when the bilateral model would require compression.

Worked example

Calculate a reaction at the bottom of a swing

Choose \(y\) upward, \(m=0.200\) kg, \(L=0.500\) m, \(\mathbf r=(0,-0.500)\) m and \(\mathbf v=(1.00,0)\) m/s. Gravity is \((0,-1.962)\) N. Thus \(\lambda=[0.200+0.981]/0.250=4.724\) N/m, giving tension \(\lambda L=2.36\) N. The net upward acceleration is \((2.362-1.962)/0.200=2.00\) m/s², independently equal to \(v^2/L\).

Second application

Detect an inconsistent input and a physically slack string

At the same bottom position, a supplied velocity \((1.00,0.100)\) m/s has \(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v=-0.0500\) m²/s. Removing its radial projection gives \((1.00,0)\) m/s before evaluating the multiplier. At the top, instead take \(\mathbf r=(0,0.500)\) m and tangential speed \(1.00\) m/s. The bilateral equation predicts \(\lambda L=0.400-1.962=-1.56\) N. A string cannot provide that outward push, so a taut-string simulation must release the constraint there.

A state has fewer independent coordinates than entries

A semi-explicit system has \(y'=f(t,y,z)\) and \(0=g(t,y,z)\). The algebraic equation supplies a condition at every instant; choosing every initial coordinate independently can violate it before time integration starts. If \(g_z\) is square and nonsingular locally, the implicit function theorem determines \(z=\psi(t,y)\) and substitution gives an ordinary differential equation for \(y\). Differentiating the constraint gives \(g_z z'=-g_t-g_y f\), so one differentiation determines the missing derivative. This is the usual semi-explicit index-one situation.

For \(y'=-y+z,\quad0=y+2z-t\), one has \(z=(t-y)/2\) and \(y'=(t-3y)/2\). If \(y(0)=2\), consistency forces \(z(0)=-1\) and \(y'(0)=-3\). Differentiating the constraint gives \(z'(0)=(1-y'(0))/2=2\). Entering both initial coordinates as zero and two in the wrong order would describe a different trajectory even if a numerical solver silently repaired their algebraic inconsistency.

Discretise both conditions at the same time

A backward Euler step at \(h=0.2\) enforces \(y_1-2=0.2(-y_1+z_1)\) and \(y_1+2z_1=0.2\). Eliminating \(z_1\) gives \(1.3y_1=2.02\), so \(y_1\approx1.553846\) and \(z_1\approx-0.676923\). Computing the second variable from the new time matters. Using \(z_0\) throughout the update and reporting it as the new algebraic variable would violate the current constraint. Report its residual together with the local or global accuracy estimate for \(y\).

Hidden constraints and the force multiplier

For a unit-mass pendulum with Cartesian position \(q\), velocity \(v\), rod length \(L\) and downward gravity vector \(g\), take \(q'=v,\ v'=g-\lambda q\) and \(q^Tq=L^2\). Differentiating the rod constraint once imposes \(q^Tv=0\); velocity must be tangent to the circle. Differentiating again gives \(v^Tv+q^Tg-\lambda L^2=0\), determining the multiplier \(\lambda=(v^Tv+q^Tg)/L^2\). The original position constraint contains no direct multiplier, so the index-one elimination used above does not apply.

At the bottom of a 1 m pendulum take \(q=(0,-1)\) m, \(v=(2,0)\) m/s and \(g=(0,-9.81)\) m/s². The multiplier is \(13.81\) s⁻² and acceleration is \((0,4)\) m/s², the required inward centripetal acceleration. A velocity with a nonzero vertical component would already violate tangency at this position. Advancing differentiated equations alone can let position and tangent constraints drift because rounding and discretisation accumulate. Check both \(q^Tq-L^2\) and \(q^Tv\) over time; projecting position alone may leave a velocity pointing slightly through the rod constraint.

An algebraic residual can hide an inaccurate state

For the linear example, change a consistent state at fixed time by \(\delta y=0.02,\ \delta z=-0.01\). Its constraint residual stays exactly zero, since those changes cancel in \(y+2z\). Its differential right-hand side changes by \(-0.03\). The perturbation lies along the constraint manifold and is invisible to any diagnostic that checks only the algebraic equation. Conversely a perturbation normal to the manifold can be easy to see in the residual even if \(y\) is accurate. Estimate time-discretisation error by a separate comparison, such as refining the time step at a common physical output time.

Project an inconsistent state in a physically weighted metric

Suppose linear constraints require \(Bq=g\), while an unconstrained time step produces \(q^\ast\) with a small violation. A correction can be selected by minimising \((q-q^\ast)^TM(q-q^\ast)/2\) subject to the constraint, with \(M\) symmetric positive definite and \(B\) of full row rank. Stationarity gives \(M(q-q^\ast)+B^T\lambda=0\). Inserting the resulting \(q=q^\ast-M^{-1}B^T\lambda\) into the constraint yields the smaller system \(BM^{-1}B^T\lambda=Bq^\ast-g\). This formula corrects exactly the component normal to the feasible plane in the chosen metric.

If \(M\) is a mass matrix for velocity variables, minimising this norm has a kinetic-energy interpretation. For position variables it is still a chosen distance measure and should be justified by the desired correction. Dependent constraint rows can make the multiplier system singular even when the geometrically projected state is unique. Nonlinear constraints require updating their Jacobian during a projection, and a large correction may find a different feasible branch. Satisfying the algebraic constraint alone does not guarantee a time-accurate differential state.

Position and velocity require two distinct projections

Let \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\), \(B=(1,1)\) and require \(q_1+q_2=2\). An unconstrained update gives \(q^\ast=(1.2,1.0)^T\), with gap 0.2. The scalar multiplier matrix is \(1+1/4=1.25\), so \(\lambda=0.16\). Since \(M^{-1}B^T=(1,1/4)^T\), the correction is \((-0.16,-0.04)^T\), leaving \(q=(1.04,0.96)^T\). A Euclidean projection would subtract 0.1 from each coordinate. Both restore the same constraint, but the mass-weighted correction moves the heavier coordinate less.

For a constant constraint value, consistent velocities must also satisfy \(Bv=0\). Suppose \(v^\ast=(2,-1)^T\), with violation one. The velocity multiplier is \(1/1.25=0.8\), giving \(v=(1.2,-1.2)^T\). Its kinetic energy is \((1.2^2+4\cdot1.2^2)/2=3.6\), compared with \((2^2+4)/2=4\) before correction. The lost 0.4 is exactly half the squared mass norm of the removed normal component \((0.8,0.2)^T\). Orthogonality eliminates the cross term in this energy decomposition.

This reduction illustrates why projecting velocities can suppress energy even if the underlying unconstrained update had no dissipative force. In a simulation of sustained motion, report correction norms or constraint impulses along with the residual after projection. With a moving constraint \(Bq=g(t)\), the velocity condition becomes \(Bv=g'(t)\), so forced boundary motion can do physical work. Applying a zero-velocity projection to that case would remove a legitimate component of the solution.

For the linear position example the chosen correction is also the smallest in the prescribed metric by direct comparison. Every other feasible correction has the form \((-0.16,-0.04)+t(1,-1)\). Its squared mass norm equals \(0.032+5t^2\) because the mass-weighted cross term vanishes. The Euclidean correction corresponds to \(t=0.06\), giving squared mass norm 0.05 rather than 0.032. This calculation makes the meaning of the multiplier solution visible without invoking a general optimisation theorem. If \(B\) varies with time, differentiating \(B(t)q=g(t)\) also introduces the term \(B'(t)q\) in the velocity condition, even for a constant prescribed value.

The derivative of the constraint must therefore be evaluated at the same time as the corrected velocities.

Chapter 13 · Implicit midpoint, reversibility and the phase of an oscillator

Imagine integrating a mechanical trajectory forward, saving only its end state and then running the same method backward. Recovering the start is a stronger demand than obtaining a plausibly small one-step error. Even exact recovery does not guarantee that an oscillation peaked at the correct time.

Build a method from a centred difference

Integrate \(y'=f(t,y)\) over an interval of length \(h\). Midpoint quadrature approximates the integral by \(h\) times its integrand at \(t_n+h/2\). The arithmetic mean of the unknown endpoint states approximates the exact midpoint state up to order \(h^2\) on a smooth trajectory. Using this mean as the derivative's state argument gives

\[y_{n+1}-y_n=h f\!\left(t_n+h/2,(y_n+y_{n+1})/2\right).\]

Errors from midpoint quadrature and from evaluating \(f\) at the approximate midpoint both contribute \(O(h^3)\) locally, giving global order two on a fixed interval under the usual regularity and stability conditions. In general the unknown appears inside \(f\), so an implicit equation must be solved. If \(f\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L_f\), direct fixed-point iteration contracts when \(|h|L_f/2<1\). Newton iteration can converge over a wider range. Neither a failed nonlinear solve nor loose tolerances inherit the exact algebraic properties of the method.

Exchange \(y_n,y_{n+1}\) and replace \(h\) by \(-h\). The left side changes sign, the arithmetic midpoint is identical and the midpoint time is identical if the reverse step starts at \(t_{n+1}\). Thus, on the same locally unique solution branch, \(\Phi_{-h}=\Phi_h^{-1}\). A round trip using variable steps must repeat the accepted steps in reverse order. Independently choosing a new adaptive mesh on the way back need not retrace the states.

Solve the step for a harmonic oscillator

Write \(q'=\omega p,\ p'=-\omega q\), with scaled momentum \(p\) in the same units as \(q\), and \(z=q+ip\). The ODE becomes \(z'=-i\omega z\). Solving the linear midpoint equation gives the scalar multiplier

\[M(u)=\frac{1-iu/2}{1+iu/2}=\exp[-2i\arctan(u/2)],\]
\[u=\omega h.\]

Its numerator and denominator are complex conjugates. Hence it preserves \(q^2+p^2\), proportional to the oscillator energy, exactly in real arithmetic. For any radius or step size \(M(-u)M(u)=1\). The rotation per step is \(\theta=2\arctan(u/2)=u-u^3/12+O(u^5)\). The numerical phase lags by \(u-\theta\) per step. After \(N\) steps the accumulated lag is \(N(u-\theta)\), increasing linearly in elapsed time even while the energy remains exactly constant.

Compare explicit midpoint Runge–Kutta. A half Euler predictor leads to \(M_{\rm RK2}=1-iu-u^2/2\). It also has order two, but \(|M_{\rm RK2}|^2=1+u^4/4\). The amplitude grows slowly at every nonzero step. A forward and reverse pair multiplies the starting amplitude by \(1+u^4/4\). In a conservative problem these two second-order procedures can produce visibly different long records.

Chapter 14 · Splitting a stiff relaxation from a slowly varying drive

A sensor tries to follow a changing reference while a fast relaxation damps departures. An explicit method may oscillate wildly even though the underlying transient decays monotonically. Treating the restoring term implicitly can resolve the slow motion using steps longer than the transient time. Whether those steps measure the transient accurately is a different question.

Construct an exact benchmark with two time scales

Let \(s(t)=\sin t\) and choose a relaxation time \(\varepsilon>0\). Consider \(y'=s'(t)+(s(t)-y)/\varepsilon\), with \(y(0)=1\). Set \(e=y-s\). Subtraction of \(s'\) gives \(e'=-e/\varepsilon\), hence the reference solution \(y(t)=\sin t+e^{-t/\varepsilon}\). The slow reference changes over times of order one, while the initial mismatch decays significantly over \(\varepsilon\). We know both pieces exactly and can diagnose errors even when a stable result looks visually smooth.

Explicit Euler applied to a perturbation of the state multiplies that perturbation by \(1-r\), with \(r=h/\varepsilon\). It damps monotonically only for \(0<r\le1\), changes sign while damping for \(1<r<2\), and grows for \(r>2\). These statements compare nearby solutions at equal drive. The full signal also contains the time variation of \(s\). At the boundary \(r=2\) a state perturbation alternates without decaying. Refining the step to follow a slow input alone can therefore give the wrong choice of \(h\).

Solve only the fast part implicitly

Split \(f_{\rm slow}(t)=\cos t\) from \(f_{\rm fast}(t,y)=(\sin t-y)/\varepsilon\). Evaluate the slow part at \(t_n\) and the fast part at \(t_{n+1},y_{n+1}\). This first-order additive implicit-explicit Euler step needs one scalar linear solve:

\[(1+r)y_{n+1}=y_n+h\cos t_n+r\sin t_{n+1}.\]

The state perturbation now multiplies by \(1/(1+r)\). It is damped at every positive step and tends to zero as \(r\) grows. Subtract \(\sin t_{n+1}\) from both sides after solving. The mismatch obeys \(e_{n+1}=[e_n+h\cos t_n-(\sin t_{n+1}-\sin t_n)]/(1+r)\). The bracketed forcing defect is \(O(h^2)\) for smooth input. In the stiff limit at fixed \(h\), the endpoint approaches the current reference. Moving the reference in the fast term to the old time would instead drag it one sample behind in that limit. The choice of what to treat implicitly includes the time at which a fast equilibrium is imposed.

Test early-time accuracy and late-time stability

For \(\varepsilon=0.02\), \(h=0.05\), \(r=2.5\). At the first step the exact value is \(\sin(0.05)+e^{-2.5}=0.1321\). Explicit Euler gives \(1+0.05(1-50)=-1.45\). The implicit-explicit value is \([1+0.05+2.5\sin(0.05)]/3.5=0.3357\). The latter is stable but visibly overestimates the surviving transient. Its damping factor \(1/3.5=0.286\) differs substantially from \(e^{-2.5}=0.0821\). A question about peak rate or initial settling requires shorter steps even if the late-time reference is recovered.

Chapter 15 · Why a boundary value problem can be easier than its shooting equation

A concentration decays across a slab, with concentrations known at both faces. Computing a solution from the first face alone can bring in an exponentially growing mode. Enforcing both faces together gives a different numerical problem with the same differential equation.

Distinguish the boundary map from the physical solution

Use a nondimensional interval \(0\le x\le1\) and write \(y''=\lambda^2y\), with \(y(0)=1\), \(y(1)=e^{-\lambda}\), \(\lambda>0\). The exact solution is \(e^{-\lambda x}\), a smooth layer of thickness approximately \(1/\lambda\). A shooting method prescribes a tentative slope \(s=y'(0)\), integrates the initial value problem, and adjusts \(s\) to meet the second boundary. The exact initial-value solution is \(\cosh(\lambda x)+(s/\lambda)\sinh(\lambda x)\). The correct slope is \(-\lambda\). The small right boundary is recovered by subtracting two large nearly equal terms.

Perturb the exact slope by \(\delta s\). Superposition gives the resulting error everywhere without a second initial-value solve:

\[\delta y(x)=\frac{\delta s}{\lambda}\sinh(\lambda x),\]
\[\frac{d\,y(1)}{ds}=\frac{\sinh\lambda}{\lambda}.\]

At \(\lambda=20\) the right-end amplification is \(1.21\times10^7\). A slope error as small as \(10^{-12}\) gives about \(1.21\times10^{-5}\) endpoint error, while the target value is just \(2.06\times10^{-9}\). The actual boundary problem is much less sensitive to changes in prescribed concentrations. Its basis functions for arbitrary face values are \(\sinh[\lambda(1-x)]/\sinh\lambda\) and \(\sinh(\lambda x)/\sinh\lambda\), each bounded by one. A stable physical boundary problem can be paired with an ill-conditioned forward initial-value calculation. The particular derivative above measures sensitivity of the forward shooting evaluation.

Enforce the equation at the interior nodes

Take \(N\) equal intervals with \(h=1/N\). The centred second difference of any smooth exact solution equals \(y''(x_j)+h^2y^{(4)}(x_j)/12+O(h^4)\), by Taylor expansion about \(x_j\). Set that second difference equal to \(\lambda^2Y_j\). Incorporate the known boundary data \(Y_0,Y_N\) into the first and last right-hand sides. The interior equations are

\[-Y_{j-1}+[2+(\lambda h)^2]Y_j-Y_{j+1}=0,\]
\[j=1,\ldots,N-1.\]

Each row now couples neighbouring nodes and no row asks for a missing starting slope. The diagonal exceeds the sum of the off-diagonal magnitudes for \(\lambda>0\). Eliminate the lower diagonal from left to right and substitute back from the right. Both work and storage grow linearly with \(N\). This finite-difference collocation approximates the differential equation at grid points. It still needs enough points inside the layer. The grid parameter \(\lambda h\) measures its resolution.

At \(\lambda=5\) and just two intervals there is one unknown midpoint. Its equation gives \(Y_1=(1+e^{-5})/(2+2.5^2)=0.1220\), while the exact midpoint is \(e^{-2.5}=0.08208\). Both boundary values are satisfied exactly. Their residual alone cannot reveal the interior error. As \(h\) is halved at fixed \(\lambda\), a well-resolved smooth solution exhibits second-order convergence.

Chapter 16 · How two decaying modes produce a growing signal

Solve a triangular system exactly, derive the energy growth criterion and separate a real transient from numerical instability.

A coupling hidden by the eigenvalues

Let \(y_1'=-y_1+K y_2,\ y_2'=-2y_2\), with time in scaled units. The eigenvalues are −1 and −2 for every \(K\). Their negative real parts guarantee asymptotic decay of this system. They do not say that \(\|y(t)\|\) must decay monotonically. The second component acts as a temporary source in the first component before being damped away.

With initial state \((0,1)\), \(y_2=e^{-2t}\). Multiply the first equation by \(e^t\) and integrate from zero to \(t\). The result is \(y_1=K(e^{-t}-e^{-2t})\). The largest absolute excursion of the first component occurs when \(2e^{-2t}=e^{-t}\), giving \(t=\log2\) and \(y_1=K/4\). At \(K=12\), a unit initial state generates a measured first component of 3.

Which initial directions grow at the initial instant?

Write \(E=\|y\|_2^2/2\). Differentiation gives \(E'=y^T S y\), with \(S=(A+A^T)/2\). Here \(S\) has diagonal \((-1,-2)\) and off-diagonal \(K/2\). A positive eigenvalue appears when the determinant \(2-K^2/4\) becomes negative. Thus some direction has positive instantaneous energy growth for \(|K|>\sqrt8\). The specific initial vector above nevertheless has \(E'(0)=-2\). Existence of a growing direction and immediate growth of every chosen initial condition are separate statements.

A numerical check with a false lead

Set \(K=8\). At \(t=\log2\) the exact components are \(2,1/4\). Forward Euler with a sufficiently small step will show an initial dip followed by a rise in the norm. Refining time steps changes the approximation to the peak but does not eliminate the physical growth. By contrast, using a coarse Euler step \(h>1\) puts the scalar mode −2 outside the forward-Euler stability interval and introduces genuine numerical growth.

Chapter 17 · Locating a grazing event between time steps

Derive a sensitivity formula for event time, then measure how a harmless error in a trajectory can create or remove two threshold crossings.

A time is an implicitly defined output

Suppose an integrator advances \(y'=f(t,y)\) and an event occurs at \(g(t,y(t))=0\). Along the solution the derivative of the event function is \(\dot g=g_t+\nabla_y g\cdot f\). A perturbation \(\delta g\) in the computed event function shifts a simple event time by \(\delta t\simeq-\delta g/\dot g\). This estimate separates trajectory accuracy from event-time accuracy. At a tangency the denominator vanishes and the formula cannot be used.

Resolve two crossings analytically

Take \(g(t)=(t-t_0)^2-d\), a local model for a turning point relative to a sensor threshold. For \(d>0\) the events are \(t_0\pm\sqrt d\). Their separation is \(2\sqrt d\), and their slopes are \(\pm2\sqrt d\). For \(d=0\) there is only a tangent touch. If \(d<0\), the sensor is never triggered. An additive constant perturbation \(\epsilon\) changes the effective parameter from \(d\) to \(d-\epsilon\).

At \(t_0=1\), \(d=0.01\) and \(\epsilon=0.001\), the first event moves from 0.9 to about 0.90513. The linear prediction is \(0.001/0.2=0.005\). At \(d=0.0004\) the same perturbation removes both crossings entirely. Reporting more digits from a polished scalar rootfinder would not recover them.

What two endpoint samples fail to tell you

With \(d=0.0025\), a step from 0.9 to 1.1 has equal positive endpoint values of 0.0075 but crosses the threshold twice. Dense interpolation followed by an extremum check can detect this pair if the interpolant resolves the turning point. A sign-change test at accepted endpoints alone cannot. An interpolant is itself an approximation, so event feasibility should be tested against an error scale at the extremum.

Applied study

Distinguish stability, positivity and accuracy when stepping a model forward in time.

How does a short step change a long trajectory?

A well-mixed reactant A converts to B at rate \(kA\). In a closed vessel \(\dot A=-kA\) and \(\dot B=kA\). For \(A(0)=1\), the exact concentration of A is \(e^{-kt}\) in the chosen units, while \(A+B\) remains constant. A numerical scheme can preserve the total exactly and still put the wrong amount in each species.

Integrating the differential equation over one time interval gives \(A_{n+1}=A_n-k\int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}}A(t)\,dt\). Replacing the integrand by its left endpoint yields forward Euler, \(A_{n+1}=(1-z)A_n\) with \(z=kh\). Using the unknown endpoint instead gives backward Euler, \(A_{n+1}=A_n/(1+z)\). Each method replaces the exact amplification factor \(e^{-z}\) by a simpler function \(R(z)\).

Distinguish stability, positivity and accuracy

For the decay mode, forward Euler damps successive values when \(|1-z|<1\), or \(0<z<2\). Keeping an initially positive concentration nonnegative requires the stronger condition \(z\leq1\). At \(z=1.5\) the forward Euler values alternate in sign as their magnitudes shrink. Backward Euler has \(R(z)=1/(1+z)>0\) for every positive step, although a large step can still give a poor quantity of reactant.

Taylor expansion gives \(e^{-z}=1-z+z^2/2+\cdots\). Euler misses a term of order \(h^2\) per step. Over a fixed time interval the number of steps is proportional to \(1/h\), producing first-order global error under stable propagation. Classical four-stage Runge–Kutta (RK4) evaluates intermediate slopes. On this linear problem its factor is \(R_4=1-z+z^2/2-z^3/6+z^4/24\). It first differs from the exponential at order \(h^5\), giving fourth-order global convergence.

Test a hand calculation against the exact decay

Take \(k=0.8\) s⁻¹ and \(h=0.625\) s, giving \(z=0.5\). After two steps, the exact fraction remaining is \(e^{-1}=0.36788\). Forward Euler gives \(0.5^2=0.25\) and backward Euler gives \((1/1.5)^2=0.44444\). Both give positive, decaying sequences, but one consumes too much A and the other too little. The signs follow from \(1-z<e^{-z}<1/(1+z)\) for \(z>0\).

RK4 gives \(R_4(0.5)=0.6067708\), hence \(A_2\simeq0.36817\). Comparing all methods at the same physical time separates time error from a simple difference in how long the reaction has proceeded.
