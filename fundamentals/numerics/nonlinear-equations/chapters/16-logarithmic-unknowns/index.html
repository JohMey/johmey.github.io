Start from a positive constitutive quantity

An ideal thermistor calibration can be written \(R/R_*=e^z\). More generally a sensor fit may require a positive amplitude \(x\) satisfying its logarithmic reading \(\log x=d\). A Newton correction on \(f(x)=\log x-d\) gives

\[x_+=x[1+d-\log x].\]

Even with a unique perfectly regular root \(e^d\), this formula can propose a negative amplitude when the initial residual exceeds one. Evaluating the next logarithm would then be impossible.

Choose \(z=\log x\) instead. The equation in the new variable is \(z=d\) and one step yields the answer. For an arbitrary residual \(f(x)\), chain differentiation gives

\[d f(e^z)/dz=x f'(x).\]

Newton moves \(z\) by \(-f/(xf')\), so the update in \(x\) is multiplicative:

\[x_+=x\exp[-f(x)/(x f'(x))].\]

Near a simple root, expanding the exponential gives the ordinary first-order correction; the difference is quadratic in the relative step. A logarithmic coordinate enforces positivity at every finite iterate. It cannot restore information missing from the measurement.

Measure failure and sensitivity separately

At \(d=0\), \(x_0=10\) gives \(x_1=10(1-\log 10)\approx-13.03\) with additive Newton. Log coordinates give \(x_1=1\). Start instead at 0.1. Additive Newton gives about 0.3303 and remains admissible. Positivity depends on the proposal relative to its scale.

If \(d=\log(1000)\) with measurement uncertainty 0.01, the parameter is about 1000 with relative uncertainty 1%. The direct sensitivity follows by differentiating the equation: \(dx/dd=x\). The same 0.01 measurement error near \(x=0.001\) means an absolute error near \(10^{-5}\), again about 1%. Changing variables improves interpretation of a relative uncertainty, without changing the information in the observation.

Tutorial on relative residuals

A model supplies \(F(x)=e^{0.1x}\) and a positive observation \(y\). How do residuals \(F-y\) and \(\log F-\log y\) weight small changes near a fit?

Worked solution

Near \(F=y\),

\[\log F-\log y\approx(F-y)/y.\]

A squared log residual weights errors approximately by relative error. Choosing it implies a different observation-noise model when fitting several readings.

If the true parameter can be exactly zero, what does the logarithmic coordinate miss?

Worked solution

The zero boundary is at \(z=-\infty\). A finite transformed iterate cannot represent it. One must allow a boundary solution explicitly when the physics admits it.
