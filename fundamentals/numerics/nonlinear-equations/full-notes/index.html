Chapter 1 · Existence, brackets and error certificates

Decide whether a scalar balance has a root, isolate it with a sign-changing interval and translate an equation residual into a bound in physical units.

From first principles

Turn a sign change into a location certificate

Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and the endpoint values have opposite signs. By the intermediate value theorem at least one zero lies between them. Evaluate the midpoint \(m=(a+b)/2\). If \(f(m)=0\), the equation has been solved. Otherwise exactly one of the two half intervals still has opposite signs at its endpoints. Keep that half and repeat. From an initial width \(w_0=b-a\), the width after \(n\) such updates is

\[w_n=\frac{w_0}{2^n},\]
\[|m_n-r|\le\frac{w_n}{2}\]

for at least one enclosed root \(r\), where \(m_n\) is the midpoint of the current interval. To target an absolute location error \(\varepsilon>0\), a sufficient number of halvings is

\[n\ge\max\!\left(0,\left\lceil\log_2\frac{w_0}{2\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right).\]

The certificate uses reliable endpoint signs and continuity. A jump can change signs without crossing zero, while a tangential root can exist without changing signs. Monotonicity is optional for existence but can establish that the enclosed root is unique.

Derivation and applications

Certify the location obtained by intersecting an endpoint secant

A sign bracket guarantees an enclosed root. If derivatives are also bounded, its endpoint secant can produce a sharper certified location without a sequence of additional function evaluations. Assume \(f\in C^2[a,b]\), opposite endpoint signs, \(|f'|\ge m>0\), and \(|f''|\le M\). The derivative cannot change sign on the interval, so there is exactly one root \(r\). Form the line joining the endpoint values and solve it for zero,

\[s=a-\frac{f(a)(b-a)}{f(b)-f(a)},\]
\[a<s<b .\]

The line vanishes at \(s\), so the true residual there is exactly the error made by interpolating \(f\) by that line. The two-node interpolation remainder and the mean value theorem applied between \(s\) and \(r\) give successively

\[|f(s)|\le\tfrac12 M(s-a)(b-s),\]
\[|f(s)|=|f'(\zeta)|\,|s-r|\ge m|s-r| .\]

Combining the inequalities gives a certified enclosure centred on the proposed root approximation,

\[|s-r|\le\frac{M}{2m}(s-a)(b-s)\le\frac{M}{8m}(b-a)^2 .\]

Intersect this enclosure with the original bracket to retain both guarantees. The constants must hold over the whole interval; an observed derivative at one point supplies neither bound by itself. When the function is linear the curvature bound vanishes and the secant solves the problem exactly.

Worked example

Enclose a square root from two evaluations

For \(f(x)=x^2-2\) on \([1.4,1.5]\), the endpoint values are \(-0.04\) and \(0.25\). The secant gives \(s=1.4137931\). Since \(M=2\) and \(m=2.8\), the sharper bound is \((s-1.4)(1.5-s)/2.8=0.000425\). The true root \(1.4142136\) indeed differs by \(0.0004205\), and the location guarantee is much tighter than half the original width.

Second application

Determine the bracket width needed for a certificate

Another calibration guarantees \(m=4\) and \(M=3\) throughout the working interval. To certify secant location error \(10^{-4}\), the uniform width criterion gives \(b-a\le\sqrt{8(4)10^{-4}/3}=0.0327\). Starting at width \(0.20\), three bisections give \(0.025\). Its worst secant error is then at most \(3(0.025)^2/32=5.86\times10^{-5}\).

OneSimulation laboratory · Locate the root of an implicit cooling step

Establish the meaning of a root

A source supplies heat at a known rate, while a cooling surface loses heat at a rate that increases with temperature. A steady state equates the two rates. Writing generation minus loss as \(F(T)\) turns a physical balance into one scalar equation. Before selecting a numerical method, specify an admissible temperature interval and state which assumptions make the relation continuous there. A temperature-dependent material table with a discontinuous switch may require separate treatment of each regime.

If \(F\) is continuous and \(F(a)F(b)<0\), the intermediate value theorem guarantees at least one root in \((a,b)\). It does not count the roots. If \(F'\) has one strict sign throughout, the function is strictly monotonic and the root is unique. An even-multiplicity root can touch zero without changing sign, as \(F(x)=(x-1)^2\) does. Conversely, a sign change across the pole of \(1/x\) guarantees nothing. An interval and its regularity assumptions are part of the mathematical answer.

Turn existence into a controlled search

Evaluate the midpoint \(m=(a+b)/2\). If \(F(m)=0\) the machine has found a zero of its evaluated residual. Otherwise keep the half whose endpoint values have opposite signs. Continuity carries the existence argument through each subdivision. After \(n\) halvings the width is \(2^{-n}(b-a)\), so the midpoint has distance at most \(2^{-(n+1)}(b-a)\) from a root. A predetermined number of evaluations can therefore guarantee a target error in the independent variable even for an unknown local slope.

For \(F(x)=x^3+x-1\) on \([0,1]\), the derivative \(3x^2+1\) is positive, while the endpoints have opposite signs. Test \(0.5\), giving \(-0.375\), and keep \([0.5,1]\). Testing 0.75 gives \(0.171875\), narrowing the bracket to \([0.5,0.75]\). The next midpoint 0.625 gives approximately \(-0.13086\), hence \([0.625,0.75]\). After ten halvings the midpoint is guaranteed within \(1/2048\), independent of how the subsequent signs turn out. A function evaluation whose uncertainty covers zero cannot safely be classified as strictly positive or negative without additional information.

Translate a balance into a physical uncertainty

Let \(r\) be the root and suppose \(|F'|\geq m>0\) on a bracket containing both \(r\) and a candidate \(x\). By the mean value theorem \(F(x)-F(r)=F'(\xi)(x-r)\), so \(|x-r|\leq|F(x)|/m\). For the cubic, \(m=1\) is valid globally, and within \([0.625,0.75]\) the sharper bound \(m=1+3(0.625)^2\) applies. A balance mismatch can thus yield an informative location estimate when the slope is well separated from zero.

Suppose instead the residual of a thermal balance is expressed in watts and its slope is at least 2 W/K. A 0.1 W mismatch guarantees at most 0.05 K error under the exact model. Reporting a temperature tolerance of 0.1 solely from that residual would silently change units. Near a fold the slope can become arbitrarily small, so a tiny mismatch can coexist with substantial uncertainty about the state. In that case report the bracket, measurement uncertainty and nearby alternatives along with the small residual.

Calibrate the stop against a noisy measurement

Suppose temperatures 300 K and 302 K bracket a steady heat balance, and the residual slope is known to exceed 4 W/K. The net power sensor is accurate only to ±0.2 W. A reported mismatch of +0.1 W may have either sign, so it cannot safely choose a half-interval. Its magnitude plus the uncertainty is at most 0.3 W, however. The slope bound then implies distance at most 0.075 K from the exact balance under the model.

This gives two ways to use the same observation. If its error interval excludes zero, the sign can tighten a bracket. If it includes zero, a magnitude bound can still constrain the root location. A stopping rule that requires a residual below the instrument’s resolution might keep iterating without improving the physical estimate. Decide the temperature accuracy of interest first, then choose an appropriate balance test.

Preserve a bracket while using slope information

A secant through the two endpoints of a sign-changing bracket usually crosses the axis at a more informative location than the midpoint. For continuous \(f\), opposite signs at \(a,b\) place the linear-interpolant root \(c=[af(b)-bf(a)]/[f(b)-f(a)]\) strictly inside the bracket. Replace whichever endpoint has the same sign as \(f(c)\). This false-position iteration preserves existence just as bisection does. It differs in one significant respect: without a further safeguard, the retained bracket width need not shrink by a prescribed factor.

Suppose \(f\) is strictly increasing and convex and has root \(r\). Its chord lies above the graph. At the chord root \(c\), therefore, \(f(c)\le0\), which places \(c\le r\). Every false-position update may replace the lower endpoint while leaving the upper one fixed. The false-position estimates themselves can still approach the root. But a stopping rule requiring a narrow bracketing interval can then fail to terminate. The distinction separates convergence of a point estimate from convergence of both bounds in a certified interval.

Prevent a distant high-value endpoint from pinning the search

For \(f(x)=x^{10}-1\) on \([0,2]\), the endpoint values are −1 and 1023. The first false-position estimate is \(2/1024=1/512\), with function value indistinguishable from −1 to ordinary displayed precision. The second estimate lies near \(1/512+(2-1/512)/1024\approx0.003904\), although the true root is one. Both steps satisfy the strict interior condition; neither meaningfully resolves the enormous function variation near the right endpoint. Bisection happens to land exactly on the root here, making the consequences of this imbalance especially easy to measure.

One safeguard accepts a secant candidate only if it lies in the central half of the current bracket. Otherwise it takes the midpoint. With exact reliable signs, accepting any point between the one-quarter and three-quarter positions guarantees the retained width is at most three quarters of its old value. A midpoint fallback cuts it in half. Thus after \(k\) accepted or fallback steps the bracket width is bounded by \((3/4)^k(b-a)\), whether or not the slope model reflects the underlying function.

To test the policy away from this exact midpoint coincidence, shift the equation to \(x^{10}-1.1=0\) on the same interval. The initial chord estimate becomes \(2(1.1)/1024\), below the central half, so the method takes one and obtains residual −0.1. The next bracket is \([1,2]\), immediately discarding the whole range of tiny false-position candidates. A steeply varying function still calls for careful arithmetic in forming the chord, especially if endpoint values approach overflow. If field measurements cannot determine the sign at an accepted point, none of these purely geometric safeguards certifies which side to retain.

The shifted root is \(1.1^{1/10}\approx1.00958\), so the first midpoint is already close despite keeping a relatively wide bracket. This again separates observed point accuracy from the available certificate. Subsequent safeguarded steps can tighten the certificate without relying on advance knowledge of this exact value.

Chapter 2 · Fixed-point iteration and contraction

Select an iteration map for a nonlinear balance, prove convergence on an invariant interval and use an observed increment to bound the remaining error.

From first principles

Bound fixed-point error by a geometric series

Rearrange an equation as \(x=g(x)\) and repeatedly set \(x_{k+1}=g(x_k)\). Suppose a closed interval contains the initial point, maps into itself under \(g\), and has a uniform slope bound \(|g'|\le q<1\). The mean value theorem gives

\[|x_{k+1}-x_k|\le q|x_k-x_{k-1}|.\]

Successive increments therefore form a geometrically bounded series. Their sum converges, and the limit lies in the closed interval and is a fixed point by continuity. Two distinct fixed points would contradict the same contraction inequality. At step \(k\ge1\), summing the remaining increments gives the a posteriori estimate

\[|x_k-x_*|\le\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q^j|x_k-x_{k-1}|=\frac{q}{1-q}|x_k-x_{k-1}|.\]

A small observed update is meaningful only in relation to the contraction factor. When \(q\) is close to one, many small updates may still remain.

Derivation and applications

Choose a relaxation parameter from verified slope bounds

A fixed-point iteration can oscillate if the derivative of its update is negative, or move slowly if it stays near one. Suppose \(g\) is differentiable on an interval with \(a\le g'(x)\le b<1\), where \(a\) may be less than \(-1\). Form a relaxed map \(G_\omega(x)=x+\omega(g(x)-x)\) with \(\omega>0\). Every fixed point of one map is a fixed point of the other. The derivative transforms affinely,

\[G_\omega'(x)=1-\omega[1-g'(x)],\]
\[1-g'(x)\in[1-b,1-a].\]

Both bounds inside the final interval are positive. The largest allowed magnitude of \(G_\omega'\) is attained at one of its endpoints. Equalizing the signed endpoint derivatives with opposite signs gives the optimal common parameter and bound,

\[1-\omega(1-b)=-[1-\omega(1-a)],\]
\[\omega_*=\frac{2}{2-a-b},\]
\[q_*=\frac{b-a}{2-a-b}.\]

The denominator exceeds the numerator because \(b<1\). A usable global contraction certificate also requires the relaxed map to preserve the chosen domain. If a closed interval is centred at \(c\) with radius \(R\), the slope bound gives a convenient sufficient condition:

\[|G_\omega(x)-c|\le q_*R+|G_\omega(c)-c|\le R .\]

This last condition can fail even when the derivative bound is excellent. In such a case the slope bounds outside the verified interval would be unknown.

Worked example

Stabilize a map with an oscillatory steep branch

Suppose \(g'\in[-3,0.5]\) on a verified domain. The unrelaxed iteration can amplify error by a factor of three. Choose \(\omega_*=2/4.5=4/9\), giving endpoint derivatives \(-7/9\) and \(7/9\). If the interval radius is 2 and the relaxed centre moves by \(0.20\), the domain check gives \(2(7/9)+0.20=1.76<2\). Both invariance and contraction are then certified.

Second application

Accelerate a map with uniformly positive slopes

In a second model \(g'\in[0.6,0.8]\). The optimal relaxed map uses \(\omega_*=2/0.6=10/3\), an extrapolation beyond the ordinary update. The slope bound falls to \(q_*=1/3\). Assuming the domain condition is independently verified, five steps reduce an initial location error of at most \(0.10\) to \(0.10/3^5=0.000412\). With the unrelaxed slope bound the corresponding guarantee is only \(0.0328\).

An equation can have several iteration maps

A fixed point satisfies \(x=g(x)\), but the rearrangement chosen for \(g\) is an algorithmic decision. A temperature balance might be written as a source update, a loss inversion or a relaxed combination of both. Every rearrangement can represent the same root and yet produce a different sequence from the same initial measurement. To judge the proposed sequence, examine how perturbations are propagated before investing in detailed model evaluations.

Let \(g\) map a closed interval \(I\) into itself, and suppose \(|g'(x)|\leq q<1\) there. Then \(|g(x)-g(y)|\leq q|x-y|\) by the mean value theorem. Starting from \(x_0\in I\), all iterates stay in \(I\). Successive changes shrink at least geometrically: \(|x_{k+1}-x_k|\leq q^k|x_1-x_0|\). Summing the remaining geometric tail shows that the sequence is Cauchy. Its limit belongs to the closed interval, and continuity gives \(r=g(r)\). Two distinct fixed points would violate the strict contraction inequality, establishing uniqueness.

Estimate what remains after an observed update

The tail estimate gives \(|r-x_k|\leq |x_{k+1}-x_k|/(1-q)\). If only the last completed increment is available, \(|r-x_k|\leq q|x_k-x_{k-1}|/(1-q)\). This is an a posteriori bound from the actual run and a proved Lipschitz constant. If \(q\) is very near one, small increments provide weak information: the remaining sum of many small changes can still be large. Invariance and contraction over a range should be distinguished from a local derivative estimate at one guessed solution.

For \(x^2+x-1=0\), take \(g(x)=1/(1+x)\) on \(I=[0.5,1]\). Its image \([0.5,2/3]\) lies within \(I\), and \(|g'|\leq4/9\). From \(x_0=1\), the sequence begins \(0.5,2/3,3/5,5/8\). The oscillation about the limit reflects the negative derivative. At \(x_4=0.625\), the last increment is 0.025, giving error at most \((4/9)/(5/9)\times0.025=0.020\). The exact root \((\sqrt5-1)/2\simeq0.61803\) confirms the guarantee is conservative.

Try a relaxed update

The alternative arrangement \(g_2(x)=1-x^2\) has slope \(-2r\simeq-1.236\) at the same positive root. Arbitrarily small generic perturbations grow under this unrelaxed iteration. The equivalent equation alone cannot make it reliable. Form a relaxed map \(g_\omega(x)=(1-\omega)x+\omega g_2(x)\). Its local multiplier is \(1-\omega+\omega g'_2(r)\). Choosing \(\omega=1/2\) changes that multiplier to approximately \(-0.118\).

In fact \(g_{1/2}'=0.5-x\) on \([0.5,1]\), so its magnitude is at most 0.5. The map sends the endpoints to 0.625 and 0.5, is decreasing on this interval, and remains inside it. This establishes global contraction on that range. Local cancellation could be made still stronger by tailoring \(\omega\) to an estimated derivative, but an inaccurate estimate can move the iteration outside the verified interval. A relaxation factor is a numerical parameter; changing it should alter convergence behaviour while leaving a converged physical state unchanged.

Transfer the same reasoning to two coupled states

For a vector iteration, a matrix norm bound replaces the scalar derivative bound. Consider \(g(x,y)=(0.2x+0.3y+1,\ 0.1x+0.4y+2)\). In the infinity norm the largest absolute row sum is 0.5. Subtraction of two evaluations therefore shows \(\|g(z)-g(w)\|_\infty\leq0.5\|z-w\|_\infty\), so the map contracts on all of \(\mathbb R^2\). Starting from zero the first two iterates are \((1,2)\) and \((1.8,2.9)\). The completed second increment has norm 0.9, bounding the remaining error by \(0.5(0.9)/(1-0.5)=0.9\).

Solving the two fixed-point equations gives \(x=8/3,\ y=34/9\), so the actual infinity-norm error after the second update is about 0.878. The bound is fairly sharp here. If the two state variables have different units, scale them before combining them into one maximum norm. The scale choice affects the usefulness of a contraction certificate while a converged balance still satisfies the same physical equations.

Check the invariant domain

A map can have derivative smaller than one on a chosen interval yet send its iterates outside it. For example \(g(x)=x/2+2\) has derivative one half on \([0,1]\) but maps zero to two. The usual contraction proof on that interval cannot even form its second iterate there. The same map on the full real line is a contraction with fixed point four. Domain and norm are part of a convergence certificate.

Eliminate a geometric error using three iterates

For a scalar iteration near a linearly attracting fixed point, successive errors often behave approximately as \(e_{k+1}=q e_k\). If the relation is exact with \(q\ne1\), three successive observations \(x_0,x_1,x_2\) determine both the geometric factor and the limiting state. The difference ratio \((x_2-x_1)/(x_1-x_0)\) equals \(q\). Summing the remaining geometric series, or eliminating \(q\) algebraically, gives the accelerated value \(x_0-(x_1-x_0)^2/(x_2-2x_1+x_0)\), provided the denominator is nonzero.

This expression is Aitken's delta-squared transformation. For a smooth nonlinear fixed-point map, the exact geometric relation is replaced by a first-order Taylor approximation about a simple fixed point. Taking \(x_1=g(x_0)\), \(x_2=g(x_1)\) and restarting from the accelerated value produces Steffensen iteration. Its local quadratic behaviour requires sufficient smoothness and \(g'(r)\ne1\). Indeed the denominator is asymptotically \((g'(r)-1)^2(x_0-r)\). If that coefficient is tiny or if function evaluations are inaccurate, two successive differences can suffer more cancellation than the fixed-point step itself.

Quantify both the gain and the sensitivity

A sensor calibration fixed point satisfies \(x=1+0.8x\). Starting at zero gives \(x_1=1\) and \(x_2=1.8\). The second difference is −0.2, so the accelerated value is \(0-1^2/(-0.2)=5\), exactly the fixed point. Ordinary iteration still has error \(5-1.8=3.2\) at this same two-evaluation budget. Here the gain is exact because the iteration contains a single geometric mode with the same factor at every distance from the root.

Perturb only the reported second iterate from 1.8 to 1.81. The inferred limit rises to \(1/0.19\approx5.263\). A perturbation of 0.01 has moved the extrapolated estimate by 0.263. Differentiating the transformed estimate with respect to \(x_2\), holding the first two values fixed, gives magnitude \((x_1-x_0)^2/(x_2-2x_1+x_0)^2=25\) at the unperturbed readings. The predicted change of 0.25 is close to the actual result. The apparent speedup has been purchased with sensitivity to slight deviations from a geometric trend.

A residual check distinguishes these two cases without knowing the exact solution. The exact extrapolated value satisfies \(g(5)-5=0\). At the perturbed estimate the residual is about −0.0526, much larger than machine precision. For a reliable contraction factor 0.8 this implies an absolute error bound about 0.263 by dividing by \(1-0.8\). In a vector problem different modes generally have different convergence factors. Applying this scalar extrapolation separately to raw components may be unreliable when those components mix modes or when a denominator crosses zero in just one coordinate.

There is a second failure mode when the original map is nearly the identity. For \(g(x)=0.999x+0.001\), starting from zero gives 0.001 and 0.001999. The second difference is only \(-10^{-6}\). If observations are stored to three decimal places, the two reported values become 0.001 and 0.002 and the computed denominator vanishes. This behaviour is predictable from the accuracy of the acquisition and does not indicate that the underlying iteration lacks a fixed point.

Choose a relaxation that contracts both modes

Near a root let the Jacobian be symmetric with eigenvalues 0.5 and 2, and use \(x^+=x-\omega F(x)\). Its two independent error modes have factors \(1-\omega\lambda_i\). Minimise the larger magnitude, allowing a sign reversal in one mode.

Problem sheet 1 · Isolating and iterating roots

Use a verified interval to certify one solution, recognise the effect of a transformation on convergence and quantify a stopping rule.

Find a root within a specified distance

Show that \(F(x)=x+\ln x-2\) has exactly one root in \([1,2]\). Carry out two bisections. Determine how many halvings guarantee an absolute midpoint error below 0.001, and give a slope-based error bound for the trial value \(x=1.56\).

Worked solution

At 1 the residual is −1, while \(F(2)=\ln2>0\). Its derivative \(1+1/x\) is at least 1.5 throughout, giving strict monotonicity. At 1.5 the residual is approximately −0.0945 and at 1.75 it is +0.3096, so after two halvings the bracket is \([1.5,1.75]\).

The initial width is one. After nine halvings the midpoint bound is \(1/1024<0.001\). At \(x=1.56\), \(F\simeq0.004686\). The mean value bound gives an error at most \(0.004686/1.5=0.00313\), assuming the exact logarithm on the stated bracket.

Compare two rearrangements

For \(x^2-3=0\) on \([1,2]\), consider \(g_1=3/x\) and \(g_2=(x+3/x)/2\). Starting at \(x_0=1.5\), compute two steps of each. Use the local derivatives to explain the different histories.

Worked solution

The first map produces 2, then 1.5, because applying the reciprocal transformation twice returns the start. At \(r=\sqrt3\) its derivative is \(-3/r^2=-1\). The second map first gives 1.75, then \((1.75+3/1.75)/2=1.7321429\).

At the root \(g_2'=(1-3/x^2)/2=0\), which cancels the linear error term. In particular, a fixed point for either formulation solves the original equation, but a repeated two-cycle of \(g_1\) need not solve it.

Interpret a small update

A contraction with known factor at most 0.95 has just changed its state by 0.002 K. Bound the current state error. How small must the next completed increment be to certify 0.01 K by the same test? Explain why stopping at a fixed increment of 0.01 K would be misleading.

Worked solution

Summing the future geometric changes from the current iterate gives a bound \(q|\Delta x|/(1-q)=19(0.002)=0.038\) K. To guarantee 0.01 K requires \(|\Delta x|\leq0.01/19\simeq0.000526\) K.

For a slowly contracting process small updates can continue in the same direction over many iterations. A rule using the increment alone ignores that accumulated tail.

Chapter 3 · Newton and secant updates

Derive derivative-based and derivative-free updates, measure their convergence near a simple zero and explain why a repeated zero changes the convergence order.

From first principles

Build one root correction from a local line

Suppose \(f\) is differentiable near a root and a trial point \(x_k\) has a nonzero derivative. Taylor expansion models the function a short displacement \(s\) away by \(f(x_k)+f'(x_k)s\). Requiring this local line to vanish gives Newton’s update,

\[s=-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)},\]
\[x_{k+1}=x_k+s .\]

The true new residual is the nonlinear remainder. For twice continuously differentiable \(f\) and a segment where \(|f''|\le M\), Taylor’s theorem yields

\[|f(x_{k+1})|\le \tfrac12 M|s|^2 .\]

Converting residual to a location error also requires a lower bound on \(|f'|\) near the root. If derivatives are unavailable, estimate the local slope by \([f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})]/[x_k-x_{k-1}]\) to obtain the secant method. Its denominator must be resolved numerically. Both methods need caution far from the root, where the line can extrapolate outside the region where it represents the function.

Derivation and applications

Recover the multiplicity of a root from derivative measurements

At a multiple zero, the ordinary Newton step no longer removes the leading location error. Suppose \(f(x)=(x-r)^m h(x)\), with integer \(m\ge1\), smooth \(h\) and \(h(r)\ne0\). Write \(e=x-r\). Differentiating the product and keeping the leading powers gives

\[f'=me^{m-1}h+e^mh',\]
\[\frac{f}{f'}=\frac{e}{m+e h'/h}=\frac{e}{m}+O(e^2).\]

It follows that ordinary Newton leaves the fraction \(1-1/m\) of the error to first order. For known \(m\), multiplying the Newton correction by \(m\) cancels the leading error. When multiplicity is unknown, consider the auxiliary function \(u=f/f'\), which has a simple zero after continuous extension to \(r\). Its derivative and Newton correction are

\[u'=1-\frac{ff''}{(f')^2},\]
\[x_{\rm new}=x-\frac{u}{u'}=x-\frac{ff'}{(f')^2-ff''}.\]

The leading powers in the twice differentiated product give a local diagnostic,

\[\frac{ff''}{(f')^2}\longrightarrow\frac{m-1}{m},\]
\[\frac{1}{1-ff''/(f')^2}\longrightarrow m .\]

Far from the zero the inferred value need not be integral or even positive. Near a flat root both derivatives and the function value can become very small, so the ratio needs sufficiently accurate evaluations. The derivation gives a local method and does not establish that a distant initial guess will enter its convergence region.

Worked example

Recover a triple zero from one set of values

For \(f(x)=(x-2)^3\) at \(x=2.2\), \(f=0.008\), \(f'=0.12\), \(f''=1.2\). The diagnostic gives \(1/[1-(0.008)(1.2)/0.12^2]=3\). The ordinary Newton step lands at \(2.13333\), while multiplying its correction by three gives 2 exactly because \(h\) is constant.

Second application

For a different function \(f(x)=x^2(1+x)\) at \(x=0.1\), the three values are \(0.011,0.23,2.6\). The apparent multiplicity is \(0.23^2/(0.23^2-0.011\times2.6)=2.18\). Newton on \(u\) gives \(x_{\rm new}=0.1-(0.011)(0.23)/(0.0243)=-0.00412\). Crossing the zero is consistent with the curvature of \(u\); the absolute location error nevertheless drops by a factor exceeding 24.

Linearise the residual where you stand

Suppose a scalar balance \(F(x)=0\) is smooth, with a simple root \(r\), meaning \(F'(r)\ne0\). Replacing the unknown function by its tangent at a current estimate \(x_k\) gives \(0\simeq F(x_k)+F'(x_k)(x_{k+1}-x_k)\). Hence \(x_{k+1}=x_k-F(x_k)/F'(x_k)\). The quotient has units of the unknown variable. When it is used in a coupled thermal model, for instance, a mismatch in watts divided by a derivative in W/K produces a temperature correction.

Assume both the current iterate and the root lie where \(|F''|\leq M\) and \(|F'|\geq m>0\). Taylor expand \(F(r)\) about \(x_k\) and insert the Newton update to obtain \(|x_{k+1}-r|\leq M|x_k-r|^2/(2m)\). For sufficiently small initial error the next iterate stays in the same neighbourhood and its error is of quadratic order. This is a local conclusion. A nearly horizontal tangent far away can give a huge update even when the desired root is simple.

Calculate the improvement explicitly

For \(F(x)=x^2-2\), Newton’s update is \((x+2/x)/2\). From 1.5 it gives \(17/12=1.4166667\), then \(577/408=1.4142157\). The absolute errors fall from approximately 0.08579 to 0.002453 and then to \(2.12\times10^{-6}\). An exact identity explains these figures: \(x_{k+1}-\sqrt2=(x_k-\sqrt2)^2/(2x_k)\). The positive sequence stays on the high side after the first update and approaches the physically selected positive root.

When analytic derivatives are expensive, replace \(F'(x_k)\) by the slope between \(x_k\) and \(x_{k-1}\). The resulting secant update uses two initial estimates and only one new residual evaluation per subsequent step. For the same quadratic, the divided difference is exactly \(x_k+x_{k-1}\); algebra gives \(e_{k+1}=e_ke_{k-1}/(x_k+x_{k-1})\), where \(e_j=x_j-\sqrt2\). Near the root the denominator is nearly constant. If \(e_{k+1}\) scales as \(e_k^p\), consistency with the product relation gives \(p^2=p+1\). The positive solution is about 1.618, superlinear but less than two.

Examine a root of higher multiplicity

For \(F(x)=(x-r)^m\), the tangent correction is only \((x-r)/m\). Newton therefore leaves \(e_{k+1}=(1-1/m)e_k\), a fixed linear reduction. With \(m=3\) and an initial error of 0.3, the first two errors are 0.2 and 0.13333. Meanwhile the residual is cubed, so even a small residual may mask appreciable positional error. Multiplying the Newton correction by \(m\) restores the exact root in one step for this pure power and restores quadratic convergence more generally when the multiplicity is known and the remaining factor is smooth and nonzero.

Numerically differentiating a residual introduces its own scale. For the forward difference \([F(x+s)-F(x)]/s\), Taylor truncation decreases as \(O(s)\), while rounding or evaluation noise in the numerator is divided by \(s\). Blindly shrinking \(s\) can therefore spoil the derivative. Recompute at a second scale, compare with an analytic derivative when available, and check the final state against the original balance. A derivative that is accurate near the starting point need not stay accurate along the entire sequence.

Set stopping tests that survive a change of units

Multiplying a residual by a nonzero constant \(c\) leaves a Newton correction unchanged because both the residual and its derivative acquire that factor. Secant updates have the same invariance. An absolute stopping threshold on the residual alone, however, changes its meaning by \(c\). For \(F=x^2-2\) the trial \(x=1.414\) has \(|F|\simeq0.000604\). Reporting the equivalent balance as \(1000F\) gives a mismatch about 0.604, with the identical prospective Newton step.

A useful report contains a scaled residual, the size of the accepted update in variable units and the conditions used to leave the iteration. Near a simple root a final undamped correction estimates the remaining positional error to first order. A small damped correction can equally be the consequence of a line search rejecting progress. Recheck the undamped balance or a bracket before treating a tiny change in the displayed state as convergence.

Prove a local quadratic bound with an explicit radius

Let \(r\) be a simple root of a twice differentiable scalar \(f\). On a surrounding interval suppose \(|f'|\ge m>0\) and \(|f''|\le M\). Taylor expansion about a trial point \(x\) gives \(0=f(r)=f(x)+f'(x)(r-x)+f''(\xi)(r-x)^2/2\) for an intermediate \(\xi\). Subtract the exact root from the Newton update \(x_+=x-f(x)/f'(x)\). The linear terms cancel and \(|x_+-r|\le [M/(2m)]|x-r|^2\). This supplies both the mechanism and a coefficient for the familiar quadratic statement.

The constants must continue to hold at every iterate. If the closed ball of radius \(R\) about the root lies in the stated interval and \(MR/(2m)<1\), any starting point in that ball maps strictly closer to the root and remains in the ball. Repeating the bound is then justified by induction. Without such an invariant region, a single Taylor estimate says nothing about what happens after a step leaves the region of controlled derivatives. A relative update tolerance cannot substitute for a verified lower derivative bound near a flat or multiple root.

Certify a square root and assess an inexact division

For \(f(x)=x^2-9\), use the interval \([2,4]\), root three, \(m=4\), \(M=2\). The resulting quadratic error coefficient is \(1/4\). From \(x_0=2.4\) the true initial error is 0.6 and the first error is bounded by 0.09. The actual Newton update is \((2.4+9/2.4)/2=3.075\), leaving error 0.075. The next bound using the actual first error is \(0.075^2/4=0.00140625\). Independently the exact update error obeys \((x-3)^2/(2x)\), giving \(0.075^2/(2\cdot3.075)\approx0.0009146\). The guaranteed number is larger because it allows the smallest derivative on the whole interval.

Now suppose the computed Newton update has an additional absolute rounding uncertainty of \(10^{-4}\) each time. The same reasoning gives \(|e_+|\le |e|^2/4+10^{-4}\). Once \(|e|\) falls below roughly 0.02, the allowed update uncertainty exceeds the quadratic contribution. Continuing iterations can still help, but the mathematical inequality no longer promises a doubling of correct digits. This transition is visible from the size of the actual input and evaluation errors and should not be fitted away as a mysterious change of convergence order.

A final distance certificate can avoid using the exact root in a real calculation. If a sign-changing bracket is known to contain the root and \(|f'|\ge4\) throughout it, then \(|x-r|\le|f(x)|/4\) for a point \(x\) inside the bracket. The local Newton proof and the residual-based certificate therefore serve complementary roles: one predicts improvement per iteration, while the other assesses the current uncertainty from computable information.

In the same square-root example a residual of 0.0008 with the iterate inside \([2,4]\) certifies error at most 0.0002, even without relying on observed convergence ratios. If one instead used the interval \([-4,4]\), the derivative lower bound would be zero and the same residual could not tell which of the two roots was nearby. Certifying a branch is part of certifying a distance.

Chapter 4 · Newton’s method for coupled balances

Assemble a Jacobian from two coupled residuals, solve for a simultaneous correction and interpret how scaling changes the meaning of a norm.

From first principles

Derive simultaneous corrections from all balance equations

Suppose two unknowns \(u,v\) must satisfy residual equations \(F_1(u,v)=0,\ F_2(u,v)=0\). Changing either unknown can affect both balances. Expand each residual around the same trial state \(x=(u,v)^T\), with a small proposed correction \(s\). The partial derivatives form the Jacobian \(J\),

\[F_i(x+s)=F_i(x)+\sum_{j=1}^2\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}(x)s_j+O(\|s\|^2).\]

If the derivatives are locally Lipschitz and the linear model is reliable, choose the correction that cancels both leading residuals together,

\[J(x)s=-F(x),\]
\[x_{\rm new}=x+s .\]

A nonsingular Jacobian guarantees a unique local correction. Solving the system directly avoids constructing its inverse. A final small residual can only establish accurate state variables if the balances are sufficiently sensitive to changes in those variables. Scaling each quantity by a physically meaningful reference helps distinguish genuine insensitivity from a misleading comparison between different units.

Derivation and applications

Eliminate a coupled balance without losing its derivative

Some models contain one equation that can be solved quickly for an auxiliary variable. Suppose \(F(u,v)=0,\ G(u,v)=0\) and, locally, \(G_v\ne0\). If the auxiliary equation gives \(v=g(u)\), the reduced residual is \(H(u)=F(u,g(u))\). Differentiating \(G(u,g(u))=0\) first accounts for the effect of a changed \(u\) on the auxiliary state,

\[g'=-G_u/G_v,\]
\[H'=F_u-F_vG_u/G_v .\]

This same combination arises even when the current auxiliary state is not perfectly equilibrated. The full two-row Newton system gives a formula for the auxiliary correction from the second row. Substituting it into the first row yields

\[\delta v=\frac{-G-G_u\delta u}{G_v},\]
\[\left(F_u-\frac{F_vG_u}{G_v}\right)\delta u=-F+\frac{F_vG}{G_v}.\]

At an equilibrated state \(G=0\), this is exactly Newton’s scalar step for \(H\). The second row’s imbalance contributes to the right-hand side otherwise. The determinant identity

\[\det J=F_uG_v-F_vG_u=G_vH'\]

shows why an apparently harmless derivative \(F_u\) can hide a nearly singular coupled model. After a finite step, directly recomputing \(g(u+\delta u)\) can differ from \(v+\delta v\) by a second-order term when \(g\) is curved. Both choices share the same first-order model.

Worked example

Calculate a simultaneous correction from an imbalanced state

Take \(F=u+2v-5,\ G=v-u^2\), initially \((u,v)=(1,0.8)\). Then \(F=-2.4,\ G=-0.2,\ G_u=-2,\ G_v=1\). The reduced derivative is 5 and the corrected right side is \(2.4-0.4=2.0\). Thus \(\delta u=0.4,\ \delta v=1.0\). The new state \((1.4,1.8)\) satisfies the linear \(F\) balance exactly; the remaining \(G=-0.16\) is the neglected quadratic term.

Second application

Identify a cancellation in a reduced slope

In a second model \(F=2u-v-1,\ G=v-1.99u\). The individual slope \(F_u=2\), but eliminating \(v\) leaves \(H(u)=0.01u-1\). Starting at zero the correct Newton correction is 100, giving \(v=199\), an exact solution of both linear balances. Using the partial slope of 2 while holding \(v\) fixed would instead propose \(u=0.5\) and miss the compensating response.

Linearise the whole model at one state

Two unknown physical quantities may influence both measured balances. Changing one to erase one residual can disrupt the other. The vector Taylor expansion \(F(x+s)=F(x)+J(x)s+O(\|s\|^2)\) collects the simultaneous first-order response. Entry \(J_{ij}\) is the derivative of residual \(i\) with respect to unknown \(j\) while holding the other unknowns fixed. A Newton step solves the linear system \(J(x)s=-F(x)\) and sets \(x_{new}=x+s\). Factor or iteratively solve that system rather than explicitly computing an inverse.

Suppose \(J\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L\) near a root, and the inverses in that neighbourhood satisfy \(\|J^{-1}\|\leq K\). Integrate \(J\) along the line from the current point to the root. The error left by replacing \(J\) with its value at the start is bounded by \(L\|x-r\|^2/2\). Multiplying by the inverse yields the local Newton bound \(KL\|x-r\|^2/2\). This derivation makes explicit which objects must stay well behaved: derivative variation and amplification by the linear solve.

Intersect an energy constraint with a matching condition

Take the dimensionless equations \(F_1=x^2+y^2-1,\ F_2=x-y\), and choose the positive branch. The linearised system at \((0.8,0.6)\) has \(F=(0,0.2)\) and rows \((1.6,1.2),(1,-1)\). The point already lies on the unit circle but violates matching. Newton requires \(s_y=s_x+0.2\); substituting in the first row gives \(2.8s_x=-0.24\). Thus \(s_x=-0.0857143,\ s_y=0.114286\) and the new state is \((5/7,5/7)\).

The linear constraint is now exact. The circle residual is \(2(5/7)^2-1=1/49\), a second-order consequence of moving along its tangent rather than along the circle itself. At the next iteration the two corrections are equal. Solving \((20/7)s=-1/49\) gives \(s=-1/140\), hence \(x=y=99/140=0.707143\). The desired value is \(1/\sqrt2=0.707107\). A different initial state could lead to the negative intersection. Physical admissibility must therefore supplement the algebraic equations.

Quantify the cost of freezing a Jacobian

A new Jacobian factorisation at every iterate can dominate the work of solving a coupled equilibrium. Fix \(B=J(x_0)\), factor it once, and take \(x_{k+1}=x_k-B^{-1}F(x_k)\). Near a simple root \(r\) write \(e=x-r\). Taylor expansion gives \(e_+=(I-B^{-1}J(r))e+O(\|e\|^2)\). With a current exact Jacobian the first-order term vanishes. A frozen Jacobian normally retains it, so convergence is generally linear and even local convergence requires control of this iteration matrix. A sufficient condition on a neighbourhood is \(\sup_x\|I-B^{-1}J(x)\|<1\), together with an invariant neighbourhood.

Consider \(F(x,y)=(x^2+y-3,x-y)^T\), starting at \((1,1)\). The fixed matrix has rows \((2,1)\) and \((1,-1)\). The second residual remains zero if both coordinates change equally. On that line the correction satisfies \(3s=-(x^2+x-3)\). The first increment is \(1/3\). The new first residual is \(1/9\). Reusing the factors gives the next increment \(-1/27\) and the state \(x=y=35/27\approx1.29630\). Refactoring at \(4/3\) would instead give increment \(-1/33\), reaching \(43/33\approx1.30303\). The exact positive root is \((\sqrt{13}-1)/2\approx1.30278\).

The local error multiplier along this line is \(1-\sqrt{13}/3\approx-0.202\), predicting alternating errors for the frozen calculation. Reusing a poor factorisation indefinitely can cost more residual evaluations than it saves. Compare actual reduction against the expected local factor and refresh the Jacobian when model changes make reuse ineffective.

Select scales and verify a derivative action

Changing units from metres to millimetres multiplies the displayed displacement by 1000 without changing the model. Yet an unscaled Euclidean norm of a state mixing displacement and temperature can then change radically. Choose characteristic scales in a positive diagonal matrix \(D_x\) and residual scales \(D_F\). Form the dimensionless unknown \(z=D_x^{-1}x\) and residual \(G=D_F^{-1}F(D_xz)\). By the chain rule \(J_G=D_F^{-1}J_FD_x\). For an exact undamped Newton solve this produces the same physical correction, but stopping tests, trust regions and finite differences now acquire interpretable scales.

To check a programmed Jacobian, choose a direction \(d\) with nonzero entries in multiple unknowns and compare \(Jd\) with \([F(x+sd)-F(x)]/s\) over several \(s\). For the circle constraint the discrepancy is exactly \(s(d_x^2+d_y^2)\), while for the linear matching condition it is zero in exact arithmetic. A one-direction check can miss a coding error orthogonal to that direction. Try additional directions emphasizing different couplings, keeping clear of nonsmooth switches. This test inspects the local derivative independently of the solver’s final residual.

Separate state sensitivity from linearisation error

Suppose the matching constraint in the intersection example is perturbed to \(x-y=\epsilon\), while the unit-circle measurement remains exact. About the positive root \(x=y=1/\sqrt2\), differentiating the circle implies \(\delta x+\delta y=0\). Differentiating the matching constraint gives \(\delta x-\delta y=\epsilon\). Thus a measurement offset of 0.02 moves the coordinates by approximately \(+0.01\) and \(-0.01\), even if the perturbed nonlinear equations are solved to roundoff.

Geometrically, uncertainty becomes much larger when the two constraint curves meet almost tangentially. Their Jacobian rows then point in nearly the same direction, leaving a poorly resolved transverse displacement. Reducing the Newton tolerance improves the algebraic solution of the supplied measurements, but cannot increase their independence. Inspecting singular directions of the scaled Jacobian near the converged state helps distinguish this physical uncertainty from difficulties caused by a poor initial guess.

Analyse the basins of a coupled two-dimensional problem

Identify a real state \((x,y)\) with \(z=x+iy\). Solving \(z^2-1=0\) is equivalent to solving \(x^2-y^2-1=0,\ 2xy=0\) for two real unknowns. The real Jacobian has rows \((2x,-2y)\) and \((2y,2x)\), with determinant \(4(x^2+y^2)\). It is nonsingular away from the origin. Complex division by \(2z\) implements exactly the real Newton correction for this system. Therefore the full update is \(N(z)=(z+1/z)/2\), and both roots \(+1\) and \(-1\) have local quadratic convergence.

To determine which root is selected, set \(w=(z-1)/(z+1)\), excluding the already solved point \(-1\). Direct substitution shows \(N(z)-1=(z-1)^2/(2z)\) and \(N(z)+1=(z+1)^2/(2z)\). Dividing yields \(w_{\rm new}=w^2\). Hence \(w_k=w_0^{2^k}\) as long as the updates remain defined. If \(|w_0|<1\), this tends to zero and \(z=(1+w)/(1-w)\) tends to one. The condition means the starting point is closer to \(+1\) than to \(-1\), precisely the open right half-plane. The left half-plane similarly converges to the other root.

Predict a basin and trace a boundary failure

Start at \(z_0=1+i\), so \(1/z_0=(1-i)/2\). The first update is \(z_1=0.75+0.25i\). Its inverse equals \(1.2-0.4i\), giving \(z_2=0.975-0.075i\). The iterates moved from above to below the real axis, but both remained in the basin of \(+1\). In transformed coordinates \(w_0=i/(2+i)=(1+2i)/5\), of magnitude \(1/\sqrt5\); after two steps the magnitude is \(1/25\). This quantifies progress without estimating the distance from \(z_1\) to either root by visual inspection.

The basin boundary is completely different. For \(z_0=i\), the first update is zero and the next Jacobian is singular. More generally a purely imaginary nonzero start remains purely imaginary for as long as the map is defined, because both \(z\) and \(1/z\) have zero real part. It cannot converge to either real root while staying on this line. Infinitesimal perturbations toward opposite sides of the line select different roots. A solver reporting a converged root from a point numerically very close to this boundary should identify which initial or rounding perturbations could change that result.

This example makes global multiplicity observable in a system whose local steps are cheap and whose Jacobian is invertible almost everywhere. A small final residual confirms that the reported state solves the equations. It says little about whether the originally intended physical branch was selected. Additional physical conditions or a continuation path from a known state can supply information that the equations and a nominal starting vector alone do not specify.

Initial guesses only \(10^{-6}\) to the right and left of the imaginary axis can therefore give final real parts differing by two. That discontinuity concerns the map from starting guess to selected solution, not a discontinuity of the original polynomial equations. It is one reason to report how physical branch selection was imposed when several roots correspond to substantially different states, even when every final residual is small.

Chapter 5 · Line searches and trust regions

Diagnose a cycling Newton sequence, compute a merit-function decrease and explain how damping and trust regions change the step while preserving the root.

From first principles

Choose a step using actual reduction in residual

A local Newton model can predict a zero far outside its useful region. To test the correction, use the merit function \(\phi(x)=\|F(x)\|_2^2/2\). Along a candidate direction \(s\), differentiate this scalar at step length zero. For a Newton direction solving \(Js=-F\), the predicted slope is strictly negative whenever \(F\ne0\),

\[\frac{d}{dt}\phi(x+ts)\bigg|_{t=0}=F^TJs=-\|F\|_2^2 .\]

Choose a fraction \(0<c<1\) of that initial decrease and accept a trial length \(\alpha>0\) when the actual residual satisfies

\[\phi(x+\alpha s)\le\phi(x)+c\alpha\,\nabla\phi(x)^Ts .\]

Try full length first and shorten if necessary. For a differentiable merit function and a descent direction, sufficiently short feasible steps pass because the linear term dominates the remainder. This establishes a local acceptance mechanism. Bounds on variables, nondecreasing directions, or stationary points of \(\phi\) with nonzero residual require additional interpretation.

Derivation and applications

Bound the number of reductions in a backtracking line search

A sufficient decrease test can be guaranteed once the step is short enough. Assume a differentiable merit \(\phi\), a nonzero direction \(p\) with \(g^Tp<0\) for \(g=\nabla\phi(x)\), and an \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient on the whole feasible trial segment. The change in merit equals an integral of directional derivative. Replacing the gradient at a displaced point by its initial value introduces a bounded error,

\[\phi(x+\alpha p)-\phi(x)=\int_0^\alpha \nabla\phi(x+tp)^Tp\,dt\le\alpha g^Tp+\tfrac12L\alpha^2\|p\|^2 .\]

To make the right-hand side no larger than the Armijo requirement \(c\alpha g^Tp\), rearrange the inequality using \(g^Tp<0\) and \(\alpha>0\). This yields a guaranteed acceptance range

\[0<\alpha\le\alpha_{\rm safe}=\frac{2(1-c)(-g^Tp)}{L\|p\|^2}.\]

Starting at \(\alpha_0\) and reducing by a fixed factor \(0<\rho<1\), the number of reductions before reaching this range is at most

\[j_{\max}=\max\left(0,\left\lceil\frac{\ln(\alpha_{\rm safe}/\alpha_0)}{\ln\rho}\right\rceil\right).\]

Larger steps can already pass because the curvature bound is only sufficient. This estimate makes the roles of a nearly tangential descent direction and a poor scale for the search direction explicit. It assumes trial points lie inside the domain on which the function and its gradient bound are valid.

Worked example

Predict a line search on an exact quadratic

Use \(\phi(x)=5x^2\), start at \(x=1\), and take \(p=-10\). Here \(L=10,\ g^Tp=-100,\ \|p\|^2=100\). With \(c=0.1\) the safe length is \(0.18\). Starting from 1 and halving reaches \(0.125\) after three reductions. The merit becomes \(5(-0.25)^2=0.3125\), below the required \(5-1.25=3.75\). The previous length \(0.25\) gives merit \(11.25\) and fails.

Second application

Check an acceptance claim under curvature uncertainty

Another model has \(-g^Tp=0.8,\ \|p\|=2,\ L=3,\ c=0.25\). The sufficient length is \(2(0.75)(0.8)/(3\times4)=0.100\). An initial \(0.80\) reduced by a factor \(0.4\) produces \(0.32,\ 0.128,\ 0.0512\); the third reduction is guaranteed to pass. Accepting \(0.128\) on the actual merit would also be consistent with the bound.

A locally excellent algorithm can cycle

Consider \(F(x)=x^3-2x+2\). Starting from zero gives \(F(0)=2,F'(0)=-2\), so Newton proposes \(x_1=1\). At one, \(F(1)=1,F'(1)=1\), and the next iterate returns to zero. The sequence will repeat indefinitely in exact arithmetic. A real root does exist between \(-2\) and \(-1\), where the function changes sign. The trouble lies in the update chosen from this starting point. It does not refute the local convergence result near a simple root.

For coupled equations define the merit function \(\phi(x)=\|F(x)\|_2^2/2\). Its directional derivative along an exact Newton step \(s\) is \(\nabla\phi^Ts=F^TJs=-\|F\|^2\), strictly negative when \(F\ne0\). Thus a sufficiently short step in that direction decreases the squared mismatch. This identity assumes \(Js=-F\) is solved accurately. With a strongly truncated inner iteration, check the actual directional derivative before treating the step as a descent direction.

Compute an accepted step

A backtracking line search starts at \(\alpha=1\) and reduces it until \(\phi(x+\alpha s)\leq\phi(x)+c\alpha\nabla\phi^Ts\) with \(0<c<1\). This sufficient-decrease condition compares actual progress with the local prediction. In the cycling example at \(x=1\) the Newton direction is \(s=-1\), the initial merit is 0.5 and its slope along \(s\) is \(-1\). With \(c=10^{-4}\), the full trial returns to zero and gives \(\phi=2\), which fails. A half-step gives \(F(0.5)=1.125\) and fails too. A quarter-step gives \(F(0.75)=0.921875\), so \(\phi\simeq0.425\), satisfying the condition.

A smaller mismatch at this point does not certify a nearby root. This example has a positive local minimum of \(\phi\) near \(x=\sqrt{2/3}\), where \(F'\) vanishes but \(F\) stays nonzero. From a poor starting point, globalisation may lead near such a stationary point. Check the residual itself, use a bracket when available and try physically motivated alternative starts. A long sequence of tiny accepted steps should prompt inspection of local derivatives or constraints rather than being counted as successful convergence.

Compare a regional model with the real residual

A trust-region method minimises the local model \(m(s)=\|F+Js\|^2/2\) subject to \(\|s\|\leq\Delta\). Its radius states how far the current linearisation is trusted in the chosen variable scales. After a trial, compute the ratio \(\rho=[\phi(x)-\phi(x+s)]/[m(0)-m(s)]\) when predicted reduction is positive. A negative ratio means the true merit increased. A ratio near one indicates accurate local prediction. One can shrink the radius after a poor trial or enlarge it when the model predicts progress well near the boundary.

In the quarter-step above the linear model predicts residual \(1-0.25=0.75\) and merit \(0.28125\), a reduction of 0.21875. The actual reduction is about 0.075, giving \(\rho\approx0.34\). The trial moved in the intended direction but the predicted gain was optimistic. Using such comparisons is especially valuable when model coefficients change sharply with state. On a simple monotone thermal stage, damped and full Newton can converge to the same root. The resulting residual and a comparison at identical time step test this expectation. Compare two nonlinear cooling solves.

Include physical bounds in a step decision

A variable representing a concentration cannot be negative. If a positive current value \(c\) receives a negative proposed correction \(s\), then \(c+\alpha s\geq0\) imposes \(\alpha\leq c/(-s)\). For a concentration of 0.04 and a direction of −0.10 in matching units, only factors up to 0.4 keep this component admissible. Starting a merit-function search at 0.36 leaves a small distance from the boundary. Across many components use the most restrictive bound before evaluating an expensive residual.

Admissibility alone cannot guarantee progress: a physically acceptable state may have a larger balance mismatch than the old one. Conversely, clipping a negative candidate to zero changes the search direction and can alter conservation. A genuinely constrained equilibrium may require a complementarity or inequality formulation rather than repeated clipping. Record which condition limited each step when diagnosing persistent stagnation near a boundary.

Derive the step along a trust-region boundary

For a smooth squared-residual objective, a local quadratic model has change \(m(s)=g^Ts+s^TBs/2\), often with \(B=J^TJ\). A trust region limits this model to \(\|s\|_2\le\Delta\) in scaled coordinates. Suppose first that \(B\) is symmetric positive definite. If the unconstrained minimiser \(-B^{-1}g\) lies inside the ball it solves the constrained problem. Otherwise the minimiser lies on the sphere. The stationarity condition there is \((B+\lambda I)s=-g\), with \(\lambda>0\) chosen so the length equals \(\Delta\).

Diagonalising \(B=Q\operatorname{diag}(\beta_i)Q^T\) turns the length condition into \(\sum_i (q_i^Tg)^2/(\beta_i+\lambda)^2=\Delta^2\). For a nonzero gradient this left-hand side decreases continuously to zero with \(\lambda\), starting at the squared unconstrained-step length. Thus the boundary multiplier exists uniquely. A positive shift damps different curvature directions by different factors. The step is generally not a scalar fraction of the full unconstrained step. If \(B\) has negative eigenvalues, positive semidefiniteness of \(B+\lambda I\) and possible singular boundary cases require additional treatment.

Measure what a restricted model predicted

Let \(B=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\) and \(g=(-2,-5)^T\). The full minimiser is \((2,1.25)^T\), of norm about 2.36. Set the permitted radius to \(\sqrt2\). At \(\lambda=1\) the shifted equations give \(s=(2/2,5/5)^T=(1,1)^T\), with exactly the required length. The low-curvature component has been reduced to one half of its unconstrained value while the second retains four fifths. That orientation change follows from the different denominators; shortening the original step to the same norm would predict a different model decrease.

The linear term at the accepted candidate is \(-7\). The quadratic term is \((1+4)/2=2.5\), so the predicted decrease is \(7-2.5=4.5\). Suppose the actual objective is this local model plus \(0.6s_1^4\), up to an irrelevant constant. The actual decrease at \((1,1)\) is 3.9 and the ratio of actual to predicted decrease is \(3.9/4.5\approx0.867\). The extra quartic term does not alter the gradient or Hessian at the base point, yet it changes the observed progress over a finite step.

If that coefficient were 6 instead, the actual change would be an increase of 1.5 while the local quadratic still predicts a decrease of 4.5. Shrinking the radius diminishes the neglected term more rapidly than the leading linear improvement. Comparing achieved and predicted reductions supplies information about model accuracy that neither the multiplier nor the trust radius alone provides.

The choice of norm defining the ball also influences the calculation. If one coordinate is measured in millimetres and the other in metres, changing only the unit of the first coordinate alters an unscaled Euclidean ball. Instead specify physically meaningful scales and constrain the length of the dimensionless step. Transform the gradient and curvature consistently with that scaling; otherwise the shifted equations describe a different model than the one used to evaluate the actual decrease.

An equivalent specification is a weighted ellipsoid in the physical variables, with semiaxes equal to admissible changes in each parameter.

Chapter 6 · Continuation and folds

Track a family of equilibria through parameter changes, identify the failure of ordinary parameter continuation at a fold and build a predictor–corrector step.

From first principles

Use the tangent of a solution curve to cross a fold

A parameter-dependent equation \(F(x,\lambda)=0\) defines a curve of equilibria when the gradient in \((x,\lambda)\) is nonzero. Differentiating along any smooth parametrization by \(s\) shows that its tangent \((\dot x,\dot\lambda)\) must be perpendicular to the gradient,

\[F_x\dot x+F_\lambda\dot\lambda=0,\]
\[\dot x^2+\dot\lambda^2=1 .\]

The second equation fixes the length in chosen, consistently scaled coordinates. Choose the sign to match the preceding direction and predict a new state \(z_p=z_0+h t_0\). The predictor generally misses the curved solution set. To correct it, solve the original equation together with a plane condition fixing its position along the tangent,

\[F(z)=0,\]
\[t_0^T(z-z_p)=0 .\]

Near a regular curve these two rows are independent. At a simple fold the parameter component of the tangent vanishes, so using \(\lambda\) alone to parameterize fails locally. The augmented system can remain nonsingular because the state component is still free to progress along the curve.

Derivation and applications

Predict the size and direction of a continuation correction

A tangent predictor from a known solution is first-order accurate along a smooth branch. Its leading transverse miss can be calculated before running a Newton corrector. Take a scalar equation \(F(z)=0\) for \(z=(x,\lambda)\) in consistently scaled coordinates. At the starting point \(z_0\) assume a nonzero gradient \(g\), a unit tangent \(t\) with \(g^Tt=0\), and sufficiently many bounded derivatives. Write \(H\) for its Hessian. Taylor expansion along the predictor \(z_p=z_0+ht\) yields

\[F(z_p)=\frac{h^2}{2}t^THt+O(h^3).\]

The usual corrector plane is perpendicular to \(t\). In two dimensions the permitted correction therefore lies along the initial unit normal \(n=g/\|g\|\). Try \(z=z_p+\delta n\). Since \(\delta=O(h^2)\), the derivative at the initial point gives the leading normal correction,

\[0=\tfrac12h^2t^THt+\delta\|g\|+O(h^3),\]
\[\delta=-\frac{h^2t^THt}{2\|g\|}+O(h^3).\]

Thus a local curvature estimate \(K=|t^THt|/\|g\|\) suggests a predictor length for a desired transverse error \(\varepsilon\),

\[\frac12Kh^2\lesssim\varepsilon,\]
\[h\lesssim\sqrt{2\varepsilon/K}.\]

The absolute curvature is invariant when \(F\) is multiplied by a fixed nonzero scalar. The estimate depends on the coordinate metric and becomes unreliable if the gradient is nearly zero or higher derivatives vary substantially over the step. A measured corrector displacement tests the prediction.

Worked example

Predict the normal correction on a circle

On \(F=x^2+\lambda^2-4=0\), start at \((2,0)\) with tangent \((0,1)\) and choose \(h=0.20\). Then \(t^THt=2,\ \|g\|=4\), and the predicted normal displacement is \(-0.0100\). At fixed \(\lambda=0.20\), the exact correction is \(\sqrt{4-0.20^2}-2=-0.01003\). Its sign points inward, restoring the radius.

Second application

Choose a step near a parabolic turning point

For \(F=\lambda-3x^2\) at the origin, take \(t=(1,0)\), \(n=(0,1)\). Here \(t^THt=-6,\ \|g\|=1\), so the exact corrector at fixed \(x=h\) is \(+3h^2\) in the parameter. To keep it below \(0.0012\), choose \(h\le0.020\). A proposed \(h=0.05\) would require correction \(0.0075\), over six times the target.

A solution curve contains more than one isolated solve

A physical model often depends on a parameter \(\lambda\), such as heat input, end displacement or applied load. Solving \(F(x,\lambda)=0\) independently at each parameter value discards information about how neighbouring states are related. On a smooth branch where \(F_x\) is invertible, differentiation gives \(F_x\,dx/d\lambda=-F_\lambda\). This linear solve predicts how the state moves under a small parameter change. Re-evaluating the full residual at the prediction and correcting it by Newton accounts for the curvature omitted by the tangent.

For a scalar illustration take \(F=x^2+\lambda-1\). The admissible solution set is a parabola in the \((x,\lambda)\) plane, with branches \(x=\pm\sqrt{1-\lambda}\) for \(\lambda<1\). Its derivative \(dx/d\lambda=-1/(2x)\) grows without bound as \(x\) approaches zero. Starting at \(x=0.5,\lambda=0.75\), a change \(\Delta\lambda=0.1\) predicts \(x=0.4\). The positive exact value is \(\sqrt{0.15}=0.38730\). The predictor is close enough for a local correction here. Increasing the load beyond one would ask for a real equilibrium that this model cannot supply.

Change the parameter used to follow the curve

The curve itself is smooth at \((0,1)\) although \(\lambda\) is a poor local coordinate there. Introduce an arclength-like parameter and a normalised tangent \(t=(t_x,t_\lambda)\) satisfying \(F_x t_x+F_\lambda t_\lambda=0\). At a known point \(z_k=(x_k,\lambda_k)\) predict \(z_p=z_k+\Delta s\,t\). Correct by solving the physical equation together with a hyperplane constraint \(t^T(z-z_p)=0\). This pseudo-arclength condition selects a nearby point on the branch while allowing the load parameter itself to reverse direction.

At the fold of the example \(F_x=0,\ F_\lambda=1\), and the tangent can be \(t=(-1,0)\). The augmented Jacobian has rows \((0,1)\) and \((-1,0)\), with determinant one. A Newton correction in the two unknowns is well posed even though the original scalar Jacobian for fixed \(\lambda\) was singular. The construction depends on a simple fold and the correct tangent. A more complicated branch intersection can require additional information to choose which path to follow.

Take one step past a turning point

Start exactly at the fold, choose the negative-\(x\) tangent and take \(\Delta s=0.1\). The predictor gives \((-0.1,1)\). Its physical residual is \(0.01\). The transverse plane now fixes \(x=-0.1\). Correcting the physical balance therefore gives \(\lambda=1-0.1^2=0.99\). The parameter has decreased while the curve coordinate has advanced. This simple calculation illustrates why choosing fixed increments of \(\lambda\) alone cannot traverse both sides of the same load maximum.

A solver can report an accurate branch even if a physical system would not settle on that branch. To test stability one needs a dynamical law. For instance, if \(\dot x=1-\lambda-x^2=-F\), then perturbing an equilibrium gives \(\dot e=-2x_\star e\). The positive branch is locally attracting and the negative branch repelling. The algebraic root residual vanishes on both. For a different specified dynamics the interpretation must be rederived. Keep separate records of equilibrium balance, tangent orientation, correction size and dynamical stability when interpreting a continuation diagram.

Check the prediction error against the step length

For the example, predict away from \((0.5,0.75)\) with the slope \(dx/d\lambda=-1\). A change of only 0.05 predicts \(x=0.45\), while the exact positive branch gives \(\sqrt{0.20}=0.447214\), an error about 0.00279. The larger change 0.10 gave error about 0.01270. Near this starting point halving the parameter step reduces the tangent prediction error by roughly a factor of four, consistent with the second-order remainder of a first-order predictor.

Approaching the fold makes that comparison deteriorate because the second derivative with respect to the load becomes unbounded. Monitoring the ratio of correction length to predictor length provides one way to shorten steps before Newton loses the branch. At a successful step, orient the new tangent to have positive dot product with its predecessor in the scaled coordinates. Without that convention, an arbitrary sign change in a computed tangent could send the next predictor back along the path already followed.

Distinguish a fold from a genuine branching point

The scalar equilibrium equation \(F(x,\mu)=x^3-\mu x=0\) describes a symmetry-breaking pitchfork. There is a trivial branch \(x=0\) for every \(\mu\) and two additional real branches \(x=\pm\sqrt\mu\) when \(\mu>0\). Ordinary parameter continuation along the trivial branch encounters no difficulty evaluating the solution on either side of zero. Yet the collection of real equilibria changes there. An algorithm that accurately follows one branch can therefore entirely miss the existence of the other two.

A fold often has \(F_x=0\) while \(F_\mu\ne0\), so the full derivative with respect to state and parameter retains rank one. At the pitchfork origin both derivatives vanish: \(F_x=3x^2-\mu=0,\ F_\mu=-x=0\). Adding one arclength condition to the original scalar equation cannot restore full rank there because the equilibrium row itself is zero. Arclength continuation avoids a poor choice of independent coordinate at a regular fold; it does not automatically select a tangent at an actual branch crossing. Higher-order terms or an independent symmetry condition carry the missing information.

Follow the stable state through a loss of stability

To distinguish algebraic solutions from dynamically attracting equilibria, embed the example in \(x'=\mu x-x^3=-F(x,\mu)\). Linearising near an equilibrium gives perturbation growth rate \(\mu-3x^2\). At \(x=0,\mu=-0.04\), the rate is \(-0.04\) and small perturbations decay. At \(x=0,\mu=+0.04\), it becomes \(+0.04\) and perturbations grow. The additional equilibria are \(x=\pm0.2\), both with linear rate \(0.04-3(0.04)=-0.08\). A static Newton solve initialised at zero on the positive side returns zero residual immediately, even though that solution is now dynamically unstable.

To switch branches deliberately, use the state amplitude as the small parameter. Cancelling the known factor \(x\) on a nontrivial branch gives \(\mu=x^2\). At \(x=0.1\) the predicted parameter is 0.01; at \(x=-0.1\) the same parameter gives the mirror branch. This cancellation is valid only because the branch sought has nonzero amplitude. Dividing the original equilibrium equation by \(x\) everywhere would have silently deleted the entire trivial branch.

The potential \(V=x^4/4-\mu x^2/2\) provides an independent physical check. At \(\mu=0.04\), the trivial state has energy zero, whereas either nontrivial state has energy \((0.2)^4/4-0.04(0.2)^2/2=-0.0004\). Its lower energy and positive second derivative agree with the negative linear growth rate. Near a bifurcation, record branch existence, stability and which branch a continuation procedure actually followed as three separately testable facts.

If small external imperfections break the symmetry, replacing the equation by \(x^3-\mu x-\epsilon=0\) eliminates the exact \(x=0\) branch whenever \(\epsilon\ne0\). At \(\mu=0\) the remaining equilibrium then has amplitude \(\epsilon^{1/3}\). A forcing imperfection of \(10^{-6}\) produces displacement 0.01, much larger than a linear response of the same size. This calculation identifies why branch diagrams near an ideal symmetry-breaking point are sensitive to tiny unmodelled loads.

A careful numerical continuation can map the resulting changes in the number of equilibria before interpreting any single sweep from one initial condition.

Locate the fold created by an imperfect pitchfork

An imperfect pitchfork can be analysed by solving the equilibrium and fold conditions together. With \(F=x^3-\mu x-\epsilon\), a fold requires \(F_x=3x^2-\mu=0\), hence \(\mu=3x^2\). Substitution into \(F=0\) gives \(-2x^3-\epsilon=0\), therefore \(x_f=-(\epsilon/2)^{1/3}\) and \(\mu_f=3(\epsilon/2)^{2/3}\) for positive imperfection. This is a nonanalytic dependence on the forcing perturbation. Halving the perturbation multiplies the fold displacement by \(2^{-1/3}\), rather than by one half as a linear sensitivity would predict.

For \(\epsilon=0.002\), the fold occurs at \(x_f=-0.1,\mu_f=0.03\). Indeed \(x^3-0.03x-0.002=(x+0.1)^2(x-0.2)\), showing the merging pair and the separate simple positive equilibrium. At this point \(F_\mu=0.1\ne0\) and \(F_{xx}=-0.6\ne0\), restoring the usual regular-fold conditions away from the perfect pitchfork. At \(\mu=0\) there is only the positive cube root of 0.002. Above 0.03 the negative pair splits into two simple real roots, so the number of equilibria becomes three. This count follows also by inspecting the local maximum at negative \(x\) and local minimum at positive \(x\). An observed delayed change in the number of equilibrium branches can therefore measure an imperfection that a symmetric idealisation omits.

Reject an inadmissible tangent

Solve \(\ln x=0\) on \([0.5,4]\), starting Newton’s method at 4. Compute the full trial, then two successively halved trials. Which is the first to reduce \(\ln^2 x/2\)? How could the bracket protect a failed update?

Worked solution

The direction is \(-4\ln4\simeq-5.5452\), so the full trial is negative and the logarithm undefined. At half length the trial is 1.2274, with merit about 0.021 versus 0.961 initially, already a decrease. A quarter-step gives 2.6137, also decreasing merit but less strongly.

Reject an inadmissible trial before evaluating its logarithm. A fallback midpoint of 2.25 is admissible and cuts the sign-changing interval to \([0.5,2.25]\); the interval maintains a root guarantee independent of tangent quality.

Choose the continuation coordinate

For \(F(x,\lambda)=x^2+\lambda-4\), find the two solutions at \(\lambda=3\), their slopes \(dx/d\lambda\), and the turning point. From that point take a pseudo-arclength predictor of length 0.2 in the positive-\(x\) direction and correct to the curve.

Worked solution

At \(\lambda=3\) the roots are \(\pm1\). Their slopes are \(-1/2\) at +1 and \(+1/2\) at −1. The branches meet at \((0,4)\), where these slopes cease to be finite.

The tangent there is \((1,0)\), so the predicted point is \((0.2,4)\). The hyperplane constraint fixes \(x=0.2\), and the physical residual gives \(\lambda=3.96\). Its reduction in \(\lambda\) is compatible with forward arclength progress.

Reverse the dynamics

Use the same \(F\) at \(\lambda=3\), first with \(\dot x=-F\) and then with \(\dot x=F\). Determine which equilibrium is stable in each model and the linearised time constant at its stable root. Can a static root-finding algorithm distinguish these dynamics from its roots alone?

Worked solution

For \(\dot x=-F\) the linearised rate at a root is \(-2x_\star\), so +1 is stable with decay rate 2 and −1 is unstable. Reversing the evolution gives rate \(+2x_\star\), hence −1 becomes stable with the same time constant \(1/2\) in the time units of the equations.

Chapter 7 · Polynomial roots and coefficient sensitivity

Evaluate a polynomial and its derivative in one sweep, then estimate how far a coefficient error can move a simple root.

From first principles

Derive the change in a simple root from coefficient errors

Let \(p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n a_j x^j\) have a simple real or complex root \(r\), meaning \(p'(r)\ne0\). Change the coefficients by small amounts \(\delta a_j\), and suppose the root changes continuously to \(r+\delta r\). Keeping first-order terms in the perturbed root equation gives

\[0\simeq p(r)+p'(r)\delta r+\sum_{j=0}^n\delta a_jr^j,\]
\[\delta r\simeq-\frac{\sum_j\delta a_jr^j}{p'(r)} .\]

For a factored polynomial the derivative at a simple root has a direct interpretation,

\[p'(r_i)=a_n\prod_{j\ne i}(r_i-r_j).\]

Nearby roots can make this derivative small and the inferred root sensitive to modest coefficient uncertainty. The relation is a local estimate, with coefficient sizes and variable units affecting what counts as a small perturbation. At an exactly repeated root the derivative vanishes and the first-order expression cannot be used.

Derivation and applications

Measure a computed polynomial root against its coefficient budget

A raw polynomial residual can be changed by multiplying every coefficient by the same constant. A more informative diagnostic asks how much the specified coefficients must change to make the reported root exact. Consider a polynomial \(p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^na_jx^j\), a trial real root \(z\), and admissible coefficient changes \(|\delta a_j|\le\eta w_j\), with nonnegative weights \(w_j\) chosen from measurement tolerances. Requiring \(z\) to solve the perturbed equation gives

\[\sum_j\delta a_j z^j=-p(z),\]
\[|p(z)|\le\eta\sum_jw_j|z|^j .\]

For real data and real trial root, each term can be aligned in sign against the residual. If the denominator is positive, scaling those aligned perturbations simultaneously attains equality. The smallest admissible coefficient multiplier is therefore

\[\eta_*=\frac{|p(z)|}{\sum_jw_j|z|^j}.\]

A zero weight freezes the corresponding coefficient; taking \(w_j=|a_j|\) tests componentwise relative backward error. To compare this diagnostic with a forward root error, linearize about the trial value provided \(p'(z)\ne0\) and higher terms are small,

\[r-z\simeq-\frac{p(z)}{p'(z)} .\]

A small coefficient backward error and large root displacement can coexist near a multiple root. Conversely, an accurately approximated location can appear to have a large raw residual when all coefficients carry a large common scale. This sign construction must be reconsidered if a complex trial root is tested using only real coefficient perturbations.

Worked example

Compute a scale-invariant certificate for a trial root

For \(p(x)=x^2-3x+2\), take \(z=1.01\) and \(w_j=|a_j|\). The residual is \(-0.0099\); the denominator is \(1.0201+3.03+2=6.0501\). The backward error is \(0.00164\), despite the \(0.010\) location error relative to the root at one. Multiplying the polynomial and all its weights by 1000 leaves the ratio unchanged.

Second application

Test which coefficient is actually uncertain

For a different polynomial \(p(x)=x^2-4\), suppose only the constant term can be adjusted, with tolerance \(0.020\). The trial \(z=2.01\) gives \(p(z)=0.0401\). Its required tolerance multiplier is \(0.0401/0.020=2.005\), so it falls outside the specified data envelope. Indeed the constant would have to move from \(-4\) to \(-4.0401\), which directly checks the diagnostic.

A root belongs to a set of coefficients

Write \(p(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0\) and suppose a simple real root \(r\) satisfies \(p'(r)\ne0\). Perturb the coefficients and write the displaced root as \(r+\delta r\). Keeping first-order terms in the equation gives \(p'(r)\delta r+\sum_{j=0}^n\delta a_j r^j=0\). Consequently the effect of a given coefficient error depends on both the slope at the root and the size of the corresponding power. An evaluation routine can be backward accurate and still return an uncertain root when the input coefficients are insufficiently precise.

For \(p=(x-1)(x-1.01)=x^2-2.01x+1.01\), the derivative at 1 is \(-0.01\). Increasing the constant coefficient by \(10^{-6}\) moves this root upward by approximately \(10^{-4}\). The exact perturbation is easier to see by completing the square: \(p=(x-1.005)^2-0.000025\). The new lower root is \(1.005-\sqrt{0.000024}\approx1.00010102\). The first-order estimate captures the direction and scale. A coefficient change of \(0.000025\) merges the roots; a larger upward change removes both real roots.

Carry derivative information through Horner evaluation

Collect the polynomial as a sequence of nested multiply-add operations starting with \(b=a_n\). Appending the next coefficient replaces \(b\) by \(xb+a_j\). Differentiating that operation shows that a derivative accumulator \(d\) must be replaced by \(xd+b\), where \(b\) is its old value. Starting \(d=0\) and updating it before updating \(b\) yields \(p(x)\) and \(p'(x)\) without forming high powers. For \(x^3-3x+1\) at \(x=2\), the successive values of \(b\) are \(1,2,1,3\) and the derivative accumulator ends at 9. A Newton step gives \(2-3/9=5/3\).

Operation count alone says little about cancellation: near a root, large terms can subtract to produce a tiny value. A scale for the accumulated terms is \(s(x)=\sum|a_j||x|^j\), also computable by a Horner sweep. Reporting \(|p(x)|/s(x)\) when the denominator is nonzero measures how small a relative componentwise coefficient change could explain that residual. Dividing by the slope provides an approximate forward correction for a simple isolated root.

The cost of removing a root

Dividing the polynomial by \(x-\hat r\) after locating one root reduces subsequent work. Synthetic division leaves a remainder \(p(\hat r)\). Discarding it changes the equations for every remaining root. In the close-pair example, using \(\hat r=1.001\) produces quotient \(x-1.009\) and remainder \(-0.000009\). The remaining estimate shifts by the same 0.001 magnitude. Recheck candidate roots against the original polynomial; where coefficients represent uncertain measurements, a range of compatible roots can communicate more information than many printed digits.

Choose a formula by the size of the root

For \(x^2-10^8x+1=0\), the usual formula for the small root subtracts two numbers close to \(10^8\). Rounding in their last digits can dominate the small difference. Compute the large root instead by adding the positive square root and dividing by two. The product of the two roots is one, so the small root follows as its reciprocal, approximately \(10^{-8}\). This change removes a source of arithmetic cancellation. Whether the underlying coefficients determine the desired root accurately can then be assessed separately using the derivative-based sensitivity formula.

Count real roots before iterating on their locations

A sign change between two endpoints certifies an odd number of crossings for a polynomial when all roots in the interval are simple. It does not show whether the number is one or three. A Sturm sequence begins with the polynomial \(p_0\) and its derivative \(p_1\), then repeatedly sets \(p_{j+1}\) to the negative remainder from dividing \(p_{j-1}\) by \(p_j\), stopping at a constant for a square-free polynomial. For a real test value \(x\), count sign changes in the sequence of evaluated values, skipping zero entries. The decrease in this count between two nonroot endpoints equals the number of distinct roots between them.

The signs can change count only when some sequence member crosses zero. For an intermediate member, the remainder relation at its zero makes its two neighbours opposite in sign; the sign-change count across that triple is one on both sides. At a simple zero of the original polynomial, the next member is its nonzero derivative. Immediately to the left the polynomial has opposite sign to its derivative, and immediately to the right it has the same sign. The count therefore decreases by exactly one. These observations explain why the negative remainder sign and the assumption on multiple roots matter.

Isolate three roots of a cubic without choosing Newton starts

Take \(p=x^3-3x+1\) and \(p'=3x^2-3\). Dividing the cubic by the derivative leaves remainder \(-2x+1\), so use \(p_2=2x-1\). The remainder of \(p'\) on division by \(p_2\) equals its value at \(x=1/2\), namely \(-9/4\), producing final positive constant \(9/4\). At \(x=-2\) the signs are \((-,+,-,+)\), giving three variations. At \(x=2\) all entries are positive and the count is zero. The cubic therefore has exactly three roots in \((-2,2)\), a more informative conclusion than the change of sign of \(p\) alone.

At \(x=-1\), the sequence is positive, zero, negative, positive; ignoring the zero gives two variations. At zero its signs are \((+,-,-,+)\), also two. At \(x=1\) the signs are negative, zero, positive, positive, giving one variation. It follows that \((-2,-1)\), \((0,1)\) and \((1,2)\) each contain a single root, whereas \((-1,0)\) contains none. Bisection or safeguarded Newton can now refine each isolating interval without ambiguity about missing another real root.

For a polynomial represented by rounded measured coefficients, this count addresses the specific polynomial actually supplied. Near a repeated root, small coefficient changes can alter the number of real roots. Exact rational arithmetic or certified sign enclosures may be needed if sequence values are close to zero; evaluating a delicate remainder sequence with ordinary rounding cannot by itself certify the sign variations of the intended polynomial.

As a check on isolation, the candidate intervals account for all three roots allowed by the degree. Further sign-preserving splits in the empty interval can never uncover a real root there. This accounting reduces the risk of reporting the same attractive Newton root twice while missing a separate crossing.

Chapter 8 · Root enclosures with interval Newton

Combine interval bounds and a single function evaluation to exclude roots or isolate one with a verifiable enclosure.

From first principles

Extract a root enclosure from the mean value theorem

Suppose a differentiable scalar \(f\) has a root \(r\) in a current interval \(X\), and choose a midpoint or other point \(m\in X\). The mean value theorem relates \(f(m)\) to the derivative at an unknown intermediate point. If an interval \(D\) contains every derivative on \(X\) and excludes zero, then

\[0=f(m)+f'(\xi)(r-m),\]
\[r\in m-\frac{f(m)}{D}.\]

Dividing by the entire derivative interval accounts for all possible intermediate slopes. Intersect with the original interval to retain both facts about the root,

\[X_{\rm new}=X\cap\left(m-\frac{f(m)}{D}\right).\]

An empty intersection proves there was no root in \(X\). A nonempty intersection alone does not establish existence; a verified sign bracket supplies a separate existence proof. Outward-rounded evaluations of the function and derivative bounds are necessary for a numerical certificate. If \(D\) contains zero, ordinary interval division cannot justify this simple enclosure.

Derivation and applications

Include function-evaluation uncertainty in an interval Newton step

A derivative enclosure is insufficient for a reliable root bound when the value at the midpoint is itself uncertain. Suppose every root under consideration lies in a real interval \(X\), and \(f'\) stays between two verified positive bounds \(0<d_-\le d_+\) throughout \(X\). At \(m\in X\) the numerical or measured value supplies only \(f(m)\in[u,v]\). For any root \(r\) in the interval the mean value theorem now gives

\[r=m-\frac{f(m)}{f'(\xi)}\in m-\frac{[u,v]}{[d_-,d_+]} .\]

If \(0\le u\le v\), all ratios are nonnegative. The smallest ratio uses the smallest numerator and largest denominator; the largest uses the largest numerator and smallest denominator. Consequently

\[X_{\rm new}=X\cap[m-v/d_-,\,m-u/d_+] .\]

If \(u<0<v\), both extremes instead use the smallest denominator. The new candidate interval becomes

\[X_{\rm new}=X\cap[m-v/d_-,\,m-u/d_-] .\]

These enclosures retain any root in \(X\); existence still needs independent evidence such as verified opposite signs at the ends. A sign-uncertain midpoint can prevent further narrowing once evaluation error dominates. Tightening only the derivative interval need not improve the result materially in that regime. For machine-certified endpoints, rounded arithmetic must widen each interval as needed.

Worked example

Enclose a square root with a rounded residual

For \(f(x)=x^2-2\) on \([1.4,1.5]\), use \(m=1.45\), verified derivative interval \([2.8,3.0]\), and a coarse residual enclosure \([0.102,0.103]\). Then \(X_{\rm new}=[1.45-0.103/2.8,\,1.45-0.102/3.0]\), which is contained in the outward-rounded interval \([1.41321,1.41600]\). The endpoint signs of the original interval establish existence, and the positive derivative makes the root unique.

Second application

Recognize the resolution floor of a sensor

A separate monotone balance has derivative in \([4,5]\). At \(m=3\), its instrument reads zero within \(\pm0.020\). The sign-uncertain formula gives \(r\in[2.995,3.005]\) for any root in the working bracket. To certify a half-width \(0.001\) using the same lower derivative bound, the residual measurement must be resolved to \(\pm0.004\) or better unless additional location information is available.

From a point step to an enclosure

Let \(f\) have a continuous derivative throughout a closed interval \(I\). Choose \(m\in I\) and compute an interval \(D\) containing every derivative value on \(I\). If \(0\notin D\), any root \(r\in I\) satisfies \(0=f(m)+f'(\xi)(r-m)\) by the mean value theorem. Therefore \(r\in N=m-f(m)/D\). Intersecting \(N\) with \(I\) retains every possible root. An empty intersection certifies that the original interval contained none. Since the derivative has a fixed sign, there can be at most one.

Existence needs an additional argument. Opposite endpoint signs suffice by continuity. Alternatively if \(N\) lies strictly inside \(I\), its extremal corrections force a sign change or a zero at \(m\): for an increasing function and \(f(m)>0\), moving left by \(f(m)/\min D\) decreases the function by at least \(f(m)\). That point lies in \(I\). The other signs follow by reversing the interval or taking \(-f\). This argument explains why proper containment has stronger meaning than partial overlap.

Enclose a square root

For \(f=x^2-2\) on \([1,2]\), derivatives lie in \([2,4]\). The midpoint gives \(f(1.5)=0.25\), so \(N=[1.5-0.25/2,1.5-0.25/4]=[1.375,1.4375]\). Its width is one sixteenth of the starting width. Ordinary Newton at 1.5 would give \(1.416\overline6\); an accurate single estimate carries a different type of evidence from an enclosure with guaranteed endpoints.

Choose \(m=1.40625\) in the new interval. Now \(f(m)=-0.0224609375\) and \(D=[2.75,2.875]\). The next interval lies between \(1.40625+0.0224609375/2.875\) and \(1.40625+0.0224609375/2.75\), approximately \([1.414062,1.414418]\) with endpoints rounded outward. For narrower intervals the variation in \(1/f'\) shrinks proportionally to width when \(f'\) is Lipschitz and bounded away from zero. Because \(f(m)\) is itself proportional to width near the root, the new width often decreases quadratically.

Keep the certificate valid

Each arithmetic operation needs outward rounding when performed in floating point. Rounding inward can exclude a root even when the algebra is exact. If a derivative interval crosses zero, ordinary interval division cannot give a single bounded correction. Subdivide and test again, or use an independent enclosure argument. For \(x^2\) on \([-1,1]\), repeated subdivision approaching zero encounters a true double root where the derivative vanishes. The usual simple-root contraction argument does not apply there. A sign-change test also misses this root because the function just touches the axis.

Use a stronger derivative enclosure when possible

For \(f(x)=x+x^3\) on \([-1,1]\), the derivative is \(1+3x^2\). Multiplying two independent copies of the interval \([-1,1]\) gives the loose product \([-1,1]\), leading to the derivative enclosure \([-2,4]\). Treating the expression as an actual square instead gives \([1,4]\), excluding zero. The latter bound allows the interval Newton step at the midpoint to collapse directly onto the root zero. Both interval results enclose every derivative, but keeping algebraic dependence between two identical factors greatly improves the certificate.

Extend enclosure to a system using a preconditioned map

For a differentiable vector function \(F\), choose an approximate inverse Jacobian \(C\), a box \(X\) and a centre \(m\) inside it. Roots of \(F\) are fixed points of \(G(x)=x-CF(x)\), and the converse holds when \(C\) is nonsingular. The integral mean-value identity expresses \(G(x)\) as \(m-CF(m)\) plus an averaged derivative \([I-CJ]\) acting on \(x-m\). If every true Jacobian on the box is enclosed in an interval matrix, interval arithmetic produces an outer enclosure for the complete image \(G(X)\). This is the basis of a Krawczyk-type inclusion test.

A simple sufficient certificate is to bound the induced infinity norm of \(I-CJ(x)\) by \(q<1\) throughout the box and show that \(G(X)\subseteq X\). The closed box is complete, so the contraction theorem then gives a unique root in it. In a cube of radius \(\rho\) around \(m\), it suffices that \(\|CF(m)\|_\infty+q\rho\le\rho\). If this test fails, no conclusion follows: the box may be too large, \(C\) may be a poor inverse, or the interval enclosures may have lost correlation between entries.

Certify a coupled nonlinear equilibrium with one matrix bound

Let \(F_1=x+0.1y^2-1,\ F_2=y+0.1x^2-1\). Use centre \(m=(0.90,0.90)^T\), cube radius 0.05 and \(C=I\). Each central residual is \(0.90+0.081-1=-0.019\). The derivative of \(G(x,y)=(1-0.1y^2,1-0.1x^2)\) has zero diagonal and off-diagonal entries \(-0.2y,-0.2x\). On the cube both coordinate magnitudes are at most 0.95, so every absolute row sum is at most 0.19. The total displacement bound is \(0.019+0.19(0.05)=0.0285<0.05\). This verifies existence and uniqueness within the box without assuming that the answer has equal coordinates.

The first map evaluation gives \((0.919,0.919)\). A tighter direct image uses the monotonic decrease of \(1-0.1s^2\) for positive \(s\): both coordinates land in \([1-0.1(0.95)^2,1-0.1(0.85)^2]=[0.90975,0.92775]\). Uniqueness together with symmetry under swapping coordinates forces the root in this box to satisfy \(x=y=s\). The scalar equation \(s+0.1s^2=1\) then gives \(s=-5+\sqrt{35}\approx0.91608\), which lies within the certified interval.

The other scalar root \(-5-\sqrt{35}\) lies far outside the box. The local certificate excludes no roots elsewhere and must not be reported as global uniqueness. In floating-point certification, round every bound outward, particularly evaluations that nearly touch a face. A convenient approximate Newton iterate can suggest the centre, while the independent box computation supplies the actual existence claim.

Consider expanding the initial certificate all the way to the cube \([-6,6]^2\). The corresponding row-sum bound would be 1.2, violating the simple contraction criterion, and the scalar symmetry reduction exhibits two roots in that cube. Thus in this enlarged domain failure of the uniqueness test correctly reflects an actual multiplicity problem. On a moderately larger domain the very same bound might merely be too conservative. Either situation calls for subdivision or sharper derivative information, rather than interpreting a failed sufficient test as evidence of nonexistence.

Subdividing preserves the distinction because the certificate can be applied independently on each smaller closed box.

Chapter 9 · Secant information for systems

Derive Broyden's rank-one Jacobian update and decide whether the direction it learned is sufficient for the next step.

From first principles

Update a Jacobian using what one step measured

Suppose a system has residual map \(F\) and the current Jacobian approximation is \(B\). Moving by a nonzero displacement \(s\) yields an observed residual change \(y=F(x+s)-F(x)\). A local linear map consistent with this observation must satisfy \(B_{\rm new}s=y\). Look for the smallest adjustment \(D=B_{\rm new}-B\) in Frobenius norm. For each row the minimum-length vector with prescribed dot product against \(s\) is parallel to \(s\). Thus

\[Ds=y-Bs,\]
\[D=\frac{(y-Bs)s^T}{s^Ts}.\]

Multiplying by \(s\) checks that the observation is reproduced exactly. For every vector \(v\) perpendicular to \(s\), \(Dv=0\), so the action in such directions is preserved. The resulting rank-one update is

\[B_{\rm new}=B+\frac{(y-Bs)s^T}{s^Ts}.\]

One secant identifies only one directional response. Curvature and noise both affect \(y\), making a tiny or poorly resolved step a weak basis for updating an entire Jacobian model.

Derivation and applications

Make a secant update respect physical coordinate scales

The smallest change in the entries of a Jacobian depends on the units used for each input variable. To construct a scale-aware update, choose a positive definite state metric \(W\), so that squared step length is \(s^TWs\). Write scaled coordinates \(z=W^{1/2}x\), leaving the output units fixed. If \(B\) approximates the derivative with respect to physical \(x\), the corresponding scaled derivative and step are

\[\widetilde B=BW^{-1/2},\]
\[\widetilde s=W^{1/2}s,\]
\[\widetilde B\widetilde s=Bs .\]

Apply the ordinary smallest-Frobenius-change secant update in the scaled coordinates. Conversion back to \(x\) multiplies the resulting matrix on the right by \(W^{1/2}\). Simplifying gives

\[B_{\rm new}=B+\frac{(y-Bs)s^T W}{s^TW s}.\]

The secant equation holds for every positive definite metric. The action preserved outside the measured direction is now determined by weighted orthogonality,

\[B_{\rm new}s=y,\]
\[(B_{\rm new}-B)v=0\quad\text{if }s^TWv=0 .\]

This construction assumes the selected scales describe relevant changes in each variable and that the step is observed accurately. It is invariant under a common multiplication of \(W\) by a positive scalar. Different relative scales legitimately produce different unmeasured directional predictions, since a single step cannot identify those predictions from data alone.

Worked example

Compare scaled updates for an imbalanced step

Take one output, \(B=(0,0)\), step \(s=(1,10)^T\) and observed change \(y=2\). An unscaled update yields \((2,20)/101\). If typical variable scales are 1 and 10, choose \(W=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.01)\). Then \(s^TWs=2\), giving \(B_{\rm new}=(1,0.1)\). Both estimates give dot product 2 on the observed step. Their predictions on \((1,0)\) differ by a factor of 50.5.

Second application

Check invariance after a unit conversion

Suppose \(s=(2,1)^T,\ W=I,\ B=0,\ y=5\). The new row is \((2,1)\). Express the second step component in units one hundred times smaller: \(s'=(2,100)^T,\ W'=\operatorname{diag}(1,10^{-4})\). The scaled update is \((2,0.01)\), which gives the same output change \(4+1=5\). A future physical unit displacement along the second coordinate also gives the same response under either representation.

What one trajectory identifies

After a step \(s=x_{k+1}-x_k\), two measured residuals provide \(y=F(x_{k+1})-F(x_k)\). Integrating the Jacobian along the connecting segment yields \(y=\int_0^1 J(x_k+ts)s\,dt\). Thus \(y\) measures the action of an averaged Jacobian on just one direction. Rebuilding an unrestricted \(n\times n\) Jacobian from these \(n\) numbers is underdetermined. Impose the secant equation \(B_{\rm new}s=y\) and prefer the smallest possible change to the current approximate Jacobian \(B\) in Frobenius norm.

Set \(w=y-Bs\). Each row of the correction must have specified dot product with \(s\). The shortest such row is a multiple of \(s^T\); any added component orthogonal to \(s\) increases its squared length. Solving the scalar normalisation in each row gives

\[B_{\rm new}=B+\frac{(y-Bs)s^T}{s^Ts}.\]

The new matrix is exact on \(s\) for the observed change and leaves every vector perpendicular to \(s\) unchanged. An earlier secant equation can be disturbed if its direction has a component along the latest step. This update approximates the Jacobian; updating an inverse directly requires its own formula and conditions.

Observe two independent directions

Let \(F(x)=Ax-b\), \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(B_0=I\). A displacement \(s_1=(1,0)^T\) gives \(y_1=(2,1)^T\). The first correction changes column one, giving \(B_1=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). An independent orthogonal displacement \(s_2=(0,1)^T\) gives \(y_2=(1,3)^T\) and recovers the second column exactly. After these two measurements the whole linear operator is known. Such orthogonal probe steps illustrate identifiability; an actual solver selects directions from its approximate Newton equations, so its steps need not be orthogonal.

Instead repeat the first step scaled by two. The predicted and observed residual changes now agree on that direction and the update vanishes, even though \(B_1\) is still wrong on \(e_2\). Small secant corrections by themselves therefore do not demonstrate that all directions of the linearisation are reliable.

When to request a new Jacobian

Given \(B_k\), solve \(B_ks=-F(x_k)\), test the step and update with the displacement actually accepted. Reusing the proposed full displacement after a line search violates the secant equation for the measurements taken. If function data contain errors of size \(\epsilon\), then dividing a change in their difference by a tiny step can corrupt the learned derivative by order \(\epsilon/\|s\|\). Compare achieved reduction with the predicted reduction, monitor the size of the residual itself and occasionally recompute a Jacobian if the model becomes unreliable. These diagnostics matter most when evaluating the underlying residual is substantially cheaper than assembling its derivatives.

Judge a secant across a bend

For the scalar residual \(f=x^2-1\), moving from one to two supplies secant slope three, the average of endpoint slopes two and four. Reusing it near two underestimates the current slope by one. Over a step from 1.9 to two the secant slope is instead 3.9. This simple example isolates two types of Jacobian discrepancy: even perfectly accurate data constrain only the sampled direction, and nonlinear variation makes that directional measurement an average over a finite segment. Shorter accepted steps reduce the latter discrepancy until residual evaluation noise becomes important.

Update an inverse without rebuilding a factorisation

Suppose an invertible Jacobian approximation \(B\) has inverse \(H\). A secant update of the form \(B_+=B+uv^T\) has inverse \(H_+=H-Huv^TH/(1+v^THu)\) provided the scalar denominator is nonzero. Multiply this candidate by \(B+uv^T\) to verify the formula: the two added rank-one terms from the individual products are cancelled by the cross term after factoring the common denominator. If the denominator vanishes, \(B_+Hu=(1+v^THu)u=0\) and the updated matrix is singular whenever \(u\ne0\).

For Broyden's Jacobian update \(u=y-Bs,\ v=s/(s^Ts)\), the denominator becomes \(s^THy/(s^Ts)\). Substitution yields \(H_+=H+(s-Hy)s^TH/(s^THy)\). This satisfies the inverse secant condition \(H_+y=s\). It is specifically the inverse of the minimal-change Jacobian update. Directly minimising the Frobenius change in the inverse subject to this condition gives a different formula in general, because minimum changes in a matrix and its inverse are not equivalent objectives.

Distinguish harmless secant data from a singular update

Start with \(B=H=I\), take \(s=(1,0)^T\) and observe \(y=(2,1)^T\). The new Jacobian replaces its first column with \(y\) and leaves its second unchanged, giving \(B_+=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Here \(s^THy=2\). The inverse update adds \((s-y)s^T/2\) to the identity, producing \(H_+=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1/2&0\\-1/2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Multiplication confirms both \(H_+B_+=I\) and \(H_+y=s\). Applying it to a new residual \((0,1)^T\) leaves that residual unchanged before the sign change for the Newton correction.

Now observe \(y=(0,1)^T\) for the same step. The updated Jacobian has identical columns \((0,1)^T\) and determinant zero. This need not mean the true Jacobian is singular. For example the perfectly invertible exchange matrix with columns \((0,1)^T\) and \((1,0)^T\) gives exactly that observation. The problem is that the unmeasured old second column has become incompatible with the newly learned first one. One directional observation cannot determine which of those explanations is correct.

If the observed first component were a small \(\epsilon\) rather than exactly zero, the inverse update would amplify certain residuals by order \(1/|\epsilon|\). Monitoring the update denominator relative to the norms of \(s\) and \(Hy\), and verifying the residual reduction after the step, can detect this failure mechanism. Rebuilding independent derivative information is more informative than applying repeated almost-singular rank-one corrections derived from essentially the same trajectory.

One diagnostic is to probe the unmeasured direction directly before committing to a new inverse. In the exchange-matrix example choosing an independent displacement \(s_2=e_2\) yields measured change \(e_1\), while the singular provisional matrix predicts \(e_2\). The disagreement localises the missing derivative information in a particular column. Returning to the original linear equations then gives an invertible Jacobian with determinant minus one. A diagonal perturbation of the singular provisional matrix would also make a solve possible, but it would not reveal which physical direction the model couples to the second coordinate. Additional measurements and algebraic regularisation address different uncertainties in the step.

In a larger system, probe selection can focus on directions where the model repeatedly gives inaccurate residual predictions.

Chapter 10 · Solving Newton equations inexactly

Allocate work between the inner linear iteration and outer nonlinear iteration, and estimate the accuracy needed from a Jacobian action.

From first principles

Track the residual left by an approximate Newton solve

A large nonlinear system may make an exact Newton correction too expensive. Suppose the computed step \(s_k\) solves its linearized equation only approximately. Define the linear defect \(d_k=J(x_k)s_k+F(x_k)\) and require its norm to be a fraction \(\eta_k\) of the starting imbalance,

\[\|d_k\|\le\eta_k\|F(x_k)\|,\]
\[0\le\eta_k<1 .\]

If the Jacobian is \(L\)-Lipschitz along the trial segment, integrating its change along the step bounds the nonlinear remainder. Substituting the observed defect into the Taylor expansion gives

\[\|F(x_k+s_k)\|\le\|d_k\|+\tfrac12 L\|s_k\|^2 .\]

The first term is controllable by the inner solver; the second is inherent in using a local linear model. Solving the inner equation much more accurately than the nonlinear remainder demands can waste effort. Close to a regular root, progressively smaller forcing fractions permit faster outer convergence when derivative products and residual evaluations are also accurate enough.

Derivation and applications

Derive a subtraction-free directional derivative for analytic residuals

A matrix-free Newton iteration needs products \(J(x)v\). A complex perturbation can obtain such a product without subtracting two nearly equal real residuals. Assume each component of \(F\) is real for real arguments and has an analytic extension along \(x+zv\) near \(z=0\). The implementation must propagate complex arithmetic through the same analytic formulas. Expanding a scalar component in the direction \(v\) gives

\[F(x+ihv)=F(x)+ihJv-\frac{h^2}{2}D_v^2F-\frac{ih^3}{6}D_v^3F+O(h^4).\]

Real coefficients make even powers real and odd powers imaginary. Extracting the imaginary part and dividing by \(h\) therefore produces a second-order directional approximation for sufficiently smooth analytic data,

\[\frac{\operatorname{Im}F(x+ihv)}{h}=Jv-\frac{h^2}{6}D_v^3F+O(h^4).\]

The unperturbed real value never appears in a cancellation-prone subtraction. If a product approximation \(\widetilde Jv\) is used within an inexact Newton solve, the true linear defect still includes its directional error. For the proposed step \(s\),

\[\|Js+F\|\le\|\widetilde J s+F\|+\|Js-\widetilde J s\|.\]

A small reported inner residual can conceal inaccurate Jacobian actions. An operation that discards imaginary parts, takes an absolute value, or branches differently under complex inputs violates the argument. Extreme steps can also underflow or interact with inaccurate library computations, so agreement across several steps is a useful check.

Worked example

Check the truncation error on a cubic

For \(F(x)=x^3,\ x=2,\ v=1,\ h=0.1\), the true action is \(3x^2=12\). The imaginary part of \((2+0.1i)^3\) is \(1.2-0.001=1.199\), giving \(11.99\) after division. Halving \(h\) gives \(11.9975\), quartering the error exactly because all higher derivatives vanish.

Second application

Expose a nonanalytic implementation

Suppose a real residual is implemented as \(F(x)=|x|\). At \(x=2\), its real derivative is one. A direct complex call at \(2+ih\) returns the real modulus \(\sqrt{4+h^2}\); its imaginary quotient is zero for every \(h\). For a proposed correction \(s=-0.1\), the Jacobian action is thus wrong by \(0.1\), far above an inner defect budget of \(0.001\). Shrinking the complex perturbation cannot repair the implementation mismatch.

The linear defect survives into the next residual

For a differentiable system \(F(x)=0\), an exact Newton step cancels the first two terms in the Taylor expansion \(F(x+s)=F(x)+J(x)s+R(s)\). If the Jacobian is Lipschitz continuous with constant \(L\) on the segment, integrating its variation gives \(\|R(s)\|\leq L\|s\|^2/2\). For a computed step define the linear defect \(d=F(x)+J(x)s\). An inner iterative solver can stop once \(\|d\|\leq\eta_k\|F(x)\|\). Its dimensionless forcing factor \(\eta_k\) controls how much of the old residual may survive the linear solve.

Near a simple root assume \(\|J^{-1}\|\le M\) and full steps. Then \(\|s\|\le M(1+\eta_k)\|F(x)\|\), because \(Js=-F+d\). Substitution gives

\[\|F(x+s)\|\leq\eta_k\|F(x)\|+\tfrac12 LM^2(1+\eta_k)^2\|F(x)\|^2.\]

A forcing factor bounded below one permits local linear convergence. A factor tending to zero gives superlinear convergence, and \(\eta_k=O(\|F(x)\|)\) restores a quadratic local bound when units and scaling have been fixed. Solving early linear systems to near machine precision may waste work because model curvature dominates the outer error.

Observe the uncancelled term

For \(f(x)=x^2-2\), start at \(x=1.5\), where \(f=0.25\) and \(J=3\). The exact step is \(-1/12\); its new residual equals \(s^2=1/144\). If the computed step is \(-0.075\), the defect is \(0.25-0.225=0.025\), a forcing factor of 0.1. The actual residual at 1.425 is \(0.030625=0.025+0.005625\). Most of this residual now comes from the inner solve. Halving the step simply for caution would increase the linear defect again; a line search and an inner tolerance address different questions.

Apply a Jacobian without storing it

A Krylov solver can request a directional action through \(Jv\approx [F(x+hv)-F(x)]/h\). With normalised \(v\), a smooth residual and absolute evaluation uncertainty \(\epsilon\), the error behaves like \(C_1 h+C_2\epsilon/h\). Minimising that model gives a scale proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\), with units and scales supplied by \(x\), \(v\) and \(F\). Automatic directional differentiation can avoid this differencing error when the residual implementation permits it.

An inner iteration using approximate products may report a small residual for its own approximate linear model. Verify a candidate full step by evaluating the actual nonlinear function, and compare the measured reduction with the forcing prediction. Once errors in evaluating \(F\) exceed the requested outer tolerance, neither decreasing the forcing factor nor making difference steps smaller can supply the claimed accuracy.

Produce a Jacobian action by forward differentiation

A matrix-free Newton solve asks for \(J(x)v\). Finite differences subtract two complete residual evaluations. Forward-mode automatic differentiation instead propagates a directional derivative alongside each intermediate value. Represent an intermediate by \(a+\dot a\varepsilon\), with \(\varepsilon^2=0\). Addition combines the two components separately. Multiplication gives \(ab+(a\dot b+\dot a b)\varepsilon\). A smooth scalar operation \(\phi\) gives \(\phi(a)+\phi'(a)\dot a\varepsilon\). Applying these rules to the operations of the original residual program is just repeated use of the chain rule.

Seed each independent input with its component of \(v\). The final values give \(F(x)\). The accompanying derivative components give \(J(x)v\) in the same sweep. There is no differencing increment to select. Errors in ordinary floating-point arithmetic and the accuracy of the underlying residual program still matter. A branch switch or an absolute value at zero can leave the mathematical derivative undefined. Differentiating the particular executed branch cannot supply the missing derivative. For a full dense Jacobian the method generally requires a separate direction for each independent column.

For example use the shared intermediate \(s=x+y\) in \(F=(s e^y-1,s^2+x-4)^T\). At \((2,0)\) propagate direction \((1,-2)\). The pairs for \(x,y\) are \((2,1)\) and \((0,-2)\). \(s\) becomes \((2,-1)\), and \(e^y\) becomes \((1,-2)\). The first output is therefore \((1,-5)\), the second \((2,-3)\). Thus \(F=(1,2)^T\) and the Krylov product is \((-5,-3)^T\). As a separate check the analytic Jacobian at this point has rows \((1,3)\) and \((5,4)\), whose dot products with the seed give the same two numbers.

Scale the forcing ratio for coupled balances

Consider two model balances expressed in newtons and millimetres. A raw Euclidean norm compares numerical values in these unrelated units. Changing the length unit alone can then make an inner solve appear more or less successful. Introduce a diagonal row scaling \(W\) based on meaningful force and length scales, and test \(\|W(F+Js)\|\le\eta\|WF\|\). With characteristic magnitudes 1000 N and 0.1 mm, physical defects of 1 N and 0.001 mm become dimensionless defects 0.001 and 0.01. The second equation is now the larger unmet balance, even though its dimensional number is smaller.

Budget a matrix-free central derivative separately

When the linearised equation is solved through matrix-vector actions, the inner solver works with estimates of \(Jv\). For a unit search direction \(v\), set \(\psi(t)=F(x+tv)\). The centred difference \([\psi(h)-\psi(-h)]/(2h)\) cancels even Taylor terms and has truncation error bounded by \(M h^2/6\) if the third directional derivative has norm at most \(M\) on the segment. If each residual evaluation carries absolute error bounded by \(\epsilon\), subtraction and division can contribute up to \(\epsilon/h\). This is a worst-case model; paired evaluations with strongly correlated errors can behave differently.

Minimising \(M h^2/6+\epsilon/h\) gives \(h=(3\epsilon/M)^{1/3}\) for positive \(M\). This balance is specific to the centred, second-order formula and requires evaluating the residual twice for each direction. A derivative already available by an analytic action or automatic differentiation has a different accuracy and cost model. Moreover \(M\) depends on both the operating state and the direction; a single unscaled absolute step is rarely well calibrated for a system mixing quantities with strongly different units.

Compare the derivative error with the requested inner accuracy

For \(f(x)=x^3-2\) at \(x=1\), the true derivative is three and the exact centred estimate at step \(h\) is \(3+h^2\), so \(M=6\). Let each function evaluation have absolute uncertainty at most \(2\times10^{-9}\). The worst-case derivative error is bounded by \(h^2+2\times10^{-9}/h\), minimised at \(h=0.001\). The truncation contribution is then \(10^{-6}\) and the evaluation contribution is \(2\times10^{-6}\), giving a combined bound of \(3\times10^{-6}\). Reducing the step to \(10^{-5}\) makes the evaluation contribution \(2\times10^{-4}\), about sixty-seven times the previously balanced total error.

The exact Newton correction at this state is \(s=1/3\). If the inner solver uses the approximate scalar derivative \(3+e\), it can solve its own equation exactly by taking \(s=1/(3+e)\). The true linear defect is then \(-1+3/(3+e)=-e/(3+e)\). For the balanced derivative bound this can be about \(10^{-6}\) in size. An inner residual display near machine zero therefore need not establish a true forcing ratio of \(10^{-8}\). The derivative action, rather than the Krylov stopping tolerance, has become the limiting approximation.

A practical way to expose this discrepancy is to repeat a few directional actions over a geometric sequence of \(h\) values, looking first for the expected quadratic truncation trend and then for deterioration. Such comparisons estimate sensitivity to evaluation and discretisation errors; they are not a rigorous lower bound on what all directions achieve. Recompute the actual nonlinear residual after an accepted step to discover whether the reduced model accuracy still suffices for useful outer progress.

If the finite-difference increment represents a physical parameter change that crosses a material switch, the required third derivative need not exist on the segment. A tidy numerical plateau across several step sizes can then obscure a change of branch. Verify that both directional evaluations use the same smooth model region before interpreting the calculated optimum increment.

Chapter 11 · Gauss–Newton and residual curvature

Derive Gauss–Newton from a local residual model and locate when omitted curvature or insufficient information changes the step.

From first principles

Derive Gauss–Newton from a local model of measured residuals

Let \(r(x)\) contain the differences between model predictions and measured data, expressed in comparable units or whitened by their known uncertainties. With more measurements than unknowns, exact agreement may be impossible. Minimize \(\phi(x)=\|r(x)\|^2/2\). A first-order local model of each differentiable residual produces a linear least-squares problem for a step \(s\),

\[r(x+s)\simeq r(x)+Js,\]
\[\min_s\tfrac12\|r+Js\|^2 .\]

At the minimum the predicted residual is orthogonal to every column of \(J\), yielding the Gauss–Newton equations

\[J^TJs=-J^Tr .\]

If \(J\) has independent columns, the local model has a unique minimizer. In computation a QR or singular-value solve can be preferable to forming \(J^TJ\). Residual functions with strong curvature may make a full model step inaccurate, particularly when the best fit still has large residuals. Comparing actual and predicted decrease tests the local approximation.

Derivation and applications

Predict local Gauss–Newton convergence when the best residual is nonzero

For a nonlinear least-squares problem a stationary point can retain a mismatch with the observations. That mismatch multiplies residual curvature in the exact Hessian and can control the iteration speed. To expose the effect, take one scalar parameter \(x\), residuals \(r_i(x)\) with two continuous derivatives, and objective \(\phi=\tfrac12\sum_i r_i^2\). Differentiate twice to separate two contributions,

\[g=\phi'=\sum_i r_i r_i',\]
\[\phi''=H+S,\]
\[H=\sum_i(r_i')^2,\]
\[S=\sum_i r_i r_i'' .\]

Gauss–Newton replaces the actual curvature by the always nonnegative sensitivity term \(H\). Assume \(x_*\) has \(g(x_*)=0\) and \(H(x_*)>0\). Differentiate the update map \(G(x)=x-g(x)/H(x)\). At the stationary point the term involving \(H'\) cancels exactly because \(g=0\), giving

\[G'(x_*)=1-\frac{\phi''(x_*)}{H(x_*)}=-\frac{S(x_*)}{H(x_*)}.\]

The local error thus behaves as

\[e_{k+1}=-\frac{S(x_*)}{H(x_*)}e_k+o(e_k),\]
\[e_k=x_k-x_* .\]

If all residuals vanish, the linear error term disappears. A strict local minimum of the true objective need not attract the full Gauss–Newton iteration: positive total curvature \(H+S\) alone does not enforce \(|S/H|<1\). Damping changes the local factor by multiplying the modeled correction by a fraction.

Worked example

Predict oscillation near a weakly curved mismatch

Take \(r_1=x,\ r_2=1+0.2x^2\). At \(x_*=0\), \(H=1\), \(S=0.4\), giving local factor \(-0.4\). Starting at \(x=0.10\), \(g=0.14008\), \(H=1.0016\), and the full update lands at \(-0.03986\), close to the linear prediction \(-0.040\). The fitted residual of one in the second channel controls the alternating approach.

Second application

Stabilize a minimum that repels full steps

For \(r_1=x,\ r_2=1+x^2\), the origin is a strict minimum since \(\phi''(0)=3\). The full-step factor there is \(-2\), so small errors grow. A half-step changes the factor to \(1-0.5(3/1)=-0.5\), restoring local contraction. The residual value at the optimum is identical in both examples; its curvature determines the different behaviour.

More equations than unknowns

Repeated measurements generally leave a residual even at the best parameters. For \(r(x)\in\mathbb R^m\), minimise \(\Phi(x)=\|r(x)\|^2/2\). Differentiating gives \(\nabla\Phi=J^Tr\). At a stationary point the residual is perpendicular to every first-order change accessible by varying the parameters. It may remain large if the model cannot reproduce the observations. Different physical units require weights or a clear rescaling before squaring coordinates; otherwise a change of units silently changes what solution is optimal.

Approximate \(r(x+s)\) by \(r+Js\) and minimise its squared norm. The least-squares condition is \(J^TJs=-J^Tr\). In practice a QR or singular-value factorisation of \(J\) avoids squaring its condition number. The exact Hessian is \(J^TJ+\sum_i r_i\nabla^2r_i\). Thus the neglected term vanishes at a zero-residual solution, but can matter for strongly curved residuals with a poor fit. A descent direction follows with full column rank whenever the gradient is nonzero: multiplying the normal equations by \(s^T\) gives \(s^T\nabla\Phi=-\|Js\|^2<0\).

Fit two incompatible observations

A parameter \(x\) predicts \(x\) for one sensor and \(x^2\) for another. Suppose their dimensionless observations are 1 and 2. Then \(r=(x-1,x^2-2)^T\). At \(x=1\) the residual is \((0,-1)^T\) and \(J=(1,2)^T\). The linear model chooses \(s=2/5=0.4\), moving to 1.4. The objective drops from 0.5 to \((0.4^2+0.04^2)/2=0.0808\). An exact fit is impossible because \(x=1\) would require the other observation to be one.

At the original point the exact second derivative is \(5-2=3\), so a full scalar Newton step on the objective would be \(2/3\), quite different from the Gauss–Newton step. At 1.4 the gradient is \(0.4+2.8(-0.04)=0.288\). Another Gauss–Newton step is \(-0.288/(1+2.8^2)\approx-0.03258\). The iteration reverses direction as the two sensor discrepancies begin to balance. Monitoring only the second sensor would favour a different parameter.

Regularise the step model

Minimising \(\|r+Js\|^2+\lambda\|s\|^2\) gives \((J^TJ+\lambda I)s=-J^Tr\). A positive \(\lambda\) controls overly large steps along weakly observed directions. With \(\lambda=5\) at the original point the step becomes 0.2 and the true objective at 1.2 is 0.1768. This is a smaller first improvement but may be more reliable elsewhere. Compare the actual decrease with that predicted by the local model to adjust damping. This temporary penalty on the step differs physically from assuming in advance that the final parameter itself must be small.

Changing one sensor's reliability changes the fit

Suppose the squared residual of the second sensor receives weight one quarter because its standard deviation is twice that of the first. In the worked example at \(x=1\), the scalar normal-equation numerator becomes \(0+(1/4)\,2(-1)=-0.5\); its denominator becomes \(1+(1/4)\,2^2=2\). The step is now 0.25. The model has moved less far from the exactly matched first sensor because the second sensor's discrepancy now carries less statistical weight. Repeated observations with correlated errors would require a full covariance model to interpret this weighting as a statistical fit.

Derive a bounded-influence residual fit

Ordinary least squares makes a single residual's contribution to the gradient proportional to its magnitude. If rare gross errors are plausible, choose a loss that is quadratic for small discrepancies and linear for large ones. For the Huber loss with transition \(\delta>0\), use \(\rho(r)=r^2/2\) for \(|r|\le\delta\) and \(\delta |r|-\delta^2/2\) otherwise. Matching value and derivative at both transition points ensures a continuously differentiable convex objective in a location parameter. Its score is \(\psi(r)=\max(-\delta,\min(r,\delta))\).

For differentiable model residuals \(r_i(x)\) the stationarity equation becomes \(J^T\psi(r)=0\). Set \(w_i=\psi(r_i)/r_i\) for nonzero residuals and one at zero. The score is now a weighted residual and solving a succession of weighted least-squares problems supplies an iteratively reweighted method. The weights must be recomputed from each current fit. Treating them as externally measured inverse variances would misinterpret their statistical role. Convexity of \(\rho\) in \(r\) does not by itself guarantee convexity with respect to nonlinear parameters.

Trace the effect of a contaminated reading

Four sensors estimate the same scalar location and report 0, 1, 2 and 20 in common standardised units. Least squares fits their mean \(23/4=5.75\). Use \(\delta=1\) instead, with residuals \(x-b_i\). In the interval \([1,2]\) the first sensor contributes score \(+1\) and the last contributes \(-1\). The two central readings contribute \(x-1\) and \(x-2\). The sum is \(2x-3\), which vanishes at 1.5. Convexity makes this a global minimum. Increasing the last reading to 200 leaves its score at minus one throughout the same interval and does not change the fitted location.

At \(x=1.5\) the four weights are \(2/3,1,1,1/18.5\). A weighted-average check confirms stationarity more transparently through the signed weighted residuals: \(+1,+0.5,-0.5,-1\) sum to zero. The final gross discrepancy is still 18.5 units. A robust fit limits how much that observation can move the fitted value; it does not explain why the instrument returned such a reading. Record it for investigation rather than replacing it silently by a reconstructed value.

For a physical model with differently scaled sensors, first divide discrepancies by appropriate instrument uncertainty scales or choose distinct physically defensible transition values. A single threshold measured in raw units can suppress every reading from one instrument merely because its unit is larger. Conversely an overly large threshold reduces to ordinary least squares over the whole observed residual range. Compare fits under plausible thresholds and investigate whether the prediction of interest changes, especially when a group of related sensors shares the same fault.

A gross error in an input coordinate is a further distinction. A calibration point at an extreme input can have large leverage through its Jacobian row even after its residual score has been capped. Thus the bounded score controls sensitivity to residual amplitude, while the geometry of the design still controls how much that bounded discrepancy can rotate or translate the final fit.

Chapter 12 · Inequalities and active constraints

Translate one-sided equilibrium into complementarity equations, identify an active set and verify both force and feasibility.

From first principles

Express one-sided contact as a complementarity condition

Consider a point constrained to have gap \(g\ge0\) above a frictionless rigid stop. A compressive reaction \(p\ge0\) can prevent penetration, but a separated stop exerts no force. These requirements give the scalar conditions

\[g\ge0,\]
\[p\ge0,\]
\[gp=0 .\]

They allow either an open gap with zero force or closed contact with a nonnegative force. To connect this with optimization, suppose the physical coordinate is the gap itself and the unconstrained stored energy is differentiable and convex. At a constrained minimum on \(g\ge0\), every feasible first-order change has nonnegative energy change. Thus the equilibrium derivative obeys

\[E'(g)=p,\]
\[p\ge0,\]
\[gE'(g)=0 .\]

In the interior one may move in both directions, forcing zero derivative. At the boundary only increases in gap are feasible, allowing positive derivative. The balance equation for a specific spring or load supplies the remaining relationship needed to determine the gap and force.

Derivation and applications

Quantify the gap introduced by smoothing a contact condition

Algebraic regularization can replace the sharp switch between an open and a closed contact with a differentiable approximation. First scale the gap and reaction into dimensionless variables \(g,p\), using stated positive reference scales. One exact residual is \(\sqrt{g^2+p^2}-g-p\): setting it to zero implies \(g+p\ge0\), and squaring gives \(gp=0\), which together force nonnegativity. Introduce a smoothing parameter \(\mu>0\),

\[\phi_\mu(g,p)=\sqrt{g^2+p^2+2\mu}-g-p .\]

At a zero, \(g+p>0\), and squaring gives \(gp=\mu\). Thus both quantities are positive even where the physical contact would be exactly open or closed. For a one-degree-of-freedom linear balance \(p=k(g-g_0)\) with positive scaled stiffness \(k\) and unloaded gap \(g_0\), solve the positive quadratic root:

\[k g(g-g_0)=\mu,\]
\[g_\mu=\frac{g_0+\sqrt{g_0^2+4\mu/k}}{2},\]
\[p_\mu=\frac{\mu}{g_\mu}.\]

For an exactly grazing load \(g_0=0\), the smoothing affects gap and force on square-root scales,

\[g_\mu=\sqrt{\mu/k},\]
\[p_\mu=\sqrt{k\mu}.\]

Far from this switching point the smaller of the two quantities decays linearly with \(\mu\). A small residual of the smoothed equation only proves that the regularized balance has been solved. The value of \(\mu\) and the physical reference scales determine the discrepancy from exact contact.

Worked example

Measure parasitic force across an open gap

Let \(k=4,\ g_0=0.50,\ \mu=0.010\). The exact unsmoothed balance is \(g=0.50,\ p=0\). The smoothed solution gives \(g_\mu=(0.50+\sqrt{0.26})/2=0.50495\), with \(p_\mu=0.01980\). Multiplication gives \(g_\mu p_\mu=0.0100\), an independent check on the source of this residual force.

Second application

Choose smoothing at a grazing contact

With \(k=9\) and dimensionless admissible gap error \(0.002\), a grazing contact requires \(\sqrt{\mu/9}\le0.002\). Thus \(\mu\le3.60\times10^{-5}\). At this upper value the induced reaction is \(0.0180\). If the admissible reaction is only \(0.009\), the stricter requirement is \(\mu\le0.009^2/9=9.0\times10^{-6}\).

A stop can push in only one direction

A constrained coordinate \(u\) must remain nonnegative and has an elastic energy \(E(u)=ku^2/2-fu\), \(k>0\). Minimising a differentiable energy on a half-line gives an interior stationary point when \(u>0\). At the endpoint zero, only nonnegative variations are possible, so the one-sided derivative must be nonnegative. Introduce the reaction \(w=ku-f\). These conditions read \(u\ge0,\ w\ge0,\ uw=0\). They express that at least one of gap and reaction vanishes. A similar rule applies componentwise to a vector of gaps coupled through a stiffness matrix.

The scalar solution is \(u=\max(f/k,0)\) and \(w=\max(-f,0)\). Increasing \(f\) through zero changes the slope of the load–displacement graph. The solution stays continuous while its derivative jumps. Near that change an unconstrained differentiable root solver can produce a negative gap or an inadmissible attractive reaction unless its equations include the inequalities.

Couple two potential contact points

Let \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(f=(-1,1)^T\), \(w=Ku-f\) and \(u_i\ge0\). The unconstrained equilibrium gives \(u=K^{-1}f=(-1/3,1/3)^T\), violating the first gap. Test the set with \(u_1=0\), \(w_2=0\). The second equation becomes \(2u_2-1=0\), hence \(u_2=1/2\). Recover the first reaction \(w_1=-1/2+1=1/2\). Every gap and reaction is now nonnegative and every product vanishes. Positive definiteness of \(K\) makes the quadratic energy strictly convex, so a feasible stationary state satisfying these inequalities is its unique minimum.

For a changed load \(f=(-0.4,1)^T\), the same assumed active set yields \(w_1=-0.1\), which is inadmissible. Release the first coordinate and solve \(Ku=f\). Using \(K^{-1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)/3\) gives \(u=(1/15,8/15)^T\), positive in both coordinates, with zero reactions. A small change of loading has switched the equations that determine the state.

A residual for switching equations

After compatible nondimensionalisation, \(\min(u_i,w_i)=0\) is equivalent to the three componentwise complementarity conditions. Where \(u_i<w_i\), linearising the minimum selects the gap equation; where \(w_i<u_i\), it selects the equilibrium equation. At equality the derivative is not unique, which explains the need for a switching rule or a nonsmooth Newton formulation. A product alone is insufficient: a negative gap and zero reaction give a zero product. Report the largest feasibility violation and a complementarity residual together. At the scalar switching load \(f=0\), both gap and reaction vanish and the active designation is ambiguous even though the state itself is unique.

Read the reaction as the price of a constraint

Return to a single gap with stiffness 2 and load −4. Its minimum over \(u\ge0\) occurs at zero with reaction 4. Now move the lower stop to \(u\ge d\) for a small positive \(d\). The constrained minimum becomes \(E(d)=d^2+4d\), whose derivative at zero is 4, precisely the original reaction. A small imposed displacement of the active stop requires work to first order. For a positive load producing a strictly open gap, the same small stop shift would initially have no effect and the reaction would be zero. These energy changes provide an independent check on the reaction sign.

Derive a discrete obstacle problem from virtual work

Consider a membrane represented by three interior displacements with fixed zero displacements at its ends. In scaled units the elastic energy is one half the sum of squared neighbouring differences, including the terms at both supports. Its stiffness matrix has diagonal two and neighbouring entries minus one. An obstacle at height \(g_j\) requires \(u_j\ge g_j\). Minimising \(E(u)=u^TKu/2-f^Tu\) over this convex feasible set gives a simple test involving every feasible variation: \((Ku-f)^T(v-u)\ge0\) for every feasible \(v\).

To see necessity, move a small fraction of the distance from \(u\) toward \(v\) and differentiate the energy at zero step. For sufficiency, the exact energy difference equals that linear term plus \((v-u)^TK(v-u)/2\), which is nonnegative and strictly positive for a nonzero displacement because both endpoints are fixed. At an interior free node both signs of variation are allowed, so its reaction vanishes. At an active obstacle only upward variations are feasible, so the signed reaction is nonnegative. These properties give complementarity between the gap \(u-g\) and the reaction \(Ku-f\).

Determine which part of an obstacle actually supplies force

Let \(g=(0,1,0)^T\), \(f=0\). Setting all displacements equal to the obstacle would give \(Ku=(-1,2,-1)^T\), with negative endpoint reactions at the first and third interior nodes. Those two active guesses are inadmissible. Keep only the middle node in contact and solve free-node equilibrium: \(2u_1-u_2=0\) and \(2u_3-u_2=0\). With \(u_2=1\) this yields \(u=(0.5,1,0.5)^T\), whose reactions are \((0,1,0)^T\). The end gaps are both positive and the central gap is zero, satisfying the complete optimality test.

The energy is half the sum of four squared slopes \(0.5,0.5,-0.5,-0.5\), hence 0.5. The candidate that hugs the entire obstacle has slopes \(0,1,-1,0\) and energy one. Lifting the two free nodes lets the membrane distribute curvature more economically while remaining above the obstacle. Independently the sum of the support reactions balances the unit central contact force, since each outer spring slope has magnitude 0.5 in these scaled units.

Apply an additional positive force \(f_2=t\) at the centre while keeping the obstacle fixed. The contact-state reaction becomes \(1-t\). Up to \(t=1\) the position stays fixed and the contact load decreases. For \(t>1\) the contact can no longer sustain a negative reaction; solving the unconstrained equations gives \(u=(t/2,t,t/2)^T\), now above every obstacle node. This piecewise response exhibits an observable change of slope in displacement at the contact transition. Reusing the old active set without checking the sign of its reaction would incorrectly predict an unchanging displacement for arbitrarily large lifting forces.

A force test and a gap test remain necessary even when the computed energy looks sensible. For example \(u=(0.4,1,0.4)^T\) is feasible and has energy only 0.52, close to the optimal 0.5. Yet its outer reactions are each −0.2 at strictly open gaps. The complementarity equations identify the reducible energy error even without knowing the exact energy minimum.

Problem sheet 6 · Misfit and one-sided equilibrium

Distinguish a stationary residual fit from an exact root and identify which unilateral constraints carry load.

Fit incompatible sensors

Two dimensionless residuals are \(r_1=x-2,\ r_2=2x-3\). Compute the exact least-squares state, both residuals and their dot product with the Jacobian. How many undamped Gauss–Newton steps suffice from zero?

Worked solution

The gradient is \((x-2)+2(2x-3)=5x-8\), giving \(x=1.6\). The residual is \((-0.4,0.2)^T\) and the dot product with \((1,2)^T\) is zero.

The residual is affine, so its linear model is exact everywhere. One step from zero produces 1.6 even though neither individual observation is exactly satisfied.

A stationary point with a poor model

For the scalar residual \(r=x^2-1\), evaluate the residual, gradient and curvature of \(\Phi=r^2/2\) at zero. Analyse the undamped Gauss–Newton linear least-squares subproblem there, and compare it with a trial step to \(x=0.1\).

Worked solution

At zero \(r=-1\), \(J=0\) and \(Jr=0\). Exact curvature \(J^2+rr''=-2\) identifies a strict local maximum.

The model \(r+Js=-1\) has the same size for every step, so cannot select a descent direction. Directly testing \(x=0.1\) gives \(\Phi=0.99^2/2=0.49005\), below 0.5. A small gradient alone cannot certify a minimum.

Locate the switching load

Use \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(f=(t,2)^T\), \(u,w\ge0\), \(w=Ku-f\), \(u_iw_i=0\). Assume the first gap is closed. Determine the range of \(t\) for which that assumption holds and solve the case \(t=0\).

Worked solution

With \(u_1=0\), the open second gap has \(2u_2=2\), so \(u_2=1\). The first reaction is \(-1-t\), nonnegative precisely for \(t\le-1\). At the threshold the closed coordinate has zero reaction.

At \(t=0\) release the first gap. Solving \(3u_1-u_2=0\), \(-u_1+2u_2=2\) gives \((0.4,1.2)^T\) with both reactions zero. The positive gaps verify the new active set.

Locate and follow the two equilibria of a fold

Consider equilibria of the dimensionless evolution u′ = λ − u². For λ = 0.04 find both roots, classify their stability, and estimate the movement of the positive root when λ increases by 0.002. Then find out why stepping in λ fails near zero and construct a continuation step through the fold.

Worked solution

The equilibria are u = ±0.2. A small perturbation obeys δu′ ≈ −2u δu, so +0.2 attracts while −0.2 repels. Both are perfectly valid zeros of the steady residual. A solver’s ability to converge to either root has no direct implication for dynamical stability.

Differentiating u²−λ = 0 gives du/dλ = 1/(2u). At the positive root the predicted change is +0.005. The exact change is √0.042 − 0.2 ≈ 0.00494. At the negative root the sign reverses. As the fold approaches, sensitivity diverges even though the equilibrium curve itself is the smooth parabola λ = u².

At (u,λ) = (0,0) choose the tangent along increasing u. An arclength predictor of length 0.05 gives (0.05,0). Keep the correction normal to that tangent by imposing u = 0.05 together with the residual. The corrected point is (0.05,0.0025). The two-equation Jacobian remains invertible there and at the fold. A parameter sweep fixing λ below zero would have no real point to correct to.

Applied study

Bracket a root and decide when a Newton step is reliable.

Why solve an equation inside a time step?

A thermal mass cools by radiation. Its temperature obeys \(\dot T=-\beta(T^4-T_a^4)\). For \(T>T_a\) the cooling rate decreases as the body cools. Backward Euler samples this rate at the end of the step. Calling the unknown end temperature \(x\), write the discrete energy balance as a root problem

\[F(x)=x-T_n+h\beta(x^4-T_a^4)=0.\]

Assume a positive time step \(h\), coefficient \(\beta>0\), and \(T_n>T_a>0\). The physical interval is \([T_a,T_n]\). Since \(F(T_a)=T_a-T_n<0\), \(F(T_n)>0\), and \(F\) is continuous, a root exists. Its derivative \(1+4h\beta x^3\) is positive throughout this interval, so the root is unique. These facts suggest an algorithm even before choosing a starting guess.

Trade guaranteed progress for local speed

Bisection evaluates \(F\) at the midpoint and keeps the half containing a sign change. Each iteration halves the interval width. The midpoint error is at most half the remaining width, even when derivatives are unavailable.

Newton’s method draws the tangent at \(x_j\), \(F(x_j)+F'(x_j)(x-x_j)\), and takes its zero \(x_{j+1}=x_j-F(x_j)/F'(x_j)\). Near a simple root \(r\), Taylor expansion about \(x_j\) gives \(x_{j+1}-r=[F''(\xi)/(2F'(x_j))](x_j-r)^2\). If the derivative stays away from zero and the second derivative is bounded, this explains local quadratic convergence. Farther from the root, a practical safeguard is to replace a tangent step that leaves the bracket with bisection.

Residual and temperature error have a particularly simple relation here. For an approximation \(x\) in the physical bracket, the mean value theorem gives \(|F(x)|=F'(\eta)|x-r|\geq|x-r|\). An absolute residual of \(10^{-6}\) K certifies a root error no greater than \(10^{-6}\) K for this scaled equation.

Follow two tangent steps

Set \(T_n=2\) K, \(T_a=1\) K, \(h=1\) s and \(\beta=0.0015\) K⁻³s⁻¹. This deliberately scaled thermal example keeps the arithmetic visible. Starting at \(x_0=2\), the residual is \(0.0015(16-1)=0.0225\) K and the slope is \(1+4(0.0015)2^3=1.048\). The first update gives \(x_1=1.97853053\) K and \(F(x_1)\simeq1.65\times10^{-5}\) K.

Re-evaluating the slope at \(x_1\) gives \(x_2=1.97851479\) K, with residual about \(8.7\times10^{-12}\) K before rounding the iterate for display. This is the root for one backward Euler step. It still differs from the continuous-time temperature at one second because the rate varied during that second.

Problem class

Budget the bisections

Start from \([1,2]\) K. After how many bracket halvings is the midpoint guaranteed to be within \(10^{-5}\) K of the root without evaluating a derivative?

Show the calculation

After \(n\) halvings, the width is \(2^{-n}\) K. Midpoint error is bounded by \(2^{-(n+1)}\) K. Thus \(n\geq\lceil\log_2(1/(2\times10^{-5}))\rceil=16\). Fifteen halvings still allow an error greater than the target.

Test a fixed-point iteration

Consider \(g(x)=2-h\beta(x^4-1)\). For \(h=1\) s and \(h=25\) s, does the maximum slope magnitude on \([1,2]\) certify contraction? What can a failed bound tell you?

Show the calculation

The maximum \(|g'|\) is \(4h\beta\,2^3=0.048h\) with \(h\) in seconds. Both maps take the bracket into itself: at one second the image is \([1.9775,2]\), at 25 seconds it is \([1.4375,2]\). At one second the maximum slope is 0.048, which certifies convergence from anywhere in the bracket. At 25 seconds the maximum is 1.2. The global contraction test is then inconclusive. Local convergence depends on the slope near the root.
