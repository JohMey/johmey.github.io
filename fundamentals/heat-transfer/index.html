How long does a metal part take to cool?

Balance stored thermal energy against heat loss to air and calculate the time to a target temperature.

Prerequisites Energy conservation and exponential decay.

Write an energy budget for a cooling part

A warm metal part is set on an insulating support in a room. It cools fastest at first. How long will it take to come within 10 K of the room temperature, and which properties of the part or the surrounding air determine that time?

Draw the system boundary around the part. Assume it has a nearly uniform temperature \(T(t)\), mass \(m\) and constant specific heat \(c_p\). Its total heat capacity is \(C=mc_p\) in J/K. A small temperature change \(dT\) changes its stored energy by \(C\,dT\). With constant surrounding temperature \(T_\infty\), convection carries heat out through exposed area \(A\) at rate \(hA(T-T_\infty)\). Neglect heat generation, radiation and conduction through the support. Energy conservation gives

\[C\,\frac{dT}{dt}=-hA(T-T_\infty).\]

For a body warmer than the room the right-hand side is negative. As the temperature difference shrinks, the heat loss per second decreases.

Find the time constant and check the uniform-temperature assumption

Set \(\theta=T-T_\infty\). For constant \(h\), separation of variables gives

\[d\theta/\theta=-(hA/C)\,dt.\]

Integration from the initial temperature yields

\[\frac{T(t)-T_\infty}{T_0-T_\infty}=e^{-t/\tau},\]
\[\tau=\frac{C}{hA}.\]

After one time constant the temperature difference is \(1/e\) of its initial value. A check on the single-temperature model is the Biot number \(Bi=h(V/A)/k\), where \(V\) is the body volume and \(k\) its conductivity. A value well below 0.1 means resistance to heat conduction inside the part is small compared with resistance at its surface. A poorly conducting body may have a cool surface while its centre stays warm.

Further study · MIT’s Transient Heat Transfer connects the uniform-temperature assumption to the ratio of internal and surface thermal resistances.

Cool from 350 K to 310 K

Take \(T_\infty=300\) K, \(C=900\) J/K, \(A=0.060\) m² and \(h=15\) W/(m²·K). The thermal conductance is \(hA=0.90\) W/K and \(\tau=900/0.90=1000\) s. The initial heat loss is

\[0.90(350-300)=45\,\mathrm{W}.\]

A target of 310 K leaves 10 K of the original 50 K temperature difference. Solve \(10/50=e^{-t/1000}\) to get \(t=1000\ln 5=1609\) s, or 26.8 min. During this interval the part releases \(900(350-310)=36\,000\) J. Dividing that energy by the initial 45 W would underestimate the time, because the heat loss has fallen to 9 W at the target.

Tutorial on cooling measurements

1. Infer a convection coefficient

A repeat experiment starts at 350 K in the same 300 K room. After 700 s the measured part temperature is 325 K. Estimate \(h\) using the given area and heat capacity. When should it reach 312.5 K under those conditions?

Solution to the cooling measurement

The excess temperature has halved, so

\[e^{-700/\tau}=1/2,\]
\[\tau=700/\ln 2=1010\,\mathrm{s}.\]

Thus \(h=C/(A\tau)=14.9\) W/(m²·K). A further half-life of 700 s takes the excess from 25 K to 12.5 K. The target occurs 1400 s after the start.

2. Decide whether a single thermometer is enough

Consider two samples with \(V/A=0.0050\) m exposed to \(h=15\) W/(m²·K). One has \(k=200\) W/(m·K), the other \(k=0.10\) W/(m·K). For which sample would a surface temperature be a reasonable estimate of the core temperature during cooling?

Solution to the temperature-gradient check

The Biot numbers are

\[15(0.0050)/200=0.000375,\]
\[15(0.0050)/0.10=0.75.\]

In the first sample conduction readily equalises internal temperatures. In the second an appreciable gradient can develop. A surface sensor can reach a chosen threshold while the core is still warmer, so a core measurement or spatial conduction model is needed.
