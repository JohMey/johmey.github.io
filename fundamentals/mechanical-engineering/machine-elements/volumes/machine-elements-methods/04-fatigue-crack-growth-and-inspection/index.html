Fatigue crack growth, optical inspection and the meaning of remaining life

A polished test coupon contains a measurable crack and experiences repeated tensile cycles. A stress–life curve for a polished, initially uncracked specimen cannot directly give the remaining growth interval from this measured state. Use calibrated crack-growth data to predict a laboratory observation and quantify what the inference depends on.

Connect load history to a crack-tip driving range

Outside a small process zone the leading elastic opening stress near an ideal crack tip scales like \(K_I/\sqrt{2\pi r}\). The dimension of \(K_I\) is stress times square root of length. In a geometrically similar family under remote uniaxial tension, linearity in stress and similarity in dimensions give

\[K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a},\]

where the dimensionless geometry factor depends on crack shape, relative crack length and loading. Define whether \(a\) denotes crack depth, half of a central crack, or an edge crack length before using a calibration.

Under constant-amplitude cycles at a fixed stress ratio, stable intermediate-rate measurements often obey the empirical Paris relation. With approximately constant \(Y\), separation of variables explains why successive millimetres of growth take different numbers of cycles:

\[\frac{da}{dN}=C(\Delta K)^m=C(Y\Delta\sigma\sqrt\pi)^m a^{m/2},\]
\[N=\frac{a_f^{1-m/2}-a_i^{1-m/2}}{C(Y\Delta\sigma\sqrt\pi)^m(1-m/2)}.\]

For exponent two the integral instead gives a logarithm. The units of the coefficient depend on both exponent and stress-intensity units. A coefficient expressed with MPa and metres cannot be inserted unchanged with Pa and millimetres. Use positive growth range and verify a physically increasing final crack length gives positive cycles. Near threshold, near fast fracture, at changing load ratio or in large-scale yielding the assumptions leading to this simple expression require reconsideration.

Predict two distinctly different stages of growth

Take an illustrative constant geometry factor 1.10, range 80 MPa, coefficient \(10^{-11}\) in metres per cycle per cubed MPa square-root metre, and exponent three. The driving range grows from 4.93 to 9.87 MPa square-root metres as the crack grows from 1 to 4 mm. Integration gives 833,350 cycles. The instantaneous growth rate at 4 mm is eight times the rate at 1 mm. Replacing the whole history by the initial rate would significantly overpredict the number of cycles. Using the final rate would err in the opposite direction.

At maximum tensile stress 120 MPa, the maximum stress intensity at 4 mm is 14.8 MPa square-root metres. A terminal criterion based on a known fracture toughness uses the maximum intensity while a growth calculation uses the range. Those quantities answer different experimental questions. Finite width can raise the geometry factor with increasing crack size. In that case integrate \(1/[C\Delta K(a)^m]\) on the measured geometry instead of treating the earlier constant value as universal. Refine the length increments until the predicted cycle count is stable compared with optical measurement uncertainty.

Link an optical length to a calibrated loading history

Use the same reference marks and magnification at each inspection and record loading cycles including interruptions. Surface observations describe a surface trace, which can grow differently from a subsurface front. Locally estimate growth rate from two nearby length readings divided by their cycle separation and assign a representative crack length within the interval. Widely spaced readings average a rapidly changing rate; too narrow a spacing lets registration error dominate the difference. Repeat the fit with both larger and smaller intervals and inspect residuals.

Compare the bonded-overlap stress study with a nominal uniform-stress assumption. Its smooth transfer field supplies loading information upstream of a crack-tip analysis.

Use the inverse crack-length plot to plan a coupon observation

Plot accumulated cycles vertically and crack length horizontally. The slope gives the number of additional cycles per increment of length, which falls as the crack grows. A flatter curve near the end therefore means faster crack growth. Increasing stress range compresses the entire vertical axis because the constant-amplitude Paris equation is separable. Changing only the initial crack size removes an especially slow portion of the growth history. Try a small change at the initial end and then the same absolute change at the final end; compare how strongly each affects predicted cycles.

Open the live experiment

On a real finite-width coupon the geometry factor can also change with length. That change alters the curvature of the graph and cannot generally be represented by rescaling the cycle axis. A lack of collapse after rescaling curves from two different loads may indicate a changing stress ratio, crack closure or a load-dependent material response. Recheck cycle counting and optical length units before selecting a more elaborate law.

Tutorial · Partition the predicted interval in half by crack length

For the coupon above estimate cycles spent between 1 and 2 mm and between 2 and 4 mm. Explain why the longer second length interval takes less time.

Compare the differences of reciprocal square roots

For exponent three the cycle count is proportional to \(a_i^{-1/2}-a_f^{-1/2}\). The first interval occupies about 58.58 percent of the total, or 488,200 cycles. The second takes about 345,200 cycles. As the stress-intensity range rises with square root of crack size, the cubed growth law accelerates faster than length itself. These fractions are independent of the common coefficient provided it truly stays the same. A rate plot offers a separate check: doubling length at constant stress increases the local rate by \(2^{3/2}\).

Tutorial · Determine sensitivity to one calibration error

Load calibration overestimates the true stress range by five percent. All other quantities are independently correct. Find the resulting error in the predicted growth interval, and compare with a ten-percent coefficient error.

Use the inverse growth-law dependence

Using the high stress gives a cycle count multiplied by \(1.05^{-3}=0.864\), about 13.6 percent low. Using a coefficient ten percent high gives a factor of \(1/1.10=0.909\), about 9.1 percent low. A stress calibration error can also bias the fitted coefficient if the same load cell supplied calibration data. Shared errors should then be propagated jointly rather than assumed independent. Agreement obtained using one calibration frame does not guarantee transfer to a second one.

Tutorial · Distinguish no detected crack from a zero-length crack

An instrument detects a reference flaw of 0.8 mm only intermittently. A student inserts zero as the initial size after an image returns no indication and reports an infinite life for exponent three. Explain the mathematical result and design a more informative coupon test.

Identify the extrapolation and its observational limit

The integral diverges at zero because the ideal long-crack power law predicts an arbitrarily small rate at vanishing crack length. The experiment supplied only a non-detection at finite resolution. Crack initiation, small-crack behaviour and detection probability lie outside the fit. Seed coupons with measured flaws spanning the optical threshold, take repeated coded images, and compare against a second length measurement. Report a detection curve and a conditional range of growth times for resolved starting sizes. That produces a testable inference without interpreting an undetected flaw as zero.
