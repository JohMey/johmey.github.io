    Contents

    1. Cam displacement laws, contact geometry and follower acceleration

      An inspection station uses a roller to lift a specimen for imaging, hold it and return it. Specifying a ten-millimetre rise determines neither the force on the guide nor whether the follower can follow the prescribed geometry. Construct two different motions through the same endpoints and separate their geometric and dynamic consequences.

    2. Helical gears, mesh forces and bearing reactions

      A motor supplies a known torque to a helical pinion. Select the free body carefully: torque fixes one component of the tooth load, the helix creates thrust, and the location of that thrust can alter bearing reactions even in a parallel-shaft transmission. Build the force diagram and reverse it from measured support loads.

    3. Sliding wear, real contact area and life inference from a coupon

      A sliding carriage develops measurable clearance while its normal force remains almost constant. Derive a practical volume-loss rule from microscopic contacts, estimate its coefficient from a mass trace, and test whether a single fitted value can be transferred to another load history. Geometry, material condition and instrument resolution enter at different stages.

    4. Fatigue crack growth, optical inspection and the meaning of remaining life

      A polished test coupon contains a measurable crack and experiences repeated tensile cycles. A stress–life curve for a polished, initially uncracked specimen cannot directly give the remaining growth interval from this measured state. Use calibrated crack-growth data to predict a laboratory observation and quantify what the inference depends on.
