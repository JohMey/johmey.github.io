Elastic curves and virtual work

Engineering mechanics

Predict a specified displacement from the bending-moment field and distinguish a stiffness limit from a stress limit.

From first principles

Why cantilever stiffness scales as the inverse cube of span

Take a straight slender beam clamped horizontally, with a downward end force \(P\). The empirical inputs are small-strain linear elasticity and the beam assumption that plane sections remain normal to the deflected centreline. Denote its centroidal bending rigidity by \(EI\). With constant section and modulus the stress moment of the stretched and compressed fibres is proportional to curvature. With downward deflection positive, equilibrium of a cut at \(x\) from the clamp supplies

\[M(x)=P(L-x),\]
\[w''(x)=M(x)/(EI).\]

Curvature is the rate at which slope changes. Integrating it once gives the slope, and integrating slope gives deflection. Combine both integrations into a weighted curvature integral: curvature occurring near the clamp rotates a longer remaining segment than curvature near the tip. The clamp fixes initial slope and displacement to zero, hence

\[w(L)=\int_0^L(L-x)w''(x)\,dx=\frac{P}{EI}\int_0^L(L-x)^2dx=\frac{PL^3}{3EI}.\]

This is a displacement prediction under small slopes, not just a stress limit. Its input \(M=EIw''\) is established from individual fibre strains and the neutral-axis condition in the detailed derivation. Short beams can also require shear deflection.

Background · Second moment of area
\[I=\int_A y^2\,dA\]

Measure the transverse coordinate from the stated bending axis. This section property has units of length to the fourth power and enters the flexural rigidity.

Derivation and applications

From the extension of a fibre to cantilever curvature and displacement

Why does section depth enter a bending calculation through a second moment of area? Take a straight prismatic cantilever with small slopes, uniform Young’s modulus \(E\), linear elastic response and slender dimensions. Euler–Bernoulli kinematics assumes that cross-sections remain plane and normal to the deflected centreline. Measure \(x\) from the clamp and transverse deflection \(w\) downward. Measure cross-section coordinate \(y\) downward from an initially arbitrary reference line. Sections separated by \(dx\) rotate relative to one another by \(d\theta\); a fibre at \(y\) shortens relative to the reference line by \(y\,d\theta\).

\[\epsilon_x(y)=\epsilon_0-y\,d\theta/dx\simeq\epsilon_0-yw''(x),\]
\[\sigma_x=E\epsilon_x .\]

With no imposed axial resultant, the integral of this normal stress must vanish. Writing \(\bar y=A^{-1}\int_A y\,dA\), we obtain the strain of the reference line, and thereby identify the zero-strain fibre:

\[0=N=\int_A E(\epsilon_0-yw'')\,dA,\]
\[\epsilon_0=\bar y w'',\]
\[\sigma_x=-E(y-\bar y)w''.\]

Define bending moment positive for this downward curvature. Each fibre contributes its force times its lever arm about the neutral axis. Integrating introduces the centroidal second moment \(I\):

\[M=-\int_A(y-\bar y)\sigma_x\,dA=EIw'',\]
\[I=\int_A(y-\bar y)^2\,dA.\]

A downward tip load \(P\) gives \(M(x)=P(L-x)\) by equilibrium of the portion beyond the cut. For constant \(EI\), integrate twice and enforce the actual clamp conditions \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\):

\[EI w'=P(Lx-x^2/2),\]
\[EI w=P(Lx^2/2-x^3/6),\]
\[w(L)=PL^3/(3EI).\]

The slope and displacement constants follow from compatibility at the support. The zero moment at the free tip follows independently from the load on the right-hand portion. Plane sections can also remain plane under pure bending of a short specimen, but shear deformation near loads and supports can matter in short cantilevers.

When a beam is strong enough but too flexible

A tool rail may stay below its elastic limit while its cutting head moves outside a position tolerance. The maximum bending stress depends on the largest moment at one section. Deflection accumulates the effect of curvature over a length. A displacement sensor near an unloaded end can therefore move even if the nearby bending moment is zero. To predict it, identify both the point and direction of the required displacement.

Consider a straight, slender Euler–Bernoulli beam. Cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the deformed centreline. Take \(w\) positive upward and \(M\) positive in sagging. For small slopes the signed curvature is \(w''\), with \(\kappa=M/(EI)\). Integrate once to find the slope and once more to find the displacement. Each constant of integration has physical meaning. A clamp fixes displacement and rotation. A simple support fixes its vertical displacement while permitting rotation. At an ordinary join without a hinge, both displacement and slope are continuous.

Work through a tip reading

Clamp a beam at \(x=0\). Apply a downward point load \(P\) at \(x=a\) on a beam of total length \(L>a\). The signed moment in \(0<x<a\) is \(-P(a-x)\). For \(x>a\) the free segment has no external force and no moment. Taking constant stiffness and using the clamp conditions gives

\[EI w'(x)=-P(ax-x^2/2),\]
\[EI w(x)=-P(ax^2/2-x^3/6),\quad 0\le x\le a.\]

At the load point the displacement is \(-Pa^3/(3EI)\) and the slope is \(-Pa^2/(2EI)\). The free extension beyond it is straight but tilted. Use those values as initial conditions for the unloaded segment:

\[w(L)=w(a)+(L-a)w'(a)=-Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI).\]

This expression correctly vanishes when the force is applied at the clamp and reduces to the standard end-load expression when \(a=L\).

Calculate both position and slope

Let \(EI=8400\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}\), \(L=1.20\) m, \(a=0.80\) m and \(P=100\) N. Under the load the downward displacement is 2.03 mm and the rotation magnitude is

\[100(0.80)^2/[2(8400)]=0.00381\,\mathrm{rad}.\]

The 0.40 m overhang contributes another 1.52 mm at the tip, for a total downward reading of 3.56 mm. The slope is small compared with a radian, consistent with the linear curvature assumption. A gauge at the load and one at the tip would give different stiffness estimates if their positions were omitted.

Obtain one displacement directly

There is another way to compute a single sensor reading. Introduce a fictitious force \(Q\) at the sensor in its desired positive direction. The moment becomes \(M_P+Qm\), where \(m\) is the moment per unit sensor force. The bending energy is

\[U=\int(M_P+Qm)^2/(2EI)\,dx.\]

Differentiating with respect to \(Q\) gives the displacement conjugate to that force. Set \(Q=0\) afterwards to recover the original beam.

\[\delta=\int_0^L\frac{M_P(x)m(x)}{E(x)I(x)}\,dx.\]

For a downward unit force at the free tip, \(m=-(L-x)\). Only the portion up to \(a\) contributes because \(M_P=0\) further along. The integral gives \(Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI)\), the positive downward reading obtained above. This form is especially useful for stepped sections: split the integral where either stiffness or loading changes. Its positive self-flexibility for a load and a sensor pointing the same way is a useful sign check.

Exchange the load and the gauge

The unit-load formula supplies a test that can be made on the same beam without knowing its exact stiffness distribution. Put the same 100 N force at the tip and move the displacement sensor to \(x=0.80\) m. The new integral has exactly the previous pair of moment fields with their roles exchanged. Since their product is unchanged, the new reading is also 3.56 mm downwards. This Maxwell–Betti reciprocity applies to these work-conjugate forces and displacements in a linear elastic structure with unchanged restraints. A slipping clamp, a support that lifts off or motion large enough to change the load geometry can cause discrepancies between reciprocal tests. Swapping load and sensor positions is therefore also a practical check of the assumed support behaviour.

Quantify the deformation omitted by a bending-only model

The straight-normal assumption of Euler–Bernoulli theory omits transverse shear distortion. For a slender beam this is often small, but for a deep beam or a material with a small effective shear modulus it can dominate a displacement measurement. In a simple Timoshenko description let \(\kappa_s GA\) be the effective shear stiffness. The dimensionless shear correction factor \(\kappa_s\) accounts for the nonuniform cross-sectional shear field when a single transverse shear resultant \(V\) is used. For the illustration use \(\kappa_s=5/6\) for a homogeneous rectangular section.

For an end-loaded cantilever the bending moment magnitude is \(P(L-x)\) and the shear force magnitude is \(P\) throughout the span. Add the elastic shear energy to the bending energy. Differentiating with respect to the end force yields the total deflection under that force:

\[U=\int_0^L\left[\frac{P^2(L-x)^2}{2EI}+\frac{P^2}{2\kappa_sGA}\right]dx,\]
\[\delta=\frac{PL^3}{3EI}+\frac{PL}{\kappa_sGA}.\]

The shear contribution grows linearly with span while the bending contribution grows cubically. Their ratio is \(3EI/(\kappa_s GA L^2)\). Writing \(I/A=h^2/12\) for a rectangle gives \([E/(4\kappa_sG)](h/L)^2\). This form identifies both the geometric slenderness and material stiffness ratio required for a sensible model choice. A fixed rule based on span-to-depth alone can be poor for a sandwich section with a compliant core.

Interpret a short cantilever stiffness test

An aluminium fixture projects 120 mm from a rigid clamp. Its cross-section is 20 mm wide and 40 mm deep in the bending direction. Take \(E=70{,}000\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(G=26{,}000\,\mathrm{MPa}\), and an end load of 500 N. The area is 800 mm² and the bending second moment is

\[20(40)^3/12=106667\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

In consistent N–mm units the bending deflection is

\[500(120)^3/[3(70000)(106667)]=0.0386\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The shear deflection is

\[500(120)/[(5/6)(26000)(800)]=0.00346\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

A dial indicator would measure about 0.0420 mm under these ideal assumptions.

Fitting a bending-only formula to this reading suggests an apparent modulus of about \(70(0.0386/0.0420)=64.2\,\mathrm{GPa}\). The discrepancy arises from omitted shear deformation despite a perfectly elastic material. Repeat the test at twice the overhang while keeping the force unchanged: bending deflection rises by eight, shear deflection by two. The fractional shear error falls to one quarter of its original value. Measuring several spans can therefore separate an incorrect modulus from a shear correction. Clamp compliance is another candidate in a real experiment. A clamp rotation produces a term with a different span dependence, and a gauge mounted close to the fixture can reveal that motion independently.

For equal applied loads at spans \(L\) and \(2L\), express measured compliances as \(C_1=B+S\) and \(C_2=8B+2S\), where \(B\) and \(S\) are the original bending and shear contributions per unit load. Then \(B=(C_2-2C_1)/6\) and \(S=C_1-B\). A negative inferred \(S\) signals measurement uncertainty or an omitted boundary deformation, since the assumed elastic shear energy must be positive. This inversion makes the two-span proposal a quantitative model test.

Taking three spans instead of two permits a residual check, because the third measured displacement is then predicted without adjusting either fitted contribution.

Stress transformation and combined loading

Engineering mechanics

Find principal stresses and elastic yield measures at a shaft surface carrying bending and torsion.

From first principles

Recover principal normal stresses from traction balance

For a small material tetrahedron, equilibrium of the tractions on its faces gives Cauchy’s relation \(\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\). In a classical continuum without body couples, local moment balance makes \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) symmetric. In plane stress select a section normal \(\mathbf n=(n_x,n_y)\) in the sheet. A principal section carries traction parallel to its normal, so it solves \(\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n=\lambda\mathbf n\). A nonzero normal exists only if

\[(\sigma_x-\lambda)(\sigma_y-\lambda)-\tau_{xy}^2=0 .\]

Solve the quadratic by completing the square about the mean normal stress. This gives both principal values:

\[\sigma_{1,2}=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}.\]

The associated real eigenvectors are orthogonal when the values differ. For a pure shear, the principal stresses are equal and opposite even though normal stresses on the original coordinate cuts are zero. For a traction-free sheet the third principal value is zero; this third direction must be included when applying a three-dimensional yield criterion.

Derivation and applications

Find the plane carrying the largest shear traction

A tensile test measures a normal stress on one specified cut. In a machine part another cut through the same point can carry shear even when the original axes coincide with the loading. Assume plane stress: the free faces normal to \(z\) have zero traction and the nonzero components are \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy}\). Choose an in-plane unit normal \(\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) and tangent \(\mathbf s=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\). Cauchy’s traction relation gives the force per actual area on that cut:

\[\mathbf t=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n=(\sigma_x\cos\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin\theta,\ \tau_{xy}\cos\theta+\sigma_y\sin\theta).\]

Project onto the normal and tangent. Introduce \(m=(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)/2\) and \(d=(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)/2\). The double-angle identities rearrange the two projections into a rotation:

\[\sigma_n-m=d\cos2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta,\]
\[\tau_{ns}=-d\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta.\]

Squaring and adding cancels the cross terms. This supplies both the in-plane principal stresses and the range of shear traction:

\[(\sigma_n-m)^2+\tau_{ns}^2=d^2+\tau_{xy}^2=R^2,\]
\[\sigma_{1,2}=m\pm R,\]
\[|\tau_{ns}|_{\max}=R .\]

Planes of largest in-plane shear are rotated \(45^\circ\) from principal planes. The three-dimensional maximum also includes planes involving the out-of-plane principal stress, which equals zero under plane stress. That distinction matters whenever both in-plane principal stresses have the same sign.

Worked example

Resolve the stress on a particular cut

Take \((\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy})=(90,30,40)\) MPa. The centre and radius are \(60\) and \(50\) MPa, giving in-plane principal stresses \(110\) and \(10\) MPa. On a cut with normal at \(45^\circ\), the projections give \(\sigma_n=100\) MPa and \(\tau_{ns}=-30\) MPa. Their point lies on the circle because \(40^2+30^2=50^2\). Including the zero out-of-plane stress gives absolute maximum shear \(55\) MPa.

A section resultant does not specify a failure plane

The free-body diagram of a shaft gives bending moments and torque. A potential crack, however, can cut the shaft on any plane. To compare the local stress with a material test, first evaluate the stress components at the same material point. At the outer fibre of a circular shaft the bending normal stress may be large. At the neutral axis the bending stress vanishes. Values from different points cannot be combined into one stress state.

For a circular shaft of diameter \(d\) bending moment \(M\) gives surface stress \(32M/(\pi d^3)\), and torque \(T\) gives surface shear \(16T/(\pi d^3)\). The torsion relation uses \(J=\pi d^4/32\). The combination assumes elastic small deformation in a smooth prismatic portion, sufficiently far from shoulders, load introduction and keyways. At a traction-free outer surface the radial traction is zero. Use a local basis with longitudinal and circumferential axes for the remaining plane-stress components.

Change the orientation of a cut

In two dimensions write the stress tensor with normal components \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y\) and shear \(\tau_{xy}\). The traction vector on a cut with unit normal \(\mathbf n\) is \(\mathbf t=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\). Project that vector along and perpendicular to \(\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). The normal stress becomes a constant average plus a sinusoidal term; the shear varies with twice the same angle:

\[\sigma_n=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta.\]

The largest and smallest in-plane normal stresses differ from the mean by \(R=\sqrt{[(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)/2]^2+\tau_{xy}^2}\). The shear on those principal planes vanishes. Taking the third principal stress as zero allows a three-dimensional maximum-shear check: evaluate half the difference of the largest and smallest of all three principal stresses. In a biaxial tensile state, ignoring the zero third stress can underestimate this difference.

Compare principal stresses on a machine shaft

Let a 40 mm diameter steel shaft carry \(M=300\) N·m and \(T=200\) N·m. At its tensile bending surface, \(\sigma_x=47.75\) MPa, \(\sigma_y=0\) and \(\tau_{xy}=15.92\) MPa. The in-plane mean is 23.87 MPa and the radius is 28.69 MPa. The principal stresses are about 52.57 and −4.82 MPa. The plane of greatest tension is rotated through half of \(\operatorname{atan2}(2\tau_{xy},\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\), here 16.8° from the original plane normal. The maximum shear magnitude is 28.7 MPa.

A useful check is the trace: the principal stresses sum to 47.75 MPa, the same sum as the original normal components. A second check is the determinant: the two principal values have opposite signs since the determinant of this particular matrix is \(-\tau_{xy}^2\). A picture with the two directions marked prevents confusing the angle of a plane normal with that of the line drawn for the plane itself.

Interpret an equivalent stress

For isotropic ductile materials a common onset-of-yield model uses von Mises equivalent stress. Under plane stress it is \(\sqrt{\sigma_x^2-\sigma_x\sigma_y+\sigma_y^2+3\tau_{xy}^2}\), giving 55.1 MPa here. Compare this scalar with the uniaxial yield strength from a material test under a comparable temperature and loading rate. A given yield strength of 220 MPa corresponds to a nominal static factor of about 4.0. That number answers a defined static question. A shaft rotating under a stationary transverse force cycles the tensile and compressive stress of a given material point once per revolution. Evaluating its fatigue resistance requires the stress range and a separate material model.

Change the mean stress without changing the distortion

Apply a uniform external hydrostatic pressure of 50 MPa to a material point whose original principal stresses are 100, 0 and 0 MPa. The new principal stresses are 50, −50 and −50 MPa, since pressure acts on every plane. Their pairwise differences remain 100, 0 and −100 MPa, so the von Mises stress remains 100 MPa. This illustrates why a distortion-based yield condition must include all three normal directions under confinement. Applying a plane-stress formula after adding pressure only to the in-plane directions would represent a different three-dimensional loading.

Derive the two membrane stresses of a thin cylinder

A hydraulic accumulator behaves differently in its axial and circumferential directions even if it is made of isotropic steel. Consider a long cylindrical shell of mean radius \(r\), wall thickness \(t\ll r\), closed ends and internal gauge pressure \(p\). Away from nozzles and end transitions, symmetry permits a membrane model with tensile stresses uniform through the thickness. The radial stress changes from approximately \(-p\) at the inner wall to zero outside. Its magnitude is small compared with \(pr/t\) if the shell is thin.

Cut the cylinder longitudinally into two halves of length \(L\). The net force from pressure acts on the projected rectangle of area \(2rL\), not on the curved wetted area. The two cut edges carry hoop tension. A different cut across the full circular end isolates the cap thrust and the single axial membrane stress. The resulting balances are

\[2\sigma_\theta tL=p(2rL),\]
\[2\pi rt\sigma_z=p\pi r^2,\]
\[\sigma_\theta=pr/t,\]
\[\sigma_z=pr/(2t).\]

The factor of two follows from geometry and end load transmission. If the ends are open and pressure thrust returns through a separate frame, the cylinder may carry little axial membrane force. Always follow the cap force into the structure before assigning axial stress. A cap anchored externally and a welded pressure shell have different load paths even at the same gauge pressure.

Infer shell strain and the effect of axial restraint

For a closed vessel with \(r=0.20\,\mathrm m\), \(t=4.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(p=1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}\), the membrane stresses are 60 MPa circumferentially and 30 MPa axially. These directions are principal at an unperturbed surface. Under plane stress the von Mises stress is

\[\sqrt{60^2+30^2-60(30)}=52.0\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

With \(E=200\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and \(\nu=0.30\), an outer hoop gauge reads \((60-0.30\times30)/200000=255\) microstrain and an axial gauge reads only \((30-0.30\times60)/200000=60\) microstrain. The radius increases by about \(0.20(255\times10^{-6})=51\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The smaller axial signal is particularly sensitive to temperature correction.

Now imagine an axial restraint strong enough to hold the length constant without affecting the radius directly. The condition \(\varepsilon_z=0\) gives

\[\sigma_z=\nu\sigma_\theta=18\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

The cap thrust has not disappeared. An external axial reaction must remove the difference of 12 MPa integrated over the shell area \(2\pi rt\), about 60.3 kN. A gauge-based interpretation using the unrestrained 30 MPa estimate would miss this load. Conversely, where the restraint attaches, local bending and stress concentration may dominate the smooth membrane solution. Locate comparison gauges several characteristic wall dimensions away from such load introduction.

The force calculation can be audited directly. Pressure thrust on the end cap is \(1.2\pi(200)^2=151\,\mathrm{kN}\) in MPa and millimetres. An 18 MPa shell stress carries

\[18[2\pi(200)(4)]=90.5\,\mathrm{kN}.\]

Their difference, 60.3 kN, is precisely the required external load path. A restraint instrumented with a load cell would test the assumed axial boundary condition independently of the strain readings.

Extend the traction balance to three dimensions

In three dimensions a cut normal has three components. Cauchy’s relation remains \(\mathbf t=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\). Project onto the unit normal to obtain signed normal stress, then remove that projection to obtain the shear traction. Rotating a diagram does not rotate the tensor physically; changing its components does.

\[\sigma_n=\mathbf n^T\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n,\]
\[\mathbf t_s=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n-\sigma_n\mathbf n.\]

The eigenvectors give principal cut directions with zero shear traction. The following notebook evaluates the symmetric eigensystem and independently verifies both the tensor reconstruction and the orthogonality of each shear traction to its cut normal.

"""Compute principal stresses with OneSimulation and show Cauchy traction in 3D."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from onesim.numerics.linear.matrices.dense_linear_algebra import symmetric_eigensystem

# Symmetric Cauchy tensor in MPa; edit the entries to reproduce the browser controls.
sigma=np.array([[80.,30.,15.],[30.,20.,0.],[15.,0.,-30.]])
principal, axes = symmetric_eigensystem(sigma)
assert np.allclose(axes@np.diag(principal)@axes.T,sigma)
assert np.allclose(axes.T@axes,np.eye(3))
print('Principal stresses [MPa]:',np.round(principal,5))
print('Absolute maximum shear [MPa]:',round((principal[-1]-principal[0])/2,5))
phi=np.linspace(0,2*np.pi,12,endpoint=False)
theta=np.linspace(.25,np.pi-.25,6)
n=np.array([[np.sin(t)*np.cos(p),np.sin(t)*np.sin(p),np.cos(t)] for t in theta for p in phi])
traction=n@sigma.T
normal=np.sum(n*traction,axis=1)
shear=traction-normal[:,None]*n
assert np.max(abs(np.sum(n*shear,axis=1)))<1e-12
assert normal.min()>=principal[0]-1e-10 and normal.max()<=principal[-1]+1e-10
fig=plt.figure(figsize=(9,4.2),layout='constrained');ax=fig.add_subplot(121,projection='3d')
ax.quiver(*n.T,*(traction/120).T,length=.5,normalize=False,color='#19596a',linewidth=.7)
ax.scatter(*n.T,c=normal,cmap='coolwarm',s=12,vmin=-100,vmax=100)
for j in range(3):ax.plot(*np.array([-axes[:,j],axes[:,j]]).T,label=f'{principal[j]:.1f} MPa')
ax.set(xlabel='Normal x',ylabel='Normal y',zlabel='Normal z',title='Traction vectors on unit-normal sphere')
ax.set_box_aspect((1,1,1));ax.legend(fontsize=7)

Torsional stiffness of shafts and closed sections

Engineering mechanics

Derive torque–twist relations and explain the large difference in stiffness between an intact tube and an open strip.

From first principles

Derive the torque–twist relation from local shear deformation

Consider a straight circular shaft in small elastic torsion. In Saint-Venant torsion of a circular section, each cross-section can rotate as a rigid plane relative to the next. Let its rotation be \(\phi(x)\) and take two sections separated by \(dx\). At radius \(r\) their relative tangential displacement is \(r\,d\phi\). Dividing by the axial spacing gives engineering shear strain and, through the measured shear modulus \(G\), shear stress:

\[\gamma_{x\theta}=r\,d\phi/dx,\]
\[\tau_{x\theta}=Gr\,d\phi/dx .\]

Each patch of area \(dA\) carries tangential force \(\tau\,dA\) with moment arm \(r\). Sum these moments over the section and integrate the resulting twist rate along the shaft:

\[T=\int_A r\tau\,dA=GJ\phi',\]
\[J=\int_A r^2\,dA,\]
\[\Delta\phi=\int \frac{T(x)}{G(x)J(x)}dx .\]

For a homogeneous solid circular shaft \(J=\pi R^4/2\), so doubling radius strongly increases torsional stiffness. Noncircular sections generally warp and their torsion constant need not equal this polar area moment. The circular-shaft kinematics should not be used unchanged for an open thin-walled section.

Derivation and applications

How two bonded shaft materials share one applied torque

Two shafts in series carry the same torque; a bonded core and sleeve instead undergo the same twist per length. Consider a circular core of radius \(a\) and shear modulus \(G_c\), bonded to a concentric sleeve with outer radius \(b\) and modulus \(G_s\). Use a long prismatic region away from load introduction, small elastic deformations, perfect bond and no interfacial slip. Let \(\phi(x)\) describe section rotation. Two cross-sections separated by \(dx\) acquire a relative tangential motion \(r\,d\phi\) at radius \(r\):

\[\gamma_{x\theta}=r\phi',\]
\[\tau_i(r)=G_i r\phi' .\]

Compatibility makes \(\phi'\) common. The shear stress may change at the material boundary because the moduli differ. The torque contribution from a thin annulus is its area \(2\pi r\,dr\) times shear stress times radius. Integrating in each material gives

\[T_c=G_cJ_c\phi',\]
\[T_s=G_sJ_s\phi',\]
\[J_c=\frac{\pi a^4}{2},\]
\[J_s=\frac{\pi(b^4-a^4)}{2}.\]

The applied torque \(T\) is their sum. Solving first for the common twist and then for the load split yields

\[\phi'=\frac{T}{G_cJ_c+G_sJ_s},\]
\[\frac{T_c}{T}=\frac{G_cJ_c}{G_cJ_c+G_sJ_s}.\]

The denominator has units of N m². Setting both shear moduli equal recovers the homogeneous solid shaft independently of the arbitrary division at \(a\). If the sleeve is unbonded and torque is applied only to the core, equal twist is no longer a valid compatibility condition.

Their stresses at \(a\) are \(15.1\) and \(5.68\) MPa respectively despite equal shear strain there.

How much torque does the sleeve actually carry?

A steel core and an aluminium sleeve extend the full length of a circular shaft. They are bonded along their cylindrical interface, and the end fittings transmit the resultant torque to the complete section. A student dividing the torque in proportion to cross-sectional area obtains a plausible answer that misses both lever arm and material stiffness. We can reconstruct the division from the displacement at the interface before writing any torque balance.

Let \(a\) denote the core radius and \(b\) the outside radius. A cross section rotating by angle \(\varphi(x)\) moves a point at radius \(r\) tangentially by \(r\varphi\). Over a short axial interval the resulting engineering shear strain is \(\gamma_{x\theta}=r\,d\varphi/dx\). Bonding requires a common twist per length \(\varphi'\) for the two materials. The shear on a cross-sectional end face is \(G r\varphi'\), so it can jump at \(r=a\) even though the strain there is continuous. It is the traction on a radial-normal interface that must equilibrate on opposite sides of the bond. Confusing it with the traction on the shaft end face would wrongly force the plotted shear stresses to coincide.

The torque on an annular element is the shear times its area times its lever arm. Integration of \(r(Gr\varphi')(2\pi r\,dr)\) gives

\[T=[G_cJ_c+G_sJ_s]\varphi',\]
\[J_c=\pi a^4/2,\]
\[J_s=\pi(b^4-a^4)/2.\]

Use \(a=12\) mm, \(b=20\) mm, \(G_c=79\) GPa and \(G_s=27\) GPa. Under 100 N m the core carries 30.35 N m and the sleeve 69.65 N m. A 600 mm length rotates by 0.405 degrees. Immediately inside the interface the end-face shear is 11.18 MPa. On the other side of that same radius the lower modulus reduces it to 3.82 MPa; continuing outward increases it again. An accurate shaft twist cannot by itself identify the local peak stress unless the section and material distribution are known.

In the notebook, integrate the stress directly over the core and sleeve and compare the total with the imposed torque. Evaluate the two integrals separately so a numerical trapezoid does not smear the material jump. Then make the two moduli equal: the end-face shear curve becomes straight across the entire section, while the fraction of torque in the core equals \((a/b)^4\). This limit offers an independent check against a uniform solid shaft.

A separate way to check the torque split uses stored energy. In a unit length of shaft the strain energy density is \(\tau\gamma/2\). Integrating it over each annulus gives \(GJ(\varphi')^2/2\) for that material. Because the twist is common, the energy fractions equal the torque fractions. For the example the total strain energy is \(T\varphi/2\), about 0.35 J over 600 mm. If the two materials were joined end to end, the applied torque would be common and the angles of twist would add. The continuous cylindrical bond is the feature that selects the parallel arrangement used here.

Rotate two adjacent sections

A torque meter measures twist between two stations on a shaft. To infer torque, first ask how material between the stations deforms. A straight circular shaft in uniform, small-deformation torsion has cross-sections that rotate as rigid discs. Two discs separated by \(dx\) rotate relative to one another by \(d\theta\). A fibre at radius \(r\) moves sideways by \(r\,d\theta\), giving shear strain \(\gamma=r\,d\theta/dx\). With elastic shear modulus \(G\), the shear stress increases linearly from the centre to the surface.

Each area element transmits a torque \(r\tau\,dA\). Integrating produces the polar second moment of area \(J\), whose dimensions are length to the fourth power. With constant torque and cross-section, integration along the shaft then converts twist per length into total relative angle.

\[T=\int_A r\tau\,dA=G\theta'\int_A r^2dA,\]
\[J=\frac{\pi}{2}(r_o^4-r_i^4),\]
\[\theta=\frac{TL}{GJ}.\]

Follow a torque through a stepped shaft

A 0.75 m aluminium tube of outer radius 15 mm and inner radius 12 mm transmits 45 N m. For \(G=26\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(J=4.695\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^4}\). Its twist is

\[45(0.75)/(26\times10^9J)=0.02765\,\mathrm{rad}=1.58^\circ.\]

Surface shear is

\[45(0.015)/J=14.4\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

The inside surface still carries 80% of this stress. Drilling a central hole removes the least highly stressed material first.

Twist can also identify an unknown joint compliance. If an instrument measures 2.00° across this tube plus its end couplings, and all parts carry the same torque, the couplings contribute about 0.42°. Their combined torsional compliance is approximately \(0.42\pi/[180(45)]=1.6\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/(N\,m)}\). Such a comparison assumes that the gauges do not include a second shaft segment and that the torque is transmitted without slip.

Carry torque around a single closed cell

For a long, thin-walled closed tube with a single cell, use the shear flow \(q=\tau t\), the force per unit length of wall. Longitudinal equilibrium makes this flow approximately constant around the perimeter if no intermediate longitudinal force is introduced. A length \(ds\) carries tangential force \(q\,ds\). Its moment about an arbitrary origin is \(q\) times twice the triangular area swept by the radius vector. Around the median wall contour this gives

\[T=2A_mq.\]

For a rectangular cell with median dimensions 80 by 40 mm, a torque of 320 N m gives

\[q=320000/[2(80)(40)]=50\,\mathrm{N/mm}.\]

A 2 mm wall carries 25 MPa while a 4 mm wall carries 12.5 MPa. A slender longitudinal slit interrupts the closed path. The constant circulating flow solution then ceases to apply. A seam that reconnects the two lips must transmit the longitudinal shear flow if the component is to recover its closed-section behaviour.

Distinguish two section properties

For a noncircular closed tube, the polar area moment and torsion constant generally differ. Compatibility around a single thin-walled cell gives

\[\theta'=T\oint ds/(Gt)/(4A_m^2).\]

Making only a short wall segment very thin substantially increases this integral. A wall segment can therefore control angle of twist even when equilibrium fixes the same shear flow everywhere around the cell.

Determine torque sharing between two closed cells

A single closed cell supports one constant circulating shear flow. In an aircraft-style box or a partitioned machine boom, adjacent cells share a web. Torque balance alone cannot tell how much flow each cell carries. The missing equation is twist compatibility: because the cells are bonded along the common web, their twist per unit length must agree. Assume a long, thin-walled prismatic box of one material carrying Saint-Venant torsion with negligible distortion. Use median-line areas and length-to-thickness ratios throughout.

Assign positive clockwise circulations \(q_1,q_2\) to both cells. They oppose each other on the common web, where physical flow becomes \(q_1-q_2\) measured in the first cell’s direction. Let \(S_1,S_2\) denote \(\int ds/t\) over the exterior walls of each cell and \(S_w\) that quantity along their common web. The torque and the two contour compatibility relations become

\[T=2A_1q_1+2A_2q_2,\]
\[2A_1G\theta'=(S_1+S_w)q_1-S_wq_2,\]
\[2A_2G\theta'=(S_2+S_w)q_2-S_wq_1 .\]

These equations express the same shear-strain compatibility that leads to \(J_t=4A_m^2/\oint ds/t\) for one cell. If both cells and exterior wall thicknesses are mirror images, symmetry requires equal circulations and zero common-web flow under pure torque. The common web may still carry transverse shear from a separate load case. A missing web flow under pure torque is therefore a property of that loading and symmetry.

Compute the stress in an internal dividing web

Take adjacent median rectangles 40 mm high, 60 and 30 mm wide, with every wall 2 mm thick. Then

\[A_1=2400,\ A_2=1200\,\mathrm{mm^2}.\]

Their three exterior walls give \(S_1=(60+40+60)/2=80\) and \(S_2=(30+40+30)/2=50\), while \(S_w=20\). Equal twist requires \((100q_1-20q_2)/2400=(70q_2-20q_1)/1200\), giving \(140q_1=160q_2\). Thus \(q_2=0.875q_1\), larger than one might expect from the smaller area alone.

Applying \(T=345\,\mathrm{N\,m}=345000\,\mathrm{N\,mm}\) gives \(345000=4800q_1+2400(0.875)q_1\), so \(q_1=50.0\) and \(q_2=43.75\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). The dividing web carries only 6.25 N/mm, or 3.13 MPa. The primary exterior walls carry 25.0 and 21.9 MPa. Using \(G=27000\,\mathrm{MPa}\), either contour gives \(\theta'=(100\times50-20\times43.75)/(2\times2400\times27000)=3.18\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/mm}\), about 1.82° per metre. Checking the other contour independently catches a reversed sign on the shared wall. If a weld along that wall fails, equal twist and the closed paths that led to these flows can no longer be assumed.

Individual cell torque contributions are informative when interpreting a section cut. The larger cell contributes \(2(2400)(50)=240\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), and the smaller contributes \(2(1200)(43.75)=105\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Their 345 N m sum is independent of the coordinate origin. These are bookkeeping circulations rather than two torques applied independently to separate tubes. Cutting through the common web changes the compatibility and available flow paths, so using the original partition after a cut is not justified. Surface strain measurements on the upper exterior walls and the shared web would distinguish the predicted 50, 43.75 and 6.25 N/mm flows more effectively than measuring a single outer-wall strain alone.

A comparison with a thin unpartitioned box is possible without changing outer dimensions. For the outer median rectangle 90 by 40 mm, \(A_m=3600\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) and \(\oint ds/t=2(90+40)/2=130\). Its single-cell torsion constant is

\[4A_m^2/130=398769\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

The partitioned solution implies

\[J_t=T/(G\theta')=345000/[27000(3.183\times10^{-5})]\simeq401500\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

Under this specific pure torque the additional web therefore raises torsional stiffness by less than one percent. It experiences little shear because the neighbouring circulations are similar. Its contribution to transverse load sharing, panel buckling resistance or local attachment stiffness can nevertheless be important and needs separate calculations.

Move the web to the centre, producing identical cells of median width 45 mm. A numerical section calculation that predicts double the torsional stiffness simply because there are now two cells has likely treated the common web as two independent exterior walls.

From a strain rosette to principal stresses

Engineering mechanics

Recover a complete plane-strain measurement at a surface from three gauges and infer the corresponding plane-stress state.

From first principles

Calculate the extension recorded by an angled strain gauge

A bonded straight gauge records fractional change of length along its own axis. Let adjacent material points be separated by \(d\mathbf x=\mathbf n\,ds\), where \(\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) is a unit direction. For a smooth small displacement field \(\mathbf u\), their relative displacement is approximately \((\nabla\mathbf u)d\mathbf x\). To first order only the projection along the original separation changes its length:

\[\frac{\delta ds}{ds}=\mathbf n^\mathsf T(\nabla\mathbf u)\mathbf n=\mathbf n^\mathsf T\boldsymbol\epsilon\,\mathbf n,\]
\[\boldsymbol\epsilon=(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^\mathsf T)/2 .\]

The antisymmetric part represents infinitesimal rotation and has zero quadratic form. Writing engineering shear as \(\gamma_{xy}=2\epsilon_{xy}\) yields the observation equation

\[\epsilon_\theta=\epsilon_x\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_y\sin^2\theta+\gamma_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta .\]

Three independently oriented grids provide three scalar observations of a uniform in-plane strain tensor. For directions \(0,45,90\) degrees, their combination \(2\epsilon_{45}-\epsilon_0-\epsilon_{90}\) recovers engineering shear. Converting this strain to stress additionally requires elastic properties and a local plane-stress or plane-strain model.

Derivation and applications

Remove transverse gauge sensitivity before reconstructing strain

A resistance grid responds primarily to extension along its long axis. Its transverse sensitivity may still matter when a small shear strain is inferred from differences of readings. Model a gauge at angle \(\theta\) as reporting \(g_\theta=\epsilon_\theta+\xi\epsilon_{\theta+90^\circ}\) after longitudinal calibration, with known dimensionless transverse response \(\xi\). Assume uniform infinitesimal in-plane strain over a rectangular \(0^\circ,45^\circ,90^\circ\) rosette, identical temperature compensation and the same \(\xi\) for each grid. Readings and strains below are dimensionless.

At the two axial gauges the transverse direction of one is the longitudinal direction of the other. Sum and subtract their equations to separate mean extension from normal-strain difference:

\[g_0+g_{90}=(1+\xi)(\epsilon_x+\epsilon_y),\]
\[g_0-g_{90}=(1-\xi)(\epsilon_x-\epsilon_y).\]

At \(45^\circ\) and \(135^\circ\), small-strain transformation gives normal strain \((\epsilon_x+\epsilon_y)/2\pm\gamma_{xy}/2\), where \(\gamma_{xy}\) is engineering shear strain. Inserting both projections into the calibrated reading gives

\[2g_{45}=(1+\xi)(\epsilon_x+\epsilon_y)+(1-\xi)\gamma_{xy}.\]

Remove the trace using the first equation and recover the corrected shear and principal strains:

\[\gamma_{xy}=\frac{2g_{45}-g_0-g_{90}}{1-\xi},\]
\[\epsilon_{1,2}=\frac{\epsilon_x+\epsilon_y}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(\epsilon_x-\epsilon_y)^2+\gamma_{xy}^2}.\]

If \(\xi=0\) this reduces to the ideal rosette. As \(\xi\) approaches unity all readings respond to the trace and directional information becomes poorly conditioned. A common temperature offset cancels from this shear combination but changes the inferred mean strain if no thermal correction is made.

Worked example

For \(\xi=0.0200\) suppose \((g_0,g_{45},g_{90})=(298,200,-94)\) microstrain. The true strain sum is \(204/1.02=200\) microstrain, while its difference is \(392/0.98=400\) microstrain. Thus \(\epsilon_x=300\) and \(\epsilon_y=-100\) microstrain. The corrected engineering shear is \((400-298+94)/0.98=200\) microstrain. The principal values are \(100\pm223.6\), or \(323.6\) and \(-123.6\) microstrain.

What a bonded gauge actually measures

A resistance gauge aligned with a line on a free surface records extension along that line. A single gauge cannot distinguish uniaxial tension from combined stretching and shear arranged to give the same directional extension. Bond three gauges at different angles near enough together that the local strain is approximately uniform. A rectangular rosette uses directions of zero, 45 and 90 degrees, all referred to the same positive surface axis.

Under infinitesimal deformation, a unit direction vector \(n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) experiences normal strain \(n^T\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}n\). Engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{xy}\) is twice the off-diagonal strain tensor component. Expanding the directional expression yields

\[\varepsilon_\theta=\varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta+\varepsilon_y\sin^2\theta+\gamma_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta .\]

The zero and 90° readings give \(\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y\) directly. The middle reading gives

\[\gamma_{xy}=2\varepsilon_{45}-\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y.\]

A gauge at minus 45° measures the opposite shear contribution. A sketch of the physical gauge labels avoids reversing the inferred shear sign during data reduction.

Introduce the local stress assumption

At an unloaded outer surface the normal and shear tractions on that surface vanish. With \(z\) normal to it, this gives local plane stress. For isotropic linear elasticity and mechanical strains obtained after temperature correction, invert the plane-stress compliance equations and use the shear modulus for the remaining component.

\[\sigma_x=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}(\varepsilon_x+\nu\varepsilon_y),\]
\[\sigma_y=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}(\varepsilon_y+\nu\varepsilon_x),\]
\[\tau_{xy}=\frac{E\gamma_{xy}}{2(1+\nu)} .\]

A strain rosette measures only the in-plane quantities. A nonzero through-thickness strain is consistent with plane stress because the in-plane stresses cause Poisson contraction. Beneath a contact patch the local traction condition changes, so the same stress recovery would need additional information.

Interpret the readings on a plate

Suppose a steel surface gives \(400,\ 250,\ -100\) microstrain at zero, 45 and 90 degrees. The recovered shear strain is \(500-400+100=200\) microstrain. With \(E=210\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and \(\nu=0.30\), the stresses are \(85.38,\ 4.62,\ 16.15\,\mathrm{MPa}\) for \(x,y,xy\), respectively. The shear is positive under the stated gauge orientation. Converting microstrain to \(10^{-6}\) before multiplying by MPa is essential.

The principal stresses are the eigenvalues of this symmetric matrix. Its mean normal stress is 45.0 MPa, and its Mohr-circle radius is

\[\sqrt{[(85.38-4.62)/2]^2+16.15^2}=43.50\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

Thus the two in-plane principal stresses are approximately 88.5 and 1.5 MPa. The major direction is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}(2\tau,\sigma_x-\sigma_y)=10.9^\circ\) from the zero gauge. A strain reading of −100 microstrain along \(y\) therefore does not imply compressive normal stress along that axis: Poisson contraction from the dominant \(x\) load accounts for much of that reading.

Resolve a uniform thermal signal

Uniform heating of an unconstrained isotropic plate adds the same free thermal strain \(\alpha\Delta T\) to each gauge reading, after allowing for the gauge instrumentation itself. It cancels from the inferred engineering shear because \(2-1-1=0\), but it persists in both recovered normal components. For a 20 K temperature rise and linear expansion \(12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), subtract 240 microstrain from each reading before applying the mechanical plane-stress relation.

Reconstruct a surface strain from a delta rosette

The zero, 45 and 90 degree rosette is convenient but not the only useful placement. Introduce the strain-circle coordinates \(m=(\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y)/2,\ d=(\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y)/2,\ g=\gamma_{xy}/2\). Then each directional reading is \(e_\theta=m+d\cos 2\theta+g\sin2\theta\). Every installed gauge supplies one linear equation in these three unknowns. Gauge directions that are nearly parallel give nearly dependent equations, so a small error in an individual reading can then cause a large error in a recovered component.

For a delta rosette take directions \(0,60,120^\circ\). The doubled angles are equally spaced round the strain circle, so their cosine and sine values each sum to zero. Adding all three readings gives

\[m=(e_0+e_{60}+e_{120})/3.\]

The first reading then gives

\[d=e_0-m;\]

subtracting the last two cancels both \(m\) and their identical cosine terms and gives

\[g=(e_{60}-e_{120})/\sqrt3.\]

The in-plane principal strains and their physical orientation follow without a material assumption:

\[e_{1,2}=m\pm\sqrt{d^2+g^2},\]
\[\theta_1=\tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}(g,d).\]

The half-angle is necessary because a straight gauge has the same reading after a physical rotation of 180°. For an isotropic elastic solid the principal stress directions coincide with these strain directions. Orthotropic laminates can give stress and strain axes that differ, so converting a strain circle into stresses would require the material compliance in the specimen’s axes.

Yield fronts and plastic bending capacity

Engineering mechanics

Track the spread of yielding through a rectangular section and distinguish the first-yield moment from the fully plastic moment.

From first principles

Separate first yield from the plastic moment of a section

Take a homogeneous symmetric beam section in pure bending, with plane sections remaining plane and elastic-perfectly plastic material of yield stress \(\sigma_y\). Let \(c\) be the largest distance from the neutral axis. For small curvature \(\kappa\) longitudinal strain varies as \(\kappa y\), so the elastic stress distribution and bending moment are

\[\sigma_x=E\kappa y,\]
\[M=\int_A \sigma_x y\,dA=EI\kappa .\]

First yield occurs when the outermost stress magnitude reaches \(\sigma_y\). Continuing to bend can still raise the moment as deeper fibres reach yield. In the ideal fully plastic limit, the tensile and compressive blocks have stresses \(\pm\sigma_y\). Symmetry makes their axial forces cancel. The two distinct moment scales are therefore

\[M_y=\sigma_y I/c,\]
\[M_p=\sigma_y\int_A|y|\,dA .\]

For a rectangle of breadth \(b\) and total depth \(2c\), the moments become \(2b\sigma_y c^2/3\) and \(b\sigma_y c^2\). Their ratio \(3/2\) measures the stress redistribution available in the section. A complete frame mechanism generally needs several suitable plastic hinges, so section yield alone does not determine the collapse load.

Derivation and applications

Predict springback after partial plastic bending

A bent coupon can retain curvature after the applied moment is removed. Consider a homogeneous rectangular section of breadth \(b\), half-depth \(c\), Young’s modulus \(E\) and yield stress \(\sigma_y\). Assume an elastic-perfectly plastic material in monotone pure bending, plane sections and symmetric tension/compression response. Take curvature \(\kappa>0\) and magnitudes on the upper half. After yielding begins the elastic core extends from the centre to \(y_e=\sigma_y/(E\kappa)\); beyond it stress has saturated.

Integrate stress times lever arm separately in the core and yielded zones, then double by symmetry:

\[M=2b\!\left[\int_0^{y_e}E\kappa y^2\,dy+\int_{y_e}^{c}\sigma_y y\,dy\right]=b\sigma_y(c^2-y_e^2/3).\]

At first yield

\[\kappa_y=\sigma_y/(Ec);\]

the fully plastic limiting moment is

\[M_p=b\sigma_y c^2.\]

Eliminating \(y_e\) relates peak moment to peak curvature:

\[M/M_p=1-\tfrac13(\kappa_y/\kappa)^2,\qquad \kappa\ge\kappa_y .\]

Upon unloading, initially all fibres respond elastically. The removed moment corresponds to a change \(M/(EI)\) in curvature, with \(I=2bc^3/3\). Since \(M_p/(EI)=3\kappa_y/2\), the residual curvature is

\[\kappa_r=\kappa-\frac{M}{EI}=\kappa-\tfrac32\kappa_y\!\left[1-\tfrac13(\kappa_y/\kappa)^2\right].\]

At first yield the predicted residual is zero. The derivation assumes the unloading increment does not drive any fibre into reverse yield; that can be checked by its surface stress change. Real strain hardening or different moduli during unloading change the inferred springback.

Worked example

Calculate the unloaded radius of a bent strip

Take \(E=200\) GPa, \(\sigma_y=250\) MPa and thickness \(2c=5.00\) mm. First-yield curvature is \(0.500\) m⁻¹. Bend to \(\kappa=1.00\) m⁻¹ so only the middle half of the depth remains elastic. The peak moment is \((11/12)M_p\), unloading removes \(0.6875\) m⁻¹, and \(\kappa_r=0.3125\) m⁻¹. The strip springs back from a \(1.00\) m radius to a radius of \(3.20\) m.

First yield occurs at a surface

A beam can retain moment capacity after its first surface fibres yield. Consider a straight rectangular bar of width \(b\), total depth \(2c\), equal tensile and compressive yield stresses \(\sigma_Y\), and an elastic, perfectly plastic law. Assume pure bending about its centroid, small strains and a compact section that avoids local buckling. Plane sections remain plane, so longitudinal strain varies as \(\kappa y\). Symmetry keeps the neutral axis at mid-depth for this material and section.

While the whole section is elastic, \(\sigma=E\kappa y\). As curvature increases, the first yield occurs when \(E\kappa_Y c=\sigma_Y\). Integrating stress times moment arm gives the elastic first-yield moment \(M_Y=EI\kappa_Y=\sigma_Y I/c\). A section with a narrow outer flange or unequal tensile and compressive material behaviour requires a fresh stress distribution and force equilibrium.

Integrate the elastic core and yielded layers

At a greater curvature let \(a=\sigma_Y/(E\kappa)\) be the half-depth of the remaining elastic core. Stress in the core grows linearly as \(\sigma_Y y/a\). Outside the core it remains at the yield magnitude. Each half of the section contributes the same bending moment. Splitting the integral at the moving yield front gives

\[M=2b\left[\int_0^a\frac{\sigma_Yy^2}{a}\,dy+\int_a^c\sigma_Yy\,dy\right]=b\sigma_Y(c^2-a^2/3).\]

At \(a=c\) this reproduces \(M_Y=(2/3)b\sigma_Yc^2\). As \(a\) approaches zero, \(M\) tends to \(M_p=b\sigma_Y c^2\), which is 1.5 times first yield for this solid rectangle. The yield fronts advance towards the centre while progressively larger curvature increments produce smaller changes of moment. The bending stiffness at first loading is therefore history dependent after yielding begins.

Quantify the onset and extent of plastic deformation

A 20 mm wide bar is 40 mm deep, with \(E=200\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and \(\sigma_Y=250\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Its first-yield curvature is \(250/(200000\times20)=6.25\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}\), or 0.0625 m⁻¹. First yield occurs at 1.333 kN m and the limiting plastic moment is 2.000 kN m. At twice the yield curvature the elastic core half-depth has fallen to 10 mm. Substitution gives

\[M=20(250)[400-100/3]=1.833\,\mathrm{kN\,m}.\]

Upon unloading, assume the stress changes elastically without reverse yield. The immediate moment–curvature slope is then \(EI\), so the recovered curvature is \(M/(EI)\), where \(I=20(40)^3/12=106667\,\mathrm{mm^4}\). It is \(8.59\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}\). The peak curvature was \(1.25\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}\), leaving about 0.0391 m⁻¹ residual curvature. A permanent bend can therefore coexist with zero applied bending moment. The residual internal stresses balance their own force and moment across the section.

Compare equal areas in different locations

Redistributing material towards the outer fibres changes the reserve between first yield and full plasticity. In the limiting idealisation of two very thin equal flanges of area \(A_f\) at \(y=\pm c\), essentially all material reaches yield together. The first-yield and plastic moments both approach \(2A_f\sigma_Y c\). A shape factor of 1.5 is specific to the solid rectangle and cannot be carried over to this ideal flange pair.

Build a collapse mechanism from plastic hinges

A section that reaches its plastic moment can undergo substantial further rotation in an ideal perfectly plastic model. A structure may still support additional external load because other sections can take up incremental bending. Collapse in a rigid–plastic beam idealisation occurs when enough hinges form to allow a compatible mechanism. Use virtual work at collapse: the external forces do work through compatible virtual displacements, and each plastic hinge dissipates \(M_p\) times its relative rotation. Elastic strain energy changes are neglected in this limit calculation.

Take a uniform beam fixed at both ends under a concentrated downward force \(P\) at midspan. Assume equal plastic moments of magnitude \(M_p\) in sagging and hogging, compact sections and adequate rotation capacity. Consider hinges at the two clamps and at midspan, joined by two rigid half-spans. A small downward virtual centre movement \(\delta\) rotates each half through \(\theta=2\delta/L\). The clamp hinges each rotate by \(\theta\), while the centre hinge rotates through \(2\theta\) because its adjacent halves slope in opposite directions. Work equivalence gives

\[P\delta=M_p(\theta+2\theta+\theta)=4M_p(2\delta/L),\]
\[P_c=8M_p/L.\]

This kinematically admissible mechanism gives an upper bound on true plastic collapse load under these assumptions. Here a matching statically admissible diagram can be constructed: end moments are \(-M_p\), the centre moment is \(+M_p\), and constant shear on each half gives

\[P/2=4M_p/L.\]

The matching bounds identify the exact ideal collapse load for this geometry and loading.

Separate first yield, section capacity and structural collapse

A mild-steel rectangular strip 30 mm wide and 60 mm deep spans 1.20 m between fixed clamps. Take yield stress 240 MPa. The first-yield section moment is \(M_Y=\sigma_Ybh^2/6=4.32\,\mathrm{kN\,m}\), and the fully plastic moment is

\[M_p=\sigma_Ybh^2/4=6.48\,\mathrm{kN\,m}.\]

For a central point force, elastic symmetry and compatibility give clamp and centre moment magnitudes \(PL/8\). First yield thus occurs at \(8(4.32)/1.20=28.8\,\mathrm{kN}\) and the ideal mechanism load is

\[8(6.48)/1.20=43.2\,\mathrm{kN}.\]

The factor of 1.5 here comes from the rectangular section’s reserve as yielding spreads. The three critical sections have equal absolute moment in this symmetric loading.

Release the right clamp rotation and the mechanism changes. For the simpler limiting case with both ends pinned, only one interior plastic hinge is needed and the moment maximum is \(PL/4\). The same section would collapse at \(4(6.48)/1.20=21.6\,\mathrm{kN}\), half the fixed-ended result. The comparison explains why a connection described as fixed must actually develop bending moment and plastic rotation before its contribution is credited. A brittle welded detail, lateral instability or a shear failure can prevent the assumed redistribution. The calculation supplies a limit for the stated deformation mode, not a prediction of every possible failure mechanism.

The location of the mechanism can be checked in an overload test by measuring rotations near both supports and on either side of midspan. A large rotation at a single connection before substantial rotation elsewhere indicates that the assumed distribution of hinge resistance is not being realised.

Crack driving force and fracture assessment

Engineering mechanics

Use an energy balance and a stress-intensity model to distinguish crack-controlled fracture from yielding of the gross section.

From first principles

Relate crack extension to the energy released by elastic compliance

Consider a slowly loaded linear elastic specimen of thickness \(B\), applied force \(P\), measured displacement \(\delta\) and crack coordinate \(a\). Write \(C(a)=\delta/P\). The projected area convention is \(dA=B\,da\). On a fixed crack the linear loading curve stores strain energy equal to the triangle below its force–displacement graph:

\[U=\tfrac12P\delta=\tfrac12P^2C(a).\]

For a dead force held constant while the crack extends, the external force performs additional work \(P\,d\delta\). Subtract the load potential \(P\delta\) from the stored energy and differentiate with respect to area to obtain the driving force for extension:

\[\Pi=U-P\delta=-\tfrac12P^2C,\]
\[G=-\left.\frac{d\Pi}{dA}\right|_{P}=\frac{P^2}{2B}\frac{dC}{da}.\]

The units are J/m². An increase of compliance can free energy to create new surfaces and to dissipate energy near the crack. A material resistance measured under matching conditions is needed to predict onset. In linear elastic mode I, the local intensity relation \(G=K_I^2/E'\) connects this global energy measure with the near-tip field.

Background · Stress intensity
\[K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\]

Opening-mode intensity has units of stress times the square root of length. Geometry and the definition of crack length are part of the calibration.

Derivation and applications

How machine compliance changes the energy available to a crack

A tensile fixture can release stored elastic energy as a crack grows even when the grips do not move. To quantify this, treat a specimen of thickness \(B\) with crack coordinate \(a\) and measured opening compliance \(C(a)=\delta_s/P\). Use a single projected crack-area increment \(B\,da\) consistent with the stated convention. Let an elastic testing machine in series have constant compliance \(C_m\), and assume quasistatic linear elastic loading with negligible dissipative processes outside the crack tip. At fixed total grip opening \(\Delta\) the force must change as the specimen compliance changes:

\[\Delta=P(C+C_m),\]
\[U=\tfrac12P^2(C+C_m)=\frac{\Delta^2}{2(C+C_m)} .\]

No further external work enters at fixed \(\Delta\). Differentiate the stored energy with respect to projected area. Since \(C_m\) is independent of the crack coordinate, only the specimen derivative appears:

\[G=-\frac1B\left.\frac{dU}{da}\right|_\Delta=\frac{\Delta^2}{2B(C+C_m)^2}\frac{dC}{da}=\frac{P^2}{2B}C'(a).\]

At a given instantaneous force this has the familiar force-based value, but its variation during crack extension depends on compliance control. Take the idealized measured relation \(C=ca^3\) over a limited range. Logarithmic differentiation at fixed imposed opening gives

\[\frac{1}{G}\frac{dG}{da}=\frac2a-\frac{6C}{a(C+C_m)} .\]

For constant fracture resistance, a decreasing driving force after slight growth favours arrest. In this compliance model it occurs when \(C_m<2C\). This local comparison assumes a small crack extension within the calibrated range and a material whose fracture resistance is not changing appreciably.

Worked example

Estimate driving force from a coupon measurement

At \(a=40.0\) mm take \(C=2.00\) micrometres/N, \(C_m=1.00\) micrometre/N, \(B=10.0\) mm and \(\Delta=0.600\) mm. The force is \(200\) N, and the local cubic slope is \(C'=3C/a=1.50\times10^{-4}\) N⁻¹. Substitution yields \(G=300\) J/m². The calculated logarithmic slope is \(-50.0\) m⁻¹, predicting about a \(5\%\) decrease for an initial additional millimetre of extension.

A sharp crack changes the question

An uncracked tensile coupon can often be assessed by comparing nominal stress with a yield stress. A sharp pre-existing crack concentrates the stress into a small region near its tip. Within linear elastic fracture mechanics the leading opening-mode field has magnitude proportional to \(K_I/\sqrt{2\pi r}\) at distance \(r\) ahead of the tip. The unbounded mathematical stress signals the limits of the elastic tip description. A material toughness measured under comparable conditions instead sets a threshold for crack extension.

A common representation is \(K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\), where \(a\) is the specified crack dimension, \(\sigma\) is a stated remote stress, and \(Y\) describes the geometry and loading. For a central crack in a sufficiently wide plate \(a\) is the half-length and \(Y\) approaches one. For a short edge crack in a wide plate \(a\) is its depth and a common approximation is \(Y\approx1.12\). Finite width, bending or a different crack shape changes the factor.

Relate compliance to an energy release rate

In a linear test specimen let load \(P\), opening displacement \(u\), and compliance \(C(a)=u/P\) refer to the same load point. At fixed load the stored elastic energy is \(P^2C/2\). The external load does work \(P\,du=P^2\,dC\) as the crack extends. The released potential energy is the difference, \(P^2 dC/2\). For a through crack with one advancing front and specimen thickness \(B\), its projected area increases by \(B\,da\), giving

\[G=\frac{P^2}{2B}\frac{dC}{da}=\frac{K_I^2}{E'},\]
\[E'=E\ {\rm (plane\ stress)},\]
\[E'=E/(1-\nu^2)\ {\rm (plane\ strain)}.\]

The compliance argument explains how a distant load–displacement measurement can identify energy available at a crack. Define the crack area consistently: a symmetric specimen in which two tips advance together changes projected area twice as quickly for the same increment of half-length.

Infer a critical flaw size

Take a wide plate with an edge crack of depth 3.0 mm, tensile remote stress 160 MPa and illustrative applicable toughness \(K_c=35\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}\). The estimate \(K_I=1.12(160)\sqrt{\pi(0.003)}=17.4\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}\) uses metres for crack depth. Holding geometry factor and stress fixed, the critical depth is \(a_c=[35/(1.12\times160)]^2/\pi=0.0121\,\mathrm m\), about 12.1 mm.

At twice the stress this depth falls by a factor of four, to approximately 3.0 mm. Before using that extrapolation, check remaining net section, applicability of the wide-plate geometry and whether plasticity near the crack is small compared with crack and ligament dimensions. Toughness depends on temperature, constraint and loading rate. An inspection interval would additionally require a crack-growth law, a load history and a detection limit. The calculated critical depth describes one load state and cannot by itself predict the number of cycles remaining.

Interpret a crack-length uncertainty

At fixed remote stress and geometry factor a small relative error in crack depth gives approximately half that relative error in stress intensity, because \(dK/K=da/(2a)\). A 0.3 mm uncertainty on a 3 mm measurement therefore changes this estimated intensity by about five percent.

Integrate stable fatigue growth up to inspection

Fracture toughness characterises an instability at a given load. Damage tolerance also asks how long a detected crack takes to reach an inspection or retirement size under repeated loading. For illustration, suppose tests for the service material and stress ratio support a Paris relation \(da/dN=C(\Delta K)^m\) in its intermediate stable growth range. Use crack depth \(a\) in metres and intensities in MPa√m. The coefficient \(C\) must correspond to those units, stress ratio and environment. This empirical law generally changes near threshold and near final instability.

For constant stress range \(\Delta\sigma\), fixed geometry factor \(Y\) and exponent \(m=3\), substitute \(\Delta K=Y\Delta\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\). Crack advance per cycle then scales as \(a^{3/2}\); the same stress history grows a larger crack faster. Separate variables between measured depth \(a_i\) and removal depth \(a_f\). Integrating yields

\[N=\frac{2(a_i^{-1/2}-a_f^{-1/2})}{C(Y\Delta\sigma)^3\pi^{3/2}} .\]

This prediction decreases to zero as the two depths approach each other. It scales with the inverse cube of stress range at fixed bounds. If the geometry factor changes appreciably with crack depth, retain \(Y(a)\) inside the integral. The maximum stress in the cycle is needed separately to compare \(K_{\max}\) with applicable toughness, because it is the range that drives the adopted fatigue law.

Compare two inspection thresholds on the same plate

Consider a wide plate with \(Y=1.12\), \(\Delta\sigma=80\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(\sigma_{\max}=140\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(m=3\), and illustrative \(C=2.0\times10^{-12}\,(\mathrm{m/cycle})/(\mathrm{MPa}\sqrt{\mathrm m})^3\). Suppose the measured growth-law range includes depths between 2 and 8 mm for this loading. At 2 mm,

\[\Delta K=1.12(80)\sqrt{\pi(0.002)}=7.10\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m};\]

at 8 mm it doubles to 14.2. The growth rate at the upper limit is consequently eight times the lower-limit rate.

The integral denominator is \(2.0\times10^{-12}(89.6)^3\pi^{3/2}=8.01\times10^{-6}\) in the stated units, and the numerator is

\[2(22.36-11.18)=22.36\,\mathrm{m^{-1/2}}.\]

The estimated interval is \(2.79\times10^6\) cycles. If the smallest reliably detected crack were 4 mm, the available interval to 8 mm would fall to \(2(15.81-11.18)/(8.01\times10^{-6})=1.16\times10^6\) cycles. Improving early detection has a measurable value in this model. At retirement depth \(K_{\max}=24.9\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}\); compare that independently with the applicable toughness. Uncertainty in detection, loading, environment and growth coefficients would enter an actual inspection decision rather than treating this calculated mean growth interval as a guaranteed service interval.

Those two changes affect different checks and cannot be combined into a single linear safety multiplier. A record of both stress range and stress maximum is therefore more informative than a peak-only reading for planning inspections.

Finding a displacement when no load acts in its direction

Engineering mechanics

A displacement gauge measures the lateral travel of a truss joint under a vertical load. A force balance determines the bar forces, but cannot directly give the sideways motion. Introduce a dummy load, differentiate the stored energy and recover the gauge reading with its sign.

Link an external force coordinate to stored strain energy

For a linear elastic pin-jointed truss, neglect support movement and temperature changes and assume small displacements. Bar i has constant axial force \(N_i\), length \(L_i\), modulus \(E_i\) and area \(A_i\). Integrating its force against its extension gives

\[U_i=N_i^2L_i/(2E_iA_i).\]

Superposition makes each force linear in the external loads. A small increment of load changes the equilibrium configuration. For a linear elastic conservative system the complementary energy equals U and its derivative with respect to an independent applied force gives displacement along that force.

\[U=\sum_i{N_i^2L_i\over2E_iA_i},\]
\[\delta_Q={\partial U\over\partial Q}=\sum_i {N_i n_i L_i\over E_iA_i},\]
\[n_i={\partial N_i\over\partial Q}.\]

The derivative \(n_i\) is the member force produced per unit trial load in the requested direction, with all other independent loads held fixed. Solve this trial equilibrium even if \(Q=0\) physically. Setting \(Q=0\) before differentiating erases its entire contribution. As a dimensional check, \(n_i\) is dimensionless when Q and \(N_i\) both use newtons, and the sum has dimensions of length.

Compute the two-bar flexibility matrix

A joint hangs from two overhead pins by straight bars slanting up-left and up-right, each at 45° to horizontal. Positive joint coordinates are rightward and downward. Let V be the applied downward force and H the rightward force; bars carry tension positive. Equilibrium gives

\[N_L=(V+H)/\sqrt2,\ N_R=(V-H)/\sqrt2.\]

Define bar axial compliances \(c_i=L_i/(E_iA_i)\). Insert the forces into the energy and differentiate with respect to H and V independently:

\[\begin{bmatrix}\delta_H\\\delta_V\end{bmatrix}={1\over2}\begin{bmatrix}c_L+c_R&c_L-c_R\\c_L-c_R&c_L+c_R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}H\\V\end{bmatrix}.\]

The cross coefficients are equal because the mixed derivatives of the stored energy commute. This is Maxwell–Betti reciprocity. A cross displacement can be negative even though both direct compliances and stored energy are positive. The eigenvalues of this particular flexibility matrix are \(c_L,c_R\), corresponding to the two independent bar-extension directions.

Predict a sideways gauge reading and reverse the test

Take \(c_L=0.0020,\ c_R=0.0040\) mm/N. At \(V=100\) N and \(H=0\), both members carry 70.71 N. The vertical displacement is \(0.300\) mm, and the horizontal displacement is −0.100 mm. The more compliant right bar stretches farther; the joint moves left and down to accommodate both bar extensions. Half the vertical load times its displacement gives 15 N mm of strain energy. Summing the two bar energies independently gives the same result.

Apply instead \(H=100\) N and \(V=0\), assuming the slender right bar remains stable under compression. The vertical reading is −0.100 mm and horizontal motion is +0.300 mm. Thus the lateral response to the vertical test predicts the vertical response to a lateral test of equal magnitude. Equalizing bar areas would remove the cross response if the lengths and moduli matched. In practice a pin with clearance or a cable that goes slack can break the assumed linear bilateral response.

Exchange the left and right compliances. Before looking at the new graph, mark which readout should reverse sign and which should remain equal. Then set the two values alike and identify the symmetry that removes cross-coupling.

Why a bonded pair of strips bends under uniform heating

Engineering mechanics

Two perfectly bonded strips would expand by different amounts if separated. After bonding they must share a continuous longitudinal strain field. Solve simultaneously for its mean and its curvature and recover stresses that can be present even when the entire strip is free.

Separate total strain into free and constrained parts

Consider a narrow long strip of width b and total thickness \(2t\), with layers of equal thickness on either side of \(z=0\). The upper layer has expansion coefficient \(\alpha_+\), the lower \(\alpha_-\), and both have the same E here. Start stress-free, then impose a uniform temperature increase \(\Delta T\). With plane sections remaining plane, define signed curvature by writing longitudinal strain \(\epsilon=\epsilon_0+\kappa z\). Stress is proportional to the difference from the free thermal strain:

\[\sigma_\pm(z)=E[\epsilon_0+\kappa z-\alpha_\pm\Delta T].\]

Perfect adhesion enforces continuity of longitudinal displacement at the interface. It permits longitudinal stress to jump there because the stress-free thermal strain changes discontinuously. Traction continuity across the horizontal interface concerns its normal and shear traction components. This beam model describes the middle portion away from free ends where interfacial shear transfers load between layers.

Use both a zero axial force and a zero moment

For an unconstrained strip no external force or moment acts on its section. The thickness integral of stress must vanish, and its first moment must also vanish. Because z is measured from the symmetric section midpoint, the odd integrals of \(\epsilon_0 z\) and \(\kappa z\) cancel. Separate the thermal integrals over the two layers:

\[2t\epsilon_0=t(\alpha_++\alpha_-)\Delta T,\]
\[{2t^3\over3}\kappa={t^2\over2}(\alpha_+-\alpha_-)\Delta T .\]

Hence \(\epsilon_0=(\alpha_++\alpha_-)\Delta T/2\) and \(\kappa=3(\alpha_+-\alpha_-)\Delta T/(4t)\). The higher-expansion side takes the greater total strain at large positive z. For unequal moduli, the two equilibrium equations still work but must retain E inside the integrals; symmetry no longer removes every strain–curvature coupling term.

A tiny curvature can coexist with measurable stress

Let \(t=0.50\) mm, \(E=100\) GPa, \(\alpha_+=20\) and \(\alpha_-=10\) micrometres per metre per kelvin, with \(\Delta T=40\) K. The mean strain is 600 microstrain, curvature is 0.600 m⁻¹, and the radius of curvature is 1.67 m. Across a free 100 mm length with one end setting the reference tangent, the small-slope tip offset magnitude is

\[\kappa L^2/2=3.0\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

A free single layer with either coefficient would expand without this curvature.

At the interface the upper layer has stress \(100\,{\rm GPa}(600-800)10^{-6}=-20\) MPa and the lower +20 MPa. At the outer surfaces the bending term contributes ±300 microstrain; the upper stress becomes +10 MPa and the lower −10 MPa. If a fixture holds the same strip flat while allowing average extension, the mean strain is still 600 microstrain but the layer stresses are uniform ±20 MPa. The fixture must now supply a restraining moment. Compare the stress fields at fixed temperature before interpreting a strain-gauge reading as stress.

Static and couple balance of rotors

Machine dynamics

Represent rotating imbalance as a vector and determine the corrections needed to cancel both net force and rocking moment.

From first principles

Represent a rotating offset by a force and moment phasor

A rigid rotor carries a small mass \(m\) at eccentricity \(e\) from its shaft axis. At constant angular speed \(\omega\), its radial acceleration has magnitude \(e\omega^2\). The shaft supplies the inward force and receives an equal outward reaction whose direction rotates with the shaft. Encode the offset orientation by phase \(\phi\) and the unbalance by \(U=me\,e^{i\phi}\). Complex notation retains both perpendicular force components.

\[F_x+iF_y=\omega^2 U e^{i\omega t}.\]

For several masses spinning together, force superposition makes the net phasor proportional to \(\sum U_j\). Axially separated forces can also produce a rotating couple even when the net force vanishes. Taking moments about a reference plane weights each unbalance by its axial distance \(z_j\).

\[\sum_j U_j=0,\]
\[\sum_j z_jU_j=0 .\]

These are the two rigid-rotor balance conditions. Changing the reference plane adds a multiple of the first sum to the second, so once net force vanishes the couple balance is independent of that choice. Flexible rotor modes at operating speed require additional information.

Derivation and applications

Separate the imbalance force and couple at the bearings

A rotor can have little net rotating force yet cause large opposed bearing reactions. Treat the shaft as rigid with bearings at axial positions \(z=0\) and \(z=L\). Assume speed \(\omega\) is constant and sufficiently below the first bending resonance for a rigid load distribution. Represent each unbalance by the complex vector \(U_j=m_je_j e^{i\phi_j}\). The physical rotating force on the support is the real part of the phasor times \(e^{i\omega t}\). Summing forces and taking moments about the first support gives the transmitted load phasors \(F_A,F_B\).

\[F_A+F_B=\omega^2\sum_j U_j,\]
\[L F_B=\omega^2\sum_j z_jU_j .\]

The moment equation weights each unbalance by axial position. Solving for the individual support loads identifies the force distribution even for a resultant whose direction rotates with the shaft. The weights can be negative for overhung masses.

\[F_B=\frac{\omega^2}{L}\sum_jz_jU_j,\]
\[F_A=\omega^2\sum_j(1-z_j/L)U_j .\]

Conversely, two synchronised bearing measurements supply both the summed unbalance and its first axial moment. Their relative phases must be retained, since adding magnitude readings would erase the signature of a rotating couple.

\[U_{\rm sum}=\frac{F_A+F_B}{\omega^2},\]
\[M_U=\sum_j z_jU_j=\frac{LF_B}{\omega^2}.\]

With \(U_{\rm sum}=0\), equal and opposite support phasors can remain. Their mount loads grow with squared speed even though the instantaneous vector sum vanishes.

Worked example

Predict bearing reactions from a pure couple

Take \(L=0.600\) m, \(\omega=100\) rad/s, and equal opposite unbalances of magnitude 200 g mm in planes at 0.100 and 0.500 m. Taking the first unbalance as positive, \(M_U=0.000200(0.100-0.500)=-8.00\times10^{-5}\) kg m². The bearing load \(F_B=-1.33\) N and \(F_A=+1.33\) N. A single accelerometer measuring common support translation could miss the rotating moment.

Translate a mass offset into a periodic bearing load

A rotor can have its total mass centre on the shaft axis and still shake its housing. The reason is spatial: two equal and opposite centrifugal force vectors in separate transverse planes form a rotating couple. First describe one small excess mass \(m\) at radial offset \(e\) and clock angle \(\phi\). At constant angular speed \(\omega\), its required radial acceleration has magnitude \(e\omega^2\). The equal and opposite reaction transmitted to the rotating assembly has magnitude \(me\omega^2\).

Represent its radial unbalance by a complex number \(U=me(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)\). This is shorthand for two transverse components at a reference shaft orientation, not a complex physical force. Multiplication by \(e^{i\omega t}\) rotates all vectors together. Since both \(\omega^2\) and this rotation are common to all masses, cancellation can be carried out using \(U\) alone.

\[\sum U_j=0,\]
\[\sum z_jU_j=0.\]

Here \(z\) is axial location from a chosen reference plane. The second condition cancels the rotating transverse moment; its vector direction includes a common quarter-turn from the cross product that drops out of the zero condition. If the first condition holds, shifting the axial origin leaves the second condition unchanged.

Split one known unbalance between two planes

A rigid rotor has an 18 g excess mass at radius 40 mm, angle 0°, and axial location 120 mm between two correction planes at \(z_A=0\) and \(z_B=300\,\mathrm{mm}\). Its unbalance is \(720\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) along the reference direction. Moment balance gives \(300 U_B=-120(720)\), hence \(U_B=-288\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). Force balance then gives \(U_A=-432\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). Both corrections point at 180° because the original mass lies between the planes.

At correction radius 60 mm these vectors require added masses 4.80 g in plane B and 7.20 g in plane A. If material is removed instead, the equivalent removed masses lie at the original 0° clock position. At 1800 rpm the original force amplitude was \(0.000720(188.5)^2=25.6\,\mathrm N\). Doubling speed quadruples that force, yet the same mass corrections still balance the assumed rigid rotor.

Identify what a single-plane test can miss

Consider instead two unbalances \(+400\) and \(-400\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) in planes 200 mm apart. Their resultant force is zero at every speed. On ideal low-friction supports there is no preferred heavy side in a static test. Their couple magnitude at 1800 rpm is \(0.000400(0.200)(188.5)^2=2.84\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), sufficient to generate equal opposing reactions at bearings. Measurements in two support planes can distinguish this pattern from a net shaking force. Near a flexible rotor’s bending critical speed, shaft deflection changes the mass distribution and response phase; the rigid-body correction formulas then require a modal or measured influence-coefficient model.

Interpret correction accuracy

An angular placement error also leaves a residual vector. A correction of the correct magnitude set one degree away leaves a residual approximately \(U\pi/180\) for small angle, since the tip of the vector follows a circle. At large operating speed even this residual produces a force scaling with the square of speed.

Derive a balance correction from a trial mass

An assembled rotor rarely reveals the angular location of its excess mass directly. A once-per-revolution vibration measurement supplies an amplitude and phase referred to a shaft mark. At fixed operating speed and linear support conditions, represent the measured complex vibration \(V\) by \(V=\alpha U\), where \(U\) is the complex unbalance in one plane and \(\alpha\) is the response per unit unbalance. This coefficient includes bearing compliance, dynamic amplification and the sensor’s phase lag. The phase of largest displacement is therefore not necessarily the angle where correction mass should be added.

Record the original vibration \(V_0\), add a known trial unbalance \(U_t\) and measure \(V_t\) at the same speed with the same phase reference. Subtraction cancels the original signal and gives

\[\alpha=(V_t-V_0)/U_t,\]
\[U_c=-V_0/\alpha .\]

Remove the trial before installing the calculated total correction. If the trial remains attached, install only \(U_c-U_t\) as further correction. Subtract complex vectors, not their magnitudes. A trial too small to resolve above measurement noise makes the inferred phase and correction unreliable; an unnecessarily large trial can take the rotor outside the measured linear range. A repeat baseline after removing the mass can expose a changing thermal or mounting condition.

Convert measured shaft phase into a physical correction

At fixed speed a laser reports an original synchronous displacement \(V_0=(30+40i)\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). Add a 5.0 g trial mass at a 40 mm radius at the reference angle, so \(U_t=200\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). The new reading is \(V_t=(50+60i)\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The influence coefficient is \((20+20i)/200=0.10+0.10i\) micrometres per g mm. The required correction is

\[U_c=-\frac{30+40i}{0.10+0.10i}=-(350+50i)\,\mathrm{g\,mm}.\]

Its magnitude is \(353.6\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) and angle is 188.1° from the trial reference in the same positive rotational convention. With a correction radius of 50 mm, add about 7.07 g after removing the trial. Multiplying the correction vector by the measured coefficient gives \(-30-40i\) micrometres, cancelling the baseline in the linear model. If the trial must stay, the extra vector is \(-550-50i\) g mm. Installing the full correction in addition to the trial would leave its trial response as a predictable residual.

Single-plane correction minimises one measured response at one speed. For a long rotor with a separate couple unbalance, a response measured at one support may vanish while the opposite support continues to vibrate. Measuring both supports under trial masses in two planes produces a two-by-two complex influence matrix. Nearly proportional measured columns mean that the two trial planes cannot be distinguished reliably at those sensor locations, a conditioning issue that no amount of arithmetic precision can cure.

The initial amplitude in the example is 50 micrometres; the trial amplitude is 78.1 micrometres. Their scalar difference, 28.1 micrometres, happens to be close to the actual complex response increment’s magnitude of 28.3 micrometres, but the scalar comparison contains no correction angle. Even apparently favourable numbers cannot replace the shaft reference.

A balanced rotor can load its bearings during a turn

After a rotor is balanced, its spin angular momentum still has to change direction whenever its supporting structure turns. Consider an axisymmetric flywheel rotating rapidly at \(\omega_s\) about unit vector \(e_s\), with polar inertia \(I_s\). If its axis is steered at angular rate \(\Omega\), then \(\dot e_s=\Omega\times e_s\). Isolate the spin part of angular momentum and differentiate in an inertial frame:

\[H_s=I_s\omega_s e_s,\]
\[M_s=I_s\dot\omega_s e_s+\Omega\times H_s.\]

The first term spins the rotor up or down. The second turns its axis without changing its spin energy to leading order. For steady orthogonal steering the moment magnitude is \(I_s|\omega_s\Omega|\). Components of \(\Omega\) parallel to the spin axis do not turn the angular momentum. For slow regular precession of a fast rotor this spin-dominated relation also gives \(\Omega\simeq M/H_s\) for a transverse applied moment. Rapid steering acceleration, strong nutation or significant transverse angular momentum requires the full tensor equations.

A horizontally mounted flywheel on a turntable has \(I_s=0.12\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), spins in the positive \(x\) direction at 3000 rpm and is turned about positive \(z\) at 0.40 rad/s. Angular momentum is \(0.12(3000)(2\pi/60)=37.7\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) along \(x\). Since \(e_z\times e_x=e_y\), the bearings must exert 15.1 N m along positive \(y\) on the rotor. A 0.30 m support separation along \(x\) requires a pair of equal opposite vertical bearing forces of 50.3 N to deliver this moment, in addition to weight and ordinary radial loads. Reversing the steering reverses the couple immediately at a given speed. A rigid imbalance force at fixed shaft speed does not reverse just because steering changes direction. That distinction is a practical way to diagnose the origin of a load measured on a moving platform.

A single-plane correction should cancel the original unbalance vector. If its magnitude ratio is \(k\) and its angle differs from perfect opposition by \(\delta\), vector addition leaves the normalised magnitude \(\sqrt{1+k^2-2k\cos\delta}\).

Virtual work and the forces hidden by a constraint

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A bead follows a wire, a pendulum remains the same length, and a rolling wheel obeys a relation between translation and rotation. Their reduced equations describe the motion but can conceal the largest forces in the apparatus. Determine which forces disappear under a virtual displacement, recover their values, and decide when the assumed motion ceases to be physically admissible.

Hold the clock fixed when defining a virtual displacement

A system of particles has Cartesian positions \(\mathbf r_i\) and applied forces \(\mathbf F_i\). A holonomic constraint is an equation \(g_\alpha(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N,t)=0\). At a fixed instant an admissible infinitesimal change of configuration obeys \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\). The physical velocities obey a different relation, \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\partial_tg_\alpha=0\). A virtual displacement compares geometrically allowed configurations at the same time. It has no prescribed duration and makes no claim that the system's momentum actually points along it.

The distinction becomes measurable for a bead on a moving horizontal table. Every allowed virtual displacement has zero vertical component, so the table's upward normal force gives zero virtual work. An upward-moving table can nevertheless raise the bead and perform positive ordinary work. Ordinary power involves the actual velocity, which includes the table motion. A careless statement that constraint forces do no work confuses two different operations. For a fixed ideal frictionless surface the real velocity is tangent as well, and vanishing virtual work then does imply vanishing ordinary power.

Let \(\mathbf R_i\) be the reaction forces and use \(m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i+\mathbf R_i\). Multiply by an admissible \(\delta\mathbf r_i\) and sum over particles. We call the constraints ideal when \(\sum_i\mathbf R_i\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\) for every allowed virtual displacement. Eliminating those reactions gives d'Alembert's principle,

\[\sum_i(\mathbf F_i-m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0.\]

This is a dynamical equation expressed through a static-looking virtual-work test. It applies instantaneously along the motion. It does not assert that the total force vanishes or that the acceleration is an independent virtual displacement. One advantage is that the detailed construction of a rigid support need not be known to determine the admissible tangential acceleration; its force can be calculated afterwards when designing the support.

Pass from virtual work to equations for coordinates

Choose independent coordinates \(q_a\) with \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r_i(q,t)\) and independent virtual increments \(\delta q_a\). The velocity is \(\sum_a\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i\,\dot q_a+\partial_t\mathbf r_i\). With kinetic energy \(T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i^2/2\), differentiation at fixed \(q,t\) gives

\[\partial_{\dot q_a}T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Differentiate in time. The product rule produces an acceleration term and a second term involving \(d(\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i)/dt\). The latter equals \(\partial_{q_a}\dot{\mathbf r}_i\), with the generalised velocities held fixed, and supplies \(\partial_{q_a}T\). Hence

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_a}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_a}=\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf r_i}{\partial q_a}.\]

The projected applied force is

\[Q_a=\sum_i\mathbf F_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Independence of the coordinate variations in d'Alembert's principle gives one equation \(d(\partial_{\dot q_a}T)/dt-\partial_{q_a}T=Q_a\) per coordinate. When \(\mathbf F_i\) comes from a potential \(V(q,t)\), the conservative contribution is \(-\partial_{q_a}V\). Other contributions such as a measured resisting torque remain explicit. A coordinate expressed as an angle has a generalised force in joules per radian; a coordinate expressed as a length has one in newtons. Checking \(Q_a\,dq_a\) as work avoids mistaking a generalised force for a Cartesian component.

For a simple pendulum of length \(l\) and mass \(m\), choose \(\theta\) measured from downward vertical. Then \(T=ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\) and \(V=mgl(1-\cos\theta)\). The equation \(ml^2\ddot\theta=-mgl\sin\theta\) follows at once. The string tension vanished under the angular displacement. It has not been shown to vanish physically. Projecting Newton's equation onto the direction from the bead toward the pivot gives

\[T_{\rm string}=mg\cos\theta+ml\dot\theta^2.\]

This second observable is sensitive to speed even when the reduced angular acceleration depends only on angle.

Recover multipliers before choosing coordinates

Cartesian coordinates keep reaction directions explicit. Put all coordinates into a vector \(x\), let \(M\) be the mass matrix and let \(G\) contain the rows \(\nabla g_\alpha\). The tangent virtual displacements are precisely those satisfying \(G\delta x=0\). A reaction doing zero virtual work must be a linear combination of the normal covectors, \(R=G^T\lambda\), when the constraints are independent. Newton's equation becomes

\[M\ddot x=F+G^T\lambda.\]

Differentiate the constraints twice along the trajectory: \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\), where \(\gamma\) includes the known velocity, curvature and explicit time terms. Substituting for \(\ddot x\) determines reactions from a much smaller system,

\[(GM^{-1}G^T)\lambda=-\gamma-GM^{-1}F.\]

For any vector \(c\), \(c^TGM^{-1}G^Tc=(G^Tc)^TM^{-1}(G^Tc)\) is positive if \(G\) has independent rows and particle masses are positive. Dependent constraint equations make the individual multipliers ambiguous. For instance writing both \(g=0\) and \(2g=0\) declares the same geometrical restriction twice. Only the combined reaction is then determined. The magnitude of a raw multiplier also depends on the units and normalisation of \(g\). Reporting the force \(G^T\lambda\) makes comparisons between alternative constraint descriptions meaningful.

Use \(g=(x^2+y^2-l^2)/2=0\) for a planar pendulum, with \(y\) upward, so the gradient is the radius vector \(\mathbf r\). Newton's equation reads \(m\ddot{\mathbf r}=-mg\hat y+\lambda\mathbf r\), where the symbol \(g\) in the gravitational term denotes the acceleration magnitude. The acceleration constraint \(\mathbf r\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}=-v^2\) gives

\[\lambda=(mgy-mv^2)/l^2.\]

The inward tension is \(-\lambda l=mv^2/l-mgy/l\). Since \(y=-l\cos\theta\), this is the radial-force result already obtained. The multiplier in this convention has units of force per length. Using the radial-distance constraint instead would make its magnitude a force.

Background · What if the support can push but cannot pull?

Replacing a surface by an equality constraint assumes the object stays in contact. A particle sliding along the inside of a smooth vertical circular track illustrates the additional physical test. At the top, gravity and the inward normal reaction together must supply the centripetal acceleration.

\[N=\frac{mv^2}{R}-mg,\qquad N\geq0.\]

At a top speed below \(\sqrt{gR}\) the equality-constrained solution requires negative \(N\). An ordinary track cannot exert that pull. Its normal force then falls to zero and the particle must follow free flight until it meets the track again. A bead threaded on a wire could receive the oppositely directed force and would follow a different trajectory. Both models have the same geometrical path during contact.

Recognise an inequality at a contact

A bead threaded on a stiff loop can experience reaction in either direction, whereas a string can pull only. A surface without adhesion can push only. Derive the constrained motion, calculate its reaction, and compare the required sign with the physical mechanism. When a calculated tension turns negative, replace the constrained trajectory by free flight until a new contact event. Continuing to integrate the pendulum equation would retain a mathematically valid circle and a physically unavailable pushing string.

A mass starts at the bottom of a frictionless vertical loop with speed \(v_b\). Energy conservation gives \(v_{\rm top}^2=v_b^2-4gl\), since the top is \(2l\) higher. A string just remaining taut at the top requires \(T_{\rm top}=mv_{\rm top}^2/l-mg\ge0\), giving \(v_b^2\ge5gl\). The energy-only criterion for reaching the elevation is \(v_b^2\ge4gl\); the interval between these two values illustrates why a trajectory test must include reactions. With \(l=0.80\,{\rm m}\), the threshold bottom speed is \(6.26\,{\rm m/s}\). At this threshold the bottom tension is \(6mg\), six times the weight, even though the tension is about to vanish at the top.

Recover the forces on a wedge that is free to slide

Place a wedge of mass \(M\) on a smooth horizontal floor and a block of mass \(m\) on its smooth upper face. Let the slope descend to the right at angle \(\alpha\). Choose \(X\) as the wedge's horizontal position and \(s\) as distance down the face. The block's coordinates apart from fixed offsets are \(x=X+s\cos\alpha,\ y=-s\sin\alpha\). Its relative velocity points down the slope but its absolute acceleration need not. The kinetic energy contains a mixed term \(m\dot X\dot s\cos\alpha\). Dropping that term is equivalent to holding the wedge stationary while allowing it to recoil, two incompatible mechanical models.

The reduced equations are \((M+m)\ddot X+m\ddot s\cos\alpha=0\) and \(\ddot s+\ddot X\cos\alpha=g\sin\alpha\). The first is conservation of total horizontal momentum, since the block–wedge normal force is internal and the floor is frictionless. Elimination gives

\[\ddot s=(M+m)g\sin\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha),\]
\[\ddot X=-mg\sin\alpha\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

The acceleration down the moving face exceeds \(g\sin\alpha\) because the wedge moves left as the block slides right. Nevertheless the absolute vertical acceleration of the block remains less than or equal to free fall because the upper face pushes upward.

To find the block's normal force, project its absolute acceleration onto the outward unit normal \((\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)\). The part proportional to \(\ddot s\) cancels, leaving \(\ddot X\sin\alpha\). Newton's equation along that direction gives

\[N=m(g\cos\alpha+\ddot X\sin\alpha)=mMg\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

With \(M=3.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=1.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(\alpha=30^\circ\), one finds \(\ddot s=6.04\,{\rm m/s^2}\),

\[\ddot X=-1.31\,{\rm m/s^2},\]
\[N=7.84\,{\rm N}.\]

The normal based on a stationary wedge would be \(8.50\,{\rm N}\). A force sensor under the moving face could distinguish the two models even when a short record of relative slope displacement cannot resolve the difference.

Which reaction does no virtual work here? The normal force on the block has virtual work \(N\hat n\cdot(\delta X\hat x+\delta s\hat t)=N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\). The opposite normal force on the wedge gives \(-N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\), so the sum cancels. Considering only the block and asserting zero work from the normal for arbitrary \(\delta X\) would be incorrect. At the real velocities the same reaction transfers energy between the bodies even though its net internal power on their pair is zero. The floor's normal reaction balances the vertical momentum change of the whole system. During the slide it is \((M+m)g-m\ddot s\sin\alpha\), less than total weight while the block accelerates downwards.

Take the limits as an additional test. At fixed \(m,\alpha\), increasing \(M\) to infinity returns the familiar fixed-wedge results. At a nearly horizontal face, downslope acceleration and wedge recoil vanish, restoring the full block weight as normal force. At a vertical face the normal vanishes and the block falls. These limits hold because the contact has no friction and can be maintained with a nonnegative normal. A moving wedge of independently prescribed acceleration would replace the horizontal momentum equation by a control input, changing both the relative acceleration and the support force.

Distinguish rolling constraints from purely geometric restrictions

A cylinder of radius \(R\), mass \(m\) and axial inertia \(I\) rolls without slip down a fixed rough incline. Choose coordinate \(s\) down the incline and spin angle \(\phi\) in the corresponding sense. The constraint \(\dot s=R\dot\phi\) is integrable in this one-dimensional motion, so it can be inserted as \(s-R\phi=\mathrm{constant}\). The resulting kinetic energy is \((m+I/R^2)\dot s^2/2\). The reduced equation gives

\[\ddot s=g\sin\alpha/[1+I/(mR^2)].\]

Reconstruct the static friction from translation, \(f=g m\sin\alpha-m\ddot s\), directed up the slope, and compare it with the available maximum \(\mu_s mg\cos\alpha\).

For a homogeneous solid cylinder \(I=mR^2/2\), the speed-up is \(2g\sin\alpha/3\) and friction magnitude \(mg\sin\alpha/3\). A ring of the same mass and radius has \(I=mR^2\), half the gravitational downslope acceleration and half the downslope weight component as friction. At \(30^\circ\) the minimum static friction coefficients are respectively \(\tan30^\circ/3=0.192\) and \(\tan30^\circ/2=0.289\). Two objects on the same surface can therefore diverge qualitatively because the ring slips while the cylinder still rolls. Treating an assumed no-slip relation as exact without testing that inequality can produce an unphysical result despite apparently consistent energy conservation.

The friction on a rigid body rolling without slip on a fixed surface has zero power because its instantaneous point of contact is stationary. The force still changes both linear and angular momentum. On a moving conveyor the contact velocity is no longer zero in the laboratory; friction can supply mechanical energy even while the relative contact velocity vanishes. For a wheel steering on a plane the restrictions \(\dot x=R\dot\phi\cos\psi,\ \dot y=R\dot\phi\sin\psi\) depend on heading \(\psi\). In general they cannot be integrated into two configuration equations relating \(x,y,\phi,\psi\) globally. d'Alembert's fixed-time allowed variations still project out ideal rolling reactions, but replacing those velocity constraints by invented holonomic coordinates gives the wrong dynamics. The distinction can be seen geometrically by rolling and steering through a closed sequence of steering operations that leaves a net displacement.

Find loads on two supports of a suspended bar

A rigid horizontal bar of mass \(m\) and length \(2a\) is supported at its ends by two vertical inextensible light cords. At one instant both cords are vertical and the bar is horizontal. An extra downward load of magnitude \(F\) is applied at horizontal coordinate \(s\) relative to the centre. Ask first for a static experiment. The two vertical reaction forces \(T_L,T_R\) obey force balance \(T_L+T_R=mg+F\) and moment balance \(a(T_R-T_L)=sF\). This gives

\[T_R=(mg+F+sF/a)/2,\]
\[T_L=(mg+F-sF/a)/2.\]

The lever arms encode geometry that the vertical balance alone cannot provide.

The same result can be obtained from virtual variations if the reactions are kept as multipliers until the end. For small vertical translation \(y\) of the centre and small tilt \(\theta\), the endpoint vertical displacements are \(\delta y-a\delta\theta\) on the left and \(\delta y+a\delta\theta\) on the right. The load point displacement is \(\delta y+s\delta\theta\). Before eliminating the endpoint constraints, project all applied and reaction forces against arbitrary independent \(\delta y,\delta\theta\); vanishing of both coefficients gives the preceding balances. After imposing both endpoint constraints directly, these two variations vanish and the reduced model for vertical motion contains no equation from which the support loads can be read. Removing a degree of freedom can remove precisely the observable of interest unless reactions are reconstructed.

For \(m=2.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.40\,{\rm m}\), \(F=12\,{\rm N}\) and \(s=0.25\,{\rm m}\), the total load is \(31.62\,{\rm N}\) and the load difference \(7.50\,{\rm N}\). Hence \(T_R=19.56\,{\rm N}\), \(T_L=12.06\,{\rm N}\). Exchanging the load from right to left reverses the difference while preserving the sum. This symmetry is a calibrating manoeuvre for two load cells with different offsets: subtract readings from mirrored configurations to isolate the lever-arm response, and compare their sum to the known total weight and applied force.

Now imagine three vertical supports at \(-a,0,+a\). Their three tensions enter only two independent balances. Perfectly rigid constraints determine no unique allocation to the centre support. The ambiguity is physical; the stiffnesses, preloads and small height differences of real supports decide the result. With three identical unstrained linear springs and a centred load \(F\), each spring extends equally if the bar stays level, so each carries one third of the total weight. If the middle spring is twice as stiff and all have the same unloaded height, it carries one half and each outer spring one quarter. Both answers satisfy exactly the same force and moment balances. Reporting a unique value from a singular ideal multiplier system would manufacture an unmeasured constitutive assumption.

Test an acceleration constraint against drift of the position itself

Solving \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\) guarantees the correct instantaneous normal acceleration at a state lying on the constraint manifold with tangent velocity. It does not magically repair earlier position or velocity errors in approximate time integration. For a particle constrained to a circle, monitor \(g=(r^2-l^2)/2\), \(\dot g=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\), and the computed force. A small tangential displacement can give tiny instantaneous force error while the radius gradually drifts. Integrating an acceleration-level constraint twice without a position or velocity correction can cause substantial mismatch over many revolutions.

A simple projection illustrates what each correction does. Normalise an approximate position vector to length \(l\), then remove the radial component of velocity by \(\mathbf v\leftarrow\mathbf v-\hat r(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)\). The corrected point and tangent satisfy the constraints, but the velocity correction removes kinetic energy \(m(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)^2/2\), and the position change can alter potential energy. Projection is therefore a numerical operation whose physical invariant errors must be measured. A structure-preserving constrained integrator builds the position and momentum conditions into the step and often gives better long-time behaviour. Compare convergence of reaction, constraint residual and energy separately instead of treating a rounded radius as proof of a correct force.

A pendulum on a free cart tests the complete mass matrix

Mount a pendulum pivot on a cart of mass \(M\) moving horizontally on a smooth track. The bob has mass \(m\), length \(l\) and downward angle \(\theta\). Choose the cart position \(X\) and use bob coordinates \(X+l\sin\theta,-l\cos\theta\). The kinetic energy is \((M+m)\dot X^2/2+ml\dot X\dot\theta\cos\theta+ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\). Two different off-diagonal mass terms would contradict its origin as a quadratic form; the same factor \(ml\cos\theta\) multiplies the companion acceleration in each equation. This is a useful check on a manually assembled multibody model.

The equations are

\[(M+m)\ddot X+ml(\ddot\theta\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2\sin\theta)=0,\]
\[l\ddot\theta+\ddot X\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0.\]

In the small-angle regime the horizontal centre of mass moves uniformly. Choosing zero total momentum gives

\[\dot X\simeq-ml\dot\theta/(M+m).\]

Substitute its derivative into the angular equation to obtain

\[\ddot\theta+[(M+m)g/(Ml)]\theta=0.\]

Releasing the support raises the oscillation frequency because the effective angular inertia falls while the gravitational stiffness stays the same. The result is a statement about the relative pendulum angle, measured from an inertial vertical, with an unforced horizontally moving support.

For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=0.20\,{\rm kg}\) and \(l=0.50\,{\rm m}\), the small-angle period is

\[2\pi\sqrt{Ml/[(M+m)g]}=1.229\,{\rm s}.\]

Fixing the cart with a clamp gives \(1.419\,{\rm s}\) instead. At zero total horizontal momentum the cart displacement from its equilibrium is approximately \(-0.125\,\theta\) metres, so a \(0.10\,{\rm rad}\) angle corresponds to \(12.5\,{\rm mm}\) of recoil. Simultaneous position measurements on cart and bob should reconstruct a horizontal centre of mass that is stationary to within tracking uncertainty. A missing cart inertia or a wrongly filled cross term can alter both period and recoil; checking just one number may not identify which error occurred.

The reaction on the moving bob can again be recovered. Its radial acceleration along the outward string direction is \(\ddot X\sin\theta-l\dot\theta^2\). Thus the tension is \(T=m[g\cos\theta-\ddot X\sin\theta+l\dot\theta^2]\). The acceleration of the support appears explicitly. Reusing the fixed-pivot formula at all angles would omit a term that vanishes accidentally at the bottom. To test the sign, observe a rightward tilted pendulum whose pivot accelerates right: the support's acceleration has an outward projection along the tilted string, reducing the inward tension needed for the same relative motion. Substituting a directly measured cart acceleration provides an independent load-cell prediction.

Decide which force a sensor actually reports

Reaction forces can be reported as tension along a member, normal traction at a contact, total support load on a floor, or a single Cartesian component read by a load cell. A rigid-body idealisation alone does not specify a sensor's orientation or the weight of its housing. For a fixed pendulum, for example, the string carries tension \(T\), while a vertical force channel at its pivot reads the vertical string contribution \(T\cos\theta\). A horizontal channel reads \(T\sin\theta\). The force on the pivot is opposite the force exerted on the bob. Selecting the wrong sign or leaving out the fixture's own weight can create a discrepancy even when the dynamical calculation is correct.

Suppose an ideal load cell with its sensitive axis vertical is tared with the fixture installed but the \(0.40\,{\rm kg}\) bob removed. When the bob is held at \(60^\circ\) by a separate purely tangential actuator and is otherwise stationary, the radial balance gives

\[T=mg\cos60^\circ=1.96\,{\rm N}.\]

The pivot's vertical reading attributable to the string is \(0.981\,{\rm N}\). The tangential actuator supplies the remaining vertical support as well as a horizontal component. Once the actuator releases, instantaneously the same tension and reading hold because radial speed is still zero. At the bottom of the subsequent swing the load jumps to the dynamical value obtained earlier, even though no upper fixture mass has changed.

A student hangs the bob at the same \(60^\circ\) angle using a separate horizontal cord instead of a tangential actuator. Should the pivot cell read the preceding \(0.981\,{\rm N}\)?

Work through the solution

A schematic that shows only the bob angle is insufficient to infer the support load without saying how the angle is maintained.

In the free cart experiment bearing drag is weak but measurable. Can an observed frequency shift distinguish it from a wrongly assumed cart mass?

Work through the solution

Locate contact loss on a smooth convex surface

A small block starts sliding from near rest just to one side of the top of a smooth fixed sphere of radius \(R\). Measure angle \(\theta\) from upward vertical. Energy gives \(v^2=2gR(1-\cos\theta)\) during contact, while the inward radial equation reads \(mg\cos\theta-N=mv^2/R\). Thus \(N=mg(3\cos\theta-2)\). Contact first vanishes at \(\cos\theta=2/3\), or \(48.2^\circ\). For a sphere of radius \(0.50\,{\rm m}\), this occurs after a vertical drop \(R/3=0.167\,{\rm m}\) and at speed \(1.81\,{\rm m/s}\). Once the block leaves, the same spherical coordinates can still be used to describe position, but \(r=R\) is no longer an equation of motion. Continue with the measured tangential velocity and free gravitational acceleration. Enforcing the sphere condition beyond release would make the computed normal negative and predict a surface that pulls on the block. The zero normal, the geometric release angle and the subsequent ballistic arc provide three independent observables for the same force reconstruction.

Action density, stress waves and the boundary that reflects them

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A chain of coupled masses becomes an elastic field when the wavelength is long compared with the spacing. Derive the field equation and its boundary traction from the same variation, follow energy and stress across a junction, and decide whether a measured pulse is limited by material waves or by numerical dispersion.

Pass from a discrete rod to an action density

Let equal masses of spacing \(a\) move longitudinally by \(u_j(t)\), with nearest-neighbour spring constant \(k_s\) and mass \(m_s\) per site. Their Lagrangian is \(\sum_j[m_s\dot u_j^2/2-k_s(u_{j+1}-u_j)^2/2]\). For variations smooth on the spacing, \((u_{j+1}-u_j)/a\to u_x\) and \(\sum_j a\,f_j\to\int f(x)\,dx\). Hold the line density \(\rho A=m_s/a\) and axial rigidity \(EA=k_s a\) fixed. The continuum Lagrangian density per unit reference length is

\[{\cal L}=\rho A\,u_t^2/2-EA\,u_x^2/2,\]

with \(A\) area, \(\rho\) volume density and \(E\) Young modulus. Both terms have units of joules per metre. This is a slender-rod model with small strain, primarily uniaxial stress and wavelengths long compared with the cross-sectional dimensions.

Vary the field in the action over \([0,L]\times[t_1,t_2]\). Integration of \(\rho A\,u_t\delta u_t\) by parts in time, with fixed endpoint fields, produces \(-\rho A\,u_{tt}\delta u\). Integration of \(-EAu_x\delta u_x\) by parts in space produces \(+\partial_x(EAu_x)\delta u\) and a remaining term \(-[EAu_x\delta u]_0^L\). Thus the interior equation is \(\rho A u_{tt}=\partial_x(EAu_x)\), and the end force conjugate to displacement is axial force \(N=EAu_x\). Adding external work \(F_L(t)\delta u(L)\) at the right face yields

\[N(L)=F_L.\]

A prescribed displacement instead imposes \(\delta u(L)=0\), leaving its reaction as an output. Keeping this boundary term prevents prescribing displacement and independent traction simultaneously at the same end of a second-order rod.

Determine the energy flux and characteristic impedance

Assume time-independent coefficients. Multiply the field equation by \(u_t\) and rearrange to obtain

\[\partial_t[\rho A u_t^2/2+EAu_x^2/2]+\partial_x[-EAu_xu_t]=0.\]

The energy flux is \(-Nu_t\), with positive sign denoting energy moving towards increasing \(x\). The minus sign follows the convention of positive tensile \(N\): positive mechanical power entering through a right face is \(Nu_t\), which is inward with respect to the rod on its left. This identity controls energy transport across both smooth material changes and sharp joints.

For a homogeneous right-moving wave \(u=F(x-ct)\), \(c=\sqrt{E/\rho}\), particle velocity \(v=u_t=-cF'\) and axial force \(N=EAF'=-Zv\), where \(Z=\rho A c=EA/c\). A left-moving wave has \(N=+Zv\). The impedance is force divided by particle velocity and has units \({\rm kg/s}\). If one instead uses stress, impedance is \(\rho c\) per unit area. Doubling the area of the same material keeps wave speed unchanged but doubles the force–velocity impedance. A transit-time measurement and a reflection measurement therefore provide independent material or geometry information.

For a rod with \(E=70\,{\rm GPa}\), \(\rho=2700\,{\rm kg/m^3}\) and \(A=1.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\), \(c\approx5092\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(Z\approx1375\,{\rm kg/s}\). A right-going compressive pulse with particle speed \(0.020\,{\rm m/s}\) carries axial force \(-27.5\,{\rm N}\), compressive stress \(0.275\,{\rm MPa}\), and instantaneous power \(Zv^2=0.550\,{\rm W}\) across a section. A rectangular approximation lasting \(100\,\mu{\rm s}\) carries about \(55\,\mu{\rm J}\) and occupies \(0.509\,{\rm m}\). The pulse speed is kilometres per second; the material particles move only millimetres per second. Treating these as the same velocity would spoil force, impulse and energy estimates.

Match a bonded interface and distinguish amplitude from power

At a perfect massless bond displacement and axial force are continuous. For an incident right-going velocity amplitude \(v_i\) in rod 1, let \(v_r\) be reflected left-going velocity and \(v_t\) transmitted right-going velocity. Velocity continuity gives

\[v_i+v_r=v_t.\]

Force continuity gives

\[-Z_1v_i+Z_1v_r=-Z_2v_t.\]

Thus

\[r_v=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[t_v=\frac{2Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[{\cal R}=r_v^2,\]
\[{\cal T}=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1}t_v^2=\frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1+Z_2)^2}.\]

The power fractions add to one for real positive impedances. The reflected force coefficient has opposite sign to the reflected velocity coefficient because the wave direction reverses. At a free end \(Z_2\to0\), reflected velocity equals incident velocity, so end velocity doubles while total force vanishes. At a rigid end \(Z_2\to\infty\), velocity reverses and force doubles. The sign a strain gauge reports can therefore differ from that of a velocity-sensitive optical measurement at the same reflection.

A step from the worked rod into one of the same material with twice the cross-sectional area gives \(Z_2=2Z_1\), \(r_v=-1/3\), \(t_v=2/3\), and power fractions \(1/9\) and \(8/9\). A reduced transmitted velocity amplitude does not imply that most energy is reflected. If the interface includes a concentrated mass \(M_b\), force jumps instead obey \(N_2-N_1=M_b\dot v_b\) with signs determined by forces exerted on that mass from either side. At high frequency the reaction of that mass can mimic a much stiffer termination. Frequency-dependent reflection is then an expected boundary effect, rather than evidence that the bulk modulus varies with frequency.

Connect continuum modes to their lattice predecessor

For a periodic chain the exact discrete equation is

\[m_s\ddot u_j=k_s(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}).\]

Substitute \(u_j=U e^{i(kja-\omega t)}\) to obtain

\[\omega=2\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.\]

In the first positive Brillouin zone, \(\omega/(ck)=\sin(ka/2)/(ka/2)\) and group velocity \(d\omega/dk=c\cos(ka/2)\), where \(c=a\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\). The continuum rod retains only the leading low-\(ka\) term. A wave with eight sites per wavelength has phase speed about \(0.9745c\) and group speed \(0.9239c\). A narrowband packet's peak can arrive later than the continuum prediction even when a single crest is timed fairly accurately.

A finite-difference code approximating the continuum rod can produce the same spatial dispersion with its mesh spacing in place of a physical lattice spacing. Distinguish them experimentally or numerically by changing the computational mesh while holding the physical structure fixed. Lattice effects persist as the numerical grid is refined; artificial spatial dispersion diminishes. A separate time-step study is needed if a time integrator also disperses the wave. Comparing both peak arrival and phase at two frequencies tests more of the dynamics than fitting a single transit time.

Derive transverse beam waves from curvature energy

A slender beam bending laterally is governed by a different elastic energy from the axial rod. Let \(w(x,t)\) denote small transverse deflection of its neutral axis. Under the Euler–Bernoulli assumption that cross sections remain plane and normal to that axis, the axial strain a distance \(z\) from the neutral axis is approximately \(-z w_{xx}\). Integrating the local elastic energy \(E\epsilon_{xx}^2/2\) across the section gives \(EI_Aw_{xx}^2/2\) per unit length, where \(I_A=\int z^2\,dA\) is a geometrical second moment of area, with units \({\rm m^4}\). It is distinct from a mass moment of inertia. The kinetic contribution uses line density \(\rho A\) as before.

Varying \({\cal L}=\rho A w_t^2/2-EI_Aw_{xx}^2/2\) requires integrating twice by parts in space. With constant coefficients the interior variation gives

\[\rho A w_{tt}+EI_A w_{xxxx}=0.\]

The boundary terms are \(-[EI_Aw_{xx}\delta w_x]+[(EI_Aw_{xx})_x\delta w]\). Thus slope variations pair with bending moments and displacement variations with shear forces, with signs set by the chosen face orientation. A clamped end prescribes both deflection and slope; a free end sets moment and shear to zero. A simply supported end prescribes deflection and zero bending moment. Using only one boundary condition per end, as for the axial rod, leaves the fourth-order beam problem underdetermined.

A sinusoid \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\) gives

\[\omega=\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}\,k^2.\]

Phase speed grows proportionally to wavenumber and group speed is twice phase speed in this model. A narrow transverse pulse in a long beam consequently spreads even in a perfectly homogeneous non-dissipative continuum. By contrast the axial rod was nondispersive in its long-wavelength continuum approximation. At high enough frequency the simple bending model omits rotary inertia, shear deformation and eventually three-dimensional elastic behaviour. Extrapolating its unbounded speed to arbitrary \(k\) would use the slender, low-frequency approximation far beyond its derivation.

For a simply supported beam with length \(L\), the sine modes satisfy both end conditions and give

\[\omega_n=(n\pi/L)^2\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}.\]

For a rectangular section of width \(w_b\) and thickness \(d\) in the bending direction, \(I_A=w_bd^3/12\) and \(A=w_bd\). Doubling thickness doubles all the natural angular frequencies at fixed \(L,E,\rho\), whereas doubling width alone leaves the ideal frequencies unchanged. The stiffness increases and so does the inertia. A beam with \(L=0.50\,{\rm m}\), \(d=5.0\,{\rm mm}\) and the earlier aluminium properties has \(\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}\approx7.35\,{\rm m^2/s}\), giving fundamental \(46.2\,{\rm Hz}\). The second bending mode is near four times that frequency, a clear difference from the approximately harmonic axial mode sequence.

Match a force pulse to a support spring and dashpot

A right termination exerts force on the rod through a dashpot with mechanical impedance \(Z_L\). For a real positive dashpot impedance the reflected velocity fraction is \(r=(Z-Z_L)/(Z+Z_L)\). Taking \(Z_L=Z\) gives zero reflection at the termination even though energy leaves a finite rod: the dashpot dissipates the arriving power. With \(Z_L=3Z\) one obtains a reflected velocity of \(-1/2\) of the incident value, a reflected energy fraction one quarter and absorbed power fraction three quarters. A free or rigid end also reflects energy, but it absorbs no net power in the ideal limit. A measured weak reflection alone is therefore not proof of weak damping inside the rod.

For a pure end spring of stiffness \(k_L\) attached to a rigid support, the velocity impedance is \(Z_L=k_L/(i\omega)\) under \(e^{i\omega t}\). It is imaginary, so the reflection coefficient has unit magnitude at every frequency and a frequency-dependent phase. At low frequency the spring impedance is large and the end looks nearly fixed. At high frequency its impedance is small and the end looks nearly free within the lumped approximation. An impulsive signal contains many frequencies and can return with a changed waveform even without any transmitted or dissipated energy. A support combining a resistor-like dashpot and a spring gives both amplitude loss and phase variation.

A measurement with strain gauges at two positions can separate forward and backward waves if their path delays are resolved. In a narrow frequency band the two records are linear combinations of advancing and returning phase factors. If the gauge spacing corresponds to an integer number of half wavelengths, these combinations become linearly dependent and the separation is ill-conditioned. Change the spacing, use a broadband pulse, or combine a velocity measurement with a strain measurement at one station so that the two characteristic directions enter with opposite signs. The relation \(v=(v_++v_-)\), \(N=Z(-v_++v_-)\) then gives a direct local decomposition.

Derive sound propagation from mass and momentum conservation

The displacement-action argument has a fluid analogue. Take an initially uniform fluid of density \(\rho_0\) and pressure \(p_0\), moving little from equilibrium in one spatial dimension. Write small perturbations \(\rho',p',v\). Linearised mass conservation is

\[\partial_t\rho'+\rho_0\partial_x v=0.\]

Momentum balance for a short fluid parcel is \(\rho_0\partial_tv=-\partial_xp'\). If compression over an acoustic cycle is approximately isentropic, \(p'=c_s^2\rho'\) with \(c_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\). Differentiate the first equation in time, eliminate the velocity derivative with momentum balance, and obtain \(\partial_t^2p'=c_s^2\partial_x^2p'\).

The thermodynamic derivative matters because pressure can respond differently when compression is slow enough for heat exchange. For a perfect gas with ratio of specific heats \(\gamma\), isentropic perturbations give

\[c_s^2=\gamma p_0/\rho_0.\]

Taking an isothermal derivative would give \(p_0/\rho_0\) instead. For \(p_0=101\,{\rm kPa}\), \(\rho_0=1.20\,{\rm kg/m^3}\), and \(\gamma=1.40\), the isentropic speed is about \(343\,{\rm m/s}\), while the isothermal calculation gives \(290\,{\rm m/s}\). A measured transit time can discriminate between these constitutive conditions. Wall heat exchange in a very narrow tube can alter which approximation is appropriate over a chosen frequency band.

For a right-moving sound wave \(p'=\rho_0c_s v\), with the pressure positive in compression. This differs in sign from the tensile-positive axial force used for the rod, so match physical force conventions before borrowing formulas across the two examples. The acoustic power per area is \(p'v\). For sinusoidal pressure with rms amplitude \(1.0\,{\rm Pa}\) in the stated gas, the mean intensity is

\[p_{\rm rms}^2/(\rho_0 c_s)=2.43\times10^{-3}\,{\rm W/m^2}.\]

A peak amplitude of \(1.0\,{\rm Pa}\) would yield half this number. Writing rms or peak explicitly prevents a routine factor-of-two error when comparing a microphone measurement with an energy flux.

Determine whether a measured transit reveals a modulus or a geometry

Suppose two axially loaded slender rods have measured wave speeds agreeing within their uncertainties. That alone determines only the ratio \(E/\rho\) under the slender-rod assumptions. Measuring force–velocity impedance \(Z=\rho A c\) and cross section as well gives \(\rho=Z/(Ac)\) and \(E=Zc/A\). With \(Z=1375\,{\rm kg/s}\), \(c=5092\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(A=1.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\), the inversion gives

\[\rho=2700\,{\rm kg/m^3},\]
\[E=70.0\,{\rm GPa}.\]

If area were overestimated by five percent, both inferred quantities would be underestimated by almost five percent while their ratio remained correct.

Now examine a thick elastic solid under a narrow compressional disturbance with wavelengths short compared with its transverse dimensions. Lateral deformation can be constrained by surrounding material. In an isotropic three-dimensional continuum the longitudinal plane-wave modulus is \(\lambda+2\mu_s\), where \(\lambda,\mu_s\) are Lamé coefficients, rather than the uniaxial-stress Young modulus of a slender rod. The shear-wave speed satisfies \(c_T^2=\mu_s/\rho\) and longitudinal speed \(c_L^2=(\lambda+2\mu_s)/\rho\). Measuring both, together with density, can recover the two independent elastic moduli. Using only one longitudinal speed to infer Young modulus without specifying the deformation geometry can give a physically incorrect value.

For \(\rho=2500\,{\rm kg/m^3}\), \(c_L=5000\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(c_T=3000\,{\rm m/s}\), these relations give

\[\mu_s=22.5\,{\rm GPa},\]
\[\lambda=17.5\,{\rm GPa}.\]

The implied Young modulus \(E=\mu_s(3\lambda+2\mu_s)/(\lambda+\mu_s)=54.8\,{\rm GPa}\), while \(\rho c_L^2=62.5\,{\rm GPa}\). Both numbers represent material stiffness, but they enter different mechanical boundary conditions. Derive orthogonality when a rod changes material along its length

For a rod with positive varying density \(\rho(x)\), area \(A(x)\) and modulus \(E(x)\), a separated free oscillation \(u(x,t)=\phi(x)\cos\omega t\) satisfies

\[-[EA\phi']'=\omega^2\rho A\phi.\]

Suppose two modes \(\phi_n,\phi_m\) satisfy the same homogeneous fixed or free boundary conditions and the correct continuity of displacement and axial force across every perfectly bonded interface. Multiply the first equation by \(\phi_m\), the second by \(\phi_n\), subtract and integrate. Interior terms produced by integration by parts cancel pairwise, including the interface flux terms, and the endpoint terms vanish. Thus

\[(\omega_n^2-\omega_m^2)\int\rho A\phi_n\phi_m\,dx=0.\]

Mode shapes with distinct frequencies are orthogonal under the mass-weighted inner product, not generally under a simple average of displacements measured at uniformly spaced stations. An initial velocity distribution \(v_0(x)\) projects onto mode \(n\) with coefficient \(\int\rho A v_0\phi_n\,dx/\int\rho A\phi_n^2\,dx\). In a rod with a heavy section, the same imposed velocity there stores more energy and carries more modal weight. An experiment interpreting raw camera amplitudes without line-density weighting can misassign energy among modes. Normalising each measured mode shape to unit maximum does not fix the mass weighting needed for energies.

Multiplying one eigenvalue equation by its own shape gives

\[\omega_n^2=\int EA(\phi_n')^2dx/\int\rho A\phi_n^2dx.\]

For an admissible trial function satisfying prescribed displacement conditions, the same quotient bounds the fundamental squared frequency from above when rigid translations are excluded. Consider a uniform fixed–free rod and trial \(\phi=x/L\). The numerator is \(EA/L\) and denominator \(\rho A L/3\), giving \(\omega_{\rm trial}=\sqrt3\,c/L\). The exact fundamental is \(\pi c/(2L)\), so this one-parameter shape overestimates frequency by about \(10.3\%\). It need not satisfy the free-end traction condition itself because that is a natural condition of the variational minimiser; it does need to satisfy the essential fixed-end displacement.

For a free–free rod the constant displacement mode has zero frequency. Using an arbitrary positive trial function without removing its mass-weighted mean can drive a quotient towards zero by adding a large constant. To estimate the first deforming mode impose orthogonality to the rigid translation, \(\int\rho A\phi\,dx=0\). This has an immediate mechanical interpretation: the deformation mode should not translate the centre of mass of an isolated rod. The null mode and its mass-weighted constraint reappear in numerical eigenproblems built from discretised stiffness and mass matrices.

Viscous stresses, duct resistance and wall-driven flow

Fluid mechanics

Derive a developed velocity profile from momentum balance and distinguish a moving-wall contribution from a pressure-driven contribution.

From first principles

Derive the parabolic velocity profile between stationary plates

Consider steady fully developed flow between wide parallel plates at \(y=0\) and \(y=H\). Assume incompressible Newtonian fluid, constant viscosity \(\mu\), no slip, no variation across the channel width and no streamwise body force. Let \(G=-dp/dx>0\) drive flow in the positive \(x\) direction. Since the axial velocity depends only on \(y\), the convective acceleration is zero. A thin fluid layer balances pressure force against the change of shear stress across its thickness.

\[0=-\frac{dp}{dx}+\frac{d\tau_{xy}}{dy},\]
\[\tau_{xy}=\mu\frac{du}{dy},\]
\[\mu u''=-G.\]

Integrate twice and apply zero velocity at both walls. The resulting velocity is largest halfway between the walls, where the shear stress vanishes. Integrating the profile gives discharge per unit width.

\[u(y)=\frac{G}{2\mu}y(H-y),\]
\[\frac Qb=\int_0^Hu(y)\,dy=\frac{GH^3}{12\mu}.\]

The cube of the gap arises because widening the channel increases both conducting area and the speed at a given pressure gradient. A moving wall adds a linear shear-driven profile through the boundary condition because the governing equation is linear.

Derivation and applications

Quantify the effect of wall slip on laminar pipe resistance

A pressure-flow calibration of a small tube can reveal a change in the assumed wall condition. Consider steady, fully developed, axisymmetric flow of a Newtonian liquid of viscosity \(\mu\) in a round tube of radius \(R\). Take the pressure gradient magnitude \(G=-dp/dz>0\) and neglect body forces along the tube. Balance pressure force against viscous drag on a coaxial fluid cylinder of radius \(r\). Positive axial speed decreases approaching the wall.

\[G\pi r^2=-\mu\frac{du}{dr}\,2\pi r,\]
\[\frac{du}{dr}=-\frac{Gr}{2\mu}.\]

Allow a Navier slip length \(b\geq0\) at the stationary wall: the wall speed extrapolates to zero a distance \(b\) beyond it in a local linear approximation. This gives

\[u(R)=-b\,u'(R).\]

Integrating the shear rate from the wall into the flow adds a uniform velocity offset to the usual parabola.

\[u(r)=\frac{G}{4\mu}(R^2-r^2+2bR).\]

Integrate the velocity over annuli to turn a local change of boundary condition into a measurable discharge. The contribution from the offset is proportional to the full area because the velocity addition is constant over the cross-section.

\[Q=2\pi\int_0^R u(r)r\,dr=\frac{\pi GR^4}{8\mu}\left(1+\frac{4b}{R}\right).\]

The shear stress needed to balance a fixed pressure gradient is unchanged. Effective slip lowers the required pressure gradient for a fixed flow. When \(b/R\) is small the fractional change is proportional to \(b/R\), making small tubes more sensitive at a given slip length.

Worked example

Calculate the extra discharge of a coated tube

Take \(R=100\) µm, \(b=5.00\) µm, \(\mu=1.00\) mPa s and \(G=10.0\) kPa/m. The no-slip flow is \(\pi(10^4)(10^{-4})^4/(8\times10^{-3})=0.393\) µL/s. Slip increases it by \(4b/R=0.200\), yielding 0.471 µL/s. The predicted wall speed \(GbR/(2\mu)=2.50\) mm/s could be examined by a near-wall tracer.

Transport momentum between neighbouring layers

Push two parallel plates past one another with liquid in the gap. Even at constant velocity the moving plate requires force. In a Newtonian fluid the tangential shear stress on a plane parallel to the motion is \(\tau_{xy}=\mu\,du/dy\). Dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) has units Pa·s. Divide it by density to obtain kinematic viscosity \(\nu\), an indicator of how rapidly momentum spreads through the fluid. Viscous resistance depends on a velocity gradient; uniform translation of the entire fluid would not create such stress.

Take two long horizontal plates separated by \(H\). Far from inlet and side walls assume constant density, constant viscosity and steady fully developed laminar motion \(u=u(y)\). The lower plate is fixed and the upper moves at speed \(U\). Represent the constant driving pressure gradient by \(G=-dp/dx\), positive for pressure falling downstream. A differential slab balances the downstream pressure force with the change in shear, giving \(0=G+d\tau_{xy}/dy=G+\mu u''\).

Impose the two wall velocities

The first integration gives

\[\mu u'=-Gy+C_1.\]

Integrating again and imposing no slip \(u(0)=0,\ u(H)=U\) determines the constants. The result is the sum of a straight wall-driven profile and a parabolic pressure-driven one:

\[u(y)=U\frac{y}{H}+\frac{G}{2\mu}y(H-y),\]
\[\frac{Q}{b}=\int_0^H u\,dy=\frac{UH}{2}+\frac{GH^3}{12\mu}.\]

Superposition here follows because the reduced equation and boundary conditions are linear in these inputs. It is not a universal property of the full nonlinear fluid equations. A negative \(G\) represents an adverse pressure rise and can produce a reverse-flow layer close to the stationary wall even when the moving wall drags fluid forward elsewhere.

Predict the pressure a moving surface can hold

An oil film has \(\mu=0.050\) Pa·s, \(H=1.0\) mm and \(U=0.20\) m/s. The wall-driven flux per unit width is \(UH/2=1.0\times10^{-4}\) m²/s. An adverse pressure gradient sufficient to reduce net flux to zero must satisfy \(G=-6\mu U/H^2=-60\,000\) Pa/m. Over 50 mm, pressure rises by 3.0 kPa. Zero net transport does not make the oil stationary.

Set \(s=y/H\) and insert the zero-flux gradient. The velocity is \(u/U=3s^2-2s\), which changes sign at \(s=2/3\) inside the film. The velocity gradient at the upper moving wall is \(4U/H=800\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so the wall supplies shear of magnitude 40 Pa. Even though the pressure-driven and wall-driven volume rates cancel, the moving surface still performs work, dissipated through the internal shear field.

Connect a local solution to a network

In a horizontal circular tube with fixed wall, axisymmetry changes the area that carries shear. Balancing forces on a cylinder of radius \(r\) gives

\[|\tau|=Gr/2.\]

Integrating the velocity gradient with no slip yields

\[u=G(R^2-r^2)/(4\mu),\]
\[Q=\pi R^4\Delta p/(8\mu L).\]

Thus a straight developed pipe behaves as a hydraulic resistance \(8\mu L/(\pi R^4)\) in Pa·s/m³. Series resistances carry the same \(Q\) and their pressure drops add. Parallel branches share pressure drop and their flows add. The analogy requires consistent units and a laminar regime; short entrance sections and strongly accelerating junctions may add losses outside the fully developed solution.

Express pipe resistance as a friction factor

For a circular pipe of diameter \(D=2R\), the developed laminar solution gives

\[\Delta p=32\mu L\bar u/D^2.\]

Define the Darcy friction factor by \(\Delta p=f_D(L/D)\rho\bar u^2/2\). Equating these expressions produces \(f_D=64/\mathrm{Re}_D\). For 2 m of 10 mm pipe with mean speed 0.10 m/s, water viscosity 0.001 Pa s and density 1000 kg/m³, the Reynolds number is 1000 and the pressure drop is 64 Pa. A friction factor listed in the Fanning convention is one quarter of the Darcy value, so identify the convention before substituting a tabulated number into a loss calculation.

Derive the profile of a shear-thinning liquid in a pipe

A Newtonian viscosity inferred in one duct may give poor predictions for a suspension or polymer solution at another rate. In a simple power-law model the shear stress magnitude is \(\tau=K|\dot\gamma|^n\), with consistency \(K\) in Pa sⁿ and index \(n\). A liquid with \(n<1\) has apparent viscosity decreasing with shear rate. Assume this law is supported over the shear range being sampled, flow is steady, axisymmetric, laminar and fully developed, and the circular pipe has no wall slip. The axial force balance is independent of rheology: at radius \(r\), \(\tau=Gr/2\) for driving gradient \(G=-dp/dx>0\).

Since the positive downstream velocity decreases towards the wall, write \(-du/dr=(Gr/2K)^{1/n}\). Integrate from radius \(r\) to the stationary wall at \(R\), then integrate the result over the circular cross-section to obtain

\[u(r)=\frac{n}{n+1}(G/2K)^{1/n}(R^{1+1/n}-r^{1+1/n}),\]
\[Q=\frac{\pi n}{3n+1}(G/2K)^{1/n}R^{3+1/n}.\]

Setting \(n=1,\ K=\mu\) gives the parabolic profile and \(Q=\pi GR^4/(8\mu)\). For \(n<1\) the velocity is flatter towards the centre and the relationship between pressure gradient and flow rate is nonlinear. The ideal power law can imply unrealistic apparent viscosity near zero shear at the axis; a fitted model with finite limiting viscosities is preferable when measurements resolve those regimes.

Infer a pressure demand from wall shear

A model resin follows \(n=0.50,\ K=0.80\,\mathrm{Pa\,s^{0.5}}\) in the operating range. A 2.0 m pipe of radius 5.0 mm experiences a pressure drop of 8.0 kPa, giving \(G=4000\,\mathrm{Pa/m}\) and wall shear \(\tau_w=GR/2=10.0\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The wall shear rate is

\[(10/0.80)^2=156.25\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.\]

Writing the mean speed in terms of this wall value gives

\[\bar u=nR|\dot\gamma_w|/(3n+1)=0.15625\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

The corresponding flow is \(\pi(0.005)^2(0.15625)=1.227\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\), about 0.736 L/min.

To double flow in this same pipe, the power-law relation requires the gradient to rise by \(2^n=\sqrt2\). The new pressure drop is 11.3 kPa, and wall shear rate doubles to 312.5 s⁻¹. A linear resistance model extrapolated from the original measurement would predict 16 kPa. Conversely at fixed gradient a 10% radius increase multiplies rate by \(1.1^{3+1/n}=1.61\), a stronger dependence than the Newtonian fourth power. Verify the resulting shear rate still falls in the tested rheological range, and check entrance length and temperature. Temperature changes produced by viscous dissipation can otherwise mimic a shear-dependent viscosity.

Two well-spaced steady pressure tests can independently infer the unknown exponent. At fixed pipe geometry,

\[\ln Q=(1/n)\ln\Delta p+\text{constant}.\]

If pressure drop rises from 8.0 to 12.0 kPa while flow rises from 0.736 to 1.66 L/min, the log slope is \(\ln(1.66/0.736)/\ln(12/8)\simeq2.00\), giving \(n\simeq0.50\). Repeating the higher-pressure point after cooling checks whether heating caused hysteresis between runs. Using an inlet pressure transducer alone would also include a changing downstream pressure; both end pressures or a differential gauge are needed to identify the flow law from this experiment.

The pressure taps should bracket the same developed length at every setting because entrance losses may change differently with flow.

How does a local pressure gradient accelerate a fluid particle?

A duct solution generally begins by cancelling terms in the momentum balance. To decide which terms can be cancelled, follow a small volume of fluid. Its acceleration includes the change in the velocity at its present location and the change as it moves into a different part of the velocity field. Applying the chain rule along its trajectory gives

\[D\mathbf u/Dt=\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.\]

Consider the two faces normal to the first coordinate axis. Their net surface force per unit volume in direction \(i\) approaches \(\partial_x\sigma_{ix}\) as the face separation tends to zero. Summing the three face pairs and adding weight gives

\[\rho Du_i/Dt=\sum_j\partial_j\sigma_{ij}+\rho g_i.\]

For an incompressible Newtonian liquid of constant viscosity \(\mu\), insert \(\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\mu(\partial_j u_i+\partial_i u_j)\). The cross derivative sums to \(\partial_i(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)=0\). The remaining terms are the local equations

\[\rho[\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u]=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u+\rho\mathbf g,\]
\[\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.\]

Take a horizontal slice through an ideal planar stagnation region, away from confining walls. The steady field \(u=ax,\ v=-ay\) satisfies continuity. Every velocity component is linear, so its Laplacian vanishes. Nevertheless the material acceleration is \((a^2x,a^2y)\). Integrating the two horizontal gradients gives

\[p(x,y)=p_0-\rho a^2(x^2+y^2)/2,\]

where \(p_0\) is the stagnation-point pressure at this elevation. For water at \(1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \(a=4\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(y=0.10\,\mathrm m,\ x=0\), pressure is 80 Pa below \(p_0\). Fluid entering along positive \(y\) moves towards the origin but accelerates in positive \(y\): it slows as pressure rises. At the stagnation point itself both velocity and acceleration vanish. Specifying \(p_0\) fixes the pressure datum. The velocity gradients determine the spatial change.

A viscous squeeze film as a rate-dependent support

Fluid mechanics

Bring two smooth circular disks slowly together with a thin layer of liquid between them. The force needed depends strongly on gap size even when neither surface is elastic.

Decide which motion the gap permits

Take rigid parallel disks of radius \(R\) separated by \(h(t)\ll R\), filled with an incompressible Newtonian liquid of viscosity \(\mu\). The lower disk is fixed and the upper disk approaches at speed \(V=-\dot h>0\). Assume an axisymmetric film discharging to ambient pressure at the edge, no slip on both disks and negligible inertia. The latter requires viscous stress to dominate transient and convective inertia, including the scale \(\rho Vh/\mu\ll1\). Fluid displaced from the centre must travel sideways through the thin film. Across-gap derivatives dominate radial derivatives of velocity, and the leading pressure is uniform through the thickness.

\[\mu\,\partial_z^2 u_r=\frac{dp}{dr},\]
\[u_r(0)=u_r(h)=0 .\]

Two integrations give

\[u_r=(dp/dr)z(z-h)/(2\mu).\]

The pressure gradient must be negative for outward flow. Integrating velocity over the gap gives radial volume flow per circumferential length \(q_r=-h^3(dp/dr)/(12\mu)\). The cubic power comes from a velocity scale proportional to \(h^2\) multiplied by the available gap \(h\).

Balance the displaced volume inside every circle

The film inside radius \(r\) loses volume at rate \(\pi r^2 V\). With no compressibility or leakage through the disks this must equal \(2\pi r q_r\). Hence \(q_r=rV/2\). Match to the viscous flow relation, integrate inward from \(p(R)=p_a\), and sum the excess pressure over the wetted disk:

\[\frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{6\mu Vr}{h^3},\]
\[p(r)-p_a=\frac{3\mu V}{h^3}(R^2-r^2),\]
\[F=\int_0^R [p(r)-p_a]\,2\pi r\,dr=\frac{3\pi\mu V R^4}{2h^3}.\]

The pressure distribution is parabolic; its area average is half its centre value. A fixed-speed actuator sees a rapidly increasing load as the film narrows. Conversely a constant load makes the approach slower. An experiment must state which of speed or force is controlled before two records can be compared.

Worked example · halve a film thickness

Let \(R=20\) mm, \(h=0.20\) mm, \(\mu=0.10\) Pa s and \(V=0.10\) mm/s. The centre excess is

\[3(0.10)(10^{-4})(0.020)^2/(0.00020)^3=1500\,\mathrm{Pa}.\]

The net force is 0.942 N. Reducing the gap to 0.10 mm at unchanged speed raises pressure and force by eight, giving 12.0 kPa and 7.54 N. Reducing speed by eight at the thinner gap would recover the original force. The model consequently distinguishes a viscous support from a spring by repeating the same gap at different rates.

Worked example · integrate the approach at constant load

Rearrange the force law to \(-dh/dt=2Fh^3/(3\pi\mu R^4)\). Separation of variables gives

\[t=[3\pi\mu R^4/(4F)](h_f^{-2}-h_i^{-2}).\]

Under a constant 1.00 N load the example disk takes 2.83 s to go from 0.20 to 0.10 mm. The ideal time to reach zero mathematical thickness diverges. Real surfaces eventually develop solid contact at roughness peaks, and departures from parallel alignment can redirect much of the pressure distribution.

Coordinates, energy and equivalent inertia

Machine dynamics

Reduce a constrained drive to one coordinate and use a power balance to check its equation of motion.

From first principles

Refer moving masses to one shaft by matching kinetic energy

Choose shaft angle \(\theta\) as the independent coordinate of a rigid mechanism. Suppose the angular speeds of its rotating components and the linear speeds of its translating components are fixed multiples of \(\dot\theta\). Assume ideal constraints with no slip, so specifying \(\theta\) and \(\dot\theta\) fixes the motion. Total kinetic energy is the sum of the individual translational and rotational energies.

\[T=\sum_i\tfrac12m_i v_i^2+\sum_j\tfrac12J_j\omega_j^2=\tfrac12 J_{\rm eq}\dot\theta^2 .\]

Here rotational inertias are about the component centres or fixed rotation axes as appropriate, with no motion counted twice. Dividing by \(\dot\theta^2/2\) gives the inertia seen at the chosen shaft.

\[J_{\rm eq}=\sum_i m_i\left(\frac{v_i}{\dot\theta}\right)^2+\sum_jJ_j\left(\frac{\omega_j}{\dot\theta}\right)^2 .\]

A force on a translating part contributes the shaft torque that gives the same instantaneous power,

\[T_i\dot\theta=F_iv_i.\]

For constant speed ratios and constant equivalent inertia, energy balance then gives

\[\sum T_i=J_{\rm eq}\ddot\theta.\]

The square on the speed ratio in inertia reflection follows from energy and is essential when changing the reference shaft.

Background · Generalised inertia
\[M(q)=2T/\dot q^2\]

This scalar definition applies to one independent moving coordinate with purely quadratic kinetic energy. Changing the coordinate changes the numerical inertia.

Derivation and applications

Derive the extra drive term for position-dependent inertia

A motor can require a torque at constant angular speed if a mechanism changes the linear speed of its payload during a revolution. Use a single angular coordinate \(\theta\), input inertia \(J_0\), and a guided mass \(m\) with position \(x=f(\theta)\). Assume a rigid lossless linkage and a horizontal guide, so gravity does no work. The kinematic constraint sets the payload velocity and fixes the instantaneous reflected inertia by equating the total kinetic energy to that of one shaft.

\[\dot x=f'(\theta)\dot\theta,\]
\[T=\tfrac12[J_0+m(f')^2]\dot\theta^2=\tfrac12 J(\theta)\dot\theta^2 .\]

Differentiate the energy with respect to time. The shaft input supplies the full change in kinetic energy through its power \(\tau\dot\theta\). Equating the two and cancelling speed gives the required drive torque. At a turning point the same equation follows directly from Lagrange's equation.

\[\tau=J(\theta)\ddot\theta+\tfrac12J'(\theta)\dot\theta^2.\]

For an ideal sinusoidal guide \(x=a\sin\theta\), substitution makes the speed-squared contribution explicit. It changes sign within each turn because the translating payload first absorbs and then releases kinetic energy.

\[J=J_0+ma^2\cos^2\theta,\]
\[\tau=J\ddot\theta-ma^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2.\]

A constant-inertia approximation omits this alternating torque even if it predicts the total energy averaged over a turn. Its integral over a full constant-speed revolution vanishes as the payload returns to its initial speed.

Follow the motion through a mechanism

A motor accelerating a load feels inertia from components that turn at different speeds. Simply adding their physical moments of inertia would treat all of those speeds as equal. Instead, choose one independent coordinate and express every kinetic energy in terms of it. No-slip belts, rigid gears and inextensible cables give useful constraints when their compliance and backlash are negligible over the motion of interest.

Consider a drum of radius \(R\) winding a cable carrying a vertically guided mass \(m\). Choose the drum angle \(\theta\) positive when the mass rises. If the effective winding radius is constant, displacement, velocity and acceleration satisfy \(x=R\theta\), \(\dot x=R\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot x=R\ddot\theta\). These equalities are kinematic. A torque relation requires force balance or work in addition. When the cable builds successive layers on a spool, the effective radius varies with angle and the constant-ratio assumption must be revisited.

Background · What changes when the reflected inertia depends on position?

For a piston linkage let the piston position be \(x(q)\), where \(q\) is shaft angle. Its kinetic energy contributes \(m(dx/dq)^2\) to the shaft inertia. As the linkage moves through a stroke this contribution varies, even for a piston of constant mass. Use the energy derivative to find the generalised inertial torque.

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q}=J(q)\ddot q+\tfrac12J^{\prime}(q)\dot q^2,\]
\[T=\tfrac12J(q)\dot q^2.\]

Uniform shaft speed sets the first term to zero. The second term represents kinetic energy flowing into and out of the accelerating piston. Its shaft work over a complete cycle vanishes when the mechanism returns to the same angle and speed. An average torque meter can consequently miss a substantial alternating torque.

Reflect mass and forces to a common shaft

Let \(J_d\) be the drum inertia. Add the rotational energy of the drum to the translational energy of the load, then express both in terms of the drum speed.

\[\begin{aligned} K&=\tfrac12 J_d\dot\theta^2+\tfrac12 m\dot x^2\\ &=\tfrac12\bigl(J_d+mR^2\bigr)\dot\theta^2,\\ J_{\rm eq}&=J_d+mR^2. \end{aligned}\]

A small positive angle raises the mass through \(R\,d\theta\), increasing its potential energy by \(mgR\,d\theta\). Thus gravity contributes a resisting torque \(mgR\). A positive applied torque \(T_d\) supplies power \(T_d\dot\theta\). Applying the energy balance gives the equation of motion.

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12J_{\rm eq}\dot\theta^2+mgR\theta\right)&=T_d\dot\theta,\\ J_{\rm eq}\ddot\theta&=T_d-mgR . \end{aligned}\]

The second expression also holds at zero speed by force balance. It separates steady effort, needed to hold or raise the load against gravity, from the extra effort needed to accelerate the two inertias. With the motor disconnected, an ideal light cable does not make the mass accelerate quite at \(g\), since the drum must gain rotational kinetic energy too.

Calculate a motor torque

A drum with \(R=0.10\) m and \(J_d=0.15\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) lifts 25 kg. A reduction gear gives

\[\dot\theta_m=4\dot\theta.\]

The rotor has inertia \(J_m=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Refer the train to the motor coordinate. The drum-plus-load inertia becomes \((0.15+25(0.10)^2)/4^2=0.025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), so the total at the motor is 0.045 kg·m². Gravity contributes \(25(9.81)(0.10)/4=6.13\) N·m at that shaft.

Demand upward acceleration 0.80 m/s². The drum must accelerate at 8.0 rad/s² and the motor at 32 rad/s². The motor supplies

\[6.13+0.045(32)=7.57\,\mathrm{N\,m}.\]

A separate check at load speed 1.0 m/s gives motor speed 40 rad/s. Its 303 W then divide into about 245 W raising the weight and 57.6 W increasing kinetic energy. Ideal gearing changes torque and angular speed inversely while preserving transmitted power.

Identify which approximation controls the answer

At constant lifting speed the inertial torque vanishes. At constant angular acceleration, doubling the transmission ratio decreases reflected load inertia by four but also doubles the motor acceleration for the same load motion. The rotor’s own inertia remains at the motor and eventually dominates. A gear ratio therefore cannot reduce every part of acceleration torque indefinitely. Bearings, cables and gears also dissipate energy or store it elastically. Include their effects when a measured transient shows a repeatable lag, oscillation or loss that an ideal rigid constraint cannot explain.

Is the largest reduction always preferable?

For a prescribed drum acceleration \(\alpha_d\) and reduction \(n=\omega_m/\omega_d\), collect gravity and load-side acceleration into the drum demand \(T_d=mgR+(J_d+mR^2)\alpha_d\). The motor torque becomes

\[T_m=T_d/n+J_mn\alpha_d.\]

The first term falls as reduction rises, while rotor acceleration grows. Differentiating with respect to \(n\) gives an ideal minimum at \(n=\sqrt{T_d/(J_m\alpha_d)}\). For the stated lift \(T_d=27.73\) N m and \(\alpha_d=8\) rad/s², giving \(n=13.2\) approximately. This torque minimum assumes the motor can achieve the associated speed. At a drum speed of 10 rad/s it would turn at 132 rad/s, over three times its speed with the original reduction. Shaft-speed and voltage limits can therefore select a different practical ratio.

Derive speed ripple from a torque diagram

A crank-driven machine can require very different torque at different angles even when the average load is constant. A flywheel stores kinetic energy when driving torque exceeds resisting torque, then releases it when the load peaks. Refer all inertias and torques to the same shaft and suppose the equivalent inertia \(J\) is constant. The signed difference \(M(\theta)=T_{\rm drive}-T_{\rm load}\) gives

\[J\dot\omega=M.\]

For continuous positive rotation write \(\dot\omega=\omega\,d\omega/d\theta\). Integration between two angular positions gives an exact work–energy relation.

\[\tfrac12 J[\omega_b^2-\omega_a^2]=\int_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}M(\theta)\,d\theta .\]

A periodic steady sequence requires zero net area under the torque-difference curve over a whole revolution. Its cumulative area nevertheless has a maximum and a minimum. Their difference \(\Delta E\) is the energy that must swing into and out of the rotating inertia. Define mean speed in the ripple specification as the arithmetic mean of the two extreme speeds \(\bar\omega=(\omega_{\max}+\omega_{\min})/2\) and fluctuation coefficient \(C_s=(\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})/\bar\omega\). Factoring the difference of squared speeds gives

\[J=\Delta E/(\bar\omega^2 C_s).\]

A time-average tachometer reading approximates this mean for small ripple, but the definitions are distinct for a strongly pulsating shaft.

Size a flywheel for a repeating work demand

A laboratory indexing machine runs at a nominal 300 rpm. A motor supplies approximately constant 30 N m. The resisting torque is 70 N m for the first quarter revolution and a lower constant value for the remaining three quarters. Equate total input and output work over each revolution: \(30(2\pi)=70(\pi/2)+T_l(3\pi/2)\), giving \(T_l=16.67\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). The first quarter produces a 40 N m deficit, so the cumulative energy falls by \(40(\pi/2)=62.83\,\mathrm J\). The remaining 13.33 N m surplus restores exactly that energy.

For a maximum peak-to-peak speed variation of 2% and \(\bar\omega=31.42\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), total inertia required is

\[62.83/[31.42^2(0.02)]=3.18\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.\]

If the machine already has 1.00 kg m² on this shaft, an extra 2.18 kg m² is required. A thin-ring estimate at mean radius 0.30 m gives mass \(2.18/0.30^2=24.2\,\mathrm{kg}\). A solid disc of the same radius needs twice the mass because its material lies closer to the shaft. At half speed the same torque-versus-angle work deficit requires four times the total inertia for the same fractional ripple. Startup torque or bearing load may therefore limit an inertia-based solution even when steady motor power suffices.

The torque balance also predicts where a shaft encoder should record the two speed extremes. The energy derivative with respect to angle equals the torque difference, so the maximum occurs just before the high resisting plateau begins and the minimum where that plateau ends. The maximum torque itself persists over a finite angular interval, while minimum speed occurs only at its end. This distinction matters when timing a torque pulse from a speed record: the sign of the speed derivative, rather than the absolute value of speed, identifies when the flywheel is discharging.

Why a radial slide presses sideways on a rotating guide

Machine dynamics

A low-friction carriage moves along a radial slot in a horizontal turntable. It can move at constant speed relative to the guide and still require a changing force. Keeping track of the moving basis exposes both acceleration components.

Differentiate the direction vectors

Let \(r(t)\) be displacement from the turntable centre along the guide and \(\theta(t)\) the angle of the guide. In fixed Cartesian components the radial unit vector is \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) and the tangential vector is \((-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\). Differentiating shows \(\dot{\mathbf e}_r=\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta\) and \(\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta=-\dot\theta\mathbf e_r\). Apply the product rule to the position \(r\mathbf e_r\) to obtain the velocity and then differentiate both terms.

\[\mathbf v=\dot r\mathbf e_r+r\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta,\]
\[\mathbf a=(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)\mathbf e_r+(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)\mathbf e_\theta .\]

One contribution to \(2\dot r\dot\theta\) comes from differentiating the radially directed velocity as its basis turns. The other comes from differentiating the magnitude of the tangential velocity \(r\dot\theta\). For positive rotation and outward travel, both point in the positive tangential direction. The expression already gives the acceleration in an inertial frame. Multiplying by mass gives the net real force required on the carriage.

Check sideways force with angular momentum and energy

Hold the angular speed at \(\Omega\), and drive the radial position as \(r=r_0+ut\) with constant u. Gravity and support cancel vertically. The net radial drive force is \(-mr\Omega^2\) and the guide must exert a side force \(2mu\Omega\). The resulting torque about the axis is \(2mru\Omega\). This equals the derivative of the angular momentum \(mr^2\Omega\), providing an independent sign and factor-of-two check.

\[P_r+P_\theta=(-mr\Omega^2)u+(2mu\Omega)(r\Omega)=mru\Omega^2={d\over dt}\left[{m\over2}(u^2+r^2\Omega^2)\right].\]

The side force does work in the laboratory since its contact point moves tangentially. Describing a smooth slot as doing zero work without specifying the frame would lose this energy transfer. The radial actuator absorbs power during outward constant-speed travel.

Forces and power on two passes

A 0.40 kg carriage moves from \(r=0.10\) m outwards at 0.20 m/s, with \(\Omega=5\) rad/s. At \(t=0.50\) s it reaches 0.20 m. Its radial force is −2.0 N, tangential force is +0.80 N and net mechanical power is

\[−2(0.20)+0.80(1)=0.40\,\mathrm{W}.\]

Setting u to zero removes the side force, while the guide still requires a radial restraint to maintain circular travel.

On an inward pass at −0.20 m/s, evaluated at the same radius, the radial force remains −2.0 N. The side force reverses and the turntable now receives angular momentum from the carriage. The magnitude of each force depends on a different combination of radius, radial speed and angular speed. Use the trace to distinguish the constant side load from the changing inward force.

Set radial speed to zero before changing angular speed. Restore outward motion and predict how doubling angular speed changes the two force components at the same radius. Use mechanical power as a separate sign check.

When a suspended capacitor loses its stable equilibrium

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

An electrode suspended on a spring can remain motionless at a bias that makes the calculated electrostatic force grow as it moves. Static force balance alone misses the voltage at which that equilibrium disappears. Deriving the small-signal frequency gives an independent measurement of the same instability.

Start with the electrical and mechanical control variables

A movable conducting plate of area \(A\) faces a fixed electrode. Its unstressed gap is \(g\). Define positive displacement \(x\) toward the fixed plate, with spring force \(-kx\), mass \(m\) and viscous damping coefficient \(b\). For \(0\le x<g\) assume ideal parallel plates, negligible tilt and fringing, constant \(\epsilon\), and a linear elastic spring. The capacitance is \(C(x)=\epsilon A/(g-x)\). The model ends when the plates approach contact or when the suspending structure departs from its specified elastic law. It cannot by itself calculate adhesion, contact resistance or post-contact motion.

Before differentiating electrical energy specify whether the source holds voltage or charge. At fixed charge \(Q\), electrical energy is \(Q^2/[2C(x)]\), and its negative displacement derivative gives the force toward increasing \(x\). At fixed voltage \(V\), a source supplies \(VdQ\) as the plates move. The appropriate potential for mechanical equilibrium is the spring energy plus the field energy minus \(QV\), which here is \(\mathcal U_V=kx^2/2-C(x)V^2/2\). Differentiating gives the outward spring reaction and the attractive electrical force \(F_e=V^2C'(x)/2=\epsilon A V^2/[2(g-x)^2]\). An independent derivation multiplies the conductor pressure \(\epsilon E^2/2\) by area with \(E=V/(g-x)\). Both give a positive force. Replacing the source potential with the stored energy at fixed voltage would reverse its sign.

Locate all equilibria before choosing a branch

Define \(s=x/g\) and \(\Lambda=\epsilon A V^2/(2kg^3)\). The static force equation reduces to

\[\Lambda=s(1-s)^2.\]

At a specified voltage one can find \(s\) by scanning the curve \(s\mapsto s(1-s)^2\) over the physically admissible interval \(0\le s<1\). Its derivative is \((1-s)(1-3s)\), giving an interior maximum \(4/27\) at \(s=1/3\). For \(0<\Lambda<4/27\) there are two physical roots. The smaller is a local minimum of \(\mathcal U_V\) while the larger is a local maximum. Their merger is a fold of the static equilibrium branch:

\[x_{\rm pi}=g/3,\]
\[V_{\rm pi}=\sqrt{\frac{8kg^3}{27\epsilon A}} .\]

The often-quoted one-third displacement is the result for a linear spring, ideal voltage drive and this capacitance geometry. It is not a universal travel limit determined by the gap alone. Additional mechanical restoring forces or a different electrical boundary condition change the fold. Neither positive net electrical force nor the mere existence of a real solution is evidence of stable operation. A root finder started near the almost closed gap can converge to the unstable large-displacement root with an extremely small static residual.

The two branches can be distinguished experimentally without deliberately reaching contact. At static equilibrium \(kx=F_e\). Linearise the total force about \(x_0\) while maintaining voltage at its prescribed value. The incremental restoring stiffness and corresponding weak-damping natural frequency are

\[k_{\rm eff}=k-\frac{\epsilon A V^2}{(g-x_0)^3}=k\,\frac{1-3s_0}{1-s_0},\]
\[f_{\rm small}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm eff}}m}.\]

A small push toward the fixed electrode is restored only when \(k_{\rm eff}>0\). The familiar electrostatic softening is the reason the smaller equilibrium is stable. The small-signal frequency tends to zero as the fold is approached. With appreciable damping the observed amplitude resonance and ringdown oscillation have slightly different frequencies; fit the full mechanical response rather than identifying an arbitrary peak with the undamped \(f_{\rm small}\).

Background · Can a sudden step pull in a statically stable actuator?

An ideal voltage step applied to a plate initially at rest can push it past the unstable equilibrium. During the motion at fixed voltage the effective potential per \(kg^2\) is \(u(s)=s^2/2-b/[2(1-s)]\), where \(s=x/g\) and \(b=\epsilon A V^2/(kg^3)\). Neglect damping and begin at \(s=0\). At the dynamical threshold the initial energy equals the saddle energy and the slope vanishes at that saddle.

\[u(s)=u(0)\ \Longrightarrow\ b=s(1-s),\]
\[u'(s)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ b=2s(1-s)^2.\]

The two equations meet at \(s=1/2\), \(b=1/4\). The quasistatic fold gives \(b=8/27\). Consequently the undamped step threshold is only \(\sqrt{27/32}\simeq0.919\) times the slow-ramp pull-in voltage. A device operated between the two voltages can survive a ramp but collapse after an abrupt step. Dissipation and the actual rise time change this prediction.

Reconstruct the prepared state from a frequency measurement

Take a 100 μm square electrode in vacuum, \(g=2.00\) μm, \(k=2.00\) N/m and \(m=5.00\times10^{-10}\) kg. The predicted pull-in voltage is 7.32 V, the zero-bias capacitance 44.3 fF and the zero-bias natural frequency 10.07 kHz. On the low branch \(s_0=0.20\) gives \(x_0=0.400\) μm and voltage 6.80 V. Then \(k_{\rm eff}=1.00\) N/m and the small-signal frequency is 7.12 kHz. A measured frequency ratio \(f/f_0\) can be inverted via \(s=(1-r)/(3-r)\), where \(r=(f/f_0)^2\). The result is independent of absolute optical displacement calibration if the mass and underlying spring stiffness have not changed during the bias sweep.

At the same voltage the other physical root is \(s=0.48769\). A numerical force balance at this root would give the same supply reading but a negative tangent stiffness. The ideal voltage-controlled potential in units \(kg^2\) is \(u(s)=s^2/2-\Lambda/(1-s)\). The barrier height \(kg^2[u(0.48769)-u(0.20)]\) for this example is \(7.26\times10^{-14}\) J. A sufficiently energetic disturbance can carry the plate out of the stable basin even below static pull-in. This is different from loss of the static equilibrium itself. A quasistatic model predicts the existence and local stiffness of the equilibrium; a transient simulation must additionally specify initial displacement, velocity, drive ramp and damping.

Change the preparation to fixed charge

Disconnect a perfect isolated capacitor after placing a known charge \(Q\) on it, neglecting leakage and parasitic capacitance. Then \(F_Q=Q^2/(2\epsilon A)\) is independent of the remaining gap, because \(D=Q/A\) is fixed. Static equilibrium is \(x_0=Q^2/(2\epsilon A k)\), with incremental stiffness exactly \(k\). At the same initial voltage and gap the instantaneous force agrees with the voltage-controlled value, since \(Q=C(x_0)V\). The derivatives governing subsequent motion differ. At \(x_0=0.400\) μm the required charge in the numerical example is \(0.376\) pC. After ideal electrical isolation the small-signal frequency returns to the underlying elastic value even though the loaded static position stays the same.

Ideal charge isolation is delicate in a small device. A fixed parasitic capacitance \(C_p\) from the isolated node to a reference means the conserved charge belongs to \(C(x)+C_p\). Motion changes the fraction of total charge residing on the actuator. The electrical energy becomes \(Q_{\rm total}^2/[2(C+C_p)]\), so the force is \(Q_{\rm total}^2 C'/[2(C+C_p)^2]\), generally displacement dependent. If \(C_p\gg C\), the node voltage changes little with a small displacement and behavior is closer to voltage control. Interpreting an observed softening after disconnecting the supply as evidence against electromagnetic force balance would overlook this boundary change.

A different practical preparation puts an intentional series capacitor \(C_s\) between a fixed voltage supply and the actuator. Its series combination is \(C_{\rm ser}=\epsilon A/[g-x+\epsilon A/C_s]\). For static mechanical work at fixed supply voltage use \(F=V_s^2 C_{\rm ser}'/2\). Defining an effective electrical spacing \(g_e=g+\epsilon A/C_s\), the static fold occurs at \(x=g_e/3\) if this lies within the open physical gap. If \(\epsilon A/C_s\ge2g\), contact would occur before any such static fold in the ideal model. This calculation shows a direct connection between passive circuit design and allowed mechanical travel, while making clear that the maximum actuator voltage can differ from the voltage printed on the supply.

Why a sudden voltage change can reach contact below static pull-in

For a rapid electrical step the plate has a position and velocity inherited from the previous preparation. Suppose it starts at rest at its zero-bias position, the supply steps to a constant \(V\) much faster than a mechanical period, and mechanical damping is initially negligible. Immediately after the step, energy in the mechanical potential \(u(s)=s^2/2-\Lambda/(1-s)\) is \(u(0)=-\Lambda\) in units of \(kg^2\). Motion under this constant voltage can reach the force barrier if the initial value exceeds the potential at the unstable stationary point. This question can be solved by comparing two energies without integrating an entire time history.

Let \(s_b\) be the unstable root of \(\Lambda=s_b(1-s_b)^2\). At threshold the initial state is on the separatrix, the trajectory separating oscillations in the well from excursions to the closing gap. Set \(u(0)=u(s_b)\), subtract the two expressions and cancel \(s_b\) from the nontrivial solution. The two conditions become

\[\Lambda={s_b(1-s_b)\over2},\]
\[\Lambda=s_b(1-s_b)^2 .\]

Thus \(s_b=1/2\), \(\Lambda=1/8\), and the ideal undamped step threshold is

\[V_{\rm step}/V_{\rm pi}=\sqrt{27/32}=0.919.\]

In our example this is 6.72 V. A slow increase to 6.80 V leads to the observed stable 0.40 μm deflection, whereas a sudden ideal voltage step to the same value from rest at the origin contains enough energy to escape the well. Starting on the biased equilibrium before applying a small perturbation is a different experiment. The direction of the threshold shift makes physical sense: damping removes mechanical energy during the first excursion, and a slow ramp allows the equilibrium to move with the plate. The undamped value specifies a particular ideal initial-value experiment, rather than a voltage rating for an unspecified drive waveform.

One can check a simulated step by monitoring \(m\dot{x}^2/2+\mathcal U_V\) after the supply settles. With viscous damping its derivative is \(-b\dot{x}^2\), so any sustained increase during a constant-voltage segment points to a numerical or modelling error. Before the step ends there is an explicit drive term \(-CV\dot V\) in this energy balance. The mechanical potential already includes the source work. Adding \(V\dot Q\) to the same balance a second time gives an artificial energy imbalance. A transient solver should stop at a physically specified minimum gap instead of marching across the singularity; a statement about contact time depends on what counts as contact.

A finite source resistance changes what a ringdown measures

A dc supply with series resistance \(R\) can maintain the static voltage accurately while failing to hold the actuator voltage fixed over a mechanical cycle. Let its steady node voltage be \(V\), with equilibrium capacitance \(C=C(x_0)\). A small sinusoidal displacement \(\delta x\) causes current \(i\omega(C\,\delta v+VC'\delta x)\) into the actuator. The small-signal supply voltage is zero, so \(\delta v=-R i\). Eliminating current gives

\[\delta v=-\frac{V C'}C\frac{i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}\delta x,\]
\[\tau=RC .\]

Linearise \(F_e=V^2 C'/2\) allowing both voltage and position to vary. The direct derivative is \(K_{e0}=V^2C''/2\), while the change from node voltage is \(VC'\,\delta v\). For an ideal parallel plate \(C''/2=(C')^2/C\), so the two terms combine to the simple complex relation \(\delta F_e=K_{e0}\delta x/(1+i\omega\tau)\). At low frequency the electrical force follows displacement with its fixed-voltage slope. At high frequency the slope vanishes, as it must for an isolated ideal plate carrying fixed charge over one oscillation. At intermediate frequency a motion-induced current dissipates heat in the resistor.

Bring \(\delta F_e\) to the left side of the mechanical equation. Its real part subtracts \(K_{e0}/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\) from the mechanical stiffness; its imaginary part adds \(K_{e0}\tau/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\) to the viscous damping coefficient. This damping is positive for a passive resistor. At \(s=0.20\) the static electrical stiffness is \(1.00\) N/m, the operating capacitance is 55.3 fF and \(R=400\) MΩ gives

\[\tau=22.1\,\mu\mathrm s.\]

A 7.2 kHz interrogation has \(\omega\tau\simeq1\). Only about half the static electrical slope survives and the added damping is roughly \(11\,\mu\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). Treat these as frequency-dependent coefficients in the driven susceptibility. Substituting the low-frequency stiffness into a ringdown fit at this frequency would give the wrong displacement when the fitted frequency is inverted. Fitting the complex response at several frequencies avoids that ambiguity.

An experiment can separate the circuit effect from mechanical nonlinearity. Keep the same static bias and displacement, insert two known series resistances, then measure both in-phase and quadrature response to a small sinusoidal force. The displaced equilibrium should remain essentially unchanged at dc if leakage stays small. A stiffness dispersion and dissipative peak that shift in proportion to \(1/R\) support an electrical cause. Then repeat at a smaller oscillation amplitude. Amplitude-dependent frequency shift that survives the change in resistance can arise from nonlinear spring or force curvature, which the first-order susceptibility leaves out. A simple change to the external circuit has turned the slope of an energy curve into a prediction for a measured phase lag.

Compare force intersections for voltage and charge control

The initial gap is 2.00 μm, the area is a 100 μm square and the mass is 0.50 μg. Use the voltage ratio to move the force curve through the fold. At 93% of pull-in the low-displacement fixed-voltage intersection has a positive restoring slope. The high-displacement intersection has a negative slope. The charge-controlled line is prepared at the selected voltage at the original open gap. It gives the same initial force but a different subsequent curve. The readout reports both open-gap roots even if the upper root lies beyond the plotted interval. At the baseline stiffness, static pull-in is 7.32 V.

Change the spring stiffness while holding the voltage ratio fixed. The dimensionless locations of the roots stay in place, but their dimensional force and small-signal frequency change. Use the force intersections to locate the potential maximum and sketch the area between attraction and spring reaction from the initial position. Its signed integral supplies the work which distinguishes a bounded excursion from crossing the maximum. Designing a displacement bridge at its noise limit

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A moving electrode changes two capacitances in opposite directions. Derive the displacement signal, reconstruct its noise in metres and select a carrier voltage consistent with measured mechanical stiffness and electrical losses.

Find the voltage of the moving electrode before calculating sensitivity

Consider three plane electrodes with common active area. At the centred position the middle conductor is separated by \(g\) from each outer electrode and each gap has capacitance \(C_0\). Displacement \(x\) toward the positive drive electrode reduces one gap and increases the other. Use \(s=x/g\), where \(|s|<1\). Drive the outer electrodes with coherent sinusoidal voltages \(+V_d\) and \(-V_d\), with \(V_d\) the rms amplitude of either electrode relative to ground. The middle conductor leads to a high-impedance amplifier. Its cable and input provide a fixed total capacitance \(C_p\) to ground. Leakage is initially negligible over one carrier period; the input draws negligible signal current. All formulas for carrier voltages in this project use rms amplitudes.

\[C_+=\frac{C_0}{1-s},\]
\[C_-=\frac{C_0}{1+s}.\]

The middle node has zero net charge at the carrier frequency. Its instantaneous ac charge is \(C_+(V_n-V_d)+C_-(V_n+V_d)+C_p V_n\). Set the sum to zero and solve the resulting linear equation for \(V_n\). After combining the gap capacitances the result simplifies considerably:

\[\frac{V_n}{V_d}=\frac{C_+-C_-}{C_++C_-+C_p}=\frac{2C_0s}{2C_0+C_p(1-s^2)}.\]

For zero parasitic capacitance the response is exactly \(V_d x/g\) throughout the ideal geometry. Each individual capacitance is strongly curved near an electrode. Charge conservation divides one nonlinear combination by another and removes that curvature from their voltage ratio. Additional capacitance reduces the small-signal gain and introduces an odd cubic term. Let \(\eta=2C_0/(2C_0+C_p)\). Expanding about the centre gives

\[V_n/V_d=\eta s[1+(1-\eta)s^2+\cdots].\]

The derivative at zero is \(K=\eta V_d/g\) in volts per metre. A small-signal calibration taken only at the origin implicitly accepts this cubic distortion at larger displacements.

For \(C_0=2.00\) pF, \(C_p=4.00\) pF, \(g=100\,\mu\mathrm m\) and \(V_d=1.00\) V, \(K=5000\) V/m. A displacement of 100 nm produces \(0.5000\) mV. At 20 μm, the exact signal is \(0.10204\) V against the linear prediction \(0.10000\) V. The 2.04% curvature would be apparent in a calibration over the wider range. A single proportional calibration cannot remove it. A fit to the ratio itself has a physical parameter, \(C_p/(2C_0)\), which can also be checked by measuring total input capacitance with the mechanical element held at centre.

Translate each readout fluctuation into equivalent displacement

Represent the amplifier near the carrier by an input series voltage noise density \(e_n\) and a shunt current noise density \(i_n\). With the drives treated as small-signal grounds for a noise perturbation, the central node presents \(C_T=2C_0+C_p\) at the centre. The impedance magnitude for current fluctuations is \(1/(\omega C_T)\). For mutually independent noise sources and a narrow demodulation band the corresponding input-referred displacement amplitude spectral density is

\[S_{x,e}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{e_n^2+\left(\frac{i_n}{\omega C_T}\right)^2}.\]

Use noise densities and gain referred to the same detection channel. An rms sine amplitude, a peak-amplitude display and a lock-in reading with an unreported normalisation can differ by factors involving \(\sqrt2\). Here noise figures denote the effective one-sided densities referred to the recovered rms carrier channel. For a white spectrum and an equivalent noise bandwidth \(B_e\) the displacement standard deviation equals the density times \(\sqrt{B_e}\). The displayed cut-off frequency of a multi-pole lock-in filter need not equal its equivalent noise bandwidth. A calibration using a known injected sideband measures both the gain convention and the actual band response.

At 100 kHz, \(e_n=10\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\), \(i_n=10\,\mathrm{fA/\sqrt{Hz}}\) and \(C_T=8.00\) pF, current noise produces \(1.99\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\). Adding variances gives \(10.20\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\), or \(2.04\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\) at the stated gain. A 100 Hz equivalent noise bandwidth gives 20.4 pm rms of electrical readout noise. Cable capacitance reduces the voltage made by current noise but also reduces signal gain. In fact \(C_T K=2C_0 V_d/g\), so their ratio for current noise is independent of \(C_p\) in this model. The voltage-noise contribution grows with cable capacitance. Higher carrier frequency initially reduces the current contribution but reaches a plateau set by voltage noise.

Derive backaction for a floating sensing plate

Electrical drive can change the mechanical stiffness of the sensor. For each gap take half the squared rms gap voltage times its capacitance derivative. This follows either by averaging the instantaneous plate pressure over a carrier cycle or by differentiating electrical coenergy with the middle node at its charge-balanced voltage. Mechanical motion must be slow compared with the carrier. Since \(C_-'\) is negative, the two signed forces cancel at the centre. For a small positive \(s\), write \(V_n\simeq\eta V_ds\),

\[C_+'\simeq(C_0/g)(1+2s),\]
\[C_-'\simeq-(C_0/g)(1-2s).\]

The leading imbalance is

\[F=\tfrac12 C_+'(V_d-V_n)^2+\tfrac12 C_-'(-V_d-V_n)^2\simeq\frac{2C_0 V_d^2}{g^2}(1-\eta)x .\]

The electromagnetic stiffness \(K_e=2C_0 V_d^2(1-\eta)/g^2\) subtracts from the mechanical restoring stiffness \(k\). If \(C_p=0\), the equal field magnitudes on both sides cancel exactly for every displacement in ideal plane geometry. To check directly, the positive gap field is

\[[V_d(1-s)]/[g(1-s)]=V_d/g.\]

The negative gap field has the same magnitude. A charge amplifier holding the central electrode at virtual ground would change this condition and have a different backaction; its calibration must use its own circuit. For a finite \(C_p\), the measured change of resonance with carrier voltage can identify electrical coupling independently of the signal voltage.

With \(k=0.020\) N/m the nominal configuration has \(K_e=0.00020\,\mathrm{N/m}\) at 1 V and \(0.00080\,\mathrm{N/m}\) at 2 V. At 2 V the resonance ratio for the same effective mass is

\[\sqrt{1-0.0008/0.020}=0.980.\]

A 2% frequency decrease measures roughly a 4% reduction of stiffness. If the displacement is imposed by a very stiff external positioner this effect changes the force needed to hold position. If the sample is itself a force sensor, the same effect changes the mechanical conversion between applied force and displacement. Quoting an electronic noise floor without specifying which mechanical situation is intended can hide the larger uncertainty.

Choose a feasible drive and include Brownian motion

Raising the carrier at fixed frequency increases displacement signal. It may also heat the dielectrics, exceed amplifier headroom or significantly soften the spring. Suppose each gap at centre has an effective loss tangent \(\tan\delta\), with a real conductance \(\omega C_0\tan\delta\) at the carrier. Its dissipated power is conductance times the squared rms voltage. Add both gaps to find

\[P=2\omega C_0\tan\delta V_d^2.\]

Fixed parasitic capacitance at the stationary middle node has negligible carrier voltage at exact balance in this model. An actual shield or cable dielectric connected directly to one of the drive nodes will have its own loss. A calorimetric or current phase measurement should include that extra load.

With effective \(\tan\delta=0.010\), the two active gaps dissipate 25.13 nW per squared volt at 100 kHz. A 100 nW heat budget gives \(V_d\le1.995\) V. A driver that remains linear to 2.50 V and a softening allowance \(K_e\le0.1k\) permit 2.50 V and 3.16 V respectively. The thermal bound sets the largest practical drive under these assumptions. For the electrical readout alone, the best feasible setting is thus 1.995 V and the electrical floor is about \(1.02\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\). A separate limit on the electric field or measurement amplitude could be introduced as another experimentally justified constraint.

Now let the mechanical element have viscous damping \(b=1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\) at \(T=300\) K. In thermal equilibrium the one-sided Brownian force density is

\[\sqrt{4k_BT b}=1.29\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{N/\sqrt{Hz}}.\]

Far below mechanical resonance its displacement density is this force divided by \(k-K_e\). At 1 V it is \(6.50\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\), already greater than the electrical term. At 1.995 V it is about \(6.70\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\). The combined density adds in quadrature, giving about 6.81 at 1 V and 6.78 at the heat limit. A minimum of the total can occur between them. For an imposed position of a rigid stage the Brownian motion of this compliant probe may be outside the measurement model; for a freely suspended sensing mass it is part of the signal uncertainty. The optimisation criterion follows the physical quantity the instrument measures.

Use the phase and polarity of a calibration to track false signals

Middle-node leakage to ground with conductance \(G\) changes the circuit equation to \([G+i\omega C_T]V_n=i\omega(C_+-C_-)V_d\) at small displacement. The ideal gain acquires a factor \(i\omega C_T/(G+i\omega C_T)\). For a 10 MΩ leakage path and 8 pF, the corner is 1.99 kHz. At 100 kHz the magnitude reduction is about 0.02% and the phase lead about \(1.14^\circ\). At 2 kHz both quadratures matter. A drift in humidity that lowers the resistance can look like a gain change if only the in-phase channel is recorded. Measure a known displacement at two carrier frequencies and fit both quadratures before interpreting it as a change in geometry.

Suppose the two actual drives are \(V_d(1+a)\) and \(-V_d(1-a)\). At centre the output becomes \(\eta V_da\), equivalent in the small-signal calibration to displacement \(ag\). An amplitude imbalance of one part in ten thousand gives a 10 nm offset at \(g=100\,\mu\mathrm m\), many orders above a short-band noise floor expressed in picometres. Reverse the mechanical calibration displacement to isolate odd signal, measure the voltages at the electrode terminals and compare the nulled bridge under exchange of the drive channels. Reversing both carrier phases simply reverses both desired signal and this coherently driven offset; by itself it cannot distinguish them. An offset stable over a short scan can be estimated from two symmetric calibration displacements, whereas a drifting imbalance requires a reference or simultaneous drive measurement.

Find the minimum for a compliant probe instead of guessing at it

It is possible to optimise the free probe analytically up to a scalar root. Write \(y=V_d^2\), \(K_e=hy\), and \(a\) for the readout displacement density at unit drive multiplied by one volt. Let \(d_F=\sqrt{4k_BT b}\) be the Brownian force density. At displacement frequencies well below the resonance, the total variance density has an electrical term falling as \(1/y\) and a mechanical term that grows as the spring softens:

\[S_x(y)=\frac{a^2}{y}+\frac{d_F^2}{(k-hy)^2},\]
\[{dS_x\over dy}=-{a^2\over y^2}+{2h d_F^2\over(k-hy)^3}.\]

On the stable interval the first derivative strictly increases when \(h>0\). The first term starts at negative infinity and the second diverges at the stiffness boundary. Hence there is one unconstrained minimum. Solve a bracketed scalar equation for \(y\), then compare with each experimental upper bound in volts squared. At zero parasitic capacitance the ideal floating plate has \(h=0\); total noise then falls monotonically toward an asymptotic Brownian floor. A numerical routine that divides by \(h\) would fail precisely at this informative physical limit. In either case, a force-noise specification referred to the external sample can produce a different optimum because force conversion multiplies displacement noise by stiffness.

With the baseline values \(a=2.039\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m\,V/\sqrt{Hz}}\), \(h=2.00\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{N/(m\,V^2)}\), \(d_F\simeq1.287\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{N/\sqrt{Hz}}\). Substitution brackets the stationary point between 1.45 and 1.50 V. At roughly 1.47 V the density is about \(6.72\,\mathrm{pm/\sqrt{Hz}}\), a shallow minimum compared with the heat-limited result. A tiny uncertainty in amplifier noise or in the support damping could shift the exact optimum appreciably while having very little effect on achievable resolution. Reporting a numerically optimised voltage to six decimals would imply a knowledge of the physical system absent from these inputs.

A direct bench experiment can test whether the excess really comes from compliance. Compare the demodulated spectrum with the centre plate tightly held and with it free. The electrical floor should be similar if the clamping keeps cable capacitance and carrier coupling fixed. The free probe should acquire a mechanical resonance which moves with \(V_d^2\), and the low-frequency component should track the inferred static stiffness. A large peak locked to carrier-drive amplitude modulation in both experiments suggests coherent feedthrough. Also check that the noise estimate comes from a frequency band above drift yet below resonance, and that the inferred amplitude density times the square root of the bandwidth predicts repeated stationary readings. These practical checks distinguish a readout-limited displacement estimate from a calibrated measurement of actual thermal motion.

Eddy-current braking, magnetic penetration and the heat left behind

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A conducting loop moves above a row of magnetic poles. The drag first increases with speed, but a rapid crossing need not generate proportionally greater braking. Trace the work done against the Lorentz force into circuit energy, heat and a measurable temperature change. Then decide which features of a solid plate the single-loop model can capture.

Generate a voltage from a spatially periodic field

Let a rectangular loop of height \(h\) along \(y\) and width \(w\) along \(x\) translate to the right above a stationary magnetic pattern. At the loop let the normal induction be \(B_z(x)=B_0\cos(kx)\), with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). Its two active sides are centred symmetrically about the coordinate \(x_c\). Assume the field varies negligibly along \(y\) over this height. Integrating through the loop gives

\[\Phi=h\int_{x_c-w/2}^{x_c+w/2}B_0\cos(kx)\,dx=[2hB_0\sin(kw/2)/k]\cos(kx_c).\]

A uniform translation \(x_c=ut\) therefore produces an external emf with peak amplitude \(E_{\rm pk}=K u\), where \(K=2hB_0|\sin(kw/2)|\).

This spatial averaging explains an initially surprising design feature. A loop spanning precisely one full spatial period always links zero net flux from the sinusoidal field and therefore generates no emf in this ideal geometry. A loop of half a period has maximum coupling at a specified height. If \(w\) is much smaller than the period,

\[\sin(kw/2)\simeq kw/2,\]
\[K\simeq h w B_0 k.\]

This is exactly the small-loop result from using its area times the local gradient of induction. Reversing velocity reverses the emf at a fixed position. At a fixed positive speed the temporal frequency is \(f=u/\lambda\), so the same conductor experiences different electrical frequencies at different mechanical speeds.

Include finite inductance and derive the drag curve

Model the current path by a constant resistance \(R\) and self-inductance \(L\). Its steady periodic current solves \(L\dot i+Ri=\mathcal E(t)\). In phasor notation its magnitude is \(E_{\rm pk}/\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L)^2}\), lagging the emf by \(\arctan(\omega L/R)\), where \(\omega=ku\). The current develops field energy \(Li^2/2\) and dissipates \(Ri^2\). Multiplying the circuit equation by \(i\) and averaging over a period eliminates the derivative of stored energy. The average mechanical power delivered against the drag is therefore the mean Joule heating \(P=E_{\rm pk}^2R/[2(R^2+\omega^2L^2)]\). Dividing by the speed gives the drag magnitude

\[F(u)=\frac{K^2u}{2R[1+(u/u_c)^2]},\]
\[u_c=\frac{R}{kL}.\]

At low speed the drag is proportional to velocity. At the crossover \(u=u_c\) it reaches \(K^2u_c/(4R)\), and at much larger speeds it falls approximately as \(1/u\) within the assumed lumped model. The incoming mechanical power then tends to a constant rather than growing without bound. Why is there a maximum? Increasing speed raises the emf, but it also raises the reversal frequency. An inductive current increasingly lags the driving emf, and its component in phase with the emf does the net dissipative work. A measured turnover therefore tests both an electrical time scale and the spatial scale of the magnet pattern.

Use \(h=50\,{\rm mm}\), \(w=25\,{\rm mm}\), \(\lambda=0.100\,{\rm m}\), \(B_0=0.20\,{\rm T}\), \(R=0.050\,\Omega\) and \(L=0.150\,{\rm mH}\). Then \(K=0.01414\,{\rm T\,m}\), \(u_c=5.31\,{\rm m/s}\), and maximum mean drag is \(5.31\,{\rm mN}\). At \(u=2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) the field alternates at \(20\,{\rm Hz}\), giving an emf of \(28.3\,{\rm mV}\) peak. The current is \(0.529\,{\rm A}\) peak and its phase lag \(20.7^\circ\). The resistor receives approximately \(7.00\,{\rm mW}\), and the motor maintaining translation supplies a mean force of \(3.50\,{\rm mN}\). Using peak current in \(I^2R\) without its factor one half would overstate the mechanical force by a factor two.

Determine whether the current penetrates a real plate

A continuous metal plate contains many possible current paths, unlike a preformed loop. Their resistances, inductances and mutual couplings depend on the plate boundary, magnet geometry and frequency. For a good homogeneous conductor the magnetic diffusion equation gives a skin depth \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\) under sinusoidal excitation. At a depth \(\delta\) a field entering a locally planar half-space has lost a factor \(e\) in amplitude and acquired a phase delay of one radian. The frequency to use is the frequency experienced by the moving material; the fact that the magnets are stationary in the laboratory does not make this frequency zero.

For copper with \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\,{\rm S/m}\) and permeability close to \(\mu_0\), the skin depth is about \(14.8\,{\rm mm}\) at \(20\,{\rm Hz}\) and \(1.48\,{\rm mm}\) at \(2.0\,{\rm kHz}\). A \(2.0\,{\rm mm}\) plate therefore sees little through-thickness attenuation in the first experiment but may need a diffusion calculation in the second. The plate's lateral current pattern can still matter when the through-thickness current density is nearly uniform. Slots interrupt wide current loops and usually reduce drag even when the mass and the bulk conductivity change little. Comparing a slotted and an unslotted plate directly tests the importance of current closure.

If a plate experiences sharply separated poles, its applied field in the moving frame contains a fundamental and higher harmonics. Harmonic \(n\) has a skin depth smaller by \(\sqrt n\), so checking only the fundamental can miss surface concentration associated with narrow features. A Hall scan along the track supplies the actual spatial field spectrum. Multiplying each spatial wavenumber by the speed converts it into a temporal angular frequency for a constant-speed pass. Once the speed changes significantly over one pole spacing, a time-domain calculation using the measured position trace is preferable to this fixed-frequency conversion.

Close a mechanical and thermal energy budget

Take a rotating brake as a second experiment. A flywheel of known inertia \(J_{\rm rot}=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) slows from \(120\) to \(80\,{\rm rad/s}\) while no drive supplies power. The released rotational kinetic energy is

\[J_{\rm rot}(120^2-80^2)/2=80.0\,{\rm J}.\]

A separate run with the magnet removed measures bearing and air losses. Suppose the corresponding speed-dependent losses integrated over the magnetic braking trajectory total \(8.0\,{\rm J}\). Then the electromagnetic channel receives \(72.0\,{\rm J}\). Using the reference run's total energy over an arbitrary equal time could give a different answer, since the two speed histories affect the parasitic losses.

A calorimeter enclosing the conducting rotor has effective heat capacity \(C=60\,{\rm J/K}\) and a thermal link of conductance \(G=0.12\,{\rm W/K}\) to an environment at constant temperature \(T_a\). Its balance is

\[C\,dT/dt=P_{\rm deposited}-G(T-T_a).\]

During a \(20\,{\rm s}\) run, suppose the time-averaged rise above ambient is \(0.50\,{\rm K}\). Then \(1.20\,{\rm J}\) flowed through the thermal link. If all electromagnetic heat entered the calorimeter, its final rise from an ambient start is

\[(72.0-1.20)/60=1.18\,{\rm K}.\]

A measured rise near \(0.80\,{\rm K}\) would leave an energy discrepancy of approximately \(23\,{\rm J}\). Check conduction into a shaft, heat stored outside the instrumented volume, and the estimate of parasitic torque before adjusting the electromagnetic theory.

Find the spatial features that dominate a measured braking signal

A single Fourier component of field already produces an informative turnover. In a tracked experiment the field often has a measurable third harmonic. Let \(B(x)=B_1\cos kx+B_3\cos(3kx+\varphi)\), with constant translation speed. Flux integration across the finite loop multiplies each harmonic by its own width factor. The corresponding emf peak is \(2h|B_n|u|\sin(nkw/2)|\), and its temporal angular frequency is \(nku\). The linear \(RL\) equation treats the two drives independently. Orthogonality over a complete fundamental period makes the mean of their product vanish, so total mean heat can be found by adding the individual resistive powers. Within one period the interference terms do affect instantaneous torque and power.

Suppose \(B_3/B_1=0.20\) and keep the preceding \(w=\lambda/4\). Here \(\sin(kw/2)\) and \(\sin(3kw/2)\) both have magnitude \(1/\sqrt2\). At low speed the third harmonic therefore adds about four percent to mean drag. At \(u=u_c\), however, its angular frequency sees inductive impedance \(3R\) while the fundamental sees \(R\). The third-harmonic share relative to the fundamental becomes \(0.20^2(1+1)/(1+9)=0.008\), only \(0.8\%\). A peak-to-peak field reading can change visibly without a comparable change in mean drag. Conversely, if the loop width happens to null one spatial harmonic, another smaller harmonic can supply most of the signal. A field map and the loop geometry together determine what source drives the circuit.

In a solid plate, predicting force from field and speed needs a field solution that enforces current closure. In the slowly moving conductor, \(\mathbf J=\sigma[-\nabla\phi+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B]\) when the imposed magnetic pattern is effectively time independent in the laboratory and the induced magnetic field can be neglected. Charge conservation then gives

\[\nabla\cdot[\sigma(-\nabla\phi+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)]=0.\]

On an electrically insulated plate edge, enforce \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J=0\). Solving this potential problem accounts for charge accumulation that redirects currents at a boundary. Simply setting \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf u\times\mathbf B\) can predict current flowing straight out of an insulating edge. The force follows from integrating \(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B\) through the conducting volume, and Joule heat follows from \(\int J^2/\sigma\,dV\).

A useful check on such a field calculation is a finite plate translating wholly inside an exactly uniform static magnetic field. Charges can rearrange until a uniform electrostatic field cancels \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B\). An isolated plate in this steady limit then has zero transport current and zero drag. Motion alone does not guarantee braking. At an entry edge, the moving conductor spans a spatial change of induction, and one scalar potential generally cannot cancel the motional drive around every closed path. Slits, thickness variations, a conducting contact at a boundary, and the induced field at higher speeds each change this test problem in an identifiable way.

Interpret a thermal transient without assuming it is instantaneous

If the brake runs at a stable speed and equilibrium electrical conditions, a local single-temperature model sees a nearly constant power. Its step response from ambient is \(\Delta T=(P/G)[1-\exp(-Gt/C)]\). Removing the drive gives an exponential decay with the same time constant \(C/G\). Measuring that cooling tail fixes the ratio of heat capacity to conductance but does not set either one separately. A known heater power or an independent heat-capacity measurement supplies the missing absolute scale. A slope evaluated over a long interval without correcting leakage will underestimate the input power as the sample warms.

Take a small bench calorimeter enclosing the single test loop with \(C=1.20\,{\rm J/K}\) and \(G=0.020\,{\rm W/K}\). The \(7.00\,{\rm mW}\) low-speed run gives a \(0.350\,{\rm K}\) eventual rise and a \(60\,{\rm s}\) time constant. After \(30\,{\rm s}\) the rise is about \(0.138\,{\rm K}\), whereas a thermally isolated calculation would give \(0.175\,{\rm K}\). Its stored heat \(0.166\,{\rm J}\) is less than the \(0.210\,{\rm J}\) mechanical work over that interval; the thermal link accounts for the remainder. Loading the conductor thermally with an additional copper holder increases the response time and changes the initial slope without changing the final rise if conductance to the bath remains the same.

Check the sign of instantaneous electromagnetic work

At a phase where the applied emf has just crossed zero, a finite-inductance loop may still carry considerable current. During part of a cycle the emf and current can have opposite signs. The loop briefly returns energy mechanically while its stored field decreases, and some of that release also becomes heat. A force transducer resolving separate pole passages can therefore see instantaneous variations that a mean braking-force estimate suppresses.

For the numerical \(2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) case the peak field energy is \(L I_{\rm pk}^2/2\approx21.0\,\mu{\rm J}\). The dissipated energy per full pole period is

\[7.00\,{\rm mW}/20\,{\rm Hz}=350\,\mu{\rm J}.\]

These quantities serve different purposes. The first is a reversible instantaneous store; the second is net irreversible energy delivered each time the conductor moves one spatial period. Dividing the second by the spatial period of \(0.100\,{\rm m}\) recovers the mean drag of \(3.50\,{\rm mN}\) without using the translational speed explicitly.

Work through the solution

The resistance of an ordinary metal generally increases with temperature. At a fixed speed \(F\) is proportional to \(R/[R^2+(\omega L)^2]\). Raising resistance reduces drag on the low-frequency resistive side and can increase it on the high-frequency inductive side. The maximum occurs at \(R=\omega L\) if electrical geometry and inductance stay fixed. Measuring the temperature and resistance before each run helps distinguish this effect from a weakening field or a changed gap.

Mechanics in the laboratory

A rigid pendulum can oscillate from either of two knife edges. If the periods agree at distinct distances from the centre of mass, the spacing of the edges determines gravity. Derive the cancellation, identify a second way equal periods can arise and find why a poorly chosen geometry magnifies timing errors.

Torque about an offset pivot

Take a rigid body of mass \(m\) with parallel suspension axes perpendicular to its plane of oscillation. Its moment of inertia about the centre of mass is \(I_C=m\rho^2\); \(\rho\) is the radius of gyration. For a pivot a distance \(a>0\) from the centre, the parallel-axis theorem gives

\[I_a=m(\rho^2+a^2).\]

Gravity exerts torque \(-mga\sin\theta\). In the small-angle limit,

\[T_a^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{g}\frac{\rho^2+a^2}{a},\]
\[L_a=a+\frac{\rho^2}{a}.\]

Here \(L_a\) is the length of a point pendulum with the same period. It can lie beyond the physical centre of mass. A tap applied at this distance from a pivot gives the centre of percussion when the ideal instantaneous pivot reaction is to vanish. The same combination of mass distribution and geometry appears in an impulsive and in an oscillatory measurement because both depend on angular momentum about that pivot.

Match the periods and keep both algebraic branches

Put the second knife edge on the opposite side of the centre at distance \(b\). Equal periods require

\[a+\rho^2/a=b+\rho^2/b.\]

Rearrange before dividing by \(a-b\):

\[(a-b)\left(1-\frac{\rho^2}{ab}\right)=0.\]

Two equal distances always give equal periods. That branch provides no information about \(\rho\). If the distances are distinct, equality implies

\[\rho^2=ab.\]

Both equivalent lengths then equal \(a+b\), the directly measurable edge separation. For \(a=0.200\,\mathrm m,\ b=0.450\,\mathrm m,\ \rho=0.300\,\mathrm m\), the equivalent length is 0.650 m and both periods at standard laboratory gravity are about 1.62 s. A small value of \(a-b\) is an experimental warning sign, even if the observed equality is exceptionally clean.

Background · Can unequal release amplitudes imitate a mismatch between pivots?

A rigid pendulum about either knife edge obeys the same sine nonlinearity as a simple pendulum. To leading order in its release angle \(\theta\) in radians, the measured period exceeds the small-amplitude period by the fraction \(\theta^2/16\). At matched small-amplitude periods, measurements with amplitudes \(\theta_a,\theta_b\) therefore give

\[\frac{T_a-T_b}{T_0}\simeq\frac{\theta_a^2-\theta_b^2}{16}.\]

Releasing at 6° about the first edge and 2° about the other creates a fractional mismatch of about \(6.1\times10^{-4}\). For a two-second period this is 1.2 ms. Record the two amplitudes along with the timing data and reduce both periods to the same limit before moving the trim mass. Otherwise the trim can compensate a preparation error.

Extract gravity when the two measured periods differ

Multiply the first period equation by \(a\) and the second by \(b\). Subtract to eliminate \(\rho^2\). The exact small-angle result for unequal distances is

\[g=4\pi^2\,\frac{a^2-b^2}{aT_a^2-bT_b^2}.\]

At matched periods this reduces to the simple expression in terms of the edge spacing. The general formula involves a difference in its denominator. If \(a\) and \(b\) nearly coincide, the denominator is small and two independent timing errors subtract with poor relative precision. This is a property of the inverse measurement, even if computing each individual period is perfectly well conditioned.

Once \(g\) is known, use \(\rho^2=gaT_a^2/(4\pi^2)-a^2\) to recover the body inertia per unit mass. A negative value may indicate a wrong distance measured to the centre or a timing observable that is actually a half period. Verify the result using the second pivot and recover a separately predicted period after shifting a suspension point. A single successful cancellation is weaker than reproducing an additional measurement.
