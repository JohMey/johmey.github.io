From first principles

Derive the capstan bound from a small belt arc

A flexible flat belt transmits force by friction as it wraps around a pulley. Assume a thin belt, small bending stiffness, steady low-speed operation and constant coefficient of static friction \(\mu\). Consider an arc element subtending \(d\theta\) radians with tension \(T\) at one end and \(T+dT\) at the other. The inward components of the adjoining tensions produce a normal contact force approximately \(dN=T\,d\theta\). Tangential equilibrium requires friction to carry the tension change.

\[|dT|\leq\mu\,dN=\mu T\,d\theta .\]

Integrate from the slack-side tension \(T_2\) to tight-side tension \(T_1\) over total contact angle \(\beta\). Equality describes impending gross slip in this ideal limit.

\[\ln(T_1/T_2)\leq\mu\beta,\qquad T_1/T_2\leq e^{\mu\beta}.\]

The net tangential force is the tension difference. At belt speed \(v\) the transmitted mechanical power is

\[P=(T_1-T_2)v.\]

Raising both tensions together can increase the available difference but also raises shaft and bearing loads. At high belt speed the inward acceleration of each moving element changes the normal-force balance.

Derivation and applications

Calculate belt wrap and length from pulley geometry

A small pulley loses contact angle when the two pulley radii differ. Consider an open, crossed-free belt around circular pitch radii \(R>r\) separated by centre distance \(C>R-r\). Treat the belt as thin, taut and inextensible and ignore sag. The radii to the ends of one straight span are perpendicular to its tangent. Translating that tangent parallel to itself produces a right triangle with hypotenuse \(C\) and short side \(R-r\). The resulting angle \(\alpha\) and span length \(\ell\) obey

\[\sin\alpha=\frac{R-r}{C},\]
\[\ell=\sqrt{C^2-(R-r)^2}=C\cos\alpha .\]

The small pulley loses angle \(\alpha\) at each side relative to a semicircle, while the larger one gains the same amount. Summing the two straight spans and the two arc lengths gives the exact pitch length in the ideal geometry.

\[\beta_s=\pi-2\alpha,\]
\[\beta_L=\pi+2\alpha,\]
\[L=2\ell+\pi(R+r)+2(R-r)\alpha .\]

For a long centre distance expand the square root and inverse sine in \((R-r)/C\). The leading correction to the simple sum of two centre distances and two semicircles is positive. The same geometry sets the small pulley's friction-limited tension ratio for a stationary or low-speed flat belt.

\[L\simeq2C+\pi(R+r)+\frac{(R-r)^2}{C},\qquad T_1/T_2\leq e^{\mu\beta_s}.\]

Angle in the exponential must be in radians. Increasing centre distance brings the two wraps closer to half a circle but also changes required belt length. A tensioning device must accommodate both geometry and elastic stretch.

Worked example

Determine a small pulley's available traction

For \(R=100\) mm, \(r=40.0\) mm and \(C=300\) mm, \(\alpha=\arcsin0.200=0.2014\) rad. Small-pulley wrap is 2.739 rad or 156.9°. Exact pitch length is

\[2(293.94)+\pi(140)+2(60)(0.2014)=1052\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

With \(\mu=0.300\) the limiting tension ratio is \(e^{0.300(2.739)}=2.27\), lower than the 2.57 obtained from a half-circle wrap.

Second application

Find the minimum shaft spacing for a specified wrap

Another drive uses pitch radii of 75.0 and 25.0 mm and requires at least 160° contact on the smaller pulley. This limits \(\alpha\) to 10.0°, so

\[C\geq50.0/\sin10.0^\circ=288\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

At this spacing the straight span is

\[C\cos10.0^\circ=284\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

A proposed spacing of 250 mm would give only \(180^\circ-2\arcsin(50/250)=156.9^\circ\).

Resolve the forces on a small arc

A flat belt transmits torque through friction distributed over the part of the pulley it touches. Cut out a short belt element subtending \(d\theta\). Let tension entering the element be \(T\) and tension leaving be \(T+dT\). For a flexible belt in quasistatic normal equilibrium the two tension vectors have a small inward radial resultant \(T\,d\theta\). The pulley supplies an outward normal force of the same magnitude. Neglect belt bending stiffness and centrifugal tension in the following low-speed model.

At impending gross slip, available tangential friction is \(\mu\,dN\). Tangential equilibrium gives \(dT=\mu T\,d\theta\) along the direction of increasing belt tension. Integrate from the slack to the tight end of a contact arc of angle \(\beta\) in radians. Below impending slip the resulting ratio is an inequality.

\[\ln(T_1/T_2)\le\mu\beta,\qquad T_1/T_2\le e^{\mu\beta},\qquad P=(T_1-T_2)v.\]

The final power expression comes from transmitted torque \((T_1-T_2)r\) times angular velocity \(v/r\). It is the tension difference that drives the machine. Both tensions contribute to radial loading on the shafts. In an open belt drive the smaller pulley often has the smaller contact angle and controls traction first.

Compute the least operating tensions

A flat belt must transmit 2.0 kW at 8.0 m/s. It wraps through 160° on the limiting pulley, with effective friction coefficient 0.30. The required tension difference is 250 N. Converting 160° to 2.793 rad gives a maximum ratio \(r_T=e^{0.30(2.793)}=2.31\). At the limiting ratio \(T_2=250/(r_T-1)=191\,\mathrm N\) and \(T_1=441\,\mathrm N\). Lower slack-side tension would require a ratio beyond the assumed friction limit.

For negligible centrifugal effect and approximately equal span compliances, an initial pretension estimate is \(T_0=(T_1+T_2)/2=316\,\mathrm N\) per span. If the two straight spans pull nearly parallel, the resultant transverse shaft force is approximately \(T_1+T_2=632\,\mathrm N\). If their directions differ appreciably, add the two tension vectors rather than their magnitudes. A higher pretension improves the available traction margin while increasing load on bearings and belt cords.

Decide what a loss of friction does

If oil contamination reduces the friction coefficient to 0.20 at the same wrap, the limiting tension ratio falls to

\[e^{0.20(2.793)}=1.75.\]

If the tension sum remains 632 N, the largest transferable difference is \(632(r_T-1)/(r_T+1)\simeq172\,\mathrm N\), giving at most 1.38 kW at 8 m/s. The original power demand now exceeds available traction. A larger contact angle, different belt design or altered surface condition may address the shortfall; increasing motor power alone does not change the traction bound. A toothed timing belt has a different transmission mechanism and requires its tooth engagement and cord loading to be evaluated separately.

Interpret belt speed

The pitch-line speed in the no-slip approximation is \(\pi d n/60\). A 200 mm pulley carrying the original 8 m/s belt turns at approximately 764 rpm. Speed should be measured on both shafts if slipping contact is suspected.

Include the tension needed to turn a moving belt

The quasistatic capstan law assumes the belt has negligible inertia as it changes direction round a pulley. At higher speed, each arc element of linear mass density \(\lambda\) needs inward centripetal force. Consider a segment subtending \(d\theta\) at radius \(r\). Its mass is \(\lambda r\,d\theta\) and required inward force is \(\lambda v^2 d\theta\). Radial equilibrium of the segment gives

\[T\,d\theta-dN=\lambda v^2 d\theta,\]

where \(dN\) is the outward reaction from the pulley. Available traction thus depends on the effective tension \(T-T_c\), with \(T_c=\lambda v^2\).

At incipient sliding \(dT=\mu(T-T_c)d\theta\) for constant belt speed. Integrating gives

\[(T_1-T_c)/(T_2-T_c)=e^{\mu\beta}.\]

If the allowable total tight-side tension is \(T_{\max}\) and wrap and friction are fixed, the maximum tension difference is \((T_{\max}-\lambda v^2)(1-e^{-\mu\beta})\). Multiplying by \(v\) yields a cubic in speed for ideal power capacity:

\[P_{\max}(v)=(1-e^{-\mu\beta})(T_{\max}v-\lambda v^3).\]

The derivative vanishes at \(v_*=\sqrt{T_{\max}/(3\lambda)}\), where centrifugal tension consumes one third of the allowed total tight-side tension. This optimum presumes adequate pretension, fixed allowable tension independent of speed, and acceptable bending and thermal losses. It should not be applied without checking the belt and pulley operating limits.

Choose between increasing shaft speed and increasing wrap

For an illustrative belt \(T_{\max}=700\,\mathrm N,\ \lambda=0.25\,\mathrm{kg/m},\ \mu=0.30,\ \beta=\pi\) rad. The traction factor is

\[1-e^{-0.30\pi}=0.610.\]

At 20 m/s centrifugal tension is \(0.25(20)^2=100\,\mathrm N\), giving limiting tension difference \(0.610(700-100)=366\,\mathrm N\) and deliverable power 7.32 kW. At the ideal optimum \(v_*=\sqrt{700/0.75}=30.55\,\mathrm{m/s}\), centrifugal tension is 233.3 N, difference 284.7 N and power 8.70 kW. Extra speed initially helps even as the available tension difference falls.

At 40 m/s centrifugal tension becomes 400 N and capacity falls back to \(0.610(300)(40)=7.32\,\mathrm{kW}\). Thus two pulley speeds can deliver the same calculated limiting power with very different running tensions and bending-cycle rates. Increasing wrap from 180° to 210° at 20 m/s instead changes the traction factor to \(1-e^{-0.30(3.665)}=0.667\), raising calculated capacity to 8.00 kW without higher speed. An added idler can supply this wrap but also introduces another bend and bearing load. The choice should follow the limiting failure mechanism: speed-dependent belt fatigue, inadequate traction margin and shaft overloading respond differently to the two modifications.

The effect of an allowable tension change is also nonlinear when speed is chosen optimally. Substituting \(v_*\) gives optimum ideal power proportional to \(T_{\max}^{3/2}/\sqrt\lambda\) at fixed wrap and friction. A reinforcement that increases both allowable tension and mass density must be assessed using both factors. For example, raising \(T_{\max}\) by 20% while increasing \(\lambda\) by 44% gives only \(1.2^{3/2}/\sqrt{1.44}=1.095\), or about 9.5% more ideal peak power. A heavier belt can also impose greater centrifugal tension at an existing pulley speed, so the actual duty point may benefit less than this re-optimised maximum.

The characteristic speed should consequently be recalculated for the proposed belt construction at its actual working radius.
