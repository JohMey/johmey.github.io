Force transmission and compliance near a toggle position

Two links approach alignment as a mechanical fixture closes. A small applied force can correspond to a large output reaction, but the ratio also makes the result sensitive to clearance and compliance. Derive it from displacement compatibility.

Choose a coordinate that remains measurable

Join two equal links of length L at an upper knee and place their outer ends on a common horizontal line. Pin the left end to the frame and allow the right end to slide horizontally. The two links form a symmetric tent with common angle \(\theta\) above the base. The knee coordinates are \((L\cos\theta,L\sin\theta)\), and the slider position is \(x=2L\cos\theta\). A vertical driver presses on the knee while allowing its small horizontal motion. These kinematic conditions matter because a guide fixing the knee's horizontal coordinate would impose a different linkage.

\[y=L\sin\theta,\]
\[x=2L\cos\theta,\]
\[{dx\over dy}={-2L\sin\theta\over L\cos\theta}=-2\tan\theta .\]

A descending knee extends the horizontal span. Near alignment the horizontal motion becomes small relative to the vertical stroke. An expansion in small y gives \(x=2\sqrt{L^2-y^2}\simeq2L-y^2/L\). The position deficit is quadratic in knee height.

Derive force amplification and check equilibrium

Assume rigid links, ideal pins and slow movement. Let a downward driver force have magnitude P and let a horizontal resisting load at the slider have magnitude Q. For any compatible infinitesimal motion, zero net work gives \(P|dy|=Q|dx|\). Hence

\[{Q\over P}={1\over2\tan\theta} .\]

An independent force diagram at the knee shows that each compressed link carries \(C=P/(2\sin\theta)\). Its horizontal component at the slider is \(C\cos\theta\), giving the same ratio. As alignment approaches, the linearized kinematic ratio diverges. Finite stiffness, clearances, buckling of slender links and elastic deformation at the joints then determine the actual force for a prescribed driver travel. A geometric ratio alone cannot predict the force at exact alignment.

Distinguish a force-controlled drive from a compliant load

With \(L=100\) mm and an applied \(P=60\) N, an angle of 15° gives \(Q=112\) N and link compression 116 N. At 5° the same input would balance \(Q=343\) N and link compression 344 N. Meanwhile the remaining horizontal travel to full extension shrinks from \(2L(1-\cos15^\circ)=6.81\) mm to 0.761 mm. The final part of the stroke therefore has high force ratio and little remaining travel.

Now replace the prescribed resisting force with a horizontal spring of stiffness 100 N/mm, first contacted at an angle of 10°. At 5° the additional extension is \(2L(\cos5^\circ-\cos10^\circ)=2.28\) mm. The spring force is 228 N and the required input is only \(2Q\tan5^\circ=39.9\) N. The ideal force-controlled estimate of 343 N answers a different question because the actual load follows the spring and the platform displacement. Compare the full angle range before deciding where maximum driver effort occurs.

Tutorial · recover an angle from two force readings

For a slow frictionless stroke a driver transducer reads 50 N and the horizontal reaction is 250 N. Estimate \(\theta\), link compression and residual travel for 100 mm links. What measurement could reveal pin friction independently?

Show the equilibrium and geometry

The observed ratio gives \(\tan\theta=50/(2\cdot250)=0.10\). Thus \(\theta=5.71^\circ\), \(C=250/\cos\theta=251.2\) N and residual travel is \(200(1-\cos\theta)=0.993\) mm. Compare the force needed during slow closing and opening at the same geometry and spring deflection. Ideally the elastic load gives the same relation both ways. Dry friction adds a direction-dependent component that changes that pair of readings.
