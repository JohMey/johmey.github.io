From first principles

Propagate dimensional variation into an assembly clearance

Start with the physical closing dimension of an assembly. Suppose the functional gap is \(g=A-B-C\) and the measured dimensions have stable means and variances. The signed relation comes from geometry, so taking expectations gives the mean gap regardless of independence. For a conservative interval check, the smallest gap pairs the smallest \(A\) with the largest \(B\) and \(C\).

\[\bar g=\bar A-\bar B-\bar C,\]
\[g_{\min}=A_{\min}-B_{\max}-C_{\max}.\]

For a statistical estimate, subtract the means and square the sum of deviations. When component deviations are independent, all mixed expectations vanish. The signs then disappear from the variance because each individual coefficient is squared.

\[\sigma_g^2=\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2+\sigma_C^2 .\]

If the independent dimensions are normal, \(g\) is normal and the interference probability is \(P(g<0)=\Phi(-\bar g/\sigma_g)\), with \(\Phi\) the standard normal cumulative distribution. A tolerance interval alone does not supply a standard deviation or distribution. Correlated machining errors leave mixed covariance terms in the squared sum and can increase or reduce the assembly spread.

Derivation and applications

Propagate a shared manufacturing drift through a clearance stack

Two dimensions made on the same fixture can vary together. A clearance depends on their difference, so ignoring covariance can substantially misstate the expected spread. Let \(H\) be an internal dimension and \(S\) the mating external dimension; assembled clearance is \(G=H-S\). Model a shared fixture drift by a zero-mean random variable \(Z\) with standard deviation \(\sigma_Z\). Let \(e_H,e_S\) be independent local deviations, also independent of \(Z\). The sensitivities \(a_H,a_S\) convert drift into dimensional change.

\[H=\bar H+a_H Z+e_H,\]
\[S=\bar S+a_S Z+e_S .\]

Subtract the dimensions before calculating variance. Only the difference between their sensitivities multiplies the shared term. Squaring and averaging eliminates products of independent zero-mean deviations.

\[\bar G=\bar H-\bar S,\]
\[\sigma_G^2=(a_H-a_S)^2\sigma_Z^2+\sigma_{e_H}^2+\sigma_{e_S}^2 .\]

Equivalently use the variance of each measured dimension and their covariance. Matched positive sensitivities cancel common fixture drift, while sensitivities of opposite sign amplify it. These relations require finite variances and the stated correlations; no particular distribution has yet been assumed.

\[\sigma_G^2=\sigma_H^2+\sigma_S^2-2\,\operatorname{Cov}(H,S),\]
\[\operatorname{Cov}(H,S)=a_Ha_S\sigma_Z^2 .\]

For normally distributed deviations the ratio of mean clearance to its standard deviation also gives the normal-tail estimate of interference probability. Pairing components from unrelated fixtures may remove the very covariance measured on matched components.

Worked example

Compare matched and randomly remixed parts

Take \(a_H=a_S=1\), shared drift spread 20.0 µm, and local independent spreads of 6.00 and 8.00 µm. A matched pair has clearance spread \(\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10.0\) µm. If components are randomly paired from independent fixture drifts, the spread becomes \(\sqrt{6^2+8^2+2(20)^2}=30.0\) µm. For mean clearance 30.0 µm, normally distributed matched pairs have interference probability about 0.135 percent; independent remixed pairs give about 15.9 percent.

Second application

Infer manufacturing correlation from assembly records

Another process measures standard deviations of 12.0 µm for the bore, 10.0 µm for the shaft and 8.00 µm for their assembled clearance. Rearranging the variance identity gives covariance \((12^2+10^2-8^2)/2=90.0\) µm². The inferred correlation coefficient is \(90/(12\times10)=0.750\). A reconstruction using independent component measurements would give 15.6 µm clearance spread, nearly twice the measured value.

Begin with a physical closing dimension

A drawing is useful only if the manufactured pieces can meet in the intended order and provide the required clearance. Choose a positive direction along the assembly and write the closing dimension from a single datum. For a spacer fitted between two shoulders with a washer, suppose the available nominal length is \(H\), the spacer length is \(S\) and the washer thickness is \(W\). The clearance is \(g=H-S-W\). Housing expansion or coating thickness can be included as additional signed terms if they occur in the operating condition being assessed.

With specified independent admissible intervals, the worst-case minimum is \(H_{\min}-S_{\max}-W_{\max}\). Independence is unnecessary for this conservative bound, although correlated dimensions may prevent its most extreme combination from occurring. A statistical treatment asks a different question: given a supported production distribution, what fraction is likely to achieve a required clearance? Manufacturing limits alone do not determine such a distribution.

Propagate measured spreads

Write each dimension as its mean plus a deviation. The mean clearance is the same signed sum of means. For independent dimensions the variance is the sum of their variances because squaring the coefficients removes their signs. More generally, for a dimension vector with covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) and coefficient vector \(a\), \(\sigma_g^2=a^T\Sigma a\). A common machining datum can introduce correlations that either reduce or increase clearance variation.

Assume \(H=50.00\pm0.15,\ S=48.00\pm0.12,\ W=1.70\pm0.09\,\mathrm{mm}\). The nominal clearance is 0.30 mm and limiting clearance interval is \([0.30-0.36,0.30+0.36]=[-0.06,0.66]\,\mathrm{mm}\). Thus the stated tolerances do not guarantee a positive gap. Suppose separately that production measurements support independent, centred normal models with standard deviations 0.050, 0.040 and 0.030 mm, without further sorting. The resulting standard deviation is

\[\sigma_g=\sqrt{0.050^2+0.040^2+0.030^2}=0.0707\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Interference requires clearance below zero, a deviation of \(0.30/0.0707=4.24\) standard deviations. The normal model estimates about \(1.1\times10^{-5}\) of pairs would interfere, roughly eleven per million. This small estimated fraction and the possibility in a worst-case limit calculation address different production questions. An unmeasured mean shift or sorting parts to their tolerance limits can substantially change the probability.

Combine component survival probabilities

Define reliability at an explicit mission duration as the probability that a component performs its required function through that duration. If a machine requires both of two independent components to survive, its reliability is the product. Two bearings each assessed at 0.98 mission survival give \(0.98^2=0.9604\) for both. If two independent drives are genuinely redundant and either can carry the full demand, failure requires both to fail, giving \(1-(1-0.98)^2=0.9996\).

A shared lubricating supply or housing defect can defeat independence in either calculation. Similarly, a clearance that is satisfactory on the day of assembly need not remain satisfactory after thermal growth or wear. Carry the measured operating temperature range into the same dimension chain, and carry the relevant mission duration and shared causes into the reliability calculation. Both methods depend as strongly on the physical definition of success as on the arithmetic.

Estimate a life scale without discarding run-outs

An endurance test may end with some assemblies still functioning. Their exact failure times are unknown, yet they have demonstrated survival up to the stop time. Treating each as an observed failure at that time biases a life estimate downward, while discarding them throws away survival information. For illustration, assume independent, identically produced parts under a common load and a two-parameter Weibull survival law \(R(t)=\exp[-(t/\eta)^\beta]\). Its scale \(\eta\) has units of time and is the lifetime at which survival is \(e^{-1}\), while \(\beta\) sets the hazard shape. Assume \(\beta\) has been established independently for this exercise.

Differentiating survival gives the failure density \(f(t)=(\beta/\eta)(t/\eta)^{\beta-1}R(t)\). The likelihood contribution of a failed part is \(f(t_i)\); that of a right-censored surviving part is \(R(t_j)\). If \(d\) failures occur in the test, collecting all terms depending on \(\eta\) gives \(\ln L=\text{constant}-\beta d\ln\eta-\sum_{\rm all}(t_i/\eta)^\beta\). Differentiating with respect to \(\eta\) gives the maximum-likelihood scale

\[\hat\eta=\left(\frac{\sum_{\rm all}t_i^\beta}{d}\right)^{1/\beta}.\]

The numerator includes tested times of both failed and unfailed specimens, while the denominator counts only failures. With no failures this expression has no finite scale estimate; the data can support a lower bound under additional statistical assumptions. A Weibull model also presumes the tested population and failure mechanism remain relevant to the intended use.

Interpret a six-unit endurance test for a specified mission

Six identical drives are tested under the same continuous load. Three fail at 100, 150 and 200 h; the other three are stopped in working condition at 220 h. Use \(\beta=2\). The sum of exposure times squared is \(100^2+150^2+200^2+3(220^2)=217700\,\mathrm{h^2}\). Dividing by three failures and taking the square root gives \(\hat\eta=269.4\,\mathrm h\). The fitted single-drive survival at 150 h is \(\exp[-(150/269.4)^2]=0.733\). The estimated time at 90% survival is \(\hat\eta\sqrt{-\ln0.90}=87.4\,\mathrm h\). A proposed mission should therefore specify both duration and required probability.

If a machine needs two independently manufactured drives both functioning for the 150 h mission, fitted machine survival is \(0.733^2=0.538\); if either one can take the entire load and failure of one does not affect its companion, it becomes \(1-(1-0.733)^2=0.929\). Neither system calculation includes uncertainty in the fitted scale or the assumed shape, which is substantial in a six-unit sample. If all drives share a contaminant or one common failure mode, independence can also fail. The three run-outs increase the scale relative to using only observed failures, but they do not certify service beyond their observed duration at an arbitrary reliability target.

The hazard inferred under the assumed shape is \(h(t)=2t/\eta^2\), so its value doubles from 100 to 200 h. Replacing the fitted distribution by a constant failure rate calibrated at one time would alter the conditional survival of an already-aged drive. This matters when a maintenance interval concerns parts that have already completed one mission.
