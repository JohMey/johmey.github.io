From first principles

Derive the flux through a moving lubricant film

Consider two broad bearing surfaces separated by a thin gap \(h\). Let the lower surface move at speed \(U\) and the upper one remain still. Assume incompressible Newtonian lubricant, no slip, negligible inertia and a gap changing only slowly in the streamwise direction. Pressure is nearly constant through the thickness. Locally, the streamwise momentum balance reduces to a pressure gradient balanced by viscous shear curvature.

\[\mu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}=\frac{dp}{dx},\]
\[u(0)=U,\]
\[u(h)=0 .\]

Integrating twice gives a linear velocity contribution from sliding and a parabolic contribution from the pressure gradient. Integrating that velocity across the gap gives the discharge per unit width.

\[u(y)=U(1-y/h)-\frac{1}{2\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}y(h-y),\]
\[q=\frac{Uh}{2}-\frac{h^3}{12\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}.\]

In a steady film without leakage or normal surface motion the same \(q\) crosses every section. A varying gap changes the sliding contribution, forcing a pressure gradient to preserve that flow. The integral of gauge pressure over the bearing pad gives its normal load capacity.

Derivation and applications

Relate recess pressure to restrictor resistance in a hydrostatic pad

A hydrostatic bearing can sustain load at zero sliding speed because an external supply drives lubricant through a restriction. Consider a flat circular thrust pad with a central recess of radius \(a\) and outer land radius \(b\). Let the parallel land clearance \(h\) be uniform and much smaller than its radial extent. Use incompressible Newtonian oil of viscosity \(\mu\), steady laminar radial outflow, no squeeze motion, ambient gauge pressure at \(b\), and negligible pressure drop inside the recess. The pressure-driven flow per circumferential width follows the parallel-plate law.

\[Q=-2\pi r\frac{h^3}{12\mu}\frac{dp}{dr},\]
\[p(r)=\frac{6\mu Q}{\pi h^3}\ln\frac br .\]

At the recess edge \(p(a)=p_r\). The land therefore behaves as a flow resistance \(R_L\) proportional to the inverse cube of clearance. A linear feed restrictor \(R_f\) drops supply pressure \(p_s\) to \(p_r\). Equal flow passes through the feed and land, giving a hydraulic pressure divider.

\[R_L=\frac{6\mu}{\pi h^3}\ln(b/a),\]
\[p_r=Q R_L=p_s\frac{R_L}{R_f+R_L}.\]

Integrate pressure over the uniform recess and logarithmic land. The logarithm integral contains an inner-boundary term that cancels the associated part of the recess load, leaving a simple effective area.

\[W=\pi a^2p_r+2\pi\int_a^b r p(r)\,dr=\frac{\pi(b^2-a^2)}{2\ln(b/a)}p_r .\]

Closing the pad increases land resistance, raises recess pressure and increases force opposing closure. When the land resistance is already very large, recess pressure approaches supply pressure and the available load saturates.

Worked example

Predict load at a balanced restriction

For \(a=10.0\) mm and \(b=20.0\) mm, effective area is

\[\pi(0.0200^2-0.0100^2)/(2\ln2)=6.80\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}.\]

If \(R_L=R_f\) at the operating clearance and \(p_s=0.800\) MPa, recess pressure is 0.400 MPa and supported load is 272 N. This load is present without any relative sliding of the two faces.

Second application

Infer a clearance change from pressure

A different bearing has \(p_r/p_s=0.600\) initially and 0.750 after a load change. Resistance ratios \(R_L/R_f=p_r/(p_s-p_r)\) therefore increase from 1.50 to 3.00. Since land resistance varies as \(h^{-3}\), the final gap is \(2^{-1/3}=0.794\) of its initial value. An initial 25.0 µm film becomes 19.8 µm, assuming unchanged oil viscosity and feed resistance.

Where lubricant pressure comes from

A shaft turning inside a plain bearing drags lubricant around with its surface. A load displaces the journal from the bore centre, creating a film whose gap narrows in one direction. Wall motion carries oil into this wedge, and a pressure gradient redistributes the flow so the same fluid rate passes successive sections. Pressure integrated over the bearing can then support a load. Surface motion alone in a uniform gap between surfaces with equal end pressures cannot produce this load in the ideal steady model.

Consider a broad slider bearing rather than the full journal geometry. Let a lower wall move at speed \(U\); a fixed upper wall forms a slowly varying small gap \(h(x)\). Assume incompressible Newtonian lubricant, constant viscosity, negligible inertia and negligible leakage through the sides. Pressure is nearly uniform across the thickness. The axial momentum equation reduces to \(\mu d^2u/dy^2=dp/dx\), and no slip gives

\[u(0)=U,\ u(h)=0.\]
\[u=U(1-y/h)-\frac{1}{2\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}\,y(h-y),\]
\[q=\frac{Uh}{2}-\frac{h^3}{12\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}.\]

Mass conservation makes \(q\) per unit width constant along the bearing. Where the local dragged flow \(Uh/2\) exceeds \(q\), pressure rises downstream. Where it falls short, pressure falls and supplies a forward pressure-driven contribution. The transition locates a pressure maximum.

Solve a two-depth ideal bearing

Consider a stepped pad with two equal 10 mm long regions, gaps \(h_1=0.20\) and \(h_2=0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) in the sliding direction. Both ends have ambient pressure, \(U=1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), and \(\mu=0.05\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\). Ignore the local loss at the abrupt step and treat each region as fully developed. Integration of \(dp/dx=12\mu(Uh/2-q)/h^3\) across both regions gives zero net pressure change. With equal lengths this fixes

\[q=\frac U2\frac{h_1^{-2}+h_2^{-2}}{h_1^{-3}+h_2^{-3}}=5.56\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}.\]

In the first region \(Uh_1/2=1.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). The positive pressure gradient is \(3.33\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa/m}\), giving an interface gauge pressure of 33.3 kPa. The second region has an equal opposite gradient. The resulting triangular pressure distribution integrates to a force per unit width of \(0.5(0.020)(33333)=333\,\mathrm{N/m}\). A nominal 50 mm width would thus support approximately 16.7 N under the negligible-side-leakage assumption.

Understand failure at low speed

With zero sliding speed, equal end pressures imply zero steady gauge pressure everywhere in this model, so the load capacity vanishes. Start-up and shutdown therefore differ from established hydrodynamic running. A separately pressurised hydrostatic bearing can carry load at zero shaft speed. If a solution predicts large negative absolute pressures in a diverging region, actual oil may cavitate and the full-film boundary conditions must change. Viscous heating can also lower the local viscosity. Film thickness, thermal balance and supply conditions all matter when transferring the simple slider result to a journal in service.

Predict a viscosity change

For the same slider geometry and speed with equal prescribed end pressures, the calculated flux is independent of viscosity but all gauge pressure gradients are proportional to viscosity. Halving viscosity in the ideal stepped example therefore halves its load capacity at unchanged film thickness.

Derive load carried by a closing lubricant film

Sliding motion is not the only way to develop pressure in a thin film. Two parallel circular plates moving towards each other expel viscous oil radially, producing a squeeze-film pressure. Let plate radius be \(R\), uniform gap \(h(t)\), and approach speed \(v=-dh/dt>0\). Assume incompressible Newtonian oil, no slip, negligible inertia, rigid parallel plates, negligible edge resistance and ambient pressure at the rim. The gap is much smaller than the radius. Neither plate moves radially, so depth-integrated radial flux per unit circumference is \(q_r=-h^3(dp/dr)/(12\mu)\), the stationary-wall pressure-driven slit relation.

Inside radius \(r\), incompressibility requires outward flow through its circumference to equal the volume displaced by plate motion: \(2\pi r q_r=\pi r^2v\). Thus \(q_r=rv/2\) and \(dp/dr=-6\mu rv/h^3\). Integrating inward from ambient at the edge gives

\[p(r)-p_a=\frac{3\mu v}{h^3}(R^2-r^2),\]
\[W=\int_0^R(p-p_a)2\pi r\,dr=\frac{3\pi\mu R^4v}{2h^3}.\]

The maximum pressure is at the centre, twice the area average because the pressure shape is parabolic. The load grows very steeply as the gap narrows at imposed speed. At imposed load, however, the approach slows. This distinction mirrors the need to separate displacement-controlled and force-controlled contact tests. A stationary gap with no relative normal motion produces no squeeze-generated pressure under the stated ambient boundary condition.

Estimate the time for a thrust pad to approach its stop

A circular pad of radius 25 mm settles under a constant additional normal force of 100 N on oil of dynamic viscosity \(0.15\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\). Ignore inertia after the short initial transient. Rearranging the load law gives

\[-dh/dt=2Wh^3/(3\pi\mu R^4).\]

Between initial and final gaps, integration gives

\[t=[3\pi\mu R^4/(4W)](h_f^{-2}-h_i^{-2}).\]

For \(h_i=150\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) and \(h_f=50\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) this yields 0.491 s.

At an intermediate 100 micrometre gap the instantaneous speed is

\[2(100)(10^{-4})^3/[3\pi(0.15)(0.025)^4]=0.000362\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

The centre gauge pressure is \(2W/(\pi R^2)=102\,\mathrm{kPa}\), independent of gap here because the load is prescribed and the approach speed adapts. Halving the gap at unchanged load reduces approach speed eightfold. The ideal solution takes infinite time to reach zero thickness, which should not be interpreted as assurance against surface contact: roughness, oil leakage paths, tilt and elasticity enter before an arbitrarily small gap is reached. On separation the sign of the calculated pressure reverses and cavitation or air ingress may prevent the predicted tensile liquid pressure from developing.

A load-cell test can independently discriminate squeeze action from simple dry sliding resistance. At fixed small gap and sufficiently slow normal motion the predicted force is proportional to approach speed, with a slope scaling as \(h^{-3}\). Closing the gap from 100 to 80 micrometres should multiply the force slope by \((100/80)^3=1.95\). The transient test must either measure instantaneous gap or be run with an imposed motion, since holding load constant causes speed and gap to evolve together. Comparing forces at the same clock time on two different tests would not isolate the gap dependence.
