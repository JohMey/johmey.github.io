How a polygonal sprocket imprints speed ripple on a roller chain

A small conveyor presents glass samples to an inspection camera. The driving shaft turns at accurately regulated speed and each revolution advances exactly twelve chain pitches. A camera triggered halfway between consecutive roller engagements nevertheless sees a varying sample velocity. Predict that variation from one chord of the sprocket. Then use a displacement measurement to decide what a pitch counter or a shaft encoder could have missed.

Distinguish the pitch circle from the polygon that transports the chain

A roller chain has pin centres separated by a fixed pitch \(p\). When two consecutive rollers are seated on a sprocket with \(z\) uniformly spaced teeth, the pin centres lie on the pitch circle of radius \(R\). The straight link joining them is a chord. Bisect the isosceles triangle formed by these two centres and the shaft centre. Its half-angle is \(a=\pi/z\), so \(p=2R\sin a\). The pitch radius follows immediately from the chord length. Using \(2\pi R/z\) in place of the pitch treats the chord as an arc and introduces the very discrepancy we want to understand.

The calculation describes an ideal taut straight strand with successive rollers making a geometrically sharp handover. Neglect the mass and extension of the links, tooth clearances, roller contact details and tensioner motion. Assume a sufficiently long strand that its direction can be taken as fixed. This is a kinematic reference calculation; the vibration of an elastic chain around a finite sprocket pair would additionally require equations of motion. A useful reference should predict the repeating component even when the actual shaft has no speed ripple of its own.

Set \(\theta=0\) at the middle of one engagement, when the radius to the active roller is perpendicular to the strand. During engagement \(\theta\) runs from \(-a\) to \(+a\) at shaft speed \(\omega\). The strand advances by the projection of the active roller's tangential motion on the strand direction. Measured from the start of the engagement, the longitudinal displacement and velocity are

\[x(\theta)=R(\sin\theta+\sin a),\]
\[u(\theta)=\omega R\cos\theta.\]

At \(\theta=-a\) the displacement vanishes; at \(+a\) it is precisely \(p\). The velocity takes the same value at both ends, making the periodically repeated speed continuous. The derivative of the speed changes sign at the handover. Contact compliance and roller inertia soften this feature in a measured trace. A simulation with a rigid kinematic input and an infinitely stiff tension element can turn this geometric corner into unphysical force spikes. The predicted displacement and speed are much better observables for a first comparison.

Integrate a complete engagement before choosing a reference speed

The duration of one engagement is \(2a/\omega\). Dividing one pitch by this interval gives the exact mean strand speed \(\bar u=pz\omega/(2\pi)\). Alternatively integrate \(\omega R\cos\theta\) with respect to time through the engagement and divide by the elapsed time. The two approaches agree because \(p=2R\sin a\). A third candidate, \(\omega R\), is the maximum instantaneous speed, attained at \(\theta=0\). Replacing the mean by that maximum leads to a small cumulative travel error that grows every revolution, even though the true geometric ripple resets after every pitch.

The minimum velocity occurs just as the next roller starts or ends its engagement. It is \(\omega R\cos a\). Define the peak-to-peak excursion relative to the mean as \(\delta=(u_{\max}-u_{\min})/\bar u\). Substitution and a half-angle identity give

\[\frac{u_{\max}}{\bar u}=\frac{a}{\sin a},\]
\[\frac{u_{\min}}{\bar u}=\frac{a\cos a}{\sin a},\]
\[\delta=a\tan(a/2).\]

As the tooth count becomes large, \(a\) becomes small and \(\delta\simeq\pi^2/(2z^2)\). The corrective term is of order \(z^{-4}\). Relative speed variation therefore drops by approximately a factor of four when the tooth count doubles. Holding the shaft speed fixed at the same time would double the mean strand speed. To compare motions for the same production throughput, halve the shaft speed on the larger sprocket. The engagement frequency then stays fixed, which allows a direct comparison of the phase of the observed ripple.

Where a time trigger sees the largest position error

Let \(\phi=(\theta+a)/(2a)\) be the fraction of one engagement interval completed. The uniform-speed approximation places the sample at \(p\phi\). The true position error is therefore

\[e_x=R[\sin((2\phi-1)a)+\sin a]-p\phi.\]

It vanishes at \(\phi=0\), \(1/2\) and \(1\). It changes sign under the transformation \(\phi\mapsto 1-\phi\). A camera triggered at just these three phases might report perfect positional accuracy even though another acquisition phase sees a repeatable shift.

Differentiate with respect to time to find where \(e_x\) has its extrema. The derivative is \(u-\bar u\). The extremum occurs when \(\cos\theta=\sin a/a\), where the instantaneous speed equals its mean. The velocity peaks at mid-engagement, while the position error there is zero because the early velocity deficit has just been recovered. This distinction is useful when comparing a speed trace obtained by differentiation with exposures of a marked link: their salient features occur at different instants.

Consider a chain with 12.7 mm pitch, twelve teeth and shaft speed 120 rpm. The mean speed is \(0.0127\times12\times2=0.3048\) m/s, and rollers engage at 24 Hz. The ideal peak-to-peak speed variation is 3.45% of the mean, about 0.0105 m/s. At one quarter of an engagement the sample has moved 3.148 mm rather than the 3.175 mm predicted by uniform advance. A roughly 27 µm displacement error could matter in an optical registration task, despite perfectly correct counts over every full pitch.

This estimate also explains a sampling pitfall. A sensor sampling synchronously once every engagement sees the same phase every time. With a fixed phase offset its data look like a constant positional bias and may be removed by calibrating an offset. Sampling at several known phases within the engagement recovers the actual shape. An unsynchronised slowly drifting sampling clock can make a high-frequency tooth-pass ripple appear as a slow fluctuation. Check the sampling schedule against the engagement frequency before attributing that signal to eccentric mounting.

Translate throughput and accuracy requirements into geometry

Suppose the speed specification allows a peak-to-peak variation of 0.5% of the mean. The large-\(z\) approximation predicts \(z\simeq\pi/\sqrt{0.01}\simeq31.4\). Only an integer tooth count can be manufactured. Evaluating the exact expression gives about 0.514% for 31 teeth and 0.482% for 32. Thus 32 is the first integer satisfying this geometric requirement. Bearings, packaging, minimum wrap and tension fluctuations can place other bounds on the choice. An improvement in a kinematic reference is only part of a component selection.

The notebook integrates the computed instantaneous speed over the normalised phase and verifies that its mean agrees with the independent pitch-counting formula. This provides a diagnostic for a coding error that shifts \(\theta\) by one half engagement or uses degrees as radians. Try changing shaft speed over a wide range. The relative variation and position error remain the same in the ideal model; the dimensional speed variation and the repetition frequency scale linearly with rotational speed. A measured position error that increases strongly with speed suggests a dynamic effect in addition to this geometric mechanism.

Design a measurement that can resolve the ripple

At the default 24 engagements per second, one tooth advances the chain for 41.67 ms. Suppose an optical counter measures the positions of two marks separated by exactly one pitch and estimates the mean speed from the elapsed time. In the model those marks pass at an interval of one tooth period, regardless of the phase at the first mark. Their inferred speed is exactly 0.3048 m/s. Repeating the measurement more often improves its precision and still reports the same answer. An exposure shorter than a tooth period and a known angular reference are needed to recover the speed changes within that period.

A useful check is to place a high-contrast feature on one link, take successive exposures at a deliberate quarter-pitch offset in time and subtract their measured positions. Around the midpoint of one engagement, a window extending from \(-\delta\) to \(+\delta\) in shaft angle measures the mean speed \(R\omega\sin\delta/\delta\). At the midpoint the instantaneous speed is \(R\omega\). Their ratio is a sinc factor produced by the camera's finite observation window. For a full-pitch exposure, \(\delta=\pi/12\), that factor is about 0.9886 and the measured midpoint velocity becomes the mean speed. Shortening the exposure to half the tooth period gives a ratio of about 0.9971. The geometry of the motion stays the same while the instrument changes the apparent size of its ripple.

The experiment also estimates the mean speed from four deliberately chosen shaft angles. A local OneSimulation installation supplies the Gauss quadrature rule. Compare its estimate with the mean from the uniformly sampled encoder signal to see how integration weights alter the measurement.

Exercises in timing and geometry

1. A 24-tooth sprocket replaces the 12-tooth one at the same production throughput. Calculate its shaft speed, tooth-pass frequency and peak-to-peak speed variation.

Work through the solution

The chain must still advance 24 pitches each second, so the new shaft turns once per second, at 60 rpm. The tooth-pass frequency remains 24 Hz. The dimensionless angle is \(\pi/24\), giving \(\delta\simeq0.00858\), approximately 0.858% of the mean speed. The absolute spread is about 0.00262 m/s at the original throughput.

2. A controller estimates distance as pitch radius times shaft rotation. After one revolution, by what fraction does it overestimate the chain advance for twelve teeth?

Work through the solution

Its estimate is \(2\pi R\) and the actual distance is \(zp\). Their ratio is \(a/\sin a\). For \(a=\pi/12\) this is approximately 1.0115, so the accumulated estimate is high by about 1.15%. Calibrate the mean mapping by teeth times pitch per revolution. A residual within each engagement will still follow the position-error curve.

3. Two signals have exactly the same wavelength when expressed as a fraction of an engagement, but only one preserves its micrometre amplitude as the shaft speed changes. Which observation is consistent with the rigid-chain model?

Work through the solution

The predicted position error depends on \(p\), the tooth count and phase. It is independent of shaft speed. A micrometre amplitude that stays constant is consistent with this geometric contribution. A dynamic compliance response can also happen to be nearly constant over a narrow speed interval, so test additional speeds and measure tension or shaft motion before treating the observation as unique evidence.
