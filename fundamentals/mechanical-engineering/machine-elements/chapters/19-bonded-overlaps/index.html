Why increasing the length of a bonded overlap gives diminishing returns

Two slender straps transfer a tensile load through adhesive shear. Average stress decreases in inverse proportion to overlap length, but edge stress can approach a finite plateau. Derive the difference by coupling the axial extension of each strap to its neighbour.

Convert adhesive distortion into a distributed force

Consider identical adherends of thickness t, width b and Young's modulus E. A thin adhesive of thickness \(t_a\) and shear modulus G connects them over a length L. A tensile force P enters through strap 1 at the left and leaves through strap 2 on the right. Let \(n_1,n_2\) be axial force per unit width, with \(n_1+n_2=N=P/b\). Exclude bending and peel to isolate the symmetric extensional load-transfer mechanism. This idealization is more credible for an aligned or symmetrically balanced joint than for an eccentric single-lap joint.

Choose coordinates so the transmitted shear \(\tau=-n_1'=n_2'\) is positive. Axial strains are \(u_i'=n_i/(Et)\). Taking adhesive shear in the load-transfer direction as \(\tau=(G/t_a)(u_2-u_1)\) gives

\[\tau'=G(n_2-n_1)/(t_aEt).\]

One more derivative eliminates the member forces:

\[\tau''-\lambda^2\tau=0,\]
\[\lambda=\sqrt{{2G\over t_aEt}},\]
\[\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\tau\,dx=N .\]

The inverse transfer length \(1/\lambda\) is a mechanical length scale. A stiff adhesive transfers load over a shorter distance. A stiffer or thicker adherend distributes the required relative extension over a larger region. Reversing coordinate labels reverses force derivatives consistently without changing the predicted stress magnitude.

Apply symmetry and recover the edge-to-mean ratio

Identical adherends loaded at opposite ends give a shear profile symmetric about the middle. Set \(\tau=C\cosh(\lambda x)\) and choose C so its integral equals N. At the centre and edge this yields

\[\tau(x)={N\lambda\cosh(\lambda x)\over2\sinh(\lambda L/2)},\]
\[{\tau_{\rm edge}\over N/L}={\lambda L\over2}\coth{\lambda L\over2}.\]

For a very short overlap the ratio approaches one because \(\sinh y\sim y\). For a long overlap the edge stress approaches \(N\lambda/2\) even while \(N/L\) continues to decrease. The central portion then carries little shear. The model computes a smooth nominal adhesive stress; it does not resolve singular edge detail, peel or a local failure criterion.

Compare an average-based estimate with the load-transfer profile

Take \(P=1000\) N, \(b=20\) mm, \(E=70000\) MPa, \(t=2\) mm, \(t_a=0.20\) mm and \(G=300\) MPa. The force per width is 50 N/mm and \(\lambda=0.1464\) mm⁻¹, giving a transfer length of 6.83 mm. With \(L=20\) mm the mean stress is 2.50 MPa, but the edge sees 4.07 MPa and the centre only 1.79 MPa.

At \(L=40\) mm mean stress halves to 1.25 MPa. The edge stress falls only to 3.68 MPa and the centre drops to 0.393 MPa. The long-overlap limiting edge stress is 3.66 MPa. Thus using mean stress alone would suggest substantial unused room at the ends. Doubling the width at fixed P, in contrast, halves N and every stress in this one-dimensional model. Manufacturing constraints and adhesion quality can still change which failure mechanism occurs first.

Tutorial · tune load-transfer length using adhesive thickness

At the original E, t, G and force, a preliminary model calls for a long-overlap edge stress no greater than 3.0 MPa. Find the minimum adhesive thickness implied by that asymptote. Can a finite overlap at that exact thickness satisfy the same bound?

Invert the long-overlap limit

The limit requires \(\lambda\le 2(3.0)/50=0.120\) mm⁻¹. Thus

\[t_a\ge2G/(Et\lambda^2)=0.298\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

For a finite length, \(\coth(\lambda L/2)>1\), so the edge stress at exactly that thickness stays slightly above 3.0 MPa. One must allow additional thickness, adjust the width, or evaluate a different load path. An adhesive change also alters deformation and peel, so this result is one design condition rather than a full joint qualification.
