From first principles

Relate diametral interference to frictional holding torque

An unassembled shaft can have a larger free diameter than its mating hub bore. Define positive diametral interference \(I=d_s-d_h\). Under elastic assembly the shaft surface moves inwards and the bore outwards until their radii agree. Assume axisymmetry, uniform pressure \(p\), nominal diameter \(d\), contact length \(L\), and combined radial compliance \(c_r\) giving total radial displacement per unit pressure. Compatibility gives

\[I/2=|u_s|+u_h=c_rp,\]
\[p=\frac{I}{2c_r}.\]

Under ideal Coulomb friction, the local shear traction at impending uniform circumferential slip is \(\mu p\). Every area element of the cylindrical contact has moment arm \(d/2\). Integrating the limiting traction yields

\[T_{\rm lim}=\int_A\frac d2\mu p\,dA=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2 .\]

Uniform axial sliding instead has limiting force \(F_{\rm lim}=\mu p\pi dL\). These rely on the same available friction traction, so simultaneous axial and torsional loading must share that capacity. The factor of two in compatibility matters because manufacturing limits usually specify diameters, while surface displacements are radial.

Derivation and applications

Predict how differential thermal expansion changes an interference fit

A shaft and hub assembled with a press fit can lose contact pressure when their temperatures change. Let \(d\) be their common nominal diameter at reference temperature and \(I_0>0\) the initial diametral interference, defined as free shaft diameter minus free hub-bore diameter. Assume small strains, uniform temperature in each part, free axial expansion and elastic response while contact is closed. Let \(\alpha_s,\alpha_h\) be the linear expansion coefficients and \(\Delta T_s,\Delta T_h\) the temperature changes. To first order in strain and interference-to-diameter ratio,

\[I(T)=I_0+d(\alpha_s\Delta T_s-\alpha_h\Delta T_h).\]

If \(c_r\) is the combined radial compliance per unit pressure, the radial mismatch is \(I/2\). Positive pressure requires positive interference. When the calculated interference becomes negative the unloaded circular parts have clearance; the elastic closure formula then ceases to apply.

\[p(T)=\frac{\max[0,I(T)]}{2c_r}.\]

For uniform axial contact length \(L\) and constant friction coefficient \(\mu\), the maximum tangential traction is \(\mu p\). Integrating that traction over the cylindrical surface, with moment arm \(d/2\), translates a temperature difference into a torque capacity.

\[T_{\rm lim}=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2,\]
\[\frac{T_{\rm lim}(T)}{T_{\rm lim}(0)}=\frac{\max[0,I(T)]}{I_0}.\]

This ratio assumes the elastic moduli, friction coefficient and dimensions appearing outside the interference stay effectively constant. Thermal gradients that distort the circular geometry can invalidate the uniform-pressure assumption before mean interference reaches zero.

Worked example

Estimate capacity loss after uniform warming

A 50.0 mm steel shaft in an aluminium hub has \(I_0=40.0\) µm. Their expansion coefficients are 12.0 and 23.0 µm/(m K). A common 40.0 K temperature rise reduces interference by \(0.0500(11\times10^{-6})(40.0)=22.0\) µm. Remaining interference is 18.0 µm. A measured reference capacity of 120 N m scales to \(120(18/40)=54.0\) N m in this model.

Second application

Choose a temporary assembly temperature

A separate 80.0 mm bore with expansion coefficient \(18.0\times10^{-6}\) K⁻¹ has 50.0 µm reference interference. With the shaft kept at reference temperature, a desired assembly clearance of 20.0 µm requires bore growth of 70.0 µm. The uniform hub temperature rise is \(70.0\times10^{-6}/[0.0800(18.0\times10^{-6})]=48.6\) K. The nominal interference returns after both parts settle back to the reference temperature, provided the material response and surfaces remain elastic.

What thermal clearance survives the tolerance stack?

At room temperature a ring bore is slightly smaller than its mating shaft. An employee measures a temperature rise of 100 K at the ring and sees that the nominal pair should slide together. A second assembly made to the same drawings nevertheless has very little clearance. Resolve this apparent disagreement by carrying the diameter tolerance and the warming of the shaft through the thermal expansion calculation.

Write the initial interference as \(i=d_{\mathrm{shaft},0}-d_{\mathrm{bore},0}\), positive for a press fit. In free thermal expansion a length changes by \(\alpha d\Delta T\). Thus the available diametral clearance before engagement is the hot bore diameter less the current shaft diameter. Replacing their near-equal nominal diameters in the small thermal corrections by the common scale \(d\) gives the first-order relation

\[C=-i+d(\alpha_r\Delta T_r-\alpha_s\Delta T_s).\]

All terms are diametral. Using a desired radial clearance in this formula would understate the diametral target by a factor of two. The common-diameter approximation drops terms of order \(i\alpha\Delta T\); products at that scale can matter for very tight metrology. Temperature rises here are relative to the same inspection reference.

Take \(d=50\) mm, interference between 22 and 48 µm, a ring expansion coefficient of \(12\times10^{-6}\) per kelvin and a shaft coefficient of \(11\times10^{-6}\) per kelvin. If the ring warms by 100 K its bore gains 60.0 µm. A shaft warming by only 8 K has already gained 4.4 µm. The remaining clearance consequently spans 7.6 to 33.6 µm. The largest assembly clears easily by eye, while the worst-case pair falls short of a specified 10 µm diametral clearance even though both nominal operating temperatures were observed.

Set the smallest clearance equal to the design target and solve for the ring rise. The example gives \((10+48+4.4)/0.60=104\) K. This calculation identifies the ideal free-diameter threshold. It does not set a permissible material or process temperature; gradients, heat loss before alignment, surface finish and mounting constraints require their own measurements. The notebook uses two independent temperature inputs so that the effect of the shaft can be examined directly.

For a population of assemblies it is tempting to quote the centre of the clearance range. With the nominal midpoint interference of 35 µm, the example predicts 20.6 µm of clearance. That value says nothing about the probability of meeting the target unless there is a measured distribution of part diameters. The two tolerance limits are geometric bounds. If each shaft is measured before matching it to a ring, substitute the measured interference into the same equation and retain only the uncertainty of that measurement. An assembly process using unmatched stock has to accommodate the limiting combination of the two drawings.

If both parts have the same thermal expansion coefficient and experience the same 100 K rise, what does this first-order calculation predict?

Work through the solution

The two expansions cancel at the common nominal-diameter scale, leaving essentially the original negative clearance. Using exact distinct initial diameters gives a slightly larger interference, scaled by their common expansion factor. In the default example an error of just 10 K in the presumed shaft temperature shifts the clearance by 5.5 µm. Compare that sensitivity with the required assembly margin before reducing the model to a single ring thermometer.

Convert manufacturing limits into assembly conditions

A shaft and a hub can share a nominal diameter while their manufactured diameters differ by several tens of micrometres. Name the diameters explicitly before subtracting them. For the present joint use positive diametral interference \(I=d_s-d_h\), where \(d_s\) is the free shaft diameter and \(d_h\) the free bore. Assemble the smallest shaft with the largest hole to obtain the minimum interference. The opposite pair gives the maximum. Worst-case limits do not require a probability distribution for the production process.

Consider a 40 mm connection with shaft limits 40.030 to 40.045 mm and bore limits 40.000 to 40.015 mm. The minimum difference is 0.015 mm and the maximum 0.045 mm. Every permitted pair interferes. The radial mismatch is half the diametral interference because the radius changes on both sides of a diameter. Confusing these conventions introduces a factor of two in the inferred pressure and torque.

Relate geometric mismatch to elastic deformation

When the parts return to their common assembly temperature, contact pressure compresses the shaft and expands the bore. Assume cylindrical surfaces, a short open-ended elastic hub with negligible axial stress, a solid elastic shaft and approximately uniform pressure away from the ends. Define the radial compliance \(c_r\) as the sum of the inward shaft and outward hub displacements per unit interface pressure. Compatibility across the common radius then reads

\[\delta_r=I/2=|u_s|+u_h=p\,c_r,\qquad p=\frac{I}{2c_r}.\]

The combined compliance can be found from an elastic cylinder calculation or a verified test of geometrically identical parts. Providing it separately permits a torque calculation without assuming an infinitely stiff hub. A thinner hub generally expands further at the same pressure. Its larger compliance reduces the pressure developed by a given interference. Surface roughness that flattens during assembly can also reduce the effective interference relative to a measurement over the original asperities.

Integrate friction over the contact surface

A narrow surface patch of area \(dA\) can support a tangential friction force up to \(\mu p\,dA\). All such forces act at radius \(r=d/2\) and oppose impending rotation. Under uniform contact pressure and fully mobilised Coulomb friction, integrating around the circumference and along the overlap length \(L\) gives the limiting torque. For a purely axial pull the same friction forces act along the axis and their direct sum gives the limiting pull-out force.

\[T_{\lim}=\int r\mu p\,dA=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2,\qquad F_{\lim}=\mu p\pi dL .\]

Take \(L=35\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\mu=0.12\), and \(c_r=0.50\,\mathrm{\mu m/MPa}\) in the 40 mm joint. The radial mismatches range from 7.5 to 22.5 micrometres, so calculated pressures range from 15 to 45 MPa. At the lower limit the tangential force capacity is \(0.12(15)\pi(40)(35)=7917\,\mathrm N\), and the torque capacity is \(7917(0.020)=158\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). At the upper limit pressure and capacity are three times larger. Use the lowest pressure to test transmission and the highest to evaluate hub stress.

Provide thermal clearance and account for combined service loads

Suppose only the hub is heated, so its bore grows approximately by \(\alpha d\Delta T\). To slip the worst-case pair together with a further 0.020 mm of diametral assembly clearance, the bore must grow by \(0.045+0.020=0.065\,\mathrm{mm}\). With \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) a uniform temperature rise of \(0.065/[40(12\times10^{-6})]=135\,\mathrm K\) is required in this ideal expansion calculation. Heat loss during transfer changes the actual temperature at insertion.

A shaft may also carry a sustained axial pull while delivering torque. Under the uniform-traction model the axial and circumferential components of the surface friction force share one bound. At the lower interference with a 4.0 kN pull the remaining circumferential capacity is \(\sqrt{7917^2-4000^2}=6832\,\mathrm N\), or about 137 N m. Checking the separate pure-load capacities would miss this interaction. If repeated microslip, an uncertain friction coefficient or stiffness near the hub edges is important, measurements of the actual assembly can determine which assumptions need closer investigation.

Derive interference pressure from thick-cylinder compliance

The radial compliance used above can be related to actual dimensions and materials. Consider a long enough cylindrical fit for a uniform central contact region, with free axial expansion so a plane-stress approximation is used for each cross-section. Let the common nominal radius be \(a\), outer hub radius \(b\), and positive pressure \(p\). The solid shaft experiences uniform in-plane compression \(\sigma_r=\sigma_\theta=-p\). Consequently its surface circumferential strain is \(-p(1-\nu_s)/E_s\), giving radial shrinkage magnitude \(pa(1-\nu_s)/E_s\).

For an elastic hub with free outside, radial equilibrium \(d\sigma_r/dr+(\sigma_r-\sigma_\theta)/r=0\) and axisymmetric compatibility give Lamé fields \(\sigma_r=A-B/r^2,\ \sigma_\theta=A+B/r^2\). Setting \(\sigma_r(a)=-p,\ \sigma_r(b)=0\) gives bore hoop stress \(\sigma_\theta(a)=p(b^2+a^2)/(b^2-a^2)\). Use \(\epsilon_\theta(a)=[\sigma_\theta(a)-\nu_h\sigma_r(a)]/E_h=u_h(a)/a\). Adding hub expansion and shaft contraction leads to

\[I/2=pa\left[\frac{1-\nu_s}{E_s}+\frac{(b^2+a^2)/(b^2-a^2)+\nu_h}{E_h}\right],\]

where \(I\) is the free diametral interference. The factor of two and the sign of radial pressure both affect the result. Significant axial restraint, plastic deformation, a hollow shaft or edge-pressure peaks require a different compliance model.

Compare thick and thin hubs at the same interference

A steel shaft and hub both have \(E=210\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(\nu=0.30\). Their contact radius is 20 mm, hub outer radius 40 mm and measured diametral interference 30 micrometres. In mm–MPa units the combined radial compliance is \(20[0.70+(40^2+20^2)/(40^2-20^2)+0.30]/210000=0.000254\,\mathrm{mm/MPa}\). Pressure is \(0.015/0.000254=59.1\,\mathrm{MPa}\). The tensile hoop stress at the hub bore is \(59.1(5/3)=98.5\,\mathrm{MPa}\). For engagement length 25 mm and \(\mu=0.12\), torque capacity is \(2\pi\mu p a^2L=446\,\mathrm{N\,m}\) approximately.

Now reduce the outer radius to 26 mm with the same parts at the fit surface. The hub geometry factor becomes \((26^2+20^2)/(26^2-20^2)=3.90\), giving combined radial compliance \(20(1+3.90)/210000=0.000467\,\mathrm{mm/MPa}\). Pressure falls to 32.2 MPa and torque capacity falls to about 243 N m. Yet bore hoop stress rises to \(32.2(3.90)=126\,\mathrm{MPa}\) because the thinner hub amplifies hoop demand more strongly. Reducing contact pressure alone is not evidence that the hub becomes safer. The same dimensional change can reduce torque capacity and increase peak elastic stress, so both checks should use the geometry of the actual mating parts.

For ductile elastic parts, the hub bore should also be assessed in its multiaxial state. In the thicker example its principal stresses are approximately \(+98.5,0,-59.1\) MPa under plane stress. The von Mises value is \(\sqrt{98.5^2+59.1^2+98.5(59.1)}=138\,\mathrm{MPa}\), above the tensile hoop stress by itself. For the thinner hub, using \(+126,0,-32.2\) MPa gives approximately 145 MPa. A comparison with an allowable uniaxial stress must therefore state the criterion applied. Once significant yielding occurs the linear compliance used to determine pressure is itself affected, and enlarging the nominal interference will no longer guarantee a proportional increase in transmitted torque. A pull-out or torque-to-slip test on representative surfaces checks the independent assumption about friction.

For an interference test, measuring the outside hub diameter change offers an additional observable. At the outer surface radial stress vanishes, so its hoop strain depends directly on the local tensile hoop stress.

In fact the hub’s outside hoop stress from the same Lamé field is \(2pa^2/(b^2-a^2)\). For the 40 mm outer radius it is \(2(59.1)(400)/(1600-400)=39.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\), so the predicted 80 mm outside diameter increases by \(80(39.4)/210000=0.0150\,\mathrm{mm}\). For the thinner hub it is \(2(32.2)(400)/(676-400)=93.3\,\mathrm{MPa}\), giving an outside diameter increase \(52(93.3)/210000=0.0231\,\mathrm{mm}\). The thinner outer wall moves more under a smaller interface pressure. Observing this change together with transmitted torque can separate an incorrect elastic pressure estimate from an incorrect friction coefficient.

For example, if the thick hub expands by the predicted 15 micrometres but slips at 330 N m under otherwise matching conditions, the implied mean friction coefficient is \(0.12(330/446)=0.089\). If the measured expansion were instead much smaller, an undersized effective interference, partial contact or a nonuniform fit would deserve investigation before calibrating friction. The micrometer reading is itself temperature sensitive: a uniform temperature difference of 10 K changes an 80 mm steel diameter by about 9.6 micrometres at \(12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). Stabilise the temperatures before comparing the calculated elastic expansion with metrology.

Size the working flanks and shear section of a parallel key

A shaft can drive a removable hub through the flanks of a parallel key. The load path runs from the shaft keyway into one side of the key, across its shear section, and out of the opposite side into the hub. Let \(d\) be the shaft diameter. Represent the distributed tangential contact by the resultant \(F=2T/d\) at the nominal shaft radius. This model assumes a single fitted key carries the specified torque without credit for friction from a separate clamp.

There are two different projected areas. If \(b\) is the key width and \(L_e\) the effective engaged length, the shear plane has area \(bL_e\). If \(h_e\) is the actual load-bearing radial height on the hub side, the projected bearing area on that flank is \(h_eL_e\). The full key height includes material inside the shaft. Chamfers, end relief and clearance can further reduce the effective dimensions. Under a uniform-load sizing approximation,

\[\tau_\mathrm{key}=\frac{2T}{dbL_e},\qquad p_\mathrm{hub}=\frac{2T}{dh_eL_e},\qquad L_{e,\min}=\max\left(\frac{2T}{db\tau_\mathrm{allow}},\,\frac{2T}{dh_ep_\mathrm{allow}}\right).\]

For a conveyor pulley, \(T=180\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), \(d=40.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(b=12.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(h_e=3.30\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(L_e=36.0\,\mathrm{mm}\). Work in N and mm, giving \(F=2(180000)/40=9000\,\mathrm N\). Mean shear is \(9000/(12\times36)=20.8\,\mathrm{MPa}\), whereas hub bearing pressure is \(9000/(3.30\times36)=75.8\,\mathrm{MPa}\). For design allowables of 45 and 90 MPa respectively, required engaged lengths are 16.7 and 30.3 mm. The proposed engagement passes these two static checks, with hub bearing pressure setting the longer minimum. A softer hub can therefore govern even when a much stronger key material is chosen. This calculation does not establish the shaft's fatigue strength at its keyway root. That requires the local shaft stress, particularly for rotating bending.
