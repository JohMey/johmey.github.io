    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Translate service loads into component stresses, then follow them through shafts, bolted joints and rolling contacts. The stiffness of the parts decides how a load is shared and where a local pressure develops.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Gear geometry transfers torque, fits transmit pressure, springs store energy, and belts use friction to transmit tension. Distinguish prescribed displacement from prescribed force when reading a component rating.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Brakes dissipate energy, fluid films carry loads through motion, and weld groups distribute loads in a joint. Finish by propagating variable dimensions and strengths into a probability of meeting the design requirement.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. Locating a part with six contacts and predicting datum error

      A dimensionally accurate part can still be assembled in the wrong place. Use small rigid-body motions to work out what six contacts actually locate, then predict how dirt or a tilted guide appears in a measurement.

    2. Sizing the leaves of a compliant translation guide

      A pair of thin leaves can guide a carriage with very little sliding friction. Their stiffness, stress and parasitic motion stem from the same bent shape. Derive those links before choosing a thickness or stroke.

    3. Deriving screw-drive torque, efficiency and backdrivability

      A screw trades angular travel for axial force. Unwrap one turn into an inclined plane and determine why two nominally similar threads can behave differently when the load tries to drive backward.

    4. Matching a gear ratio to load inertia and move time

      A larger reduction increases output torque and reduces speed. During acceleration the motor must also spin its own rotor faster. Refer the dynamics to a single shaft and find which reduction gives the shortest idealized move.

    5. Force transmission and compliance near a toggle position

      Two links approach alignment as a mechanical fixture closes. A small applied force can correspond to a large output reaction, but the ratio also makes the result sensitive to clearance and compliance. Derive it from displacement compatibility.

    6. How an involute gear pair keeps contact from one tooth to the next

      The tooth-count ratio gives speed reduction but says little about how many pairs carry load simultaneously. Derive the transverse contact ratio from the base circles and addendum circles, then check how centre-distance errors affect overlap.

    7. Why increasing the length of a bonded overlap gives diminishing returns

      Two slender straps transfer a tensile load through adhesive shear. Average stress decreases in inverse proportion to overlap length, but edge stress can approach a finite plateau. Derive the difference by coupling the axial extension of each strap to its neighbour.

    8. How a polygonal sprocket imprints speed ripple on a roller chain

      A small conveyor presents glass samples to an inspection camera. The driving shaft turns at accurately regulated speed and each revolution advances exactly twelve chain pitches. A camera triggered halfway between consecutive roller engagements nevertheless sees a varying sample velocity. Predict that variation from one chord of the sprocket. Then use a displacement measurement to decide what a pitch counter or a shaft encoder could have missed.

    Applied study

    Determine how load and curvature set the contact area, pressure and stresses between components.

    How much area really carries the load?

    A ball presses against a bearing race. The unloaded bodies touch at a point, which would imply infinite pressure for any finite force. Both bodies deform elastically under load, opening a contact patch whose size is part of the solution. A pin in a clevis carries a transverse load through a different combination of contact and shear. The relevant area depends on which failure mechanism is being examined.

    Start with a sphere of radius \(R\) on a flat body. Assume a smooth nonadhesive interface, negligible tangential traction, isotropic linear elasticity and a small contact patch relative to curvature and body dimensions. Hertz’s local solution then treats each solid as an elastic half-space. Their normal compliances add through the effective modulus \(1/E^*=(1-\nu_1^2)/E_1+(1-\nu_2^2)/E_2\). A grooved race with appreciable curvature can require different principal radii and an elliptical patch.

    From contact pressure to the normal force

    In a circular contact of radius \(a\), the elastic solution gives

    \[p(r)=p_0\sqrt{1-r^2/a^2},\]

    with the greatest pressure at the centre. Equilibrium of a loaded body connects this local field to a measurable force. Integrating over rings of area \(2\pi r\,dr\) gives \(F=2\pi p_0a^2/3\), so the peak pressure is 1.5 times the patch mean.

    Matching the curved gap to the elastic deflections gives

    \[a^2=R\delta,\]

    with \(\delta\) the mutual approach of the remote bodies, and \(F=(4/3)E^*\sqrt{R}\,\delta^{3/2}\). Eliminating the approach yields

    \[a=\left(\frac{3FR}{4E^*}\right)^{1/3},\]
    \[p_0=\frac{3F}{2\pi a^2},\]
    \[k_n=\frac{dF}{d\delta}=2E^*a.\]

    The tangent stiffness \(k_n\) increases as load enlarges the patch. A single constant contact stiffness can describe small oscillations around a chosen preload. Following the surface during rolling subjects material below the track to a changing stress state. For Poisson ratio 0.30 the elastic solution puts the largest shear stress on the central axis near \(z=0.48a\), with magnitude about \(0.31p_0\).

    Worked example · Steel ball on a steel seat

    Let \(R=10.0\) mm, \(F=120\) N and \(E=210\) GPa, \(\nu=0.30\) for both bodies. Their combined modulus is

    \[210/[2(1-0.3^2)]=115.4\,\mathrm{GPa}.\]

    The patch radius is 0.198 mm and the approach is

    \[a^2/R=3.93\,\mu\mathrm{m}.\]

    The pressure averaged over the patch is 971 MPa, and \(p_0=1.46\) GPa. The maximum shear is about 452 MPa near a depth of 0.095 mm.

    The 120 N force alone is a poor guide to stress at the contact. These are elastic predictions. Judging service life also requires material condition, lubrication, repeated loading and the rest of the bearing geometry. Compare how force and geometry determine nominal stress in a beam.

    Transfer problem · A pin with two shear planes

    A 12 kN load passes through a central 6 mm thick lug between two 4 mm thick clevis plates. Initially the pin diameter is 8 mm. Assume symmetric load sharing and negligible pin bending for this preliminary check. A cut through both shear planes gives mean pin stress \(\tau=F/[2(\pi d^2/4)]\). Nominal bearing pressure uses the projected pin area \(td\) in each plate, with the central lug carrying \(F\) and the outer plates \(F/2\) each.

    For the 8 mm pin the mean shear is 119 MPa. Nominal bearing pressure is \(12000/(6\times8)=250\) MPa in the central lug, with dimensions here in millimetres, and \(6000/(4\times8)=188\) MPa in each outer plate. Suppose the exercise specifies limits of 125 MPa in mean shear and 200 MPa in nominal bearing. The central lug controls the diameter. Increasing \(d\) to 10 mm gives 76.4 MPa shear, 200 MPa in the central lug and 150 MPa outside. This meets the two stated nominal checks at the limiting diameter. Edge tear-out, net-section failure and pin bending would require additional dimensions and material data.

    Exercises

    1. Increase the contact preload

    Increase the sphere load eightfold, maintaining geometry and elastic properties. Predict the factors of change in contact radius, peak pressure, approach and incremental stiffness. Calculate the new approach.

    Show the load scalings

    From \(a\propto F^{1/3}\), the radius doubles. The patch area quadruples, so \(p_0\propto F/a^2\) also doubles. The approach rises fourfold to about 15.7 µm, and the tangent stiffness doubles. A load path based on a geometrically fixed patch would overestimate the pressure increase.

    2. Widen the contact or change the material?

    At the original 120 N, compare doubling the sphere radius and halving the reduced modulus. Which choice reduces the peak pressure more, and which produces less movement?

    Show the design comparison

    Both changes double \(R/E^*\), increasing \(a\) by \(2^{1/3}=1.260\) and reducing \(p_0\) to \(2^{-2/3}=0.630\) times its former value. Since \(\delta=a^2/R\), doubling \(R\) gives \(2^{-1/3}\delta=3.12\) µm, whereas halving \(E^*\) gives

    \[2^{2/3}\delta=6.24\,\mu\mathrm m.\]

    The larger sphere gives the same pressure reduction with a stiffer response at fixed load.
