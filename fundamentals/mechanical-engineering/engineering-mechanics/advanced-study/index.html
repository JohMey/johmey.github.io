Shaft reactions, twist and rotating bending

Machine elements and contact mechanics

Trace power and belt forces into shaft reactions, then check the shaft section for yielding, twist and cyclic bending.

From first principles

Derive bending and torsional stress at a round shaft surface

A rotating shaft transmits torque through circumferential shear and can carry transverse loads through bending. Consider a slender, solid, circular shaft of diameter \(d\) under small elastic deformation. In pure bending, plane sections stay plane so longitudinal strain varies linearly with distance \(y\) from the neutral axis. In circular torsion, shear strain varies linearly with radius \(r\). Choosing stress gradients to reproduce the applied moment and torque gives

\[M=\int_A y\sigma\,dA,\]
\[\sigma=My/I,\]
\[T=\int_A r\tau\,dA,\]
\[\tau=Tr/J .\]

Here \(I=\int_A y^2dA=\pi d^4/64\) and \(J=\int_A r^2dA=\pi d^4/32\). Taking the surface distance as \(d/2\) gives the nominal maximum components.

\[|\sigma_b|_{\max}=\frac{32|M|}{\pi d^3},\]
\[|\tau_t|_{\max}=\frac{16|T|}{\pi d^3}.\]

Torque can be inferred from mechanical power using \(T=P/\omega\), while bending moment requires transverse force and support equilibrium. A stationary transverse load produces alternating bending at a material point as the shaft rotates. The formulas assume a smooth section; shoulders and keyways alter local stress.

Background · Torsional stiffness
\[k_\theta=GJ/L\]

For a uniform elastic circular shaft use its polar second moment. A noncircular section generally needs a distinct torsion constant and warping analysis.

Derivation and applications

Distribute a fixed-end shaft torque by twist compatibility

A shaft supported against rotation at both ends has an extra reaction torque that cannot be found from overall moment equilibrium alone. Consider two coaxial circular segments meeting at a collar. Apply a static torque \(T\) at the collar. The left and right outer ends are held fixed. Assume linear elastic shear, small twist, fully bonded interfaces and torque transfer without slip. Segment \(j\) has length \(L_j\), shear modulus \(G_j\) and polar second moment \(J_j\). Common collar angle \(\theta\) twists each segment through the same magnitude.

\[\theta=\frac{T_L L_L}{G_LJ_L}=\frac{T_RL_R}{G_RJ_R},\]
\[T_L+T_R=T .\]

The symbols \(T_L,T_R\) are positive magnitudes of the moments resisting the applied torque. Define torsional stiffness \(k_j=G_jJ_j/L_j\) and substitute into equilibrium. The two segments behave as parallel torsional springs when observed from the collar.

\[\theta=\frac{T}{k_L+k_R},\]
\[T_L=\frac{k_L}{k_L+k_R}T,\]
\[T_R=\frac{k_R}{k_L+k_R}T .\]

For a solid round shaft \(J_j=\pi d_j^4/32\). Local surface shear is proportional to torque times outer radius divided by \(J_j\). The increase of torque carried by a stiffer segment therefore does not translate directly into the same factor of stress.

\[\tau_{j,\max}=\frac{T_jd_j}{2J_j}=\frac{16T_j}{\pi d_j^3}.\]

If one support becomes torsionally soft, its torque approaches zero and the stiffer side carries nearly all of \(T\). The fixed-end assumption is especially important in using rotation measurements to identify a support reaction.

Worked example

Split torque across two lengths of shaft

Two segments have the same 20.0 mm diameter and shear modulus 80.0 GPa, with lengths 0.200 and 0.400 m. The shorter segment is twice as stiff. A 90.0 N m collar torque splits into 60.0 and 30.0 N m. The shorter segment reaches \(16(60)/[\pi(0.0200)^3]=38.2\) MPa surface shear. Its twist is \(60(0.200)/(80.0\times10^9 J)=0.00955\) rad, shared by the collar.

Rotation determines the torque, contacts determine the bending

A shaft transmits a given power through torque at a specified speed. Mechanical work in a small rotation is \(dW=T\,d\theta\), so power is \(T\omega\), with angular speed in radians per second. Belt tensions act at opposite sides of a pulley. Their difference gives the net driving torque. For two nearly parallel belt spans pulling in the same transverse direction their sum loads the shaft sideways. A belt with greater pretension can increase bearing loads although the transmitted power remains constant.

\[\omega=2\pi n/60,\]
\[T=P/\omega=(F_1-F_2)r,\]
\[F_\perp\simeq F_1+F_2 .\]

Choose bearing centres as ideal simple supports and identify where each torque enters or leaves the shaft. A section between an input coupling and an output pulley carries the transmitted torque. Beyond the output pulley it may carry transverse shear while carrying virtually zero torque. Mark the bending and torque diagrams separately so the combination at a critical cross-section represents loads that actually coexist there.

Determine the stress components from section geometry

For a solid circular shaft the bending second moment is \(I=\pi d^4/64\), and its polar moment is \(J=2I\). The elastic bending stress at the outer fibre is \(M(d/2)/I\); the torsional shear stress is \(T(d/2)/J\). Under proportional elastic loading, the plane-stress von Mises expression then gives a static yield check for a ductile material.

\[\sigma_b=32M/(\pi d^3),\]
\[\tau_t=16T/(\pi d^3),\]
\[\sigma_v=\sqrt{\sigma_b^2+3\tau_t^2}.\]

The cubic diameter dependence makes a shoulder important even before its local stress concentration is considered. These equations describe a smooth prismatic section and nominal stresses. At a fillet, the maximum stresses may be estimated from geometric concentration factors appropriate to bending and torsion. For cyclic design a fatigue concentration factor may differ from the elastic geometric factor because sensitivity to the notch depends on material and radius.

Size the loading at the pulley seat

A 120 mm radius pulley sits at the middle of a 0.40 m span and draws 6.0 kW at 600 rpm. The two belt forces are parallel and their sum is 1800 N. The driving coupling lies immediately outside the left bearing. The shaft is 32 mm in diameter. From \(\omega=62.83\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), the transmitted torque is 95.5 N m and the tension difference is

\[95.5/0.120=795.8\,\mathrm N.\]

Combining difference and sum gives 1298 and 502 N.

Each bearing carries 900 N. Taking moments about a cut just to the left of the pulley gives

\[M=900(0.20)=180\,\mathrm{N\,m}.\]

There the shaft still transmits full torque. Substitution gives 56.0 MPa bending and 14.8 MPa torsional shear. Thus the nominal von Mises stress is 61.6 MPa, giving a nominal yield safety factor of 4.06 for steel with 250 MPa yield strength. This calculation has not yet accounted for a keyway or the pulley seat fillet.

Follow one material point through a revolution

A fixed vertical belt pull produces a steady bending moment in the laboratory. Mark a point on the rotating shaft surface. It passes through the tensile and compressive bending fibres once each per turn, so that its bending stress is fully reversed with amplitude 56.0 MPa. Steady driving torque supplies mean shear. A fatigue test at zero mean axial load describes only one part of this combined loading history. A shaft that spends a long time at constant speed can accumulate many stress cycles while the torque meter shows a level trace.

The twist angle is a further service requirement. Integrate \(d\theta/dx=T/(GJ)\) along the torque-carrying length. With \(G=80\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(J=1.029\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^4}\), and a 0.20 m torque-carrying span, the relative twist is 0.00232 rad, or 0.133°. Bending stress varies as the inverse cube of diameter and twist at fixed torque as the inverse fourth power. A diameter selected from a strength limit can still give an unacceptable positioning error at the driven component.

Use the static deflection to estimate a shaft critical speed

The shaft diameter required to transmit torque need not satisfy the dynamic constraints of a rotating machine. For a first screening, model a relatively light simply supported shaft carrying a heavy disc at midspan. Treat the disc as a concentrated translating mass and neglect shaft mass, gyroscopic coupling, bearing flexibility and damping at first. Under a static transverse force \(F\), bending theory gives midpoint deflection \(\delta=FL^3/(48EI)\), hence lateral stiffness \(k_s=48EI/L^3\). With gravity loading only from the disc, \(\delta_g=mg/k_s\). The ideal bending natural frequency then follows without explicitly measuring disc mass:

\[\omega_n=\sqrt{k_s/m}=\sqrt{g/\delta_g}.\]

A small rotating mass eccentricity \(e\) produces lateral forcing \(me\Omega^2\) synchronous with speed. The undamped critical speed occurs when \(\Omega\) equals \(\omega_n\). Gravity sag itself is a static offset in vertical operation of this horizontal shaft; its magnitude is useful because it reveals the ratio \(m/k_s\). For a shaft with distributed mass or several mounted elements, one measured sag at a disc need not represent the relevant modal inertia. A compliant test stand also contributes to the measured deflection and can lower the assembled critical speed.

Adding simple viscous damping gives synchronous orbit-amplitude ratio \(X/e=r^2/\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}\), \(r=\Omega/\omega_n\), for the ideal isotropic single-disc model. It approaches unity far above critical speed but can be much larger near it.

Check a shaft after increasing disc mass

A machine shaft carrying a balanced test disc deflects 0.40 mm at its centre when the disc’s weight is applied. The fixture was tared for its own sag. The estimated natural frequency is \(\sqrt{9.81/0.00040}=156.6\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), corresponding to 1496 rpm. A production target of 1200 rpm gives \(r=0.802\). With small residual eccentricity \(e=0.050\,\mathrm{mm}\) and damping ratio 0.04, the predicted orbit amplitude is

\[0.050(0.802)^2/\sqrt{(1-0.802^2)^2+(0.08\times0.802)^2}=0.089\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

A new disc doubles the dominant mass at the same shaft stiffness. Sag doubles to 0.80 mm and the estimated critical speed falls to 1057 rpm. The machine must now pass through that speed during startup and operates above it, despite the torque capability of the shaft being essentially unchanged. To restore its original critical speed under the doubled mass while retaining the span and material, stiffness must double. For a smooth solid circular shaft \(I\propto d^4\), so

\[d_{\rm new}/d_{\rm old}=2^{1/4}=1.189.\]

A 30 mm shaft would need about 35.7 mm under this model. Restoring the natural frequency alone does not fix a bearing or coupling alignment issue, and a run-up through resonance requires transient analysis if the dwell time is significant relative to the decay time.

The static stiffness estimate assumes the midpoint force and the displacement gauge describe the same work-conjugate location and direction. An overhung disc or a gauge near a support can give a very different apparent stiffness. Recording the shaft layout alongside the sag value keeps the screening calculation reproducible when the assembly changes.

Why a single universal joint produces a twice-per-revolution speed fluctuation

A Hooke joint lets two intersecting shafts transmit rotation at a fixed misalignment angle \(\beta\). Its cross carries two perpendicular trunnions. Choose the input axis as \(z\), tilt the output axis about \(x\), and measure input rotation \(\theta\) and output rotation \(\phi\) from aligned angular references. A unit vector along the first trunnion is \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)\). The second rotates in the tilted output plane and can be written \((-\sin\phi,\cos\beta\cos\phi,\sin\beta\cos\phi)\). Their dot product vanishes, giving \(\tan\phi=\cos\beta\tan\theta\). Follow a continuous angular branch over a full turn.

Differentiate this constraint before averaging. Using the tangent relation to remove \(\phi\) from the derivative gives

\[\frac{\omega_\mathrm{out}}{\omega_\mathrm{in}}=\frac{d\phi}{d\theta}=\frac{\cos\beta}{1-\sin^2\beta\sin^2\theta}.\]

The extremes are \(\cos\beta\) and \(1/\cos\beta\). Both shafts complete one revolution in the same time, yet the instantaneous ratio changes twice per revolution. For small \(\beta\) in radians it is \(1-(\beta^2/2)\cos2\theta+O(\beta^4)\). This identifies a second-order dependence on small misalignment and a forcing frequency twice the shaft frequency.

A test rig drives a single joint at a constant 600 rpm with \(\beta=20^\circ\). The driven shaft ranges from \(600\cos20^\circ=563.8\,\mathrm{rpm}\) to \(600/\cos20^\circ=638.5\,\mathrm{rpm}\). Its speed ripple repeats at \(2(600/60)=20.0\,\mathrm{Hz}\). A large downstream inertia will therefore demand an alternating accelerating torque even if the external load is steady. A double-joint arrangement can cancel the kinematic ripple at the final shaft: with parallel outer shafts, equal joint angles and correctly phased forks, the second joint implements the inverse angular mapping of the first. The intermediate shaft still fluctuates, so bearing and torsional loads within the assembly merit separate treatment.

Preload, joint stiffness and separation

Machine elements and contact mechanics

Compute how a tensile service load changes bolt force and clamp force, and determine when a closed-joint calculation ceases to apply.

From first principles

Derive bolt and clamp increments from compatible deformation

A tightened bolt stretches while the clamped members compress. Before external loading their force magnitudes equal the preload \(F_i\). Apply a separating load \(P\) and assume both components respond elastically and the interfaces stay closed. A small opening displacement \(\delta\) stretches the bolt additionally and relieves the members by the same displacement. With stiffnesses \(k_b,k_m\) the increments are

\[\Delta F_b=k_b\delta,\]
\[-\Delta F_m=k_m\delta,\]
\[P=\Delta F_b-\Delta F_m=(k_b+k_m)\delta .\]

Here member force \(F_m\) is positive in compression. Eliminate the displacement to find both service forces and the fraction \(C\) of external load reaching the bolt.

\[C=\frac{k_b}{k_b+k_m},\]
\[F_b=F_i+CP,\]
\[F_m=F_i-(1-C)P .\]

Setting \(F_m=0\) predicts incipient separation at \(P=F_i/(1-C)\). Once the interface can no longer supply compressive reaction, the closed-joint stiffness partition ceases to describe the load path. The derivation explains why service bolt tension grows by only a fraction of applied load before separation.

Background · Joint stiffness fraction
\[C=k_b/(k_b+k_m)\]

Before separation, bolt and clamped-member stiffnesses decide what fraction of a separating external force increases the bolt load.

Derivation and applications

Predict preload loss caused by settlement of a bolted joint

A tightened joint may lose clamp load before it sees any external service force. Model the bolt by axial stiffness \(k_b\) and the compressed members by stiffness \(k_m\). Assume elastic incremental behaviour, closed contact, fixed temperature and a nut that does not rotate after tightening. Initial preload \(F_i\) stretches the bolt and compresses the members. A small permanent settlement \(s\) at asperities or interfaces consumes part of the initial imposed interference.

\[\Delta_{\rm installed}=F_i\left(\frac1{k_b}+\frac1{k_m}\right),\]
\[\Delta_{\rm remaining}=\Delta_{\rm installed}-s .\]

The same preload passes through the bolt and compressed stack before an external tensile load is applied. Recovering their combined elastic displacement therefore reduces the preload according to their series stiffness. The result applies as long as positive clamp load remains.

\[F_r=F_i-k_{\rm eq}s,\]
\[k_{\rm eq}=\left(\frac1{k_b}+\frac1{k_m}\right)^{-1}.\]

Now apply an external separating force \(P\). In a closed joint the bolt takes fraction \(C=k_b/(k_b+k_m)\) of that force and the clamp decreases by \((1-C)P\). Setting the residual clamp to zero gives the revised separation threshold.

\[F_{\rm clamp}=F_r-(1-C)P,\]
\[P_{\rm sep}=\frac{F_i-k_{\rm eq}s}{1-C}.\]

Settlement is a displacement, so a more compliant bolt can reduce the preload lost per micrometre. Other design constraints, including stiffness, load introduction and fatigue, remain relevant when altering bolt geometry.

Worked example

Audit a joint after a measured bedding-in displacement

Let \(k_b=200\) kN/mm, \(k_m=800\) kN/mm and \(F_i=24.0\) kN. The series stiffness is 160 kN/mm. A settlement of 15.0 µm reduces preload by \(160(0.0150)=2.40\) kN, leaving 21.6 kN. Since \(C=0.200\), the new separation load is 27.0 kN. Before settlement it would have been 30.0 kN.

Establish the state before the external load arrives

A tensioned bolt in an assembled flange has already elongated. The material clamped between head and nut has already shortened. In the ideal concentric joint these initial forces have equal magnitude \(F_i\), as a free-body diagram of either flange shows. Tightening stores elastic energy in both parts. Calling a joint unloaded usually means that the external service load is zero. The thread root can still carry a significant tensile stress.

Represent the effective bolt grip by a tensile spring \(k_b\) and the compressed members by a spring \(k_m\). For a uniform segment the axial stiffness is \(EA/L\); threaded and plain segments in series have added compliances. The member stiffness depends on the path through which compressive force spreads from the head and washer. A thin flexible flange may also bend. For this derivation consider one bolt, an axial load introduced at the outside faces and no eccentricity or prying. Let clamp force denote a positive magnitude in compression.

Background · How does a small loss of grip thickness reduce preload?

Surface roughness can flatten under load after tightening. Suppose this reduces the unloaded thickness of the clamped stack by \(s\) while the nut stays on the same thread position. The recovered bolt elongation and the elastic recovery of the clamped members must together accommodate that lost thickness.

\[s=\Delta F\left(\frac1{k_b}+\frac1{k_m}\right),\]
\[\Delta F=\frac{k_bk_m}{k_b+k_m}\,s.\]

With bolt stiffness 200 kN/mm, member stiffness 800 kN/mm and settlement 0.010 mm, preload falls by 1.6 kN. This estimate assumes the stack is still in compression and both springs retain their tangent stiffness. A longer, more compliant bolt loses less preload for the same settlement because a given length change produces a smaller force change.

Apply compatibility to the load increment

An external separating force \(P\) allows the bolt to extend a little while the clamped members recover some of their initial compression. As long as the faces remain in contact, the extension and recovery have the same magnitude \(x\). The bolt-force increase is \(k_bx\) and the clamp-force decrease is \(k_mx\). Force equilibrium across the joint gives

\[P=F_b-F_m=(k_b+k_m)x.\]

Eliminate \(x\) and introduce the load fraction \(C\).

\[C=\frac{k_b}{k_b+k_m},\]
\[F_b=F_i+CP,\]
\[F_m=F_i-(1-C)P.\]

The slopes of bolt force and clamp-force magnitude have opposite signs. The joint opens in this ideal model when \(F_m\) reaches zero, at \(P_{\rm sep}=F_i/(1-C)\). Continuing the same line beyond that point would predict tension between unbonded faces. Once open, the bolt must carry the full external tensile force and the load slope changes to unity. Both expressions give the same bolt force at the instant of separation.

Check a service cycle and a separate overload

Take \(k_b=180\,\mathrm{kN/mm}\), \(k_m=720\,\mathrm{kN/mm}\), \(F_i=25\,\mathrm{kN}\) and bolt tensile stress area \(A_t=58.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}\). A pressure pulsation creates a separating force cycling between 4 and 16 kN. Here \(C=0.20\), hence bolt force ranges from 25.8 to 28.2 kN. Clamp force falls from 21.8 to 12.2 kN and remains positive. The bolt’s alternating tensile stress is the force range divided by twice its tensile stress area.

\[\sigma_a=(28.2-25.8)10^3/(2\times58.0)=20.7\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

At the maximum ordinary load the calculated tensile stress is 486 MPa. If the specified proof stress is 600 MPa, the axial proof-load benchmark is \(600(58.0)=34.8\,\mathrm{kN}\). The estimated separation threshold is

\[25/0.8=31.25\,\mathrm{kN}.\]

A singular tensile overload of 34 kN opens the interface and brings the bolt to 34 kN, just below the supplied axial proof benchmark. It can nevertheless break the seal at the flange face. Different requirements can control the same event.

Use attainable preload, including its scatter

Increasing preload by 3 kN raises both initial forces and delays separation by \(3/(1-C)=3.75\,\mathrm{kN}\). It also raises the peak bolt stress by \(3000/58=51.7\,\mathrm{MPa}\) throughout the closed regime. Reducing bolt stiffness relative to the members can lower the alternating force. For example a longer effective grip can increase bolt compliance. Recheck the residual clamp and separation conditions whenever changing stiffness.

If tightening produces a range of preloads, assess separation with the low end and proof stress with the high end. An assembly tolerance of ±3 kN around the nominal value gives a minimum separation threshold of \(22/0.8=27.5\,\mathrm{kN}\). The largest ordinary bolt force would be \(28+0.2(16)=31.2\,\mathrm{kN}\). The preload scatter enters these checks even though the external service force is perfectly known. In a real flange, surface settling, differential thermal expansion and off-axis loading can change the available clamp force after assembly.

Resolve tightening torque into thread lift and friction

Joint preload depends on the tightening method as well as bolt and flange stiffness. For an elementary torque estimate, unwrap one turn of the thread at pitch diameter \(d_2\). Its lead \(l\) over the circumferential distance \(\pi d_2\) defines helix angle \(\lambda=\arctan[l/(\pi d_2)]\). For a square thread lifting axial load \(F_i\), resolving an applied tangential force and Coulomb friction on the incline gives

\[F_{\rm tan}=F_i\tan(\lambda+\phi),\]

where \(\tan\phi=\mu_t\). For a symmetric V-thread of half-angle \(\alpha\), a common small-helix approximation increases the effective friction to \(\mu_t/\cos\alpha\) because the flanks carry a larger normal force.

Under the head or nut, sliding friction at effective radius \(r_b\) adds torque \(\mu_b F_i r_b\). A simple total tightening estimate is

\[T\simeq F_i[(d_2/2)\tan(\lambda+\arctan(\mu_t/\cos\alpha))+\mu_b r_b].\]

The first term includes ideal screw lift and thread friction; the second is bearing-face friction. In the limit of zero friction the thread term reduces to \(F_i l/(2\pi)\), as verified by equating work over one revolution to axial load times lead. This lower bound also shows why most measured tightening torque can disappear into friction rather than useful bolt elongation. Effective bearing radius depends on contact geometry and pressure distribution, and the coefficients may differ between production lots.

Determine preload sensitivity to lubrication at fixed torque

Use an illustrative single-start thread with \(d_2=9.0\) mm, \(l=1.50\) mm, flank half-angle 30° and effective bearing radius 7.0 mm. The helix angle is

\[\arctan[1.5/(\pi9)]=3.04^\circ.\]

With \(\mu_t=\mu_b=0.15\), the effective thread friction angle is 9.83°. The thread torque arm is \(4.5\tan(12.87^\circ)=1.03\,\mathrm{mm}\), and bearing friction contributes another 1.05 mm. A tightening torque of 40 N m therefore suggests \(F_i=40000/(1.03+1.05)=19.2\,\mathrm{kN}\). The frictionless lift portion would be only \(19.2(1.50)/(2\pi)=4.59\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).

If lubrication reduces both coefficients to 0.08 at the same applied torque, the effective thread friction angle becomes 5.28°. The torque arms are now \(4.5\tan(8.32^\circ)=0.658\) mm and 0.560 mm. Implied preload rises to

\[40000/1.218=32.8\,\mathrm{kN}.\]

A tensile area of 58 mm² would then carry about 566 MPa before the service force arrived, compared with 331 MPa originally. An old torque setting may therefore raise preload substantially after a change of coating or lubricant. A direct elongation measurement can help distinguish this friction change from loss of clamp due to settling, although the effective bolt grip and modulus must also be known.

The axial stretch provides an independent expected signal. Suppose the effective uniform tensile length is 50 mm with \(E=210\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and area 58 mm². The two preload estimates imply elastic elongations \(F_iL/(EA)=0.079\) and 0.135 mm. An instrument resolving about 0.01 mm could distinguish the two states in this example if its gauge length includes the correctly modelled bolt segments. Thread and shank regions of different areas contribute as elements in series. A clamp-force reading taken after surfaces have settled can also differ from a tightening-time elongation record without a change in friction coefficient.

Tolerances, interference and torque transmission

Machine elements and contact mechanics

Turn shaft and hub limits into a range of contact pressures and check whether a cylindrical interference fit can transmit torque.

From first principles

Relate diametral interference to frictional holding torque

An unassembled shaft can have a larger free diameter than its mating hub bore. Define positive diametral interference \(I=d_s-d_h\). Under elastic assembly the shaft surface moves inwards and the bore outwards until their radii agree. Assume axisymmetry, uniform pressure \(p\), nominal diameter \(d\), contact length \(L\), and combined radial compliance \(c_r\) giving total radial displacement per unit pressure. Compatibility gives

\[I/2=|u_s|+u_h=c_rp,\]
\[p=\frac{I}{2c_r}.\]

Under ideal Coulomb friction, the local shear traction at impending uniform circumferential slip is \(\mu p\). Every area element of the cylindrical contact has moment arm \(d/2\). Integrating the limiting traction yields

\[T_{\rm lim}=\int_A\frac d2\mu p\,dA=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2 .\]

Uniform axial sliding instead has limiting force \(F_{\rm lim}=\mu p\pi dL\). These rely on the same available friction traction, so simultaneous axial and torsional loading must share that capacity. The factor of two in compatibility matters because manufacturing limits usually specify diameters, while surface displacements are radial.

Derivation and applications

Predict how differential thermal expansion changes an interference fit

A shaft and hub assembled with a press fit can lose contact pressure when their temperatures change. Let \(d\) be their common nominal diameter at reference temperature and \(I_0>0\) the initial diametral interference, defined as free shaft diameter minus free hub-bore diameter. Assume small strains, uniform temperature in each part, free axial expansion and elastic response while contact is closed. Let \(\alpha_s,\alpha_h\) be the linear expansion coefficients and \(\Delta T_s,\Delta T_h\) the temperature changes. To first order in strain and interference-to-diameter ratio,

\[I(T)=I_0+d(\alpha_s\Delta T_s-\alpha_h\Delta T_h).\]

If \(c_r\) is the combined radial compliance per unit pressure, the radial mismatch is \(I/2\). Positive pressure requires positive interference. When the calculated interference becomes negative the unloaded circular parts have clearance; the elastic closure formula then ceases to apply.

\[p(T)=\frac{\max[0,I(T)]}{2c_r}.\]

For uniform axial contact length \(L\) and constant friction coefficient \(\mu\), the maximum tangential traction is \(\mu p\). Integrating that traction over the cylindrical surface, with moment arm \(d/2\), translates a temperature difference into a torque capacity.

\[T_{\rm lim}=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2,\]
\[\frac{T_{\rm lim}(T)}{T_{\rm lim}(0)}=\frac{\max[0,I(T)]}{I_0}.\]

This ratio assumes the elastic moduli, friction coefficient and dimensions appearing outside the interference stay effectively constant. Thermal gradients that distort the circular geometry can invalidate the uniform-pressure assumption before mean interference reaches zero.

What thermal clearance survives the tolerance stack?

At room temperature a ring bore is slightly smaller than its mating shaft. An employee measures a temperature rise of 100 K at the ring and sees that the nominal pair should slide together. Resolve this apparent disagreement by carrying the diameter tolerance and the warming of the shaft through the thermal expansion calculation.

Write the initial interference as \(i=d_{\mathrm{shaft},0}-d_{\mathrm{bore},0}\), positive for a press fit. In free thermal expansion a length changes by \(\alpha d\Delta T\). Thus the available diametral clearance before engagement is the hot bore diameter less the current shaft diameter. Replacing their near-equal nominal diameters in the small thermal corrections by the common scale \(d\) gives the first-order relation

\[C=-i+d(\alpha_r\Delta T_r-\alpha_s\Delta T_s).\]

All terms are diametral. Using a desired radial clearance in this formula would understate the diametral target by a factor of two. The common-diameter approximation drops terms of order \(i\alpha\Delta T\); products at that scale can matter for very tight metrology. Temperature rises here are relative to the same inspection reference.

Take \(d=50\) mm, interference between 22 and 48 µm, a ring expansion coefficient of \(12\times10^{-6}\) per kelvin and a shaft coefficient of \(11\times10^{-6}\) per kelvin. Convert manufacturing limits into assembly conditions

A shaft and a hub can share a nominal diameter while their manufactured diameters differ by several tens of micrometres. Name the diameters explicitly before subtracting them. For the present joint use positive diametral interference \(I=d_s-d_h\), where \(d_s\) is the free shaft diameter and \(d_h\) the free bore. Assemble the smallest shaft with the largest hole to obtain the minimum interference. The opposite pair gives the maximum. Worst-case limits do not require a probability distribution for the production process.

Consider a 40 mm connection with shaft limits 40.030 to 40.045 mm and bore limits 40.000 to 40.015 mm. The minimum difference is 0.015 mm and the maximum 0.045 mm. Every permitted pair interferes. The radial mismatch is half the diametral interference because the radius changes on both sides of a diameter. Confusing these conventions introduces a factor of two in the inferred pressure and torque.

Relate geometric mismatch to elastic deformation

When the parts return to their common assembly temperature, contact pressure compresses the shaft and expands the bore. Assume cylindrical surfaces, a short open-ended elastic hub with negligible axial stress, a solid elastic shaft and approximately uniform pressure away from the ends. Define the radial compliance \(c_r\) as the sum of the inward shaft and outward hub displacements per unit interface pressure. Compatibility across the common radius then reads

\[\delta_r=I/2=|u_s|+u_h=p\,c_r,\]
\[p=\frac{I}{2c_r}.\]

The combined compliance can be found from an elastic cylinder calculation or a verified test of geometrically identical parts. Providing it separately permits a torque calculation without assuming an infinitely stiff hub. A thinner hub generally expands further at the same pressure. Its larger compliance reduces the pressure developed by a given interference. Surface roughness that flattens during assembly can also reduce the effective interference relative to a measurement over the original asperities.

Integrate friction over the contact surface

A narrow surface patch of area \(dA\) can support a tangential friction force up to \(\mu p\,dA\). All such forces act at radius \(r=d/2\) and oppose impending rotation. Under uniform contact pressure and fully mobilised Coulomb friction, integrating around the circumference and along the overlap length \(L\) gives the limiting torque. For a purely axial pull the same friction forces act along the axis and their direct sum gives the limiting pull-out force.

\[T_{\lim}=\int r\mu p\,dA=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2,\]
\[F_{\lim}=\mu p\pi dL .\]

Take \(L=35\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\mu=0.12\), and \(c_r=0.50\,\mathrm{\mu m/MPa}\) in the 40 mm joint. The radial mismatches range from 7.5 to 22.5 micrometres, so calculated pressures range from 15 to 45 MPa. At the lower limit the tangential force capacity is \(0.12(15)\pi(40)(35)=7917\,\mathrm N\), and the torque capacity is \(7917(0.020)=158\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). At the upper limit pressure and capacity are three times larger. Use the lowest pressure to test transmission and the highest to evaluate hub stress.

Provide thermal clearance and account for combined service loads

Suppose only the hub is heated, so its bore grows approximately by \(\alpha d\Delta T\). To slip the worst-case pair together with a further 0.020 mm of diametral assembly clearance, the bore must grow by \(0.045+0.020=0.065\,\mathrm{mm}\). With \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) a uniform temperature rise of \(0.065/[40(12\times10^{-6})]=135\,\mathrm K\) is required in this ideal expansion calculation. Heat loss during transfer changes the actual temperature at insertion.

A shaft may also carry a sustained axial pull while delivering torque. Under the uniform-traction model the axial and circumferential components of the surface friction force share one bound. At the lower interference with a 4.0 kN pull the remaining circumferential capacity is \(\sqrt{7917^2-4000^2}=6832\,\mathrm N\), or about 137 N m. Checking the separate pure-load capacities would miss this interaction. If repeated microslip, an uncertain friction coefficient or stiffness near the hub edges is important, measurements of the actual assembly can determine which assumptions need closer investigation.

Derive interference pressure from thick-cylinder compliance

The radial compliance used above can be related to actual dimensions and materials. Consider a long enough cylindrical fit for a uniform central contact region, with free axial expansion so a plane-stress approximation is used for each cross-section. Let the common nominal radius be \(a\), outer hub radius \(b\), and positive pressure \(p\). The solid shaft experiences uniform in-plane compression \(\sigma_r=\sigma_\theta=-p\). Consequently its surface circumferential strain is \(-p(1-\nu_s)/E_s\), giving radial shrinkage magnitude \(pa(1-\nu_s)/E_s\).

For an elastic hub with free outside, radial equilibrium \(d\sigma_r/dr+(\sigma_r-\sigma_\theta)/r=0\) and axisymmetric compatibility give Lamé fields \(\sigma_r=A-B/r^2,\ \sigma_\theta=A+B/r^2\). Setting \(\sigma_r(a)=-p,\ \sigma_r(b)=0\) gives bore hoop stress \(\sigma_\theta(a)=p(b^2+a^2)/(b^2-a^2)\). Use \(\epsilon_\theta(a)=[\sigma_\theta(a)-\nu_h\sigma_r(a)]/E_h=u_h(a)/a\). Adding hub expansion and shaft contraction leads to

\[I/2=pa\left[\frac{1-\nu_s}{E_s}+\frac{(b^2+a^2)/(b^2-a^2)+\nu_h}{E_h}\right],\]

where \(I\) is the free diametral interference. The factor of two and the sign of radial pressure both affect the result. Significant axial restraint, plastic deformation, a hollow shaft or edge-pressure peaks require a different compliance model.

Compare thick and thin hubs at the same interference

A steel shaft and hub both have \(E=210\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(\nu=0.30\). Their contact radius is 20 mm, hub outer radius 40 mm and measured diametral interference 30 micrometres. In mm–MPa units the combined radial compliance is

\[20[0.70+(40^2+20^2)/(40^2-20^2)+0.30]/210000=0.000254\,\mathrm{mm/MPa}.\]

Pressure is

\[0.015/0.000254=59.1\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

The tensile hoop stress at the hub bore is

\[59.1(5/3)=98.5\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

For engagement length 25 mm and \(\mu=0.12\), torque capacity is \(2\pi\mu p a^2L=446\,\mathrm{N\,m}\) approximately.

Now reduce the outer radius to 26 mm with the same parts at the fit surface. The hub geometry factor becomes \((26^2+20^2)/(26^2-20^2)=3.90\), giving combined radial compliance \(20(1+3.90)/210000=0.000467\,\mathrm{mm/MPa}\). Pressure falls to 32.2 MPa and torque capacity falls to about 243 N m. Yet bore hoop stress rises to \(32.2(3.90)=126\,\mathrm{MPa}\) because the thinner hub amplifies hoop demand more strongly. Reducing contact pressure alone is not evidence that the hub becomes safer. The same dimensional change can reduce torque capacity and increase peak elastic stress, so both checks should use the geometry of the actual mating parts.

For ductile elastic parts, the hub bore should also be assessed in its multiaxial state. In the thicker example its principal stresses are approximately \(+98.5,0,-59.1\) MPa under plane stress. The von Mises value is \(\sqrt{98.5^2+59.1^2+98.5(59.1)}=138\,\mathrm{MPa}\), above the tensile hoop stress by itself. For the thinner hub, using \(+126,0,-32.2\) MPa gives approximately 145 MPa. A comparison with an allowable uniaxial stress must therefore state the criterion applied. Once significant yielding occurs the linear compliance used to determine pressure is itself affected, and enlarging the nominal interference will no longer guarantee a proportional increase in transmitted torque. A pull-out or torque-to-slip test on representative surfaces checks the independent assumption about friction.

For an interference test, measuring the outside hub diameter change offers an additional observable. At the outer surface radial stress vanishes, so its hoop strain depends directly on the local tensile hoop stress.

In fact the hub’s outside hoop stress from the same Lamé field is \(2pa^2/(b^2-a^2)\). For the 40 mm outer radius it is \(2(59.1)(400)/(1600-400)=39.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\), so the predicted 80 mm outside diameter increases by \(80(39.4)/210000=0.0150\,\mathrm{mm}\). For the thinner hub it is \(2(32.2)(400)/(676-400)=93.3\,\mathrm{MPa}\), giving an outside diameter increase \(52(93.3)/210000=0.0231\,\mathrm{mm}\). The thinner outer wall moves more under a smaller interface pressure. Observing this change together with transmitted torque can separate an incorrect elastic pressure estimate from an incorrect friction coefficient.

For example, if the thick hub expands by the predicted 15 micrometres but slips at 330 N m under otherwise matching conditions, the implied mean friction coefficient is

\[0.12(330/446)=0.089.\]

If the measured expansion were instead much smaller, an undersized effective interference, partial contact or a nonuniform fit would deserve investigation before calibrating friction. The micrometer reading is itself temperature sensitive: a uniform temperature difference of 10 K changes an 80 mm steel diameter by about 9.6 micrometres at \(12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). Stabilise the temperatures before comparing the calculated elastic expansion with metrology.

Size the working flanks and shear section of a parallel key

A shaft can drive a removable hub through the flanks of a parallel key. The load path runs from the shaft keyway into one side of the key, across its shear section, and out of the opposite side into the hub. Let \(d\) be the shaft diameter. Represent the distributed tangential contact by the resultant \(F=2T/d\) at the nominal shaft radius. This model assumes a single fitted key carries the specified torque without credit for friction from a separate clamp.

There are two different projected areas. If \(b\) is the key width and \(L_e\) the effective engaged length, the shear plane has area \(bL_e\). \[\tau_\mathrm{key}=\frac{2T}{dbL_e},\]
\[p_\mathrm{hub}=\frac{2T}{dh_eL_e},\]
\[L_{e,\min}=\max\left(\frac{2T}{db\tau_\mathrm{allow}},\,\frac{2T}{dh_ep_\mathrm{allow}}\right).\]

Locating a part with six contacts and predicting datum error

Machine elements and contact mechanics

A dimensionally accurate part can still be assembled in the wrong place. Use small rigid-body motions to work out what six contacts actually locate, then predict how dirt or a tilted guide appears in a measurement.

One frictionless contact supplies one scalar condition

Take the infinitesimal rigid motion of a body to consist of a translation \(\mathbf t\) and a rotation vector \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) in radians. A point initially at \(\mathbf r_i\) moves by \(\mathbf t+\boldsymbol\varphi\times\mathbf r_i\). A rigid locating pad at that point controls the component along its inward normal \(\mathbf n_i\). If its normal height error is \(e_i\), the displacement condition is

\[\mathbf n_i\cdot\mathbf t+(\mathbf r_i\times\mathbf n_i)\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi=e_i .\]

Stack the contact rows into a six-column matrix. Six independent rows locate a rigid part in three dimensions for small changes while the contacts remain seated. Three contacts in a plane support translation normal to the plane and two tilts. Two contacts on a second face locate one further translation and the remaining rotation. One on a third face sets the last translation. A preload must keep the unilateral contacts engaged. Six pads clustered along a single line would leave rotations undetermined despite the pad count.

Compute a tilted datum from three measured pad heights

Place the first three pads at coordinates \((0,0),(a,0),(0,b)\) on the nominal support plane. Normal displacement of a point \((x,y)\) is \(w=t_z+\varphi_x y-\varphi_y x\). Evaluate at the pads and subtract the origin row. The inverse relation gives

\[t_z=e_1,\]
\[\varphi_x=(e_3-e_1)/b,\]
\[\varphi_y=-(e_2-e_1)/a .\]

The predicted height of any feature is therefore an affine interpolation of the three heights. Outside their triangle it is an extrapolation and can magnify a small discrepancy. An additional hard pad away from the plane defined by the first three requires elastic deformation, a gap or loss of another contact. Its load sharing needs a stiffness model.

A particle under a pad and an encoder offset

Use \(a=120\) mm and \(b=80\) mm. A 12 μm particle raises only pad 2. Then \(\varphi_y=-100\) μrad and \(\varphi_x=t_z=0\). A feature 180 mm from the origin in the x direction rises by 18 μm. Doubling the spacing a halves this sensitivity while leaving the particle height unchanged. The feature error varies with position and cannot be removed at every point by a single vertical zero adjustment.

Consider a horizontal translation stage whose encoder sits a vertical distance \(d=75\) mm from a tool point. A pitch change \(\varphi_y=80\) μrad produces a longitudinal difference \(d\varphi_y=6.0\) μm between the two lines of action. This is the first-order Abbe error derived from the same rigid-body rotation law. Encoder scale calibration alone cannot distinguish this error from translation at a single feature. Sizing the leaves of a compliant translation guide

Machine elements and contact mechanics

A pair of thin leaves can guide a carriage with very little sliding friction. Their stiffness, stress and parasitic motion stem from the same bent shape. Derive those links before choosing a thickness or stroke.

Enforce the root and platform boundary conditions

Consider two identical rectangular leaves connecting a fixed base to a rigid guided platform. Each leaf has length L, width b, thickness t and bending stiffness \(EI=Ebt^3/12\). Let the platform translate sideways by a small amount \(\delta\) without rotating. Ignore shear deformation, axial tension and clamp compliance in the linear bending solution. Along each unloaded span \(EIw''''=0\), so the transverse shape is a cubic polynomial. At x=0 displacement and slope vanish. At x=L impose \(w=\delta\) and \(w'=0\). With \(s=x/L\) the resulting shape and curvature are

\[w=\delta(3s^2-2s^3),\]
\[w''={6\delta\over L^2}(1-2s).\]

The curvature changes sign at midspan. Each leaf has opposite end moments and maximal bending strain at both ends. A root modeled as a free pin would remove an essential slope condition and give the wrong stiffness.

Find guide force, surface stress and secondary motion

The end shear magnitude is \(EI|w'''|=12EI|\delta|/L^3\) per leaf. Because both leaves impose the same displacement their forces add. At the outer fibre the strain magnitude is \((t/2)|w''|\). Hooke's law gives

\[F={24EI\over L^3}\delta,\]
\[|\sigma|_{\max}={3Et|\delta|\over L^2}.\]

For inextensible leaves free to shorten their projected span, the arclength exceeds the projection by approximately \(\tfrac12\int_0^L (w')^2 dx\). Substituting the cubic shape gives \(3\delta^2/(5L)\). This parasitic travel is second order and has the same direction for positive or negative displacement. Constraining that axial motion instead introduces an axial force and nonlinearity. At large stroke use an appropriate geometric model for both stress and stiffness.

A stiffness target can conflict with a stress target

Choose \(L=40\) mm, \(b=10\) mm, \(t=0.30\) mm and \(E=200\) GPa. For each leaf \(I=2.25\times10^{-14}\) m⁴. The two-leaf stiffness is 1688 N/m. At 1 mm stroke the required force is 1.69 N, peak stress is 112.5 MPa and the free projected shortening is 15 μm. This small axial travel can matter when the stage is used for optical alignment.

Doubling the leaf thickness to 0.60 mm multiplies stiffness by eight, so 13.5 N is needed at the same stroke. Peak stress doubles to 225 MPa because surface fibres are farther from the neutral axis. In contrast, doubling width doubles stiffness without changing surface stress at a specified displacement. At a specified force the comparison changes because the stiffer guide travels less. Always identify which quantity is prescribed before drawing a design conclusion.

Why increasing the length of a bonded overlap gives diminishing returns

Machine elements and contact mechanics

Two slender straps transfer a tensile load through adhesive shear. Average stress decreases in inverse proportion to overlap length, but edge stress can approach a finite plateau. Derive the difference by coupling the axial extension of each strap to its neighbour.

Convert adhesive distortion into a distributed force

Consider identical adherends of thickness t, width b and Young's modulus E. A thin adhesive of thickness \(t_a\) and shear modulus G connects them over a length L. A tensile force P enters through strap 1 at the left and leaves through strap 2 on the right. Let \(n_1,n_2\) be axial force per unit width, with \(n_1+n_2=N=P/b\). Exclude bending and peel to isolate the symmetric extensional load-transfer mechanism. This idealization is more credible for an aligned or symmetrically balanced joint than for an eccentric single-lap joint.

Choose coordinates so the transmitted shear \(\tau=-n_1'=n_2'\) is positive. Axial strains are \(u_i'=n_i/(Et)\). Taking adhesive shear in the load-transfer direction as \(\tau=(G/t_a)(u_2-u_1)\) gives

\[\tau'=G(n_2-n_1)/(t_aEt).\]

One more derivative eliminates the member forces:

\[\tau''-\lambda^2\tau=0,\]
\[\lambda=\sqrt{{2G\over t_aEt}},\]
\[\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\tau\,dx=N .\]

The inverse transfer length \(1/\lambda\) is a mechanical length scale. A stiff adhesive transfers load over a shorter distance. A stiffer or thicker adherend distributes the required relative extension over a larger region. Reversing coordinate labels reverses force derivatives consistently without changing the predicted stress magnitude.

Apply symmetry and recover the edge-to-mean ratio

Identical adherends loaded at opposite ends give a shear profile symmetric about the middle. Set \(\tau=C\cosh(\lambda x)\) and choose C so its integral equals N. At the centre and edge this yields

\[\tau(x)={N\lambda\cosh(\lambda x)\over2\sinh(\lambda L/2)},\]
\[{\tau_{\rm edge}\over N/L}={\lambda L\over2}\coth{\lambda L\over2}.\]

For a very short overlap the ratio approaches one because \(\sinh y\sim y\). For a long overlap the edge stress approaches \(N\lambda/2\) even while \(N/L\) continues to decrease. The central portion then carries little shear. The model computes a smooth nominal adhesive stress; it does not resolve singular edge detail, peel or a local failure criterion.

Compare an average-based estimate with the load-transfer profile

Take \(P=1000\) N, \(b=20\) mm, \(E=70000\) MPa, \(t=2\) mm, \(t_a=0.20\) mm and \(G=300\) MPa. The force per width is 50 N/mm and \(\lambda=0.1464\) mm⁻¹, giving a transfer length of 6.83 mm. With \(L=20\) mm the mean stress is 2.50 MPa, but the edge sees 4.07 MPa and the centre only 1.79 MPa.

At \(L=40\) mm mean stress halves to 1.25 MPa. The edge stress falls only to 3.68 MPa and the centre drops to 0.393 MPa. The long-overlap limiting edge stress is 3.66 MPa. Thus using mean stress alone would suggest substantial unused room at the ends. Doubling the width at fixed P, in contrast, halves N and every stress in this one-dimensional model. Manufacturing constraints and adhesion quality can still change which failure mechanism occurs first.

When a suspended capacitor loses its stable equilibrium

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

An electrode suspended on a spring can remain motionless at a bias that makes the calculated electrostatic force grow as it moves. Static force balance alone misses the voltage at which that equilibrium disappears. Deriving the small-signal frequency gives an independent measurement of the same instability.

Start with the electrical and mechanical control variables

A movable conducting plate of area \(A\) faces a fixed electrode. Its unstressed gap is \(g\). Define positive displacement \(x\) toward the fixed plate, with spring force \(-kx\), mass \(m\) and viscous damping coefficient \(b\). For \(0\le x<g\) assume ideal parallel plates, negligible tilt and fringing, constant \(\epsilon\), and a linear elastic spring. The capacitance is \(C(x)=\epsilon A/(g-x)\). The model ends when the plates approach contact or when the suspending structure departs from its specified elastic law. It cannot by itself calculate adhesion, contact resistance or post-contact motion.

Before differentiating electrical energy specify whether the source holds voltage or charge. At fixed charge \(Q\), electrical energy is \(Q^2/[2C(x)]\), and its negative displacement derivative gives the force toward increasing \(x\). At fixed voltage \(V\), a source supplies \(VdQ\) as the plates move. The appropriate potential for mechanical equilibrium is the spring energy plus the field energy minus \(QV\), which here is \(\mathcal U_V=kx^2/2-C(x)V^2/2\). Differentiating gives the outward spring reaction and the attractive electrical force \(F_e=V^2C'(x)/2=\epsilon A V^2/[2(g-x)^2]\). An independent derivation multiplies the conductor pressure \(\epsilon E^2/2\) by area with \(E=V/(g-x)\). Both give a positive force. Replacing the source potential with the stored energy at fixed voltage would reverse its sign.

Locate all equilibria before choosing a branch

Define \(s=x/g\) and \(\Lambda=\epsilon A V^2/(2kg^3)\). The static force equation reduces to

\[\Lambda=s(1-s)^2.\]

At a specified voltage one can find \(s\) by scanning the curve \(s\mapsto s(1-s)^2\) over the physically admissible interval \(0\le s<1\). Its derivative is \((1-s)(1-3s)\), giving an interior maximum \(4/27\) at \(s=1/3\). For \(0<\Lambda<4/27\) there are two physical roots. The smaller is a local minimum of \(\mathcal U_V\) while the larger is a local maximum. Their merger is a fold of the static equilibrium branch:

\[x_{\rm pi}=g/3,\]
\[V_{\rm pi}=\sqrt{\frac{8kg^3}{27\epsilon A}} .\]

The often-quoted one-third displacement is the result for a linear spring, ideal voltage drive and this capacitance geometry. It is not a universal travel limit determined by the gap alone. Additional mechanical restoring forces or a different electrical boundary condition change the fold. Neither positive net electrical force nor the mere existence of a real solution is evidence of stable operation. A root finder started near the almost closed gap can converge to the unstable large-displacement root with an extremely small static residual.

The two branches can be distinguished experimentally without deliberately reaching contact. At static equilibrium \(kx=F_e\). Linearise the total force about \(x_0\) while maintaining voltage at its prescribed value. The incremental restoring stiffness and corresponding weak-damping natural frequency are

\[k_{\rm eff}=k-\frac{\epsilon A V^2}{(g-x_0)^3}=k\,\frac{1-3s_0}{1-s_0},\]
\[f_{\rm small}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm eff}}m}.\]

A small push toward the fixed electrode is restored only when \(k_{\rm eff}>0\). The familiar electrostatic softening is the reason the smaller equilibrium is stable. The small-signal frequency tends to zero as the fold is approached. With appreciable damping the observed amplitude resonance and ringdown oscillation have slightly different frequencies; fit the full mechanical response rather than identifying an arbitrary peak with the undamped \(f_{\rm small}\).

Background · Can a sudden step pull in a statically stable actuator?

An ideal voltage step applied to a plate initially at rest can push it past the unstable equilibrium. During the motion at fixed voltage the effective potential per \(kg^2\) is \(u(s)=s^2/2-b/[2(1-s)]\), where \(s=x/g\) and \(b=\epsilon A V^2/(kg^3)\). Neglect damping and begin at \(s=0\). At the dynamical threshold the initial energy equals the saddle energy and the slope vanishes at that saddle.

\[u(s)=u(0)\ \Longrightarrow\ b=s(1-s),\]
\[u'(s)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ b=2s(1-s)^2.\]

The two equations meet at \(s=1/2\), \(b=1/4\). The quasistatic fold gives \(b=8/27\). Consequently the undamped step threshold is only \(\sqrt{27/32}\simeq0.919\) times the slow-ramp pull-in voltage. A device operated between the two voltages can survive a ramp but collapse after an abrupt step. Dissipation and the actual rise time change this prediction.

Reconstruct the prepared state from a frequency measurement

Take a 100 μm square electrode in vacuum, \(g=2.00\) μm, \(k=2.00\) N/m and \(m=5.00\times10^{-10}\) kg. The predicted pull-in voltage is 7.32 V, the zero-bias capacitance 44.3 fF and the zero-bias natural frequency 10.07 kHz. On the low branch \(s_0=0.20\) gives \(x_0=0.400\) μm and voltage 6.80 V. Then \(k_{\rm eff}=1.00\) N/m and the small-signal frequency is 7.12 kHz. A measured frequency ratio \(f/f_0\) can be inverted via \(s=(1-r)/(3-r)\), where \(r=(f/f_0)^2\). The result is independent of absolute optical displacement calibration if the mass and underlying spring stiffness have not changed during the bias sweep.

At the same voltage the other physical root is \(s=0.48769\). A numerical force balance at this root would give the same supply reading but a negative tangent stiffness. The ideal voltage-controlled potential in units \(kg^2\) is \(u(s)=s^2/2-\Lambda/(1-s)\). The barrier height \(kg^2[u(0.48769)-u(0.20)]\) for this example is \(7.26\times10^{-14}\) J. A sufficiently energetic disturbance can carry the plate out of the stable basin even below static pull-in. This is different from loss of the static equilibrium itself. A quasistatic model predicts the existence and local stiffness of the equilibrium; a transient simulation must additionally specify initial displacement, velocity, drive ramp and damping.

Change the preparation to fixed charge

Disconnect a perfect isolated capacitor after placing a known charge \(Q\) on it, neglecting leakage and parasitic capacitance. Then \(F_Q=Q^2/(2\epsilon A)\) is independent of the remaining gap, because \(D=Q/A\) is fixed. Static equilibrium is \(x_0=Q^2/(2\epsilon A k)\), with incremental stiffness exactly \(k\). At the same initial voltage and gap the instantaneous force agrees with the voltage-controlled value, since \(Q=C(x_0)V\). The derivatives governing subsequent motion differ. At \(x_0=0.400\) μm the required charge in the numerical example is \(0.376\) pC. After ideal electrical isolation the small-signal frequency returns to the underlying elastic value even though the loaded static position stays the same.

Ideal charge isolation is delicate in a small device. A fixed parasitic capacitance \(C_p\) from the isolated node to a reference means the conserved charge belongs to \(C(x)+C_p\). Motion changes the fraction of total charge residing on the actuator. The electrical energy becomes \(Q_{\rm total}^2/[2(C+C_p)]\), so the force is \(Q_{\rm total}^2 C'/[2(C+C_p)^2]\), generally displacement dependent. If \(C_p\gg C\), the node voltage changes little with a small displacement and behavior is closer to voltage control. Interpreting an observed softening after disconnecting the supply as evidence against electromagnetic force balance would overlook this boundary change.

A different practical preparation puts an intentional series capacitor \(C_s\) between a fixed voltage supply and the actuator. Its series combination is \(C_{\rm ser}=\epsilon A/[g-x+\epsilon A/C_s]\). For static mechanical work at fixed supply voltage use \(F=V_s^2 C_{\rm ser}'/2\). Defining an effective electrical spacing \(g_e=g+\epsilon A/C_s\), the static fold occurs at \(x=g_e/3\) if this lies within the open physical gap. If \(\epsilon A/C_s\ge2g\), contact would occur before any such static fold in the ideal model. This calculation shows a direct connection between passive circuit design and allowed mechanical travel, while making clear that the maximum actuator voltage can differ from the voltage printed on the supply.

Why a sudden voltage change can reach contact below static pull-in

For a rapid electrical step the plate has a position and velocity inherited from the previous preparation. Suppose it starts at rest at its zero-bias position, the supply steps to a constant \(V\) much faster than a mechanical period, and mechanical damping is initially negligible. Immediately after the step, energy in the mechanical potential \(u(s)=s^2/2-\Lambda/(1-s)\) is \(u(0)=-\Lambda\) in units of \(kg^2\). Motion under this constant voltage can reach the force barrier if the initial value exceeds the potential at the unstable stationary point. This question can be solved by comparing two energies without integrating an entire time history.

Let \(s_b\) be the unstable root of \(\Lambda=s_b(1-s_b)^2\). At threshold the initial state is on the separatrix, the trajectory separating oscillations in the well from excursions to the closing gap. Set \(u(0)=u(s_b)\), subtract the two expressions and cancel \(s_b\) from the nontrivial solution. The two conditions become

\[\Lambda={s_b(1-s_b)\over2},\]
\[\Lambda=s_b(1-s_b)^2 .\]

Thus \(s_b=1/2\), \(\Lambda=1/8\), and the ideal undamped step threshold is

\[V_{\rm step}/V_{\rm pi}=\sqrt{27/32}=0.919.\]

In our example this is 6.72 V. A slow increase to 6.80 V leads to the observed stable 0.40 μm deflection, whereas a sudden ideal voltage step to the same value from rest at the origin contains enough energy to escape the well. Starting on the biased equilibrium before applying a small perturbation is a different experiment. The direction of the threshold shift makes physical sense: damping removes mechanical energy during the first excursion, and a slow ramp allows the equilibrium to move with the plate. The undamped value specifies a particular ideal initial-value experiment, rather than a voltage rating for an unspecified drive waveform.

One can check a simulated step by monitoring \(m\dot{x}^2/2+\mathcal U_V\) after the supply settles. With viscous damping its derivative is \(-b\dot{x}^2\), so any sustained increase during a constant-voltage segment points to a numerical or modelling error. Before the step ends there is an explicit drive term \(-CV\dot V\) in this energy balance. The mechanical potential already includes the source work. Adding \(V\dot Q\) to the same balance a second time gives an artificial energy imbalance. A transient solver should stop at a physically specified minimum gap instead of marching across the singularity; a statement about contact time depends on what counts as contact.

A finite source resistance changes what a ringdown measures

A dc supply with series resistance \(R\) can maintain the static voltage accurately while failing to hold the actuator voltage fixed over a mechanical cycle. Let its steady node voltage be \(V\), with equilibrium capacitance \(C=C(x_0)\). A small sinusoidal displacement \(\delta x\) causes current \(i\omega(C\,\delta v+VC'\delta x)\) into the actuator. The small-signal supply voltage is zero, so \(\delta v=-R i\). Eliminating current gives

\[\delta v=-\frac{V C'}C\frac{i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}\delta x,\]
\[\tau=RC .\]

Linearise \(F_e=V^2 C'/2\) allowing both voltage and position to vary. The direct derivative is \(K_{e0}=V^2C''/2\), while the change from node voltage is \(VC'\,\delta v\). For an ideal parallel plate \(C''/2=(C')^2/C\), so the two terms combine to the simple complex relation \(\delta F_e=K_{e0}\delta x/(1+i\omega\tau)\). At low frequency the electrical force follows displacement with its fixed-voltage slope. At high frequency the slope vanishes, as it must for an isolated ideal plate carrying fixed charge over one oscillation. At intermediate frequency a motion-induced current dissipates heat in the resistor.

Bring \(\delta F_e\) to the left side of the mechanical equation. Its real part subtracts \(K_{e0}/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\) from the mechanical stiffness; its imaginary part adds \(K_{e0}\tau/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\) to the viscous damping coefficient. This damping is positive for a passive resistor. At \(s=0.20\) the static electrical stiffness is \(1.00\) N/m, the operating capacitance is 55.3 fF and \(R=400\) MΩ gives

\[\tau=22.1\,\mu\mathrm s.\]

A 7.2 kHz interrogation has \(\omega\tau\simeq1\). Only about half the static electrical slope survives and the added damping is roughly \(11\,\mu\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). Treat these as frequency-dependent coefficients in the driven susceptibility. Substituting the low-frequency stiffness into a ringdown fit at this frequency would give the wrong displacement when the fitted frequency is inverted. Fitting the complex response at several frequencies avoids that ambiguity.

An experiment can separate the circuit effect from mechanical nonlinearity. Keep the same static bias and displacement, insert two known series resistances, then measure both in-phase and quadrature response to a small sinusoidal force. The displaced equilibrium should remain essentially unchanged at dc if leakage stays small. A stiffness dispersion and dissipative peak that shift in proportion to \(1/R\) support an electrical cause. Then repeat at a smaller oscillation amplitude. Amplitude-dependent frequency shift that survives the change in resistance can arise from nonlinear spring or force curvature, which the first-order susceptibility leaves out. A simple change to the external circuit has turned the slope of an energy curve into a prediction for a measured phase lag.

Compare force intersections for voltage and charge control

The initial gap is 2.00 μm, the area is a 100 μm square and the mass is 0.50 μg. Use the voltage ratio to move the force curve through the fold. At 93% of pull-in the low-displacement fixed-voltage intersection has a positive restoring slope. The high-displacement intersection has a negative slope. Virtual work and the forces hidden by a constraint

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A bead follows a wire, a pendulum remains the same length, and a rolling wheel obeys a relation between translation and rotation. Their reduced equations describe the motion but can conceal the largest forces in the apparatus. Determine which forces disappear under a virtual displacement, recover their values, and decide when the assumed motion ceases to be physically admissible.

Hold the clock fixed when defining a virtual displacement

A system of particles has Cartesian positions \(\mathbf r_i\) and applied forces \(\mathbf F_i\). A holonomic constraint is an equation \(g_\alpha(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N,t)=0\). At a fixed instant an admissible infinitesimal change of configuration obeys \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\). The physical velocities obey a different relation, \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\partial_tg_\alpha=0\). A virtual displacement compares geometrically allowed configurations at the same time. It has no prescribed duration and makes no claim that the system's momentum actually points along it.

The distinction becomes measurable for a bead on a moving horizontal table. Every allowed virtual displacement has zero vertical component, so the table's upward normal force gives zero virtual work. An upward-moving table can nevertheless raise the bead and perform positive ordinary work. Ordinary power involves the actual velocity, which includes the table motion. A careless statement that constraint forces do no work confuses two different operations. For a fixed ideal frictionless surface the real velocity is tangent as well, and vanishing virtual work then does imply vanishing ordinary power.

Let \(\mathbf R_i\) be the reaction forces and use \(m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i+\mathbf R_i\). Multiply by an admissible \(\delta\mathbf r_i\) and sum over particles. We call the constraints ideal when \(\sum_i\mathbf R_i\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\) for every allowed virtual displacement. Eliminating those reactions gives d'Alembert's principle,

\[\sum_i(\mathbf F_i-m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0.\]

This is a dynamical equation expressed through a static-looking virtual-work test. It applies instantaneously along the motion. It does not assert that the total force vanishes or that the acceleration is an independent virtual displacement. One advantage is that the detailed construction of a rigid support need not be known to determine the admissible tangential acceleration; its force can be calculated afterwards when designing the support.

Pass from virtual work to equations for coordinates

Choose independent coordinates \(q_a\) with \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r_i(q,t)\) and independent virtual increments \(\delta q_a\). The velocity is \(\sum_a\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i\,\dot q_a+\partial_t\mathbf r_i\). With kinetic energy \(T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i^2/2\), differentiation at fixed \(q,t\) gives

\[\partial_{\dot q_a}T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Differentiate in time. The product rule produces an acceleration term and a second term involving \(d(\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i)/dt\). The latter equals \(\partial_{q_a}\dot{\mathbf r}_i\), with the generalised velocities held fixed, and supplies \(\partial_{q_a}T\). Hence

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_a}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_a}=\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf r_i}{\partial q_a}.\]

The projected applied force is

\[Q_a=\sum_i\mathbf F_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Independence of the coordinate variations in d'Alembert's principle gives one equation \(d(\partial_{\dot q_a}T)/dt-\partial_{q_a}T=Q_a\) per coordinate. When \(\mathbf F_i\) comes from a potential \(V(q,t)\), the conservative contribution is \(-\partial_{q_a}V\). Other contributions such as a measured resisting torque remain explicit. A coordinate expressed as an angle has a generalised force in joules per radian; a coordinate expressed as a length has one in newtons. Checking \(Q_a\,dq_a\) as work avoids mistaking a generalised force for a Cartesian component.

For a simple pendulum of length \(l\) and mass \(m\), choose \(\theta\) measured from downward vertical. Then \(T=ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\) and \(V=mgl(1-\cos\theta)\). The equation \(ml^2\ddot\theta=-mgl\sin\theta\) follows at once. The string tension vanished under the angular displacement. It has not been shown to vanish physically. Projecting Newton's equation onto the direction from the bead toward the pivot gives

\[T_{\rm string}=mg\cos\theta+ml\dot\theta^2.\]

This second observable is sensitive to speed even when the reduced angular acceleration depends only on angle.

Recover multipliers before choosing coordinates

Cartesian coordinates keep reaction directions explicit. Put all coordinates into a vector \(x\), let \(M\) be the mass matrix and let \(G\) contain the rows \(\nabla g_\alpha\). The tangent virtual displacements are precisely those satisfying \(G\delta x=0\). A reaction doing zero virtual work must be a linear combination of the normal covectors, \(R=G^T\lambda\), when the constraints are independent. Newton's equation becomes

\[M\ddot x=F+G^T\lambda.\]

Differentiate the constraints twice along the trajectory: \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\), where \(\gamma\) includes the known velocity, curvature and explicit time terms. Substituting for \(\ddot x\) determines reactions from a much smaller system,

\[(GM^{-1}G^T)\lambda=-\gamma-GM^{-1}F.\]

For any vector \(c\), \(c^TGM^{-1}G^Tc=(G^Tc)^TM^{-1}(G^Tc)\) is positive if \(G\) has independent rows and particle masses are positive. Dependent constraint equations make the individual multipliers ambiguous. For instance writing both \(g=0\) and \(2g=0\) declares the same geometrical restriction twice. Only the combined reaction is then determined. The magnitude of a raw multiplier also depends on the units and normalisation of \(g\). Reporting the force \(G^T\lambda\) makes comparisons between alternative constraint descriptions meaningful.

Use \(g=(x^2+y^2-l^2)/2=0\) for a planar pendulum, with \(y\) upward, so the gradient is the radius vector \(\mathbf r\). Newton's equation reads \(m\ddot{\mathbf r}=-mg\hat y+\lambda\mathbf r\), where the symbol \(g\) in the gravitational term denotes the acceleration magnitude. The acceleration constraint \(\mathbf r\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}=-v^2\) gives

\[\lambda=(mgy-mv^2)/l^2.\]

The inward tension is \(-\lambda l=mv^2/l-mgy/l\). Since \(y=-l\cos\theta\), this is the radial-force result already obtained. The multiplier in this convention has units of force per length. Using the radial-distance constraint instead would make its magnitude a force.

Background · What if the support can push but cannot pull?

Replacing a surface by an equality constraint assumes the object stays in contact. A particle sliding along the inside of a smooth vertical circular track illustrates the additional physical test. At the top, gravity and the inward normal reaction together must supply the centripetal acceleration.

\[N=\frac{mv^2}{R}-mg,\qquad N\geq0.\]

At a top speed below \(\sqrt{gR}\) the equality-constrained solution requires negative \(N\). An ordinary track cannot exert that pull. Its normal force then falls to zero and the particle must follow free flight until it meets the track again. A bead threaded on a wire could receive the oppositely directed force and would follow a different trajectory. Both models have the same geometrical path during contact.

Recognise an inequality at a contact

A bead threaded on a stiff loop can experience reaction in either direction, whereas a string can pull only. A surface without adhesion can push only. Derive the constrained motion, calculate its reaction, and compare the required sign with the physical mechanism. When a calculated tension turns negative, replace the constrained trajectory by free flight until a new contact event. Continuing to integrate the pendulum equation would retain a mathematically valid circle and a physically unavailable pushing string.

A mass starts at the bottom of a frictionless vertical loop with speed \(v_b\). Energy conservation gives \(v_{\rm top}^2=v_b^2-4gl\), since the top is \(2l\) higher. A string just remaining taut at the top requires \(T_{\rm top}=mv_{\rm top}^2/l-mg\ge0\), giving \(v_b^2\ge5gl\). The energy-only criterion for reaching the elevation is \(v_b^2\ge4gl\); the interval between these two values illustrates why a trajectory test must include reactions. With \(l=0.80\,{\rm m}\), the threshold bottom speed is \(6.26\,{\rm m/s}\). At this threshold the bottom tension is \(6mg\), six times the weight, even though the tension is about to vanish at the top.

Recover the forces on a wedge that is free to slide

Place a wedge of mass \(M\) on a smooth horizontal floor and a block of mass \(m\) on its smooth upper face. Let the slope descend to the right at angle \(\alpha\). Choose \(X\) as the wedge's horizontal position and \(s\) as distance down the face. The block's coordinates apart from fixed offsets are \(x=X+s\cos\alpha,\ y=-s\sin\alpha\). Its relative velocity points down the slope but its absolute acceleration need not. The kinetic energy contains a mixed term \(m\dot X\dot s\cos\alpha\). Dropping that term is equivalent to holding the wedge stationary while allowing it to recoil, two incompatible mechanical models.

The reduced equations are \((M+m)\ddot X+m\ddot s\cos\alpha=0\) and \(\ddot s+\ddot X\cos\alpha=g\sin\alpha\). The first is conservation of total horizontal momentum, since the block–wedge normal force is internal and the floor is frictionless. Elimination gives

\[\ddot s=(M+m)g\sin\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha),\]
\[\ddot X=-mg\sin\alpha\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

The acceleration down the moving face exceeds \(g\sin\alpha\) because the wedge moves left as the block slides right. Nevertheless the absolute vertical acceleration of the block remains less than or equal to free fall because the upper face pushes upward.

To find the block's normal force, project its absolute acceleration onto the outward unit normal \((\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)\). The part proportional to \(\ddot s\) cancels, leaving \(\ddot X\sin\alpha\). Newton's equation along that direction gives

\[N=m(g\cos\alpha+\ddot X\sin\alpha)=mMg\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

With \(M=3.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=1.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(\alpha=30^\circ\), one finds \(\ddot s=6.04\,{\rm m/s^2}\),

\[\ddot X=-1.31\,{\rm m/s^2},\]
\[N=7.84\,{\rm N}.\]

The normal based on a stationary wedge would be \(8.50\,{\rm N}\). A force sensor under the moving face could distinguish the two models even when a short record of relative slope displacement cannot resolve the difference.

Which reaction does no virtual work here? The normal force on the block has virtual work \(N\hat n\cdot(\delta X\hat x+\delta s\hat t)=N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\). The opposite normal force on the wedge gives \(-N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\), so the sum cancels. Considering only the block and asserting zero work from the normal for arbitrary \(\delta X\) would be incorrect. At the real velocities the same reaction transfers energy between the bodies even though its net internal power on their pair is zero. The floor's normal reaction balances the vertical momentum change of the whole system. During the slide it is \((M+m)g-m\ddot s\sin\alpha\), less than total weight while the block accelerates downwards.

Take the limits as an additional test. At fixed \(m,\alpha\), increasing \(M\) to infinity returns the familiar fixed-wedge results. At a nearly horizontal face, downslope acceleration and wedge recoil vanish, restoring the full block weight as normal force. At a vertical face the normal vanishes and the block falls. These limits hold because the contact has no friction and can be maintained with a nonnegative normal. A moving wedge of independently prescribed acceleration would replace the horizontal momentum equation by a control input, changing both the relative acceleration and the support force.

Distinguish rolling constraints from purely geometric restrictions

A cylinder of radius \(R\), mass \(m\) and axial inertia \(I\) rolls without slip down a fixed rough incline. Choose coordinate \(s\) down the incline and spin angle \(\phi\) in the corresponding sense. The constraint \(\dot s=R\dot\phi\) is integrable in this one-dimensional motion, so it can be inserted as \(s-R\phi=\mathrm{constant}\). The resulting kinetic energy is \((m+I/R^2)\dot s^2/2\). The reduced equation gives

\[\ddot s=g\sin\alpha/[1+I/(mR^2)].\]

Reconstruct the static friction from translation, \(f=g m\sin\alpha-m\ddot s\), directed up the slope, and compare it with the available maximum \(\mu_s mg\cos\alpha\).

For a homogeneous solid cylinder \(I=mR^2/2\), the speed-up is \(2g\sin\alpha/3\) and friction magnitude \(mg\sin\alpha/3\). A ring of the same mass and radius has \(I=mR^2\), half the gravitational downslope acceleration and half the downslope weight component as friction. At \(30^\circ\) the minimum static friction coefficients are respectively \(\tan30^\circ/3=0.192\) and \(\tan30^\circ/2=0.289\). Two objects on the same surface can therefore diverge qualitatively because the ring slips while the cylinder still rolls. Treating an assumed no-slip relation as exact without testing that inequality can produce an unphysical result despite apparently consistent energy conservation.

The friction on a rigid body rolling without slip on a fixed surface has zero power because its instantaneous point of contact is stationary. The force still changes both linear and angular momentum. On a moving conveyor the contact velocity is no longer zero in the laboratory; friction can supply mechanical energy even while the relative contact velocity vanishes. For a wheel steering on a plane the restrictions \(\dot x=R\dot\phi\cos\psi,\ \dot y=R\dot\phi\sin\psi\) depend on heading \(\psi\). In general they cannot be integrated into two configuration equations relating \(x,y,\phi,\psi\) globally. d'Alembert's fixed-time allowed variations still project out ideal rolling reactions, but replacing those velocity constraints by invented holonomic coordinates gives the wrong dynamics. The distinction can be seen geometrically by rolling and steering through a closed sequence of steering operations that leaves a net displacement.

Find loads on two supports of a suspended bar

A rigid horizontal bar of mass \(m\) and length \(2a\) is supported at its ends by two vertical inextensible light cords. At one instant both cords are vertical and the bar is horizontal. An extra downward load of magnitude \(F\) is applied at horizontal coordinate \(s\) relative to the centre. Ask first for a static experiment. The two vertical reaction forces \(T_L,T_R\) obey force balance \(T_L+T_R=mg+F\) and moment balance \(a(T_R-T_L)=sF\). This gives

\[T_R=(mg+F+sF/a)/2,\]
\[T_L=(mg+F-sF/a)/2.\]

The lever arms encode geometry that the vertical balance alone cannot provide.

The same result can be obtained from virtual variations if the reactions are kept as multipliers until the end. For small vertical translation \(y\) of the centre and small tilt \(\theta\), the endpoint vertical displacements are \(\delta y-a\delta\theta\) on the left and \(\delta y+a\delta\theta\) on the right. The load point displacement is \(\delta y+s\delta\theta\). Before eliminating the endpoint constraints, project all applied and reaction forces against arbitrary independent \(\delta y,\delta\theta\); vanishing of both coefficients gives the preceding balances. After imposing both endpoint constraints directly, these two variations vanish and the reduced model for vertical motion contains no equation from which the support loads can be read. Removing a degree of freedom can remove precisely the observable of interest unless reactions are reconstructed.

For \(m=2.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.40\,{\rm m}\), \(F=12\,{\rm N}\) and \(s=0.25\,{\rm m}\), the total load is \(31.62\,{\rm N}\) and the load difference \(7.50\,{\rm N}\). Hence \(T_R=19.56\,{\rm N}\), \(T_L=12.06\,{\rm N}\). Exchanging the load from right to left reverses the difference while preserving the sum. This symmetry is a calibrating manoeuvre for two load cells with different offsets: subtract readings from mirrored configurations to isolate the lever-arm response, and compare their sum to the known total weight and applied force.

Now imagine three vertical supports at \(-a,0,+a\). Their three tensions enter only two independent balances. Perfectly rigid constraints determine no unique allocation to the centre support. The ambiguity is physical; the stiffnesses, preloads and small height differences of real supports decide the result. With three identical unstrained linear springs and a centred load \(F\), each spring extends equally if the bar stays level, so each carries one third of the total weight. If the middle spring is twice as stiff and all have the same unloaded height, it carries one half and each outer spring one quarter. Both answers satisfy exactly the same force and moment balances. Reporting a unique value from a singular ideal multiplier system would manufacture an unmeasured constitutive assumption.

Test an acceleration constraint against drift of the position itself

Solving \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\) guarantees the correct instantaneous normal acceleration at a state lying on the constraint manifold with tangent velocity. It does not magically repair earlier position or velocity errors in approximate time integration. For a particle constrained to a circle, monitor \(g=(r^2-l^2)/2\), \(\dot g=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\), and the computed force. A small tangential displacement can give tiny instantaneous force error while the radius gradually drifts. Integrating an acceleration-level constraint twice without a position or velocity correction can cause substantial mismatch over many revolutions.

A simple projection illustrates what each correction does. Normalise an approximate position vector to length \(l\), then remove the radial component of velocity by \(\mathbf v\leftarrow\mathbf v-\hat r(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)\). The corrected point and tangent satisfy the constraints, but the velocity correction removes kinetic energy \(m(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)^2/2\), and the position change can alter potential energy. Projection is therefore a numerical operation whose physical invariant errors must be measured. A structure-preserving constrained integrator builds the position and momentum conditions into the step and often gives better long-time behaviour. Compare convergence of reaction, constraint residual and energy separately instead of treating a rounded radius as proof of a correct force.

A pendulum on a free cart tests the complete mass matrix

Mount a pendulum pivot on a cart of mass \(M\) moving horizontally on a smooth track. The bob has mass \(m\), length \(l\) and downward angle \(\theta\). Choose the cart position \(X\) and use bob coordinates \(X+l\sin\theta,-l\cos\theta\). The kinetic energy is \((M+m)\dot X^2/2+ml\dot X\dot\theta\cos\theta+ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\). Two different off-diagonal mass terms would contradict its origin as a quadratic form; the same factor \(ml\cos\theta\) multiplies the companion acceleration in each equation. This is a useful check on a manually assembled multibody model.

The equations are

\[(M+m)\ddot X+ml(\ddot\theta\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2\sin\theta)=0,\]
\[l\ddot\theta+\ddot X\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0.\]

In the small-angle regime the horizontal centre of mass moves uniformly. Choosing zero total momentum gives

\[\dot X\simeq-ml\dot\theta/(M+m).\]

Substitute its derivative into the angular equation to obtain

\[\ddot\theta+[(M+m)g/(Ml)]\theta=0.\]

Releasing the support raises the oscillation frequency because the effective angular inertia falls while the gravitational stiffness stays the same. The result is a statement about the relative pendulum angle, measured from an inertial vertical, with an unforced horizontally moving support.

For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=0.20\,{\rm kg}\) and \(l=0.50\,{\rm m}\), the small-angle period is

\[2\pi\sqrt{Ml/[(M+m)g]}=1.229\,{\rm s}.\]

Fixing the cart with a clamp gives \(1.419\,{\rm s}\) instead. At zero total horizontal momentum the cart displacement from its equilibrium is approximately \(-0.125\,\theta\) metres, so a \(0.10\,{\rm rad}\) angle corresponds to \(12.5\,{\rm mm}\) of recoil. Simultaneous position measurements on cart and bob should reconstruct a horizontal centre of mass that is stationary to within tracking uncertainty. A missing cart inertia or a wrongly filled cross term can alter both period and recoil; checking just one number may not identify which error occurred.

The reaction on the moving bob can again be recovered. Its radial acceleration along the outward string direction is \(\ddot X\sin\theta-l\dot\theta^2\). Thus the tension is \(T=m[g\cos\theta-\ddot X\sin\theta+l\dot\theta^2]\). The acceleration of the support appears explicitly. Reusing the fixed-pivot formula at all angles would omit a term that vanishes accidentally at the bottom. To test the sign, observe a rightward tilted pendulum whose pivot accelerates right: the support's acceleration has an outward projection along the tilted string, reducing the inward tension needed for the same relative motion. Substituting a directly measured cart acceleration provides an independent load-cell prediction.

Decide which force a sensor actually reports

Reaction forces can be reported as tension along a member, normal traction at a contact, total support load on a floor, or a single Cartesian component read by a load cell. A rigid-body idealisation alone does not specify a sensor's orientation or the weight of its housing. For a fixed pendulum, for example, the string carries tension \(T\), while a vertical force channel at its pivot reads the vertical string contribution \(T\cos\theta\). A horizontal channel reads \(T\sin\theta\). The force on the pivot is opposite the force exerted on the bob. Selecting the wrong sign or leaving out the fixture's own weight can create a discrepancy even when the dynamical calculation is correct.

Suppose an ideal load cell with its sensitive axis vertical is tared with the fixture installed but the \(0.40\,{\rm kg}\) bob removed. When the bob is held at \(60^\circ\) by a separate purely tangential actuator and is otherwise stationary, the radial balance gives

\[T=mg\cos60^\circ=1.96\,{\rm N}.\]

The pivot's vertical reading attributable to the string is \(0.981\,{\rm N}\). The tangential actuator supplies the remaining vertical support as well as a horizontal component. Once the actuator releases, instantaneously the same tension and reading hold because radial speed is still zero. At the bottom of the subsequent swing the load jumps to the dynamical value obtained earlier, even though no upper fixture mass has changed.

A student hangs the bob at the same \(60^\circ\) angle using a separate horizontal cord instead of a tangential actuator. Should the pivot cell read the preceding \(0.981\,{\rm N}\)?

A schematic that shows only the bob angle is insufficient to infer the support load without saying how the angle is maintained.

In the free cart experiment bearing drag is weak but measurable. Can an observed frequency shift distinguish it from a wrongly assumed cart mass?

Locate contact loss on a smooth convex surface

A small block starts sliding from near rest just to one side of the top of a smooth fixed sphere of radius \(R\). Measure angle \(\theta\) from upward vertical. Energy gives \(v^2=2gR(1-\cos\theta)\) during contact, while the inward radial equation reads \(mg\cos\theta-N=mv^2/R\). Thus \(N=mg(3\cos\theta-2)\). Contact first vanishes at \(\cos\theta=2/3\), or \(48.2^\circ\). For a sphere of radius \(0.50\,{\rm m}\), this occurs after a vertical drop \(R/3=0.167\,{\rm m}\) and at speed \(1.81\,{\rm m/s}\). Once the block leaves, the same spherical coordinates can still be used to describe position, but \(r=R\) is no longer an equation of motion. Continue with the measured tangential velocity and free gravitational acceleration. Enforcing the sphere condition beyond release would make the computed normal negative and predict a surface that pulls on the block. The zero normal, the geometric release angle and the subsequent ballistic arc provide three independent observables for the same force reconstruction.

Moving constraints, boundary work and a changing energy

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A pendulum with a reeled string can gain energy while its support force does no virtual work. A bead on a spinning guide can accelerate without gravity. Follow the work done by the apparatus, derive the relation between a reduced energy and laboratory energy, and identify which quantity a measurement tests.

Parameterise the motion of a guide as well as the bead

Let a prescribed moving guide define positions \(\mathbf r(q,t)\). Its relative velocity along the guide is \(\mathbf r_q\dot q\) and its imposed local velocity is \(\mathbf r_t\), where \(t\) is differentiated at fixed coordinate. The kinetic energy \(T=m|\mathbf r_q\dot q+\mathbf r_t|^2/2\) includes a quadratic term in \(\dot q\), a mixed term linear in \(\dot q\), and a term independent of \(\dot q\). Dropping the last two merely because the guide motion is prescribed generally changes the bead equation. A term independent of \(\dot q\) may still depend on its position \(q\).

For \(L=T-V\) define \(p=\partial_{\dot q}L\) and \({\cal E}=p\dot q-L\). Differentiating along a solution of the Euler–Lagrange equation gives \(d{\cal E}/dt=-\partial_tL\) in the absence of extra nonconservative generalised forces. This identity involves the partial time derivative at fixed \(q,\dot q\). The laboratory energy is

\[E_{\rm lab}=m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2+V.\]

Direct substitution gives \({\cal E}=E_{\rm lab}-m\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r_t\) for a single particle and ordinary position potential. Thus a time-independent reduced Lagrangian can conserve a quantity different from the laboratory mechanical energy. Which observer supplies a force, and which independent variables are held fixed, determine the energy being tracked.

Work supplied by a steadily rotating straight slot

A smooth horizontal guide turns about its inner end with prescribed angle \(\varphi=\Omega t\). Let \(r\) be the bead's distance from the pivot. The velocity is \(\dot r\hat r+r\Omega\hat\varphi\), and the reduced Lagrangian is \(m(\dot r^2+\Omega^2r^2)/2\). Its Euler–Lagrange equation is

\[\ddot r=\Omega^2r.\]

With \(r(0)=r_0,\dot r(0)=0\) the solution is \(r_0\cosh\Omega t\) until the bead leaves the slot. In the laboratory no force acts along the radial tangent of the slot, yet radial acceleration in polar coordinates is \(\ddot r-r\Omega^2\), precisely zero. The tangential acceleration \(2\Omega\dot r\) is provided by the slot wall.

The wall reaction \(R_\varphi=2m\Omega\dot r\) exerts a torque \(rR_\varphi\) on the bead. The driving motor balances the opposite reaction on the slot and supplies power \(\Omega rR_\varphi=2m\Omega^2r\dot r\). Differentiating \(E_{\rm lab}=m(\dot r^2+\Omega^2r^2)/2\) and using the radial equation gives the same power. The reduced energy \(m(\dot r^2-\Omega^2r^2)/2\) is constant. Its difference from \(E_{\rm lab}\) equals \(-\Omega L_z\), with \(L_z=mr^2\Omega\). This is a consequence of uniform imposed rotation, not a negative physical rotational kinetic energy.

For \(m=0.10\,{\rm kg}\), \(r_0=0.050\,{\rm m}\) and \(\Omega=4.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), consider \(t=0.25\,{\rm s}\). The radial position is \(77.2\,{\rm mm}\), radial speed \(0.235\,{\rm m/s}\), and wall reaction \(0.188\,{\rm N}\). The motor supplies about \(0.0580\,{\rm W}\) at that instant. Laboratory energy has risen from \(0.00200\,{\rm J}\) to approximately \(0.00752\,{\rm J}\). Measuring only the angular motor speed would miss the incoming mechanical work. The torque on the drive shaft or the changing moment of inertia supplies the other observable required for an energy budget.

A pendulum with prescribed length

Let a point pendulum have length \(l(t)>0\) and angle \(\theta\) from downward vertical. Using \(x=l\sin\theta\), \(y=-l\cos\theta\) gives \(T=m(\dot l^2+l^2\dot\theta^2)/2\) and \(V=-mgl\cos\theta\) for a fixed gravitational datum. The angular Euler–Lagrange equation becomes

\[\frac{d}{dt}(ml^2\dot\theta)=-mgl\sin\theta,\]
\[\ddot\theta+2\frac{\dot l}{l}\dot\theta+\frac{g}{l}\sin\theta=0 .\]

The term proportional to \(\dot l\dot\theta\) comes directly from differentiating angular momentum about the pivot. With increasing length the angular speed at fixed angular momentum decreases; reeling in has the opposite effect. It can look like damping or amplification in the angular equation even with no friction. A mechanical energy balance distinguishes the reversible exchange with a reel from energy lost as heat.

The radial Newton equation, taking outward as positive, is \(m(\ddot l-l\dot\theta^2)=mg\cos\theta-T_{\rm string}\). Hence \(T_{\rm string}=m(g\cos\theta+l\dot\theta^2-\ddot l)\). The tension power on the bob is \(-T_{\rm string}\dot l\). This has the correct sign when a heavy nearly vertical bob is lowered slowly: \(\dot l>0\) and the gravitational potential decreases as the string takes energy out. At the bottom of a swing the tension is larger, so a reel shortening the string there does more positive work per unit length than it would at a turning point. Timing a cyclic length change can pump an oscillation while the guide returns to precisely its initial length.

With \(m=0.50\,{\rm kg}\), \(l=1.0\,{\rm m}\), \(\dot\theta=2.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), and the bob at the bottom, a short interval of constant-rate shortening at \(\dot l=-0.030\,{\rm m/s}\) has tension \(6.91\,{\rm N}\) and delivers \(0.207\,{\rm W}\). When the bob is momentarily at rest at \(30^\circ\), the tension for the same length and zero radial acceleration is \(4.25\,{\rm N}\). Reversing the reel rate there would remove only \(0.127\,{\rm W}\). A complete finite cycle requires integrating the time-varying tensions and checking the acceleration during each reel reversal.

Translate a pivot and separate real forcing from a coordinate choice

For a pivot translating horizontally at prescribed \(X(t)\) and constant string length \(l\), write \(x=X+l\sin\theta\), \(y=-l\cos\theta\). The kinetic energy includes \(ml\dot X\dot\theta\cos\theta\). In the angular Euler–Lagrange equation the terms proportional to \(\dot X\dot\theta\sin\theta\) cancel, leaving \(l\ddot\theta=-g\sin\theta-\ddot X\cos\theta\). A constant pivot velocity therefore does not force the relative angle. Replacing the pivot velocity by its magnitude as a driving force would wrongly violate Galilean invariance. A horizontal pivot acceleration does force the angle; it appears as the opposite inertial acceleration in the translating frame.

For \(|\theta|\ll1\) and \(X=X_0\cos\omega t\), the equation becomes

\[\ddot\theta+\omega_0^2\theta=(X_0\omega^2/l)\cos\omega t,\]
\[\omega_0^2=g/l.\]

At drive frequencies well below \(\omega_0\),

\[\theta\simeq-\ddot X/g.\]

The bob nearly aligns with the effective gravity in the moving frame. Far above \(\omega_0\), the particular solution is approximately \(-X/l\); then the bob's absolute horizontal coordinate \(X+l\theta\) changes little even while the pivot moves. Near resonance the undamped steady particular amplitude is inappropriate, and the initial transient or a physical damping model matters.

Calculate work done by a moving wall in an elastic collision

A particle of mass \(m\) moves along a line and collides elastically with a wall whose prescribed laboratory velocity is \(U\) during the short contact. In the instantaneous wall frame the incoming relative velocity \(v^--U\) reverses, so

\[v^+=2U-v^-.\]

In the laboratory the impulse is \(J=m(v^+-v^-)=2m(U-v^-)\) and the kinetic energy change is \(\Delta K=m[(v^+)^2-(v^-)^2]/2=UJ\). The energy gained by the particle equals wall velocity times the impulse delivered by the wall. A perfectly elastic contact still changes laboratory kinetic energy when the wall moves, since elasticity was defined for the relative normal velocity.

Suppose a particle of mass \(0.20\,{\rm kg}\) moves right at \(3.0\,{\rm m/s}\) into a right wall moving left at \(0.50\,{\rm m/s}\). Its final velocity is \(-4.0\,{\rm m/s}\), and the impulse on the particle is \(-1.40\,{\rm N\,s}\). The particle's kinetic energy rises from \(0.90\) to \(1.60\,{\rm J}\), exactly \(UJ=0.70\,{\rm J}\). The actuator keeping the wall on schedule supplies the energy. When the same wall recedes to the right at \(0.50\,{\rm m/s}\), the outgoing velocity is \(-2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) and kinetic energy falls by \(0.50\,{\rm J}\). The difference is not symmetric under reversal of wall speed at fixed incoming particle velocity, because that reversal also changes the relative approach speed.

Now trap a particle between a fixed wall and a length \(l(t)\) varying slowly compared with the collision period. To leading order a right-wall collision changes the particle's speed by \(-2\dot l\), and such collisions occur about once in \(2l/v\). Hence \(dv/dt\simeq-(v/l)\dot l\), so \(vl\) is approximately constant. The one-dimensional action around a full bounce cycle is

\[J_{\rm act}=(2\pi)^{-1}(2mvl)=mvl/\pi.\]

This elementary wall argument explains the adiabatic action without requiring the smooth-force approximation at the instantaneous collision. If the wall speed varies quickly within a round trip, the collision phases matter and the averaged invariant can fail.

Starting with \(l_0=0.40\,{\rm m}\) and speed \(4.0\,{\rm m/s}\), slow compression to \(0.30\,{\rm m}\) predicts \(v\simeq5.33\,{\rm m/s}\). Its kinetic energy increases by a factor \(16/9\), supplied through the moving boundary. A pressure sensor at the wall measures impulse per time, \(2mv\) times the collision frequency \(v/(2l)\), giving average force \(mv^2/l\) at leading order. Multiplying this force by the inward speed gives the power required by the predicted energy law. An instantaneous compression while the particle is elsewhere would instead change no kinetic energy until a later collision. The final length alone does not determine the energy unless the rate protocol is specified.

Follow a string through a moving boundary in configuration and time

For a vertical moving pivot with height \(Y(t)\), coordinates \(x=l\sin\theta\), \(y=Y-l\cos\theta\) give kinetic energy \(m[\dot Y^2+2l\dot Y\dot\theta\sin\theta+l^2\dot\theta^2]/2\). Derive the angular equation by differentiating \(p_\theta=ml^2\dot\theta+ml\dot Y\sin\theta\). The terms containing \(\dot Y\dot\theta\cos\theta\) cancel as in horizontal translation; the remaining equation is

\[l\ddot\theta=-(g+\ddot Y)\sin\theta.\]

A vertical acceleration changes the restoring coefficient multiplicatively, unlike a horizontal displacement whose small-angle effect is an additive forcing term. The distinction is visible even when the two pivot motions have identical amplitudes and frequencies.

For \(Y=A\cos\Omega t\), the effective gravity oscillates as \(g-A\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\). The perfectly vertical solution remains an exact solution at any drive amplitude. A small deviation need not remain small, because its linear equation has a periodic coefficient. An observed growth of swing from a tiny initial disturbance near twice the natural frequency is a parametric effect. A camera viewing the pivot and bob together can separate relative angle from large imposed vertical displacement. Estimating angular amplitude from absolute bob height alone introduces a component at the drive frequency unrelated to the swing.

At modest drive \(A=1.0\,{\rm mm}\), \(\Omega=20\,{\rm rad/s}\), the pivot acceleration amplitude is \(0.40\,{\rm m/s^2}\), about four percent of gravity. A pendulum of length \(0.10\,{\rm m}\) has small-angle frequency \(9.90\,{\rm rad/s}\), putting this drive near twice its natural frequency despite the small displacement. For an exactly stationary vertical bob relative to the pivot, the string tension is \(m(g+\ddot Y)\) as long as that value is positive. A force measurement thus contains a direct drive-frequency signal even before transverse oscillation develops. Combining an angle sensor with a load cell helps distinguish contact loss from growth of parametric oscillation.

A fast vertical drive can also modify the slow stability of the inverted position. A quantitative treatment requires separating a fast small angular ripple from the slow tilt and averaging its feedback, with the support acceleration and frequency satisfying the relevant ordering. The instantaneous sign of \(g+\ddot Y\) alone is insufficient to establish the stability of the inverted solution. An upright motion that briefly samples a favourable sign each cycle may still drift away over many cycles. Compare initial perturbations at several drive phases and fit the envelope over complete periods before drawing a stability conclusion.

Treat the drive itself as a degree of freedom

A prescribed rotating slot conceals the energy source behind a fixed pattern speed. Replace the motor by a free rotating turntable with moment of inertia \(I_0\), and let its angle \(\varphi\) evolve dynamically. In the horizontal frictionless problem the total kinetic energy is \(m\dot r^2/2+(I_0+mr^2)\dot\varphi^2/2\). The angle is cyclic, so the total angular momentum \(P=(I_0+mr^2)\dot\varphi\) is constant. As the bead moves out, the turntable slows. The radial equation remains \(\ddot r=r\dot\varphi^2\), but its coefficient now decreases according to the total angular momentum instead of remaining externally fixed.

Eliminate \(\dot\varphi\) from the total energy to find

\[E=m\dot r^2/2+P^2/[2(I_0+mr^2)].\]

The reduced radial potential decreases outwards and feeds radial kinetic energy, but the total energy available is finite. Differentiating the potential gives a radial acceleration \(P^2r/(I_0+mr^2)^2\), consistent with the original radial equation. One should not simply insert \(\dot\varphi=P/(I_0+mr^2)\) into the original kinetic Lagrangian before varying \(r\); the correct fixed-momentum Routh reduction subtracts \(P\dot\varphi\), reversing the sign of the eliminated rotational term. The energy and Lagrangian methods agree once their controlled quantities are distinguished.

With \(I_0=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(m=0.10\,{\rm kg}\), \(r_0=0.10\,{\rm m}\), initial angular speed \(4.0\,{\rm rad/s}\) and zero radial speed, total momentum is \(0.084\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and energy \(0.168\,{\rm J}\). At \(r=0.30\,{\rm m}\) the rotation rate has fallen to \(0.084/0.029=2.90\,{\rm rad/s}\). The remaining radial kinetic energy is \(0.168-0.084^2/(2\cdot0.029)=0.0463\,{\rm J}\), giving radial speed \(0.962\,{\rm m/s}\). Under a prescribed constant angular speed of four, the radial first integral instead gives \(\dot r=4\sqrt{r^2-r_0^2}=1.13\,{\rm m/s}\) there. The two runs started with identical positions and velocities but have different power connections.

The drive can also be modelled between those limits. Let a servo apply torque \(\tau_{\rm drive}=K_{\rm s}(\Omega_{\rm command}-\dot\varphi)\). Then \(d[(I_0+mr^2)\dot\varphi]/dt=\tau_{\rm drive}\), and the rate of increase of total mechanical energy is \(\tau_{\rm drive}\dot\varphi\). A high control gain with finite response time can approximate the prescribed-speed case over part of a trajectory, yet a rapid radial change can cause a measurable speed sag. Recording shaft torque and speed together tests the drive rather than attributing its lag to a fictitious radial friction on the bead.

Infer drive work from a closed loop of length and measured tension

The reel-pendulum experiment produces synchronised records of string force and length. Over a period in which the length returns to its original value, the work on the bob from the reel is \(-\oint T\,dl\). A graph of tension against length can enclose a signed area although the string force at each instant is perpendicular to the bob's allowed virtual angular displacement. That loop area measures energy transferred by the imposed control. If the bob returns to its full mechanical state and there are no other energy sources, any net positive reel work must go into losses. If only the string length returns, the bob's energy can increase cycle after cycle.

Suppose the reel shortens by \(20\,{\rm mm}\) during a high-tension interval averaging \(7.0\,{\rm N}\), then pays out the same length while the mean tension is \(4.5\,{\rm N}\). The net mechanical work on the bob is \(0.020(7.0-4.5)=0.050\,{\rm J}\) for that cycle. A torque sensor on a reel of changing winding radius must convert torque to tension with the actual instantaneous radius. A force signal delayed relative to the length encoder can create a spurious enclosed area, especially when tension changes rapidly near a turning point. Repeating the measurement slowly with a known hanging calibration weight provides a direct check of polarity and timing.

Derive rapid-pivot stabilisation rather than replacing gravity by its mean

The vertical-pivot equation can be written \(\ddot\theta+[g/l-(A\Omega^2/l)\cos\Omega t]\sin\theta=0\). The time average of the oscillatory coefficient is zero. Nevertheless a high-frequency pivot can alter slow stability because the small fast ripple in \(\theta\) correlates with the fast acceleration. Let \(\theta=\Theta+\xi\), where \(\Theta\) varies little in a drive period and \(\xi\) is small. Keeping the leading rapidly oscillating term gives \(\ddot\xi\simeq(A\Omega^2/l)\sin\Theta\cos\Omega t\), hence \(\xi\simeq-(A/l)\sin\Theta\cos\Omega t\). Its amplitude scales with displacement ratio \(A/l\), so large acceleration and small angular ripple can coexist.

Expand \(\sin(\Theta+\xi)\simeq\sin\Theta+\xi\cos\Theta\), insert the fast solution, and average. Terms linear in the cosine average to zero but the product of ripple and imposed acceleration gives a mean cosine square of one half. To leading order the slow equation is

\[\ddot\Theta+\frac{g}{l}\sin\Theta+\frac{A^2\Omega^2}{2l^2}\sin\Theta\cos\Theta=0.\]

It derives from an effective potential per angular inertia \(U_{\rm eff}/(ml^2)=-(g/l)\cos\Theta+[A^2\Omega^2/(4l^2)]\sin^2\Theta\), up to a constant. Linearising near \(\Theta=\pi+\eta\) gives

\[\ddot\eta+[A^2\Omega^2/(2l^2)-g/l]\eta=0.\]

The inverted state is stable within the averaged approximation if \(A^2\Omega^2>2gl\). The restoring contribution depends on squared pivot speed amplitude. Replacing effective gravity by its simple time average would miss it entirely.

Choose a rigid pendulum arm with \(l=0.20\,{\rm m}\), drive amplitude \(A=0.0040\,{\rm m}\), and \(\Omega=600\,{\rm s^{-1}}\). The small displacement ratio is \(0.020\), the pivot speed amplitude is \(2.4\,{\rm m/s}\), and \(A^2\Omega^2=5.76\,{\rm m^2/s^2}\), above \(2gl=3.92\,{\rm m^2/s^2}\). Distinguish two ways of moving an endpoint in Hamilton's principle

When a constraint itself is explicitly time dependent, the identity \(\delta g=\nabla g\cdot\delta x=0\) describes a virtual change at fixed time on the moving surface. A variational problem may instead allow the endpoint time to change. If a terminal point moves along a prescribed spacetime curve \(q_b(t)\), the endpoint variation includes \(dq_b=\dot q_b\,dt\); it is not an arbitrary tangent displacement at fixed laboratory clock. The boundary term in the on-shell action is \(p\,dq-H\,dt\). Reading these two terms separately tells which physical terminal conditions a derivation has assumed.

For a free particle of mass \(m\) moving in one dimension from the origin at time zero to \(q\) at time \(T\), the extremal action is \(S=m q^2/(2T)\). Its derivatives are \(S_q=mq/T=p\) and \(S_T=-mq^2/(2T^2)=-H\). If the terminal marker moves as \(q_b(T)=uT\), the action of the connecting extremal is \(mu^2T/2\). Its derivative along that endpoint line is \(p u-H=mu^2/2\), exactly the direct derivative. Holding the endpoint position fixed while differentiating instead gives \(-mu^2/2\). Both derivative experiments are legitimate, but the different signs demonstrate why one must state what is fixed.

Modal participation and absorber tuning

Machine dynamics

Project external forcing onto vibration modes and determine when a vibration absorber suppresses displacement at a selected frequency.

From first principles

Project a physical force onto a vibration mode

For small undamped motions a structure obeys \(M\ddot{\mathbf x}+K\mathbf x=\mathbf f(t)\) with symmetric mass and stiffness matrices. Its normal modes satisfy

\[K\boldsymbol\phi_j=\omega_j^2M\boldsymbol\phi_j.\]

Expand displacement in these mode shapes, using time-dependent coefficients \(q_j\). Premultiply the result by the transpose of one mode.

\[\mathbf x=\sum_j\boldsymbol\phi_j q_j,\]
\[\sum_j(\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf TM\boldsymbol\phi_j\,\ddot q_j+\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf TK\boldsymbol\phi_j\,q_j)=\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf T\mathbf f .\]

For distinct frequencies, symmetry makes different modes orthogonal under both matrices. The corresponding off-diagonal products vanish, leaving a separate scalar oscillator for the selected mode.

\[m_i\ddot q_i+m_i\omega_i^2q_i=Q_i(t),\]
\[m_i=\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf TM\boldsymbol\phi_i,\]
\[Q_i=\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf T\mathbf f .\]

The force projection has a direct interpretation as work per unit modal displacement. A symmetric load can have zero projection onto an antisymmetric mode despite a large total force. The displacement at a particular sensor is obtained only after adding its components from all excited modes.

\[Q_j=\phi_j^\mathsf T f\]

A mode is excited according to its overlap with the force. Always state how its mode vector and modal mass were normalised.

Derivation and applications

Determine how a point force appears in collocated modal measurements

The displacement measured at the force location can have a zero between two structural resonances. Consider an undamped linear structure with two relevant modes, distinct natural frequencies \(\omega_1<\omega_2\) and modal masses \(m_j>0\). Apply a harmonic point force of complex amplitude \(F\) at coordinate \(a\) and measure displacement at the same coordinate. For modal shape component \(\phi_j(a)\), the generalised force is \(\phi_j(a)F\), and reconstruction at the sensor supplies a second factor of that component.

\[q_j=\frac{\phi_j(a)F}{m_j(\omega_j^2-\omega^2)},\]
\[H_{aa}=\frac{x_a}{F}=\frac{A_1}{\omega_1^2-\omega^2}+\frac{A_2}{\omega_2^2-\omega^2},\]
\[A_j=\frac{\phi_j(a)^2}{m_j}.\]

The residues \(A_j\) are positive when both modes are visible. Between the two poles the first denominator is negative and the second positive. Set the sum to zero and multiply through by both denominators to find the antiresonance.

\[A_1(\omega_2^2-\omega_z^2)+A_2(\omega_1^2-\omega_z^2)=0,\]
\[\omega_z^2=\frac{A_1\omega_2^2+A_2\omega_1^2}{A_1+A_2}.\]

A zero in the measured displacement can therefore result from cancellation of two finite modal displacements. Its position also reveals their relative participation without requiring the absolute force calibration.

\[\frac{A_1}{A_2}=\frac{\omega_z^2-\omega_1^2}{\omega_2^2-\omega_z^2}.\]

Separated force and response locations can give signed residues and change the interlacing. With damping a finite minimum commonly replaces the exact zero, so using its frequency as an exact antiresonance is an approximation.

Worked example

Predict the dip of a two-mode stage

For \(\omega_1=10.0\) rad/s, \(\omega_2=20.0\) rad/s and \(A_1=2A_2\), the squared zero frequency is \((2\times400+100)/3=300\) s⁻², hence \(\omega_z=17.3\) rad/s. At that frequency the first contribution is \(2A_2/(100-300)=-0.0100A_2\) and the second is

\[A_2/(400-300)=+0.0100A_2.\]

The sensor is stationary while both modes respond.

Recover motion from mode amplitudes

Knowing a structure’s natural frequencies does not yet tell you which frequencies a particular actuator will excite. For a linear undamped system write \(M\ddot x+Kx=f(t)\) and let \(\phi_j\) be a real mode shape solving \(K\phi_j=\omega_j^2M\phi_j\). Assume a complete set of independent modes and expand the displacement \(x=\sum_j\phi_j q_j\). Multiplying the equation by \(\phi_i^T\) uses mass and stiffness orthogonality to remove other modal coordinates.

\[m_i\ddot q_i+k_iq_i=\phi_i^Tf(t),\]
\[m_i=\phi_i^TM\phi_i,\]
\[k_i=\omega_i^2m_i .\]

Multiplying a mode shape by a constant changes the numerical modal mass and generalised force, while the reconstructed physical motion remains invariant. Thus a tabulated mode shape needs its normalisation if it is used to compute a physical response. A forcing pattern whose projection vanishes excites none of that mode from rest, even if the excitation frequency equals its natural frequency.

Select a mode by choosing the actuator pattern

Suppose two identical masses 2 kg each have mode shapes \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\), with frequencies 10 and 20 rad/s. Both modal masses are 4 kg. A 12 N harmonic force on the first mass projects 12 N onto each mode in this normalisation. At a forcing frequency of 5 rad/s the undamped steady modal amplitudes are

\[12/[4(10^2-5^2)]=0.0400\,\mathrm m,\]
\[12/[4(20^2-5^2)]=0.00800\,\mathrm m.\]

Recombination gives first and second physical amplitudes 0.0480 and 0.0320 m.

Now distribute the same total force as 6 N on each mass in phase. The first projection stays 12 N and the second becomes \(6-6=0\). The masses now both move 0.0400 m. Relocating or phasing actuators can therefore change observed vibration without changing any mass, spring or natural frequency.

Cause the primary displacement to vanish

Attach an auxiliary mass \(m_a\) by spring \(k_a\) to a primary mass \(m_p\) grounded by spring \(k_p\). A harmonic force of amplitude \(F\) acts on the primary mass. Seek displacement amplitudes \(X_p,X_a\). The absorber equation is

\[-k_aX_p+(k_a-m_a\omega^2)X_a=0.\]

At \(\omega^2=k_a/m_a\) this enforces \(X_p=0\). The primary equation then gives

\[X_a=-F/k_a.\]

The absorber’s spring force balances the excitation while the primary mass is stationary in this ideal steady response.

For \(m_a=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\), a measured forcing frequency of 20 Hz requires

\[k_a=m_a(2\pi20)^2=7896\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

An 8 N forcing amplitude gives absorber travel amplitude 1.01 mm. The two coupled resonances bracket this tuned antiresonance; startup or a change of shaft speed can pass through them. Damping limits the resonant peaks but generally prevents the exact cancellation obtained from these undamped equations.

At the undamped tuning frequency the absorber equation forces the primary displacement to zero. The full coupled system still has two distinct natural frequencies. With mass-normalised modes and the force and displacement at coordinate 1, the receptance is

\[\frac{X_1}{F_1}=\sum_j\frac{\phi_{1j}^2}{\omega_j^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta_j\omega_j\omega}.\]

OneSimulation obtains the eigenpairs from the mass-scaled stiffness. The eigenfrequencies are 1.36 and 1.86 Hz. Between these frequencies the two undamped real contributions have opposite signs. import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from onesim.numerics.linear.matrices.dense_linear_algebra import symmetric_eigensystem

mass = np.array([5., 0.5])  # kg, primary and absorber
support, spring = 500., 50.  # N/m, support and connecting spring
stiffness = np.array([[support+spring, -spring], [-spring, spring]])
scale = np.diag(1/np.sqrt(mass))
omega2, shapes = symmetric_eigensystem(scale @ stiffness @ scale)
modes = scale @ shapes
residues = modes[0]**2      # applied force and measured motion both at mass 1
tuned = np.sqrt(spring/mass[1])
assert abs(np.sum(residues/(omega2 - tuned**2))) < 1e-12
assert np.isclose(np.sum(residues/omega2), 1/support)

frequency = np.linspace(1.0, 2.3, 600)  # Hz

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4), constrained_layout=True)

Project a spatially distributed load onto a flexible member

A beam or string has motion distributed along its length. Its modal forcing is determined by a spatial integral, analogous to the vector dot product for discrete coordinates. For a taut uniform string of length \(L\), linear density \(\mu\) and constant tension \(H\), small transverse displacement \(w(x,t)\) obeys \(\mu w_{tt}-H w_{xx}=p(x,t)\). The tension term follows from the difference in vertical tension components on a short segment. Assume both ends fixed in displacement and neglect bending stiffness, damping and the change of tension caused by stretching. The modes are \(\phi_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)\), with frequencies \(\omega_n=(n\pi/L)\sqrt{H/\mu}\).

Expand \(w=\sum\phi_n q_n\), multiply by \(\phi_j\) and integrate over the length. The mass integral is

\[m_j=\mu\int_0^L\phi_j^2dx=\mu L/2.\]

Integration by parts of the tension term gives the modal stiffness \(k_j=m_j\omega_j^2\), with the fixed-end boundary terms vanishing. The modal input becomes

\[Q_j=\int_0^Lp\phi_jdx.\]

Under a uniform harmonic load amplitude \(p_0\),

\[Q_j=\frac{p_0L}{j\pi}[1-(-1)^j].\]

Even modes have zero generalised force because their positive and negative portions cancel exactly. A point load at \(x_0\) instead yields

\[Q_j=F\phi_j(x_0).\]

Moving an actuator or changing a load footprint can thus suppress a resonance by symmetry without changing the material, tension or natural frequency.

\[0.50[(2\pi5)^2-(2\pi2)^2]=414.5\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

Its modal amplitude is 1.23 mm.

The third mode has \(Q_3=0.170\,\mathrm N\), \(f_3=15\) Hz and amplitude \(0.170/\{0.50[(2\pi15)^2-(2\pi2)^2]\}=0.0390\,\mathrm{mm}\). At the midpoint \(\phi_1=+1\) while \(\phi_3=-1\), so the two-term prediction is approximately 1.19 mm. The next odd mode contributes with the opposite sign again, but is far stiffer at this drive frequency. The spatial convergence pattern can be estimated before adding many terms. For odd mode number \(n\) well above the forcing frequency, the uniform-load projection decreases as \(1/n\) while modal stiffness increases as \(n^2\). Each displacement contribution therefore decreases roughly as \(1/n^3\). A point actuator at midspan instead gives modal projections of essentially alternating constant magnitude for odd modes, producing only a \(1/n^2\) displacement decay. Hence a truncation that accurately predicts the response to a distributed flow load can be less accurate for an impulsive local tap. Strain or slope predictions may require still more terms because a spatial derivative introduces an additional factor of mode number. Choose the retained modes for the actual load and observed quantity.

The uniform cable problem admits an independent harmonic calculation without modal truncation. Set \(w(x,t)=W(x)\cos\Omega t\). The governing equation gives

\[W''+\kappa^2 W=-p_0/H,\]
\[\kappa=\Omega\sqrt{\mu/H}.\]

Symmetry about midspan and fixed ends select \(W(x)=[p_0/(\mu\Omega^2)]\{\cos[\kappa(x-L/2)]/\cos(\kappa L/2)-1\}\), provided the denominator is nonzero. The modal and spatial calculations thus furnish independent checks in the static, off-resonant and resonant limits.

How a longitudinal pulse reflects from a change in rod section

Machine dynamics

A tap on one end of a rod reaches a receiver after a finite delay. Two rods joined end to end can even return a pulse before the receiver sees a far-end reflection. Derive propagation speed and distinguish the displacement amplitude from the energy carried by a pulse.

From the cut force to a travelling disturbance

Measure the small axial displacement \(u(x,t)\) from a stress-free reference. In a slender homogeneous rod of area A, tensile force is

\[N=EA u_x.\]

A slice of length dx and mass \(\rho A\,dx\) accelerates because its right and left forces differ by \(N_x dx\). For constant properties, cancelling A gives

\[\rho A u_{tt}=(EAu_x)_x,\]
\[u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},\]
\[c=\sqrt{E/\rho}.\]

Either \(u=f(x-ct)\) or \(u=g(x+ct)\) satisfies this equation by differentiation. Curves of constant phase move right or left with speed c. Long-wavelength assumptions suppress lateral inertia and radial modes. Rod area cancels from wave speed but remains in the force carried at given particle velocity. For a right-moving pulse \(v=u_t=-cu_x\), so tensile force \(N=-Zv\), with mechanical impedance \(Z=\rho Ac=EA/c\). The sign reverses for a left-moving wave.

Match velocity and force at a bonded junction

Let amplitudes I, R and T denote incident, reflected and transmitted particle velocity at the junction. A massless, perfectly bonded interface requires equal velocities and transmitted force. Apply the preceding sign convention separately to each travelling direction. The two equations determine two unknowns.

\[I+R=T,\]
\[-Z_1 I+Z_1 R=-Z_2 T,\]
\[{R\over I}={Z_1-Z_2\over Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[{T\over I}={2Z_1\over Z_1+Z_2}.\]

The instantaneous power transported to the right is \(-Nv=Zv^2\) for a single right-moving wave. For the same pulse shape the reflected energy fraction is \((R/I)^2\) and the transmitted fraction is \((Z_2/Z_1)(T/I)^2\). They sum to one. A transmitted velocity amplitude exceeding the incident amplitude can thus be compatible with conservation of energy.

A stepped rod and a pulse-echo observation

Join two aluminium rods of areas 80 and 160 mm², \(E=70\) GPa and \(\rho=2700\) kg/m³. Both have \(c=5092\) m/s, but the second impedance is twice the first. An incident velocity of 0.30 m/s produces a reflected −0.10 m/s and a transmitted 0.20 m/s. The reflected pulse carries one ninth of the energy. Reversing the experiment makes the reflection positive and the transmitted velocity 0.40 m/s for the same incident amplitude. The two energy fractions are unchanged on reversal.

A receiver at the tap detects a reflected pulse 0.40 ms after launch, before any far-end reflection contaminates it. The return path covers the distance twice, so the inferred junction is \(c(0.00040)/2=1.02\) m away. Measure pulse velocity rather than strain when comparing the signs to the plot. Why the frequencies of a bending beam are not equally spaced

Machine dynamics

A distributed beam can bend into infinitely many shapes. Derive the relation between wavelength and oscillation frequency, then compare the measured response of a beam with the mode family predicted by its supports.

Combine transverse force, moment and curvature

Take a straight uniform beam with small transverse deflection \(w(x,t)\), density \(\rho\), area A and second moment of area I about the bending axis. Euler–Bernoulli kinematics keeps cross-sections perpendicular to the centreline. The bending resistance is proportional to curvature \(EI w_{xx}\). For a load-free part of the beam the gradient of moment supplies shear and the gradient of shear accelerates the mass. With restoring forces opposing deflection, the resulting equation is

\[\rho A\,w_{tt}+EI\,w_{xxxx}=0 .\]

The fourth spatial derivative calls for four boundary conditions. At a simple support the displacement vanishes and the bending moment is zero, hence

\[w=w_{xx}=0.\]

A clamp instead fixes displacement and slope. Interchanging a pin and a clamp changes the allowed shape family even if the span and material remain fixed.

Obtain the simply supported series

Try \(w=W\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)\). Two spatial derivatives bring down \(-k^2\), and four give \(+k^4\). Substitution balances the inertia and stiffness terms. The conditions at x=0 hold for every k in this sine family. At x=L the sine must vanish, yielding

\[\omega^2={EI\over\rho A}k^4,\]
\[k_n={n\pi\over L},\]
\[f_n={n^2\pi\over 2L^2}\sqrt{EI\over\rho A}.\]

A long travelling sinusoid has phase velocity \(\omega/k=k\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)}\). The group velocity \(d\omega/dk\) is twice that value in this model. A localized pulse contains many wavelengths, which can therefore separate as it propagates. Predictions at wavelengths near the thickness require accounting for rotary inertia and shear deformation.

Choose a displacement field that respects the support

Write \(w(x,t)=q(t)\phi(s)\) with \(s=x/L\), taking \(q\) as tip displacement. Choose the shape from a uniform cantilever under a static transverse tip force. The bending moment varies linearly from root to tip. Integrating curvature twice with \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\), then normalizing at \(s=1\), gives

\[\phi=s^2(3-s)/2.\]

The shape satisfies both essential clamp conditions. Its free-tip shear need not match the exact unloaded vibration mode. The energy formulation can use such admissible trial shapes.

Background · Rayleigh quotient
\[R(u)=u^\mathsf TK u/(u^\mathsf TM u)\]

For symmetric stiffness and positive mass this estimates squared angular frequency. A kinematically admissible displacement is required for a useful structural estimate.

Compute effective stiffness and mass from the same field

The strain energy density of a slender beam in bending is \(\tfrac12 EI w_{xx}^2\). Its translational kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\rho A w_t^2\) per unit length. Changing variables from x to s introduces different powers of L into the two integrals. Include a point mass M at the tip, neglecting its rotary inertia.

\[U={EI q^2\over 2L^3}\int_0^1(\phi'')^2\,ds,\]
\[K={\dot q^2\over2}\left[\rho AL\int_0^1\phi^2\,ds+M\right].\]

Since \(\phi''=3(1-s)\), the stiffness integral is 3. Squaring \(s^2(3-s)/2\) and integrating \( (9s^4-6s^5+s^6)/4\) gives \(33/140\). The Euler–Lagrange equation for q is consequently a single oscillator equation with

\[\omega_R^2={3EI/L^3\over(33/140)\rho AL+M}.\]

For M=0 this gives the dimensionless coefficient \(\sqrt{140/11}=3.5675\). Solving the exact unloaded cantilever condition \(\cosh\beta\cos\beta=-1\) gives

\[\beta_1^2=3.5160.\]

The estimate is high by about 1.5%. The Rayleigh minimum principle explains that direction of error for conservative systems with admissible trial shapes. Restricting motion to a single shape restricts the set over which the energy quotient can attain its minimum.

Weak forms and finite elements

Numerical partial differential equations

Derive a variational statement by integration by parts, assemble element matrices for a one-dimensional rod and explain what the Galerkin solution minimises.

From first principles

Obtain the weak diffusion problem by moving one derivative

For a stationary diffusion problem \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla u)=f\), suppose temperature is prescribed on part of the boundary and the outward flux \(q_n=-k\nabla u\cdot\mathbf n\) on the remainder. A classical second derivative may not exist across a material interface. Instead multiply the conservative equation by a smooth test function \(v\) that vanishes where \(u\) is prescribed. The divergence theorem moves one derivative from the flux onto \(v\):

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega=\int_\Omega fv\,d\Omega-\int_{\Gamma_N}vq_n\,d\Gamma .\]

Approximate \(u\) with continuous element shape functions \(N_j\) that are differentiable inside each element. Choose each allowable shape function as the test function. Linearity then yields element contributions

\[K_{ij}^{(e)}=\int_e k\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j\,d\Omega,\]
\[f_i^{(e)}=\int_e fN_i\,d\Omega .\]

Neighbouring elements contribute to the same global unknown at a shared node. Adding their contributions enforces weak equilibrium. The flux sign in the boundary term follows from an outward-positive definition; switching to prescribed inward heat supply reverses that sign in the load vector.

Background · Weak form
\[\int k\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dV=\int fv\,dV+\text{boundary terms}\]

Integration by parts lowers the derivative requirement. Essential and natural boundary data enter the discrete system differently.

Derivation and applications

How a Robin boundary enters both the stiffness matrix and the load

A convecting boundary couples an unknown surface temperature to an ambient temperature. Unlike a prescribed heat source it contributes to both sides of the assembled equations. Consider steady diffusion \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)=s\) in a two-dimensional region of unit thickness, with \(k>0\). On a straight boundary segment impose the empirical film law \(-k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n=h(T-T_\infty)\) using the outward normal. Multiply the field equation by a test function \(v\) vanishing on prescribed-temperature boundaries, integrate, and apply the divergence theorem:

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla v\cdot\nabla T\,dA-\int_{\partial\Omega}v k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=\int_\Omega vs\,dA .\]

Substitution on the film boundary \(\Gamma\) places the unknown surface term on the left and the ambient contribution on the right:

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla v\cdot\nabla T\,dA+\int_\Gamma hvT\,ds=\int_\Omega vs\,dA+\int_\Gamma hvT_\infty\,ds\]

with any other natural boundary terms added separately. To assemble one linear edge of length \(\ell\), use edge functions \(N_1=1-\xi\), \(N_2=\xi\), \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Insert the nodal interpolation for \(T\) and choose each \(N_i\) as test function. Constant \(h,T_\infty\) give

\[K_\Gamma=\frac{h\ell}{6}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad f_\Gamma=\frac{h\ell T_\infty}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The off-diagonal term is present because both nodes influence temperature across the same edge. In the limit \(h=0\) the boundary is insulated. A very large film coefficient constrains the temperature toward ambient but can introduce a large stiffness contrast.

Worked example

Assemble a convecting edge by hand

Use a one-metre out-of-plane thickness, \(\ell=0.300\) m, \(h=20.0\) W/(m² K) and \(T_\infty=290\) K. Then \(K_\Gamma\) has diagonals \(2.00\) W/K and off-diagonals \(1.00\) W/K. The load is \((870,870)\) W. At nodal temperatures \(300\) and \(310\) K the film residual \(K_\Gamma T-f_\Gamma\) is \((40,50)\) W. The sum \(90\) W agrees with \(h\ell[(300+310)/2-290]\).

Second application

Set both temperatures to \(290\) K, the ambient value. Each row has sum \(3.00\) W/K, so every residual is \(3(290)-870=0\). If the code includes the ambient load but forgets the matrix, it predicts \(1740\) W of heating at thermal equilibrium. This constant-field patch test detects sign errors and omission of the surface matrix independently of the volume mesh.

Reduce the differentiability required by equilibrium

A rod is fixed at \(x=0\), pushed by a distributed load \(f\) and by an end force \(t\) at \(x=L\). Axial displacement \(u\) satisfies \(-(EAu')'=f\), \(u(0)=0\) and \(EAu'(L)=t\). The product \(EA\) is positive. A trial profile assembled from linear pieces has jumps in its derivative, so differentiating it twice pointwise is unsuitable. Instead multiply the strong equation by a test displacement \(v\) vanishing at the fixed end and integrate by parts once.

The resulting equation is

\[\int_0^L EA\,u'v'\,dx=\int_0^L fv\,dx+t v(L).\]

Only first derivatives are required. The fixed displacement is imposed directly on trial and test spaces; the end traction appears in the load functional. This clarifies why prescribing both force and displacement independently at the same end can overconstrain a second-order problem. The test functions represent allowable virtual displacements, and the integral identity expresses equality of internal and external virtual work.

Background · Why can the derivative jump while the displacement stays continuous?

At a bonded interface with no concentrated force, displacement and axial force are continuous. If \(EA\) jumps, the strain \(u'\) can jump inversely even though the product \(EAu'\) is continuous. For a continuous piecewise linear displacement, the weak first derivative is the ordinary piecewise constant slope. The squared-gradient energy is therefore finite.

A displacement jump is different. Replacing a jump of size \(d\) by a straight ramp over width \(\delta\) costs strain energy \(EA d^2/(2\delta)\) for constant positive stiffness, which diverges as the ramp shrinks. A standard conforming displacement space encodes this distinction. If the mechanics really allow slip, the interface needs its own model and a space which permits the two traces to differ.

Assemble one element and connect it to its neighbour

On an element from \(x_i\) to \(x_{i+1}\) of length \(h\), use shape functions \(N_1=1-s,\ N_2=s\) with \(s=(x-x_i)/h\). Their physical derivatives are \(-1/h,\ 1/h\). For constant \(EA\), integrating products \(EA N'_aN'_b\) gives the matrix \((EA/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A constant distributed load contributes \(fh(1,1)^T/2\). Summing element contributions for a shared global node enforces balance there without prescribing the internal traction manually.

Take \(L=1,\ EA=1,\ f=2,\ t=0\) in consistent units and use two equal elements. After eliminating the fixed displacement, the stiffness matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&-2\\-2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and the load is \((1,0.5)^T\). Solving gives

\[u(0.5)=0.75,\ u(1)=1.\]

The exact solution is \(2x-x^2\) and agrees at both nodes. Inside the first element at \(x=0.25\), the linear interpolant gives 0.375, whereas the exact value is 0.4375. Nodal agreement can coexist with a substantial interior error.

Assemble a physical triangle from a reference element

A line element has one length scale. A triangle can stretch, rotate and shear, so the element area alone cannot determine its diffusion matrix. Start with the reference vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\) and the linear shape functions \(1-\xi-\eta,\xi,\eta\). Send the reference triangle to a physical cell with vertices \(P_0,P_1,P_2\) by \(x=P_0+J(\xi,\eta)^T\), where the columns of \(J\) are \(P_1-P_0\) and \(P_2-P_0\). For a nondegenerate triangle, the chain rule and area transformation give

\[\nabla_x N_i=J^{-T}\nabla_{\xi,\eta}\widehat N_i,\]
\[dx\,dy=|\det J|\,d\xi\,d\eta .\]

To see why the inverse transpose occurs, take an arbitrary displacement \(d\widehat x\). Both gradients must describe the same change in a shape function. Thus \((\nabla_xN_i)^TJ\,d\widehat x=(\nabla\widehat N_i)^Td\widehat x\) for every such displacement. For unit isotropic conductivity, the transformed gradients are constant and the physical stiffness entries reduce to

\[K_{ij}=|T|\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j,\]

with \(|T|=|\det J|/2\). A constant cell load \(f\) contributes \(f|T|/3\) at each vertex because each linear shape function has mean \(1/3\).

A sheared cell and an exact affine-field check

Take \(P_0=(0,0), P_1=(2,0), P_2=(1,1)\). The columns of \(J\) are \((2,0)\) and \((1,1)\), so the cell area is \(1\). Applying \(J^{-T}\) to the three reference gradients gives \((-1/2,-1/2),(1/2,-1/2),(0,1)\). Their dot products and the load \(f=3\) yield

\[K=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0&-1/2\\0&1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&-1/2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad b=(1,1,1)^T.\]

Every row sums to zero because a constant field has zero gradient. For a more discriminating test use \(u=x+2y\), with vertex values \(U=(0,2,3)^T\). Weighting the three gradients by these values gives \((1,2)\). The physical integral of its squared gradient equals \(1(1^2+2^2)=5\), and direct multiplication gives \(U^TKU=5\). A transposed mapping error can pass the constant test and fail this second one.

Identify the energy that is best approximated

Let \(a(u,v)=\int EA\,u'v'\) and denote the external work by \(\ell(v)\). The discrete solution \(u_h\) satisfies \(a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)\) for every test function from its finite-dimensional space. The continuous solution satisfies the same equation for every such test, so \(a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\). This Galerkin orthogonality means the error is perpendicular to the trial space in the energy inner product. For any other admissible discrete \(w_h\), write \(u-w_h=(u-u_h)+(u_h-w_h)\) and expand its squared energy norm. The cross term vanishes and \(\|u-w_h\|_a^2=\|u-u_h\|_a^2+\|u_h-w_h\|_a^2\).

Thus the conforming Galerkin solution is the best energy approximation in its space for this symmetric linear problem and exact integration. The statement concerns a specific norm and model. It does not guarantee pointwise optimal stresses or carry unchanged into nonsymmetric transport problems. In the rod example the computed strain is constant on each element, replacing a linearly varying exact strain. Refinement improves this derivative field even when a table of nodal displacements already looks exact.

Recover a support reaction

The reaction at a prescribed-displacement node is hidden if one inspects only the reduced interior system. Assemble all element internal forces \(Ku_h\) before discarding constrained rows, then subtract the full external load vector. The entries at free degrees of freedom should be near zero; the entry at a prescribed degree of freedom gives the reaction in the chosen sign convention.

In the uniformly loaded rod, the first element strain is

\[(0.75-0)/0.5=1.5.\]

Its internal nodal force at the left is \(-1.5\), while the first node receives an external distributed-load share of \(+0.5\). Their difference \(-2\) is the support reaction. It balances the total applied load \(2\cdot1=2\). Looking only at the derivative in the first element would have reported magnitude 1.5, missing that load share. A reaction from assembled balance and a stress recovered by pointwise differentiation are related but distinct quantities on a finite mesh.

Obtain actual norm errors from the Galerkin property

For \(-u''=f\) on the unit interval with homogeneous Dirichlet values, the elementary energy inner product is \(a(u,v)=\int u'v'\). Let \(I_hu\) be the continuous piecewise-linear interpolant of a sufficiently smooth exact solution at mesh nodes. On each element its slope is the average of \(u'\). Every linear finite-element test function \(v_h\) has constant derivative on that element. Consequently

\[\int_E(u-I_hu)'v_h'=v_h'[u-I_hu]_{\partial E}=0.\]

Summing proves global energy orthogonality.

The Galerkin solution satisfies the same orthogonality, and positive definiteness gives uniqueness. Thus \(u_h=I_hu\) in this one-dimensional, constant-coefficient problem with exactly integrated load and matching boundary values. Nodal errors vanish, although the field and derivative between nodes can still be inaccurate. Variable coefficients inside an element generally destroy the simple telescoping argument because the factor multiplying \(v_h'\) is no longer constant. The result also should not be transferred to multidimensional triangulations without proof.

Compare nodal, field and flux accuracy for one solution

Choose \(u=x(1-x)\), so \(f=2\). On an element starting at \(a\) write \(s=x-a\in[0,h]\). Subtract the straight interpolant from the parabola to get \(u-u_h=s(h-s)\), vanishing at both nodes and attaining \(h^2/4\) at the element centre. Its derivative is \(h-2s\); the exact and numerical slopes agree at the midpoint but differ by magnitude \(h\) at each endpoint taken from that element. Testing only the midpoint slope or the nodal values would entirely conceal these other discrepancies.

Compute the squared energy error on this element:

\[\int_0^h(h-2s)^2ds=h^3/3.\]

With \(1/h\) equal elements, the global energy error is \(h/\sqrt3\). Similarly the element squared field error is \(\int_0^hs^2(h-s)^2ds=h^5/30\), giving global \(L^2\) error \(h^2/\sqrt{30}\). For \(h=1/4\), the two errors are approximately 0.14434 and 0.01141; the maximum field error is 0.015625. Halving the mesh width halves the energy error but divides the field error by four. All these measurements concern the very same numerical solution whose nodal error is exactly zero.

For a physical diffusion flux with constant conductivity one, flux error equals minus derivative error. A cell-averaged flux test is also special here: the numerical slope equals the exact average slope on each element for the same reason used in the proof. To verify local flux resolution, compare at more than a midpoint or a cell average and retain the measure used for convergence. A reported order is a statement about an observable and a norm as well as a finite-element space.

These formulas also identify a misleading manufactured test: if an automated comparison stores only nodal differences, the estimated order is undefined because both coarse and fine errors vanish. Adding independently evaluated quadrature points inside elements exposes the field error immediately. To check a flux implementation, use a quadrature rule capable of resolving the squared linear derivative error; a one-point midpoint rule would again report zero despite the nonzero exact energy norm.

Numerical partial differential equations

A finite-element solution can satisfy the assembled equations to machine precision while still missing curvature between nodes. Integrate the residual of the computed field by parts on each element and use the resulting cell and interface terms to decide where to spend the next degrees of freedom.

Ask what the discrete solution does to an unresolved test function

Consider \(-u''=f\) on a unit interval with zero Dirichlet data. Use continuous piecewise-linear finite elements with exact integration on a possibly nonuniform mesh. The weak solution satisfies \(a(u,v)=\int u'v'=\int fv\) for every test function vanishing at the endpoints. For \(e=u-u_h\), subtract the computed field to obtain

\[a(e,v)=R(v)=\int fv-\int u_h'v'.\]

The algebraic system makes \(R(v_h)=0\) for all functions in the chosen finite-element space. Tests with more local structure expose what is missing.

\[R(v)=\sum_K\int_K (f+u_h'')v\,dx+\sum_{i\ {\rm interior}} J_i v(x_i),\]
\[J_i=u_h'(x_i^+)-u_h'(x_i^-).\]

The sign follows by adding the right boundary term of the left cell and the left boundary term of the right cell. On a linear cell \(u_h''=0\) classically. Its piecewise constant gradient jumps at interior nodes. In diffusion with varying coefficient k the corresponding jump is in \(k u_h'\), since continuity of normal flux is the conservation condition. A discontinuous material coefficient can cause an exact gradient jump without any flux imbalance.

Scale the cell and node terms before marking

Subtract the finite-element interpolant or a local quasi-interpolant from v inside the residual. Galerkin orthogonality permits this subtraction and bounds the remaining test magnitude by local length times its gradient. This leads to a computable residual indicator. One common one-dimensional choice with a symmetric edge length \(h_i\) equal here to the smaller neighbouring cell is

\[\eta_K^2=h_K^2\int_K f^2\,dx+{1\over2}\sum_{i\in\partial K\ {\rm interior}}h_i J_i^2,\]
\[\eta^2=\sum_K\eta_K^2.\]

Sharing the node contribution prevents double counting. Under the usual mesh and coefficient assumptions such estimates bound the energy error up to constants; their absolute numbers need not equal the measured error. Data oscillation can matter if the source is approximated or oscillates within a cell. To mark by bulk contribution, sort \(\eta_K^2\) in descending order and take cells until their sum exceeds a chosen fraction of the global squared estimator. Bisect marked cells, transfer the coefficient data, assemble and solve again. Reusing the old gradient jumps after refinement would skip the effect of the new trial space.

\[1(0.25)+9(0.25)=2.5.\]

The middle value is therefore 0.625. The slopes are 1.25 and −1.25, giving \(J=-2.5\). Since \(h=0.5\), the two cell terms are 0.125 and 10.125. Each side receives \((1/2)(0.5)(2.5)^2=1.5625\) from the node. The right cell contributes \(11.6875/(1.6875+11.6875)=87.38\%\) of the squared indicator. A 50% bulk mark selects only that cell.

Why two finite-element wave solvers propagate the same mode at different speeds

Numerical partial differential equations

Build the element mass matrix by integrating shape functions and track the effect of diagonal lumping on the wave spectrum.

From a continuum wave to nodal masses

For \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\), multiply by a test function and integrate by parts. On a periodic line the boundary contributions cancel. A piecewise-linear element of width \(h\) uses shape functions \(1-\xi,\xi\), with \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Integrating products of shape functions gives

\[M_e=(h/6)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right).\]

Integrating derivative products gives

\[K_e=(c^2/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right).\]

Row-sum lumping replaces each mass row by its sum on the corresponding diagonal, \(h/2\). This preserves the mass associated with a uniform velocity but changes the response to a velocity that varies within an element.

Background · Numerical dispersion
\[c_{\rm ph,h}=\omega_h(k)/k\]

A discretised wave can retain its amplitude and still propagate at the wrong speed. Compare phase and group errors over the actual resolved wavelengths.

Diagonalise the assembled periodic problem

A periodic nodal mode has \(u_j=e^{ij\theta}\). Each neighbour contributes \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) relative to node \(j\). Thus \(K\) multiplies by \(2c^2(1-\cos\theta)/h\), consistent \(M\) by \(h(2+\cos\theta)/3\), and lumped \(M\) by \(h\). Dividing stiffness by mass gives the discrete squared frequency. Relative to the exact frequency \(c\theta/h\),

\[\frac{\omega_L}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta},\]
\[\frac{\omega_C}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2+\cos\theta}}.\]

Both tend to one for long waves; the leading quadratic errors have opposite signs. This is a spatial effect, present even with exact time integration.

Two wavelengths in one mesh

At four cells per wavelength \(\theta=\pi/2\). The lumped ratio is \(2\sqrt2/\pi\approx0.9003\); the consistent ratio is about 1.1027. One wave solver lags by roughly ten per cent and the other leads by roughly ten per cent. At eight cells per wavelength the ratios move much closer to one, but repeated propagation over many wavelengths can still accumulate visible phase error.

At the shortest grid mode \(\theta=\pi\), the consistent frequency is \(\sqrt{12}c/h\) and the lumped frequency \(2c/h\). A central-difference time integrator stable for \(h_t\omega\le2\) therefore requires a smaller step for the consistent mass. Accuracy at one wavelength and stability at the grid scale are different design criteria.

Action density, stress waves and the boundary that reflects them

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A chain of coupled masses becomes an elastic field when the wavelength is long compared with the spacing. Derive the field equation and its boundary traction from the same variation, follow energy and stress across a junction, and decide whether a measured pulse is limited by material waves or by numerical dispersion.

Pass from a discrete rod to an action density

Let equal masses of spacing \(a\) move longitudinally by \(u_j(t)\), with nearest-neighbour spring constant \(k_s\) and mass \(m_s\) per site. Their Lagrangian is \(\sum_j[m_s\dot u_j^2/2-k_s(u_{j+1}-u_j)^2/2]\). For variations smooth on the spacing, \((u_{j+1}-u_j)/a\to u_x\) and \(\sum_j a\,f_j\to\int f(x)\,dx\). Hold the line density \(\rho A=m_s/a\) and axial rigidity \(EA=k_s a\) fixed. The continuum Lagrangian density per unit reference length is

\[{\cal L}=\rho A\,u_t^2/2-EA\,u_x^2/2,\]

with \(A\) area, \(\rho\) volume density and \(E\) Young modulus. Both terms have units of joules per metre. This is a slender-rod model with small strain, primarily uniaxial stress and wavelengths long compared with the cross-sectional dimensions.

Vary the field in the action over \([0,L]\times[t_1,t_2]\). Integration of \(\rho A\,u_t\delta u_t\) by parts in time, with fixed endpoint fields, produces \(-\rho A\,u_{tt}\delta u\). Integration of \(-EAu_x\delta u_x\) by parts in space produces \(+\partial_x(EAu_x)\delta u\) and a remaining term \(-[EAu_x\delta u]_0^L\). Thus the interior equation is \(\rho A u_{tt}=\partial_x(EAu_x)\), and the end force conjugate to displacement is axial force \(N=EAu_x\). Adding external work \(F_L(t)\delta u(L)\) at the right face yields

\[N(L)=F_L.\]

A prescribed displacement instead imposes \(\delta u(L)=0\), leaving its reaction as an output. Keeping this boundary term prevents prescribing displacement and independent traction simultaneously at the same end of a second-order rod.

Determine the energy flux and characteristic impedance

Assume time-independent coefficients. Multiply the field equation by \(u_t\) and rearrange to obtain

\[\partial_t[\rho A u_t^2/2+EAu_x^2/2]+\partial_x[-EAu_xu_t]=0.\]

The energy flux is \(-Nu_t\), with positive sign denoting energy moving towards increasing \(x\). The minus sign follows the convention of positive tensile \(N\): positive mechanical power entering through a right face is \(Nu_t\), which is inward with respect to the rod on its left. This identity controls energy transport across both smooth material changes and sharp joints.

For a homogeneous right-moving wave \(u=F(x-ct)\), \(c=\sqrt{E/\rho}\), particle velocity \(v=u_t=-cF'\) and axial force \(N=EAF'=-Zv\), where \(Z=\rho A c=EA/c\). A left-moving wave has \(N=+Zv\). The impedance is force divided by particle velocity and has units \({\rm kg/s}\). If one instead uses stress, impedance is \(\rho c\) per unit area. Doubling the area of the same material keeps wave speed unchanged but doubles the force–velocity impedance. A transit-time measurement and a reflection measurement therefore provide independent material or geometry information.

For a rod with \(E=70\,{\rm GPa}\), \(\rho=2700\,{\rm kg/m^3}\) and \(A=1.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\), \(c\approx5092\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(Z\approx1375\,{\rm kg/s}\). A right-going compressive pulse with particle speed \(0.020\,{\rm m/s}\) carries axial force \(-27.5\,{\rm N}\), compressive stress \(0.275\,{\rm MPa}\), and instantaneous power \(Zv^2=0.550\,{\rm W}\) across a section. A rectangular approximation lasting \(100\,\mu{\rm s}\) carries about \(55\,\mu{\rm J}\) and occupies \(0.509\,{\rm m}\). The pulse speed is kilometres per second; the material particles move only millimetres per second. Treating these as the same velocity would spoil force, impulse and energy estimates.

Match a bonded interface and distinguish amplitude from power

At a perfect massless bond displacement and axial force are continuous. For an incident right-going velocity amplitude \(v_i\) in rod 1, let \(v_r\) be reflected left-going velocity and \(v_t\) transmitted right-going velocity. Velocity continuity gives

\[v_i+v_r=v_t.\]

Force continuity gives

\[-Z_1v_i+Z_1v_r=-Z_2v_t.\]

Thus

\[r_v=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[t_v=\frac{2Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[{\cal R}=r_v^2,\]
\[{\cal T}=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1}t_v^2=\frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1+Z_2)^2}.\]

The power fractions add to one for real positive impedances. The reflected force coefficient has opposite sign to the reflected velocity coefficient because the wave direction reverses. At a free end \(Z_2\to0\), reflected velocity equals incident velocity, so end velocity doubles while total force vanishes. At a rigid end \(Z_2\to\infty\), velocity reverses and force doubles. The sign a strain gauge reports can therefore differ from that of a velocity-sensitive optical measurement at the same reflection.

A step from the worked rod into one of the same material with twice the cross-sectional area gives \(Z_2=2Z_1\), \(r_v=-1/3\), \(t_v=2/3\), and power fractions \(1/9\) and \(8/9\). A reduced transmitted velocity amplitude does not imply that most energy is reflected. If the interface includes a concentrated mass \(M_b\), force jumps instead obey \(N_2-N_1=M_b\dot v_b\) with signs determined by forces exerted on that mass from either side. At high frequency the reaction of that mass can mimic a much stiffer termination. Frequency-dependent reflection is then an expected boundary effect, rather than evidence that the bulk modulus varies with frequency.

Connect continuum modes to their lattice predecessor

For a periodic chain the exact discrete equation is

\[m_s\ddot u_j=k_s(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}).\]

Substitute \(u_j=U e^{i(kja-\omega t)}\) to obtain

\[\omega=2\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.\]

In the first positive Brillouin zone, \(\omega/(ck)=\sin(ka/2)/(ka/2)\) and group velocity \(d\omega/dk=c\cos(ka/2)\), where \(c=a\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\). The continuum rod retains only the leading low-\(ka\) term. A wave with eight sites per wavelength has phase speed about \(0.9745c\) and group speed \(0.9239c\). A narrowband packet's peak can arrive later than the continuum prediction even when a single crest is timed fairly accurately.

A finite-difference code approximating the continuum rod can produce the same spatial dispersion with its mesh spacing in place of a physical lattice spacing. Distinguish them experimentally or numerically by changing the computational mesh while holding the physical structure fixed. Lattice effects persist as the numerical grid is refined; artificial spatial dispersion diminishes. A separate time-step study is needed if a time integrator also disperses the wave. Comparing both peak arrival and phase at two frequencies tests more of the dynamics than fitting a single transit time.

Derive transverse beam waves from curvature energy

A slender beam bending laterally is governed by a different elastic energy from the axial rod. Let \(w(x,t)\) denote small transverse deflection of its neutral axis. Under the Euler–Bernoulli assumption that cross sections remain plane and normal to that axis, the axial strain a distance \(z\) from the neutral axis is approximately \(-z w_{xx}\). Integrating the local elastic energy \(E\epsilon_{xx}^2/2\) across the section gives \(EI_Aw_{xx}^2/2\) per unit length, where \(I_A=\int z^2\,dA\) is a geometrical second moment of area, with units \({\rm m^4}\). It is distinct from a mass moment of inertia. The kinetic contribution uses line density \(\rho A\) as before.

Varying \({\cal L}=\rho A w_t^2/2-EI_Aw_{xx}^2/2\) requires integrating twice by parts in space. With constant coefficients the interior variation gives

\[\rho A w_{tt}+EI_A w_{xxxx}=0.\]

The boundary terms are \(-[EI_Aw_{xx}\delta w_x]+[(EI_Aw_{xx})_x\delta w]\). Thus slope variations pair with bending moments and displacement variations with shear forces, with signs set by the chosen face orientation. A clamped end prescribes both deflection and slope; a free end sets moment and shear to zero. A simply supported end prescribes deflection and zero bending moment. Using only one boundary condition per end, as for the axial rod, leaves the fourth-order beam problem underdetermined.

A sinusoid \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\) gives

\[\omega=\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}\,k^2.\]

Phase speed grows proportionally to wavenumber and group speed is twice phase speed in this model. A narrow transverse pulse in a long beam consequently spreads even in a perfectly homogeneous non-dissipative continuum. By contrast the axial rod was nondispersive in its long-wavelength continuum approximation. At high enough frequency the simple bending model omits rotary inertia, shear deformation and eventually three-dimensional elastic behaviour. Extrapolating its unbounded speed to arbitrary \(k\) would use the slender, low-frequency approximation far beyond its derivation.

For a simply supported beam with length \(L\), the sine modes satisfy both end conditions and give

\[\omega_n=(n\pi/L)^2\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}.\]

For a rectangular section of width \(w_b\) and thickness \(d\) in the bending direction, \(I_A=w_bd^3/12\) and \(A=w_bd\). Doubling thickness doubles all the natural angular frequencies at fixed \(L,E,\rho\), whereas doubling width alone leaves the ideal frequencies unchanged. The stiffness increases and so does the inertia. A beam with \(L=0.50\,{\rm m}\), \(d=5.0\,{\rm mm}\) and the earlier aluminium properties has \(\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}\approx7.35\,{\rm m^2/s}\), giving fundamental \(46.2\,{\rm Hz}\). The second bending mode is near four times that frequency, a clear difference from the approximately harmonic axial mode sequence.

Match a force pulse to a support spring and dashpot

A right termination exerts force on the rod through a dashpot with mechanical impedance \(Z_L\). For a real positive dashpot impedance the reflected velocity fraction is \(r=(Z-Z_L)/(Z+Z_L)\). Taking \(Z_L=Z\) gives zero reflection at the termination even though energy leaves a finite rod: the dashpot dissipates the arriving power. With \(Z_L=3Z\) one obtains a reflected velocity of \(-1/2\) of the incident value, a reflected energy fraction one quarter and absorbed power fraction three quarters. A free or rigid end also reflects energy, but it absorbs no net power in the ideal limit. A measured weak reflection alone is therefore not proof of weak damping inside the rod.

For a pure end spring of stiffness \(k_L\) attached to a rigid support, the velocity impedance is \(Z_L=k_L/(i\omega)\) under \(e^{i\omega t}\). It is imaginary, so the reflection coefficient has unit magnitude at every frequency and a frequency-dependent phase. At low frequency the spring impedance is large and the end looks nearly fixed. At high frequency its impedance is small and the end looks nearly free within the lumped approximation. An impulsive signal contains many frequencies and can return with a changed waveform even without any transmitted or dissipated energy. A support combining a resistor-like dashpot and a spring gives both amplitude loss and phase variation.

A measurement with strain gauges at two positions can separate forward and backward waves if their path delays are resolved. In a narrow frequency band the two records are linear combinations of advancing and returning phase factors. If the gauge spacing corresponds to an integer number of half wavelengths, these combinations become linearly dependent and the separation is ill-conditioned. Change the spacing, use a broadband pulse, or combine a velocity measurement with a strain measurement at one station so that the two characteristic directions enter with opposite signs. The relation \(v=(v_++v_-)\), \(N=Z(-v_++v_-)\) then gives a direct local decomposition.

Derive sound propagation from mass and momentum conservation

The displacement-action argument has a fluid analogue. Take an initially uniform fluid of density \(\rho_0\) and pressure \(p_0\), moving little from equilibrium in one spatial dimension. Write small perturbations \(\rho',p',v\). Linearised mass conservation is

\[\partial_t\rho'+\rho_0\partial_x v=0.\]

Momentum balance for a short fluid parcel is \(\rho_0\partial_tv=-\partial_xp'\). If compression over an acoustic cycle is approximately isentropic, \(p'=c_s^2\rho'\) with \(c_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\). Differentiate the first equation in time, eliminate the velocity derivative with momentum balance, and obtain \(\partial_t^2p'=c_s^2\partial_x^2p'\).

The thermodynamic derivative matters because pressure can respond differently when compression is slow enough for heat exchange. For a perfect gas with ratio of specific heats \(\gamma\), isentropic perturbations give

\[c_s^2=\gamma p_0/\rho_0.\]

Taking an isothermal derivative would give \(p_0/\rho_0\) instead. For \(p_0=101\,{\rm kPa}\), \(\rho_0=1.20\,{\rm kg/m^3}\), and \(\gamma=1.40\), the isentropic speed is about \(343\,{\rm m/s}\), while the isothermal calculation gives \(290\,{\rm m/s}\). A measured transit time can discriminate between these constitutive conditions. Wall heat exchange in a very narrow tube can alter which approximation is appropriate over a chosen frequency band.

For a right-moving sound wave \(p'=\rho_0c_s v\), with the pressure positive in compression. This differs in sign from the tensile-positive axial force used for the rod, so match physical force conventions before borrowing formulas across the two examples. The acoustic power per area is \(p'v\). For sinusoidal pressure with rms amplitude \(1.0\,{\rm Pa}\) in the stated gas, the mean intensity is

\[p_{\rm rms}^2/(\rho_0 c_s)=2.43\times10^{-3}\,{\rm W/m^2}.\]

A peak amplitude of \(1.0\,{\rm Pa}\) would yield half this number. Writing rms or peak explicitly prevents a routine factor-of-two error when comparing a microphone measurement with an energy flux.

Determine whether a measured transit reveals a modulus or a geometry

Suppose two axially loaded slender rods have measured wave speeds agreeing within their uncertainties. That alone determines only the ratio \(E/\rho\) under the slender-rod assumptions. Measuring force–velocity impedance \(Z=\rho A c\) and cross section as well gives \(\rho=Z/(Ac)\) and \(E=Zc/A\). With \(Z=1375\,{\rm kg/s}\), \(c=5092\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(A=1.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\), the inversion gives

\[\rho=2700\,{\rm kg/m^3},\]
\[E=70.0\,{\rm GPa}.\]

If area were overestimated by five percent, both inferred quantities would be underestimated by almost five percent while their ratio remained correct.

Now examine a thick elastic solid under a narrow compressional disturbance with wavelengths short compared with its transverse dimensions. Lateral deformation can be constrained by surrounding material. In an isotropic three-dimensional continuum the longitudinal plane-wave modulus is \(\lambda+2\mu_s\), where \(\lambda,\mu_s\) are Lamé coefficients, rather than the uniaxial-stress Young modulus of a slender rod. The shear-wave speed satisfies \(c_T^2=\mu_s/\rho\) and longitudinal speed \(c_L^2=(\lambda+2\mu_s)/\rho\). Measuring both, together with density, can recover the two independent elastic moduli. Using only one longitudinal speed to infer Young modulus without specifying the deformation geometry can give a physically incorrect value.

For \(\rho=2500\,{\rm kg/m^3}\), \(c_L=5000\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(c_T=3000\,{\rm m/s}\), these relations give

\[\mu_s=22.5\,{\rm GPa},\]
\[\lambda=17.5\,{\rm GPa}.\]

The implied Young modulus \(E=\mu_s(3\lambda+2\mu_s)/(\lambda+\mu_s)=54.8\,{\rm GPa}\), while \(\rho c_L^2=62.5\,{\rm GPa}\). Both numbers represent material stiffness, but they enter different mechanical boundary conditions. A long thin specimen of the same material should have an axial speed closer to \(\sqrt{E/\rho}=4680\,{\rm m/s}\). Comparing the bulk and rod experiments tests the constitutive and geometrical assumptions behind the measured speed.

Derive orthogonality when a rod changes material along its length

For a rod with positive varying density \(\rho(x)\), area \(A(x)\) and modulus \(E(x)\), a separated free oscillation \(u(x,t)=\phi(x)\cos\omega t\) satisfies

\[-[EA\phi']'=\omega^2\rho A\phi.\]

Suppose two modes \(\phi_n,\phi_m\) satisfy the same homogeneous fixed or free boundary conditions and the correct continuity of displacement and axial force across every perfectly bonded interface. Multiply the first equation by \(\phi_m\), the second by \(\phi_n\), subtract and integrate. Interior terms produced by integration by parts cancel pairwise, including the interface flux terms, and the endpoint terms vanish. Thus

\[(\omega_n^2-\omega_m^2)\int\rho A\phi_n\phi_m\,dx=0.\]

Mode shapes with distinct frequencies are orthogonal under the mass-weighted inner product, not generally under a simple average of displacements measured at uniformly spaced stations. An initial velocity distribution \(v_0(x)\) projects onto mode \(n\) with coefficient \(\int\rho A v_0\phi_n\,dx/\int\rho A\phi_n^2\,dx\). In a rod with a heavy section, the same imposed velocity there stores more energy and carries more modal weight. An experiment interpreting raw camera amplitudes without line-density weighting can misassign energy among modes. Normalising each measured mode shape to unit maximum does not fix the mass weighting needed for energies.

Multiplying one eigenvalue equation by its own shape gives

\[\omega_n^2=\int EA(\phi_n')^2dx/\int\rho A\phi_n^2dx.\]

For an admissible trial function satisfying prescribed displacement conditions, the same quotient bounds the fundamental squared frequency from above when rigid translations are excluded. Consider a uniform fixed–free rod and trial \(\phi=x/L\). The numerator is \(EA/L\) and denominator \(\rho A L/3\), giving \(\omega_{\rm trial}=\sqrt3\,c/L\). The exact fundamental is \(\pi c/(2L)\), so this one-parameter shape overestimates frequency by about \(10.3\%\). It need not satisfy the free-end traction condition itself because that is a natural condition of the variational minimiser; it does need to satisfy the essential fixed-end displacement.

For a free–free rod the constant displacement mode has zero frequency. Using an arbitrary positive trial function without removing its mass-weighted mean can drive a quotient towards zero by adding a large constant. To estimate the first deforming mode impose orthogonality to the rigid translation, \(\int\rho A\phi\,dx=0\). This has an immediate mechanical interpretation: the deformation mode should not translate the centre of mass of an isolated rod. The null mode and its mass-weighted constraint reappear in numerical eigenproblems built from discretised stiffness and mass matrices.

Track work and impulse in a one-dimensional pulse experiment

A finite displacement pulse provides a useful warning about integrating strain and velocity records. A right-going strain pulse of one sign can carry net momentum \(\int\rho A v\,dx\). Because \(v=-cu_x\), its integrated material momentum is \(-\rho Ac[u(+\infty)-u(-\infty)]\) at a fixed time. A localised displacement bump that returns to the same displacement on both sides has zero net ordinary material momentum, even though its energy is positive and travels to the right. Its strain and particle-velocity records contain positive and negative parts. A unipolar velocity pulse leaves behind a displacement step, produced by a source that has supplied net impulse.

For a right-moving rectangular velocity pulse of amplitude \(0.020\,{\rm m/s}\), duration \(100\,\mu{\rm s}\) in the worked rod, the local displacement jump measured in time is \(2.0\,\mu{\rm m}\). Its impulse magnitude across a section is \(Z v\Delta t=2.75\times10^{-3}\,{\rm N\,s}\), while its transmitted energy is

\[Zv^2\Delta t=55.0\,\mu{\rm J}.\]

A second equal pulse of opposite velocity sign launched later cancels the net displacement and material momentum but doubles the transmitted energy. Thus integrating a signed velocity signal and integrating its square distinguish different properties of the same experimental record.

Recover a jump condition at an interface with a point mass

For a concentrated mass \(M_b\) joining two rods include \(M_bu_t(x_b)^2/2\) as a separate contribution to the Lagrangian. Its time variation contributes \(-M_bu_{tt}\delta u\) at the joint. The left and right rod boundary terms contribute respectively \(-N_1\delta u\) and \(+N_2\delta u\). Requiring stationarity for an independently varying shared displacement gives exactly \(N_2-N_1=M_bu_{tt}\). This derivation identifies both the sign and the constitutive assumption of a perfectly bonded, nonslipping joint.

For identical rods and a sinusoidal drive at angular frequency \(\omega\), the reflected velocity coefficient becomes \(r=-i\omega M_b/(2Z+i\omega M_b)\) under the stated phasor convention. At \(\omega M_b=2Z\) it reflects half the power and transmits half. The mass itself absorbs no cycle-averaged energy, though it stores kinetic energy during a cycle. For \(Z=100\,{\rm kg/s}\) and \(M_b=0.10\,{\rm kg}\), the equal-power frequency is about \(318\,{\rm Hz}\). A real joint that loses appreciable power at that frequency needs a damping or radiation term in addition to its measured mass.
