From first principles

Derive the force and moment conditions for a free body

Choose one body and mark the external loads and support reactions crossing its boundary. In an inertial frame, Newton's linear momentum balance for each mass element sums to a balance for the whole body. Internal action–reaction pairs cancel. Taking moments about a fixed point \(O\) similarly eliminates internal couples for ordinary continuum forces, leaving

\[\sum\mathbf F_{\rm ext}=\frac{d\mathbf P}{dt},\]
\[\sum\mathbf M_{O,\rm ext}=\frac{d\mathbf H_O}{dt}.\]

Static equilibrium requires both momenta to remain constant at zero. For planar loading these vector statements reduce to three scalar conditions. A distributed downward load \(q(x)\) contributes force and moment by summing its infinitesimal forces with their physical lever arms:

\[\sum F_x=0,\]
\[\sum F_y=0,\]
\[\sum M_O=0,\]
\[W=\int q(x)\,dx,\]
\[W\bar x=\int xq(x)\,dx .\]

The equations predict reactions only within the chosen support model. A roller can exert a normal force but no end moment. An upward-only contact cannot supply a negative normal reaction; such a mathematical solution calls for a different contact configuration.

Derivation and applications

Locate the resultant of a linearly varying distributed load

A beam support carries the effect of the complete loading, including where each small portion acts. Consider a rigid horizontal span of length \(L\) with a pin at its left end and a roller at the right. Let \(q_a\) and \(q_b\) be downward line loads at the ends, varying linearly in between. Use upward support forces \(R_A,R_B\). Ignore the beam weight unless already included in \(q\). The aim is to replace the distribution by a single force that reproduces both force and moment.

At position \(x\), a slice of length \(dx\) contributes \(q(x)dx\) downward. Its moment magnitude about the left end has the additional factor \(x\). Integrating first the force and then the moment gives

\[q(x)=q_a+(q_b-q_a)x/L,\]
\[W=\int_0^L q(x)dx=\tfrac12L(q_a+q_b).\]
\[M_A^{\rm load}=\int_0^L xq(x)dx=L^2\left[\frac{q_a}{2}+\frac{q_b-q_a}{3}\right]=\frac{L^2}{6}(q_a+2q_b).\]

If \(W\ne0\), its point of application is \(\bar x=M_A^{\rm load}/W\). Moment equilibrium about the pin and vertical force equilibrium now give the support readings:

\[R_B=\frac{L}{6}(q_a+2q_b),\]
\[R_A=\frac{L}{6}(2q_a+q_b).\]

For equal end loads the resultant is at midspan. For a triangle increasing from zero it acts at two thirds of the span from the zero end. These formulas predict external reactions; replacing the distribution by a single force would change the internal shear and moment between the supports.

Worked example

Predict the support readings of a loaded shelf

A \(2.40\) m shelf carries \(q_a=100\) N/m on the left increasing to \(q_b=400\) N/m on the right. The resultant is \(600\) N and its moment about the left end is \(864\) N m, placing it at \(1.44\) m. The right support reads \(360\) N and the left \(240\) N. Their sum and their moment reproduce both integrals independently.

Second application

Reconstruct a linear distribution from the scale readings

The same span now gives \(R_A=320\) N and \(R_B=280\) N. Adding the readings gives

\[q_a+q_b=2(600)/2.40=500\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

Subtraction gives

\[q_a-q_b=6(320-280)/2.40=100\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

Thus \(q_a=300\) and \(q_b=200\) N/m. If a point load could also be present, these two support readings alone would not uniquely determine the load shape.

Problem sheet 1 · Infer a truss area without mistaking it for a height change

Read the model setup and preparatory problems

Choose the body before writing equations

A platform scale under a shelf can read more than the weight placed directly above it. The measurement depends on all the other supports and the locations of all the applied forces. To predict it, imagine removing the shelf from its surroundings. Replace each lost contact by the force or couple that the surroundings could exert. Internal forces between parts of the isolated body cancel in pairs. The choice of boundary determines which forces are internal.

In a planar model a smooth roller on a horizontal track supplies one vertical reaction. A pin supplies two force components and admits rotation. A clamp supplies both force components and a reaction moment. A cable pulls along its length. A roller simply resting on a track can push, but a negative calculated upward reaction would require a hold-down or a change of contact. These restrictions are part of the physical model, even before a single number is inserted.

Sum forces and moments

A rigid body at rest in a plane obeys three independent scalar equilibrium equations. Choose an arbitrary point \(O\) as the moment centre. Taking upward force and counterclockwise moment as positive gives

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_i F_{x,i} &= 0,\\ \sum_i F_{y,i} &= 0,\\ \sum_i M_{O,i} &= 0. \end{aligned}\]

A force applied at \((x,y)\) relative to \(O\) contributes \(xF_y-yF_x\) to the last sum. Taking moments about a pin eliminates its two unknown forces at once. A specified couple has the same signed moment about every origin and contributes zero resultant force.

For a vertical load with downward intensity \(q(x)\) in N/m, a strip of width \(dx\) contributes a downward force of magnitude \(q(x)\,dx\). Integrate over the loaded span to obtain the force magnitude and its clockwise moment magnitude about the left end. An equivalent point load must reproduce both.

\[\begin{aligned} W&=\int q(x)\,dx,\\ W\bar x&=\int x\,q(x)\,dx,\\ \bar x&=\frac{\int x\,q(x)\,dx}{\int q(x)\,dx}. \end{aligned}\]

This replacement preserves the external reactions. When finding an internal force at a cut, integrate only the load actually lying on the chosen side.

Background · Why can the moment balance be taken about any point?

Keep all forces on the same free body, including support reactions. Let \(\mathbf a\) point from the first moment centre \(O\) to the second \(P\). Every force lever arm changes by the same vector. Applied couples have no lever arm and carry their original signs.

\[\mathbf M_P=\sum_i(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf a)\times\mathbf F_i+\sum_j\mathbf C_j=\mathbf M_O-\mathbf a\times\sum_i\mathbf F_i.\]

Once force balance holds, the two moment sums coincide. This makes a second choice of moment centre a diagnostic. A missing 100 N vertical load creates a 50 N m difference between sums taken about points 0.5 m apart horizontally. Repeating the moment sum about points displaced parallel to that missing force would hide the discrepancy.

Weigh a platform with a triangular load

A 4.0 m platform is pinned at its left end A and supported by a roller at B at its right end. Bulk material forms a triangular pile, loading the platform with intensity rising linearly from zero at A to 3.0 kN/m at B. A 2.0 kN crate sits 1.0 m from A. Platform self-weight has already been tared off. Write \(q(x)=q_Bx/L\). Integration gives

\[W=q_BL/2=6.0\,\mathrm{kN},\]
\[\bar x=(q_BL^2/3)/W=2L/3=2.667\,\mathrm{m}.\]

The resultant is closer to the heavily loaded end.

\[4B_y-6(2.667)-2(1)=0,\]
\[A_y+B_y=8.0\ {\rm kN}.\]

The right reaction is 4.50 kN and the left is 3.50 kN. There is no applied horizontal force, so \(A_x=0\). Check independently about B: the clockwise effect of the left reaction is \(3.50(4)=14.0\) kN·m and the two weights give the opposite moment \(6(1.333)+2(3)=14.0\) kN·m. Replacing the triangle by a uniform load of equal total weight would pass a force check and fail this moment check.

Predict the next scale reading

Move the crate 0.50 m to the right. Its force is unchanged, but its moment about A increases by 1.0 kN·m. The right reaction therefore rises by \(1.0/4.0=0.25\) kN and the left falls by the same amount. In practice this relation lets two load cells measure both weight and the position of a moving load. It assumes a stable support arrangement with known moment arms. Three vertical supports under a rigid platform introduce an extra unknown. Their individual readings then require information about deflections and support stiffness, which equilibrium alone cannot supply.

Find the load position from the reactions

An inverse problem uses the same equilibrium equations. A 2.0 m simply supported rail carries a single unknown load and sensors read 900 N at the left and 300 N at the right after taring the empty rail. The total applied downward load is 1200 N. Taking moments about the left support gives its position as \(300(2.0)/1200=0.50\,\mathrm m\). Repeating this reasoning for a distributed load gives the position of its resultant but cannot reconstruct its detailed distribution.

Determine when an assumed contact can exist

A support symbol on paper often hides a condition on the direction of its reaction. A dry foot resting on a horizontal floor can push on the floor, but it cannot pull the floor upwards. A stationary contact can transmit a range of tangential forces up to its friction limit. These statements convert an ordinary equilibrium problem into one with inequalities. First derive the forces required to maintain the proposed configuration. Then ask whether the contact can supply them. Assigning the maximum friction before writing equilibrium would generally give the wrong answer for a stationary body.

Consider a rectangular machine base supported on two narrow feet a distance \(b\) apart. Its centre of mass lies midway between them, its weight is \(W\), and a horizontal force \(P\) acts to the right at height \(h\). Write upward contact forces \(N_L,N_R\). Neglect the thickness of the contact patches and consider only motion in the vertical plane. Vertical equilibrium requires

\[N_L+N_R=W.\]

Taking moments about the midpoint between feet eliminates both unknown friction forces, which act along the floor. The right foot gains normal load as the left one loses it:

\[(N_R-N_L)b/2=Ph,\]
\[N_L=W/2-Ph/b,\]
\[N_R=W/2+Ph/b .\]

Both feet stay in contact only while \(P\le Wb/(2h)\). If both have the same coefficient \(\mu\), their combined available friction is

\[\mu(N_L+N_R)=\mu W.\]

Thus impending tip and impending slip are distinct limiting states. The precise sharing of tangential load between the feet depends on their compliance; these three rigid-body equations alone need not fix it.

Decide which motion starts first on a test stand

A movable test stand weighs 1800 N. Its feet are 0.80 m apart and the technician pushes horizontally on a handle 1.20 m above the floor. Suppose the measured static friction coefficient is 0.45. At a push of 400 N, the load shifts by \(400(1.20)/0.80=600\,\mathrm N\) to the right. The left reaction is 300 N and the right reaction is 1500 N. The available 810 N of total friction exceeds 400 N; a static configuration is possible. A calculation assigning each foot a friction force of 405 N would nevertheless violate the individual 135 N friction limit on the lightly loaded left foot.

Increase the push gradually. The tip threshold is \(1800(0.80)/[2(1.20)]=600\,\mathrm N\), below the 810 N sliding threshold. At 600 N the right foot alone carries 1800 N; the required friction ratio there is \(600/1800=0.333\), within the measured friction limit. Now relocate the handle to 0.60 m. The calculated tip threshold doubles to 1200 N, so the base begins sliding at 810 N while both feet still carry weight. The dividing height follows directly from equating both thresholds,

\[h=b/(2\mu)=0.889\,\mathrm m.\]

Lowering the handle changes the rotational demand without changing the total horizontal force that floor friction can transmit.
