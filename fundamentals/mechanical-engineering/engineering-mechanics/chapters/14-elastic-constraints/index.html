How a lateral restraint changes a measured modulus

A short compression test can report a larger stiffness than a tensile coupon cut from the same material. Derive the difference from the strains that the fixture permits.

Separate changes of volume and changes of shape

For small strain, uniaxial tension \(\sigma\) gives longitudinal strain \(\sigma/E\) and transverse strain \(-\nu\sigma/E\). These are experimental definitions of Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio for an isotropic material. In a linear solid the three normal coordinate loading cases can be superposed. Under tensile stresses positive, this gives

\[E\epsilon_x=\sigma_x-\nu(\sigma_y+\sigma_z),\]
\[E\epsilon_y=\sigma_y-\nu(\sigma_x+\sigma_z),\]
\[E\epsilon_z=\sigma_z-\nu(\sigma_x+\sigma_y).\]

Add the three equations. The sum of the small normal strains is the relative volume change because the product \((1+\epsilon_x)(1+\epsilon_y)(1+\epsilon_z)\) retains only first-order terms. Under compressive pressure \(p\) all three normal stresses are \(-p\). Hence

\[\frac{\Delta V}{V}=-\frac{3(1-2\nu)}{E}p=-\frac{p}{K},\]
\[K=\frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)} .\]

Large bulk stiffness can coexist with moderate shear stiffness. Near \(\nu=0.5\), changing volume becomes difficult, while changing shape can remain easy. This distinction matters when interpreting a pad compressed between smooth plates and the same pad enclosed within a rigid sleeve.

Derive the stiffness in a close-fitting sleeve

Apply axial stress \(\sigma_z\) to a specimen with both lateral strains constrained to zero. Assume uniform deformation away from the ends and identical conditions in \(x\) and \(y\). Symmetry gives

\[\sigma_x=\sigma_y=s.\]

The first strain law becomes \(0=(1-\nu)s-\nu\sigma_z\), so the sleeve must supply a lateral stress even though no lateral load was specified at the start.

\[s=\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\sigma_z,\]
\[\frac{\sigma_z}{\epsilon_z}=\frac{E(1-\nu)}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}=M .\]

The constraint modulus \(M\) increases rapidly near incompressibility. It is the ratio of a particular stress to a particular strain in this test. The material constants in the original three-dimensional law continue to apply in both fixtures. A supposedly free compression test with strong friction on the platens may fall between ideal free and fully confined behaviour.

Worked example · recover the sleeve reaction

A linearly elastic sample has \(E=30\) MPa and \(\nu=0.40\). Its thickness is 20 mm and the axial stress is −0.30 MPa. With free sidewalls the axial strain is −0.0100 and the contraction is 0.200 mm. Inside the ideal rigid sleeve, \(M=64.29\) MPa, giving contraction \(20(0.30/64.29)=0.0933\) mm. Each lateral stress is −0.200 MPa. Substitution into the original \(x\)-strain equation gives zero, a direct test of the force that was inferred from compatibility.

Worked example · infer a bulk property independently

Compress the same material hydrostatically by 0.15 MPa. The measured constants predict \(K=50.0\) MPa and a volume loss of 0.300%. A 40 cm³ specimen would lose 0.120 cm³. Using its sleeve modulus in this calculation would underestimate the volume change. In hydrostatic compression all three strains are equal. In the sleeve only one strain can be nonzero. A constitutive test must specify what is imposed on the other faces and what the measurement records.

Start with zero Poisson ratio. Explain why the two compression curves coincide. Raise the ratio to 0.45 and read the force required for equal movement in the two tests. Which part of the constitutive law causes the slopes to separate?

Tutorial · identify a Poisson effect from two tests

An axial test gives \(E=24\) MPa. A hydrostatic test gives \(K=40\) MPa. Infer the Poisson ratio and predict the axial stress required for 0.5% compression in an ideal sleeve. Which additional measurement would reveal slipping or a clearance in the sleeve?

Reconstruct the constraint

Solving \(E=3K(1-2\nu)\) gives \(\nu=0.40\). The constraint modulus is

\[24(0.60)/(1.40\times0.20)=51.43\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

For \(\epsilon_z=-0.005\) the stress is −0.257 MPa. The ideal lateral strain is zero everywhere in the uniform test section. A transverse displacement measurement along the midsection tests this condition directly. Near the ends the same reading can include fixture effects.
