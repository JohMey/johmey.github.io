From first principles

Obtain shear and moment from the equilibrium of a beam slice

Let a planar beam carry a downward line load \(q(x)\). Use positive sagging moment \(M\) and the shear sign convention that a positive shear raises \(M\) with increasing \(x\). Isolate the material between two closely spaced vertical cuts. The shear on the right face differs from the left by its spatial change, while the distributed load has resultant \(q(x)dx\) to first order. Vertical force balance gives

\[V(x+dx)-V(x)=-q(x)\,dx,\]
\[dV/dx=-q(x).\]

Moment balance about the left cut includes the shear acting at a lever arm \(dx\). The distributed load on this short interval produces only a term proportional to \(dx^2\). Dividing by \(dx\) and taking the limit leaves

\[dM/dx=V,\]
\[M(b)-M(a)=\int_a^b V(x)\,dx.\]

Support reactions set integration constants. A downward concentrated force creates a downward jump in shear, found by integrating across its position; an applied couple causes a jump in moment. Between concentrated actions, a zero of shear identifies a stationary value of moment, which may locate the most highly stressed fibre.

Derivation and applications

Construct a bending-moment diagram directly from load integrals

The maximum bending stress depends on moment, not necessarily on where the distributed loading is largest. Consider a simply supported span \(L\) carrying a triangular line load \(q(x)=q_0x/L\) directed down. Define positive sagging moment \(M\) and positive shear \(V=dM/dx\). The pin and roller transmit no end couples. Find reactions before cutting the span: the resultant load \(q_0L/2\) acts at \(2L/3\) from the left. Its moment balance therefore gives

\[R_A=q_0L/6,\]
\[R_B=q_0L/3,\]
\[R_A+R_B=q_0L/2.\]

A cut at \(x\) isolates the left support and the load between \(0\) and \(x\). Vertical equilibrium gives the support reaction minus the integral of all line load up to that cut:

\[V(x)=R_A-\int_0^x q(s)\,ds=\frac{q_0L}{6}-\frac{q_0x^2}{2L}.\]

Every small line force contributes a moment arm \(x-s\). Equivalently integrate \(V\) and fix the integration constant by \(M(0)=0\). Both routes give

\[M(x)=R_Ax-\int_0^x(x-s)q(s)\,ds=\frac{q_0Lx}{6}-\frac{q_0x^3}{6L}.\]

At the far support \(M(L)=0\), a check on the moment boundary condition rather than an additional reaction. Since the shear decreases monotonically across the span, its unique zero locates the maximum bending moment. A point couple, if present, would require a local jump in moment and could invalidate this smooth-maximum argument.

Worked example

Find the location of the largest moment

On a \(3.00\) m span let \(q_0=6.00\) kN/m. The end reactions are \(3.00\) and \(6.00\) kN. Setting \(V=0\) gives

\[x=L/\sqrt3=1.732\,\mathrm{m}.\]

Substitution yields \(M_{\max}=q_0 L^2/(9\sqrt3)=3.46\) kN m. The location lies left of the resultant at \(2.00\) m. Replacing the triangle by a single force there would give correct reactions but an incorrect peak moment.

Second application

Reconstruct a distributed load from section readings

A second unloaded-at-the-left triangular span has \(L=2.00\) m and a measured midpoint moment \(M(L/2)=0.750\) kN m. Substituting \(x=L/2\) into the exact diagram gives

\[M=q_0 L^2/16.\]

Thus \(q_0=3.00\) kN/m. The midpoint shear should be \(q_0 L/24=0.250\) kN. Moment and shear measured independently test whether the assumed linear load profile is plausible.

Read a section cut as an equilibrium problem

A transfer rail carries a trolley and the distributed weight of its own structure. Its supports may be strong enough while the rail still bends excessively at an interior section. Start with reactions for the entire beam. Next isolate the part from its left end to a cut at coordinate \(x\). The portion discarded from the drawing exerts an axial force, a shear force and a bending moment on the cut. For a horizontal beam with vertical loading the axial force is zero.

Use downward distributed load \(q(x)>0\), take \(M>0\) for sagging and define positive shear \(V\) by the resultant upward external load on the left-hand part. Thus a positive \(V\) corresponds to a downward internal shear on its right cut face. Choose the signs once and test a cut just to the right of the left support. With a positive left reaction the first value of the shear diagram must be positive.

Build a diagram from the loading

Take an element between \(x\) and \(x+dx\). Its external load increases the downward resultant by \(q\,dx\), so \(V(x+dx)-V(x)=-q\,dx\). Moment balance retains the term \(V\,dx\); the distributed load times its half-element arm is second order in \(dx\). Passing to the limit gives

\[\frac{dV}{dx}=-q(x),\]
\[\frac{dM}{dx}=V(x).\]

A point load produces a finite jump in \(V\) and a change of slope in \(M\). The bending moment is continuous there unless an applied concentrated couple acts. On an unloaded interval shear is constant and moment is a straight line. Under uniform loading the moment is parabolic. At a simple end support without an applied end couple the bending moment is zero. These four facts are often enough to find a sign mistake before calculating stress.

Combine a point load and distributed weight

Let a simply supported 6.0 m rail carry uniform weight 2.0 kN/m over its full span and a 6.0 kN trolley 2.0 m from the left end. The total downward load is 18 kN. Moment balance about the left gives \(6R_B=12(3)+6(2)=48\) kN·m, so \(R_B=8\) kN and \(R_A=10\) kN. To the left of the trolley, \(V=10-2x\) kN and \(M=10x-x^2\) kN·m for \(x\) in metres. At \(x=2\), \(M=16\) kN·m. Shear falls from 6 kN to zero on crossing the trolley.

To the right of the trolley, \(V=4-2x\) kN and \(M=10x-x^2-6(x-2)\) kN·m. Shear is negative throughout \(2<x<6\); the moment therefore falls to zero at the right support. The largest bending moment is 16 kN·m under the trolley. At the right end \(V(6^-)=-8\) kN. The upward reaction returns it to zero, another check on the construction.

Translate the moment into a fibre stress

For a symmetric homogeneous section undergoing small elastic curvature, strain varies linearly through depth. Integrating the moments of the Hookean normal stresses about the neutral axis gives

\[M=EI\kappa.\]

A fibre a distance \(c\) from that axis has stress magnitude \(Mc/I=M/Z\), where \(Z=I/c\) is the section modulus. Take a rectangular section 80 mm wide and 160 mm deep. Then \(Z=bh^2/6=3.41\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}\), giving a nominal bending stress of 46.9 MPa under the trolley. At a positive sagging moment the bottom is in tension. A local wheel-contact stress or weld stress requires its own length scale and model.

What jumps under an applied couple?

A concentrated transverse force produces a jump in shear. A concentrated moment produces a jump in the bending-moment diagram. For example, apply only a clockwise couple of 4 kN m midway along a 2 m simply supported beam. Global moment equilibrium gives a right reaction of 2 kN upwards and a left reaction of 2 kN downwards. The shear stays at −2 kN between supports. The moment thus falls from zero to −2 kN m on the left half, jumps to +2 kN m at the couple and falls to zero at the right support. The two zero end moments provide a check on the jump direction.

Derive an influence line at a fixed section

A bending-moment diagram follows a fixed loading pattern along a beam. A travelling wheel poses the converse question: how does the moment at one strain gauge vary as the load crosses the span? Take a simply supported beam of span \(L\) and a downward point load \(P\) located at \(z\) from the left support. Equilibrium gives

\[R_A=P(L-z)/L.\]

Keep the section coordinate \(a\) fixed while changing \(z\). The moment at \(a\) must be evaluated from a cut that contains the wheel only if the wheel lies to its left.

When \(z<a\), the moment is \(R_Aa-P(a-z)\); when \(z>a\), it is \(R_Aa\). Divide through by \(P\) to obtain an influence ordinate \(i_M\), whose units are length. The two linear expressions agree when the wheel crosses the gauge:

\[i_M(z)=\begin{cases}z(L-a)/L,&0\le z\le a,\\ a(L-z)/L,&a\le z\le L.\end{cases}\]

The ordinate is zero when the force is directly over a support and reaches \(a(L-a)/L\) at the gauge. For a distributed load \(w(z)\) the moment is the integral of \(w i_M\) because a short loaded length contributes \(w(z)\,dz\) of force. This is an application of superposition in a beam with fixed geometry and fixed support conditions. A support that lifts off would invalidate the same influence line.

Place a pair of unequal wheels to maximise one reading

Consider a 6 m span with a gauge at \(a=2.0\,\mathrm m\). A carriage has a front wheel carrying 8 kN and a rear wheel carrying 4 kN, a fixed 1.5 m behind it. It travels towards increasing \(z\). To maximise the moment, move both loads along the triangular influence line. The weighted sum is piecewise linear in carriage position, so its maximum occurs at a change of slope or over a level interval. The changes happen when a wheel enters the span, passes the gauge or exits. There is no need to sample many intermediate positions.

With the front wheel over the gauge the rear wheel is at 0.50 m. The ordinates are \(2(4)/6=1.333\) m and \(0.5(4)/6=0.333\) m, giving \(M=8(1.333)+4(0.333)=12.0\,\mathrm{kN\,m}\). With the rear over the gauge the front is at 3.5 m. Its ordinate is \(2(6-3.5)/6=0.833\) m, and the moment is \(4(1.333)+8(0.833)=12.0\,\mathrm{kN\,m}\) again. Between these positions the front contribution falls at \(8(2/6)\) kN for each metre moved, exactly balancing the rear contribution rising at \(4(4/6)\) kN per metre. The peak is a plateau, so several carriage positions give exactly the same design moment at this gauge.

For a narrow strain gauge on the bottom fibre of a symmetric elastic cross-section, the measured strain follows this moment history through \(\varepsilon=M y/(EI)\). Once the two wheels straddle the gauge, a flat measured strain interval is therefore physically plausible even though both wheels continue moving. A gauge near another section has a different influence line and need not show the same plateau.

Find curvature and stress in a bonded two-material beam

A homogeneous beam has its elastic neutral axis at the geometric centroid. A timber strip bonded to an aluminium cap can carry much larger normal stress on one side of their interface even though both materials have identical longitudinal strain there. Assume the interface prevents slip, each material is linear elastic, the beam is slender and no net axial force acts. With depth \(y\) measured from a common datum, compatible strain is \(\epsilon_x=-\kappa(y-y_n)\) and stress in layer \(i\) is \(E_i\epsilon_x\). The unknown neutral height \(y_n\) follows from axial force equilibrium:

\[\sum_i E_i A_i(\bar y_i-y_n)=0,\]
\[y_n=\frac{\sum_i E_iA_i\bar y_i}{\sum_i E_iA_i}.\]

Now sum moments of the layer stresses about this axis. The effective bending rigidity is \(D=\sum_i E_i[I_{i,c}+A_i(\bar y_i-y_n)^2]\), giving curvature magnitude \(M/D\). Dividing all moduli by one reference modulus gives the equivalent transformed-section construction. Taking an arithmetic mean modulus and the geometric centroid separately generally gives the wrong neutral position as well as the wrong rigidity.

As an independent worked section, bond two strips each 20 mm wide and 5 mm thick. Let the lower strip have \(E=70\) GPa and the upper strip \(E=210\) GPa. Measured from the bottom, their centroids are at 2.5 and 7.5 mm and each area is 100 mm². Thus

\[y_n=(2.5+3\times7.5)/4=6.25\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Each layer has \(I_c=208.33\,\mathrm{mm^4}\). Their distances from the neutral axis are 3.75 and 1.25 mm. The resulting rigidity is \(1.896\times10^8\,\mathrm{N\,mm^2}\). A sagging 20 N m moment gives

\[\kappa=1.055\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}.\]

The lower surface carries 46.2 MPa tension and the upper carries 83.1 MPa compression. At the interface \(y=5\) mm the strain is continuous at about \(132\) microstrain in tension, but the two normal stresses are 9.23 and 27.7 MPa. Such a longitudinal stress jump is compatible with a sound bond. Delamination would instead invalidate the assumed strain compatibility.
