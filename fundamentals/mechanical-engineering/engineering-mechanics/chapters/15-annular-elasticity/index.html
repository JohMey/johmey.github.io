Radial displacement and hoop stress in a thick ring

As a soft sleeve presses outward on a ring, the inside and outside radii move by different amounts. A cut through the thickness reveals why replacing the entire wall by one hoop stress loses information.

Let one radius displace by a small amount

Take a long annular section or a thin annular disk loaded uniformly around its circumference. Here use the disk idealisation with free faces and plane stress. Let \(a,b\) be its inner and outer radii. Axial displacement effects near contact edges are excluded. If the material point at \(r\) moves outward by \(u(r)\), a radial line length \(dr\) changes by \(du\), while a circumference \(2\pi r\) changes by \(2\pi u\). Dividing each change by the original length gives

\[\epsilon_r=u'(r),\]
\[\epsilon_\theta=u/r,\]
\[\sigma_r=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\left(u'+\nu\frac{u}{r}\right),\]
\[\sigma_\theta=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\left(\frac{u}{r}+\nu u'\right).\]

On a small annular wedge the two radial faces have different areas. The two hoop tractions also have a net radial component, since their directions differ by the angle of the wedge. Resolving radial forces and dividing by its volume leaves \(d\sigma_r/dr+(\sigma_r-\sigma_\theta)/r=0\). Inserting the material equations eliminates both stresses.

\[u''+\frac{u'}{r}-\frac{u}{r^2}=0,\]
\[u=C_1 r+C_2/r .\]

To see where the two terms come from, try \(u=r^n\). All terms scale as \(r^{n-2}\); their coefficients add to \(n^2-1\). Both \(n=1\) and \(n=-1\) are admissible because the domain excludes \(r=0\). For a solid disk the singular term would have to vanish at its centre.

Use pressure as a radial traction

The radial and hoop stresses take the forms \(A-B/r^2\) and \(A+B/r^2\). Let compressive inner contact pressure be \(p\), and the outside be free. The normal stress conditions are \(\sigma_r(a)=-p\), \(\sigma_r(b)=0\). These two equations fix both constants.

\[A=\frac{pa^2}{b^2-a^2},\]
\[B=\frac{pa^2b^2}{b^2-a^2},\]
\[u(r)=\frac{(1-\nu)Ar+(1+\nu)B/r}{E}.\]

Stress depends on geometry and the applied tractions. Displacement additionally depends on stiffness. The outer surface can therefore carry tensile hoop stress while having zero radial traction. A strain gauge aligned around that surface responds to the hoop direction.

Worked example · compare inside and outside readings

Take \(a=20,\ b=40\) mm, \(p=3.0\) MPa, \(E=70{,}000\) MPa and \(\nu=0.30\). The constants are \(A=1.00\) MPa and \(B=1600\) MPa mm². Inner hoop stress is

\[1+1600/400=5.00\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

Outer hoop stress is 2.00 MPa. The outer radial displacement is

\[40(2/70000)=0.001143\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

At the inner wall \(\epsilon_\theta=(5-0.30(-3))/70000\); multiplying by 20 mm gives 0.001686 mm. A uniform dilation fitted to the outer reading would predict the wrong opening at the inner face.

Worked example · why a thin-wall balance still gets a resultant right

Cut the same ring along a diameter. The mean hoop stress over the wall must carry the projected inner contact force. Integrating the distribution gives \(\int_a^b(A+B/r^2)dr=A(b-a)+B(1/a-1/b)=60\) N/mm of axial depth. Dividing by wall thickness gives 3.00 MPa, precisely \(pa/(b-a)\). That average underpredicts the inner peak by 40%. Global balance is satisfied in both descriptions. A local comparison requires the radial distribution.

Keep the inside pressure fixed and increase the outer radius. Track both the inner hoop stress and the wall average. Before inspecting the thin-wall end of the slider, predict which pair of stress estimates will draw closest in relative terms.

Tutorial · recover contact pressure from an outer strain

For the same ring, an outer circumferential gauge records \(50\) microstrain. Infer \(p\) and the inner hoop stress. What geometric assumption lets one gauge determine this field?

Invert the surface relation

At the free surface \(\sigma_\theta(b)=E\epsilon_\theta=3.50\) MPa. As \(\sigma_r(b)=0\), the hoop value is

\[2A=2pa^2/(b^2-a^2)=2p/3.\]

Thus \(p=5.25\) MPa and \(\sigma_\theta(a)=8.75\) MPa. Circumferentially uniform contact and known radii reduce the unknown traction to one number. A point load or an out-of-round sleeve would require more observations.
