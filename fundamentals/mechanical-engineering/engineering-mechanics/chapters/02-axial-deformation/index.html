Axial stress, strain and compatibility

Determine how much a stepped member stretches and why a restrained temperature change produces force.

Find the force carried through a section

Two shafts that support the same suspended mass can stretch by very different amounts. A load cell measures force, while a clearance specification usually limits displacement. Connect them by taking a cut normal to a straight member and replacing the removed material by its axial force \(N\). When the load is centred, changes slowly and is far from grips or abrupt changes in section, the nominal normal stress is \(\sigma=N/A\). Adopt tension as positive. Near holes or grips the mean value remains useful for equilibrium, while local stresses can differ appreciably.

If a small material line of original length \(dx\) gains length \(du\), its engineering strain is \(du/dx\). Assume small strain, a uniform temperature across each section and a linear elastic material. Elastic strain is \(\sigma/E\), where Young’s modulus \(E\) has units of stress. Free thermal expansion adds the strain \(\alpha\Delta T\). Thus an element can change length even if its axial force is zero.

Reassemble the pieces

Multiply the strain by \(dx\) and add along the member. The displacement difference between its ends is

\[u(L)-u(0)=\int_0^L\frac{N(x)}{E(x)A(x)}\,dx+\int_0^L\alpha(x)\Delta T(x)\,dx.\]

Every term has units of length. Bars joined end to end carry the same force in a segment without an intervening axial load. Their extensions add, so their compliances \(L/(EA)\), measured in m/N, add. Bars between the same two rigid plates instead share the same extension. Their forces add, and their stiffnesses \(EA/L\), measured in N/m, add. Draw the connections before deciding which quantities are equal. The material with the larger modulus does not always carry the larger load because area and length also matter.

Stretch a two-material tie

A steel section is 0.60 m long with area 120 mm² and \(E_s=200\) GPa. An aluminium section continues another 0.40 m, has area 200 mm² and \(E_a=70\) GPa. Pull on the joined tie with 12 kN at constant temperature. Equilibrium gives 12 kN in both pieces. The nominal stresses are 100 MPa and 60 MPa respectively. Their elongations are \(12\,000(0.60)/(200\times10^9\,120\times10^{-6})=0.300\) mm and \(12\,000(0.40)/(70\times10^9\,200\times10^{-6})=0.343\) mm. The end displacement is 0.643 mm. The aluminium extends more although its nominal stress is smaller, because its compliance is larger.

Dividing total force by total displacement gives a tie stiffness of 18.7 MN/m. This is smaller than the stiffness of either isolated piece. Checking this limit catches an accidental parallel-spring calculation. The stress calculation still treats each piece separately. Averaging their areas first would conceal the location of the larger nominal stress.

Install the tie between rigid walls

Now let the same tie fit exactly between rigid walls with zero force at its installation temperature. Raise the uniform temperature by 30 K. Take \(\alpha_s=12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) and \(\alpha_a=23\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). If free, the pieces would extend by 0.216 and 0.276 mm. The walls prescribe zero total displacement, so an elastic shortening of 0.492 mm must develop. Multiplying this mismatch by the combined stiffness gives \(N=-9.18\) kN. The negative sign denotes compression.

A force balance on the entire tie merely says the two wall reactions are opposite. Their magnitude came from the displacement condition. Real supports deflect, giving part of the expansion back to the tie and reducing the force. If the tie is long and slender, check whether it can remain straight under this force before interpreting the compressive stress.

Test the difference between member force and extension

In a statically determinate pin-jointed truss the axial forces follow from joint equilibrium before the bar areas enter the displacement calculation. Predict both outputs when every bar area is doubled at unchanged geometry and load. If the compressive top chord initially carries 75 kN and the monitored joint moves down 3.219 mm, which reading changes, and by what factor? Check the two runs and explain the final rounding using \(\delta_i=N_iL_i/(E_iA_i)\). Open the truss experiment.

Integrate strain in a tapered member

A weight-saving tie may taper along its length although equilibrium still gives the same force at every cross-section. Its compliance cannot in general be calculated using the arithmetic mean area. Let a straight elastic bar of length \(L\) have uniform thickness and linearly varying width, so its area changes linearly from \(A_0\) to \(A_1\). Apply an axial end force \(P\), neglect distributed body force, take constant Young’s modulus and exclude local stress disturbances near load introduction. Isolating any segment gives \(N=P\); compatibility between adjacent slices gives \(du/dx=P/[EA(x)]\).

Set \(A(x)=A_0+(A_1-A_0)x/L\). Changing integration variable from position to area produces a logarithm:

\[\delta=\int_0^L\frac{P}{EA(x)}\,dx=\frac{PL}{E(A_1-A_0)}\ln\!\frac{A_1}{A_0}.\]

This expression is unchanged if the member is turned end for end. Its limit as \(A_1\) approaches \(A_0\) follows from \(\ln(1+z)/z\to1\), recovering \(PL/(EA_0)\). The effective uniform area for extension is therefore a logarithmic mean. Narrow regions contribute disproportionately to compliance because strain depends on the reciprocal of area. The stress maximum still occurs at the smallest cross-section; a stiffness-equivalent uniform area does not reproduce it.

Size a suspended gauge bar from two different requirements

Take an aluminium tie with \(L=1.20\,\mathrm m\), \(A_0=120\,\mathrm{mm^2}\), \(A_1=360\,\mathrm{mm^2}\), \(E=70\,\mathrm{GPa}\), and \(P=6.0\,\mathrm{kN}\). Using newtons and millimetres, its elongation is \(6000(1200)\ln3/[70000(240)]=0.471\,\mathrm{mm}\). The arithmetic mean area 240 mm² would predict 0.429 mm, about 9% too low. The actual peak nominal stress is \(6000/120=50.0\,\mathrm{MPa}\), and the far larger end sees 16.7 MPa. A strain gauge near the large end therefore underestimates the average elongation if its measured strain is simply multiplied by the whole length.

A different loading mechanism matters for a very long uniform member. Suspend a uniform steel gauge wire of length \(L\) from its top with no end load, and measure \(x\) upwards from its free bottom. The force at \(x\) carries the weight below, \(N(x)=\rho A g x\). The area cancels from the strain \(N/(EA)=\rho gx/E\), so its total extension is \(\rho gL^2/(2E)\). Taking \(L=30\,\mathrm m,\ \rho=7850\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(E=200\,\mathrm{GPa}\) gives 0.173 mm. Doubling the wire diameter halves neither the strain nor the elongation under self-weight, since the added area also adds weight. Enlarging the wire does reduce the separate extension caused by a fixed instrument hung from its end.

A practical way to separate the wire’s two contributions is to measure extension with two different end weights while leaving the wire itself in place. The difference between readings is \(\Delta P L/(EA)\), since self-weight and any fixed initial offset are the same in both tests. A plot of extension against end load therefore has slope \(L/(EA)\) and a nonzero intercept if its zero refers to the unstressed wire. If an instrument was instead zeroed after the wire was suspended, the intercept disappears from the record. Stating the reference condition prevents it from being misinterpreted as evidence for or against self-weight deformation.

Creep and relaxation put different constraints on the same bar

A polymer strip may continue to extend after a testing machine reaches its target force. In another test the machine holds the extension fixed and the force decreases. In each case the energy-storing part of the material can exchange load with a time-dependent internal mechanism. Represent the simplest such mechanism by a spring of modulus \(E_1\) in series with a viscous dashpot of viscosity \(\eta\). This Maxwell branch carries common stress \(s\) and its strains add: \(\dot\varepsilon=\dot s/E_1+s/\eta\). To represent a solid with finite long-time stiffness, put a second spring \(E_\infty\) in parallel. The specimen stress is \(\sigma=E_\infty\varepsilon+s\). Assume small uniaxial strain, uniform temperature and an initially relaxed specimen.

At prescribed step strain \(\varepsilon_0\), the dashpot cannot change length instantaneously, so both springs initially contribute. Holding the strain sets \(\dot s=-sE_1/\eta\). Hence \(\sigma(t)=\varepsilon_0(E_\infty+E_1 e^{-t/\tau_r})\) with relaxation time \(\tau_r=\eta/E_1\). Under a maintained stress \(\sigma_0\), substitute \(s=\sigma_0-E_\infty\varepsilon\) into the branch equation. The result is \(\tau_c\dot\varepsilon+\varepsilon=\sigma_0/E_\infty\), with \(\tau_c=\eta(E_\infty+E_1)/(E_\infty E_1)\). The initial strain is \(\sigma_0/(E_\infty+E_1)\) and the creep strain approaches \(\sigma_0/E_\infty\). The two time constants differ even for the same specimen.

A 100 mm strip of area 20 mm² has \(E_\infty=100\) MPa, \(E_1=300\) MPa and \(\eta=6000\) MPa s. Hold its extension at 0.50 mm. The initial stress is 2.00 MPa, corresponding to 40 N. After 20 s it is \(0.005[100+300/e]=1.052\) MPa or 21.0 N. The long-time force is 10 N. Instead hold a previously relaxed strip at 20 N. Its stress stays at 1.00 MPa, giving initial extension 0.25 mm and final extension 1.00 mm. The creep time is 80 s. After that time extension is \(1.00-(1.00-0.25)/e=0.724\) mm. Matching one force reading at one time cannot establish which boundary condition was imposed. A machine compliance comparable to the specimen’s compliance moves the test between these ideal limits.

Can a compliant support relieve thermal stress?
