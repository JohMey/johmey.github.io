From first principles

Derive Euler’s pinned-column load from transverse equilibrium

Take a straight slender column of length \(L\) with pins at both ends, constant rigidity \(EI\) and centred compressive load \(P>0\). Assume a small lateral displacement \(w(x)\), linear elastic material and negligible shear deformation. With no external transverse load, the axial force acting through the end line has a moment arm equal to the lateral displacement. With a consistent bending sign convention this gives the homogeneous equilibrium equation

\[EI w''+Pw=0,\]
\[w(0)=w(L)=0.\]

A perfectly straight shape solves this equation for every load. To find when a neighbouring bent equilibrium becomes possible, solve for a nonzero amplitude. The first pin removes the cosine term; the far pin then selects discrete wave numbers:

\[w=A\sin(\beta x),\]
\[\beta^2=P/(EI),\]
\[\sin(\beta L)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_n=n^2\pi^2EI/L^2 .\]

The lowest eigenvalue is the first elastic buckling load within this ideal model. Buckling concerns loss of stiffness to lateral perturbations. Material yielding, crookedness and other end conditions can change a measured threshold even when the axial stress is still nominally uniform.

Background · Euler load
\[P_{\rm cr}=\pi^2 EI/L_{\rm eff}^2\]

The ideal slender elastic column buckles in its lowest admissible mode. Support conditions determine effective length and change the result quadratically.

Derivation and applications

When does a continuous lateral support change the buckling mode?

A slender compressed member may rest on an elastic bed rather than being unsupported between pins. The axial compression lowers the energy of lateral deflection; bending stiffness and the support both raise it. Take a straight, prismatic Euler–Bernoulli column with span \(L\), bending rigidity \(EI\) and compressive dead load \(P>0\). Let a distributed support supply restoring force per length \(-k_f w\), proportional to transverse displacement \(w\). Neglect imperfections, shear deflection and nonlinearities beyond second-order potential energy.

For small slopes the ends approach by \(\int w'^2dx/2\). The change of potential energy therefore contains three contributions:

\[\Delta\Pi=\tfrac12\int_0^L[EI(w'')^2+k_f w^2-P(w')^2]\,dx .\]

With pins at both ends take \(w=A\sin(n\pi x/L)\), which satisfies zero deflection and zero moment there. For uniform properties these are independent eigenfunctions. With \(\beta_n=n\pi/L\), each squared sine or cosine integrates to \(L/2\), giving

\[\Delta\Pi_n=\tfrac14LA^2(EI\beta_n^4+k_f-P\beta_n^2).\]

At the threshold the coefficient of \(A^2\) first vanishes. Dividing by \(\beta_n^2\) gives a candidate compression for each mode; the physically relevant first threshold is their minimum:

\[P_n=EI\beta_n^2+k_f/\beta_n^2,\]
\[P_{\rm cr}=\min_{n\ge1}P_n .\]

A long-wavelength deflection costs little bending energy but displaces the elastic bed strongly for a prescribed end shortening. Short wavelengths avoid that displacement at the expense of large curvature. For \(k_f=0\) the first sine mode recovers Euler’s pinned-column result. A sufficiently stiff bed can instead favor a mode having more than one half-wave.

Worked example

Find the first threshold for a supported strip

Take \(L=\pi\) m, \(EI=100\) N m² and \(k_f=900\) N/m². Then \(\beta_n=n\) m⁻¹ and \(P_1=1000\) N, \(P_2=625\) N, \(P_3=1000\) N. For larger \(n\) the bending term alone exceeds \(625\) N. The first instability is therefore the two-half-wave shape at \(625\) N, even though the ends are still ordinary pins.

Second application

Specify a bed stiffness at the mode exchange

For this same strip increase \(k_f\) from zero. Equating \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) gives \(EI\beta_1^2+k_f/\beta_1^2=4EI\beta_1^2+k_f/(4\beta_1^2)\), hence \(k_f=4EI\beta_1^4=400\) N/m². Both modes then have \(P=500\) N. Raising the support stiffness increases both candidate loads but changes which one is lowest. A calculation checking only the first shape would overpredict the threshold beyond this exchange.

Equilibrium can persist while stability changes

Press gently on the ends of a thin steel ruler. The straight configuration satisfies force equilibrium over a range of compressive loads. Eventually a small lateral disturbance produces a large deflection. The issue concerns the restoring response to a disturbance, even when the nominal compressive stress is well within the material’s elastic range.

Model a slender, initially straight, uniform elastic column with ideal pins at both ends. Apply a conservative compressive load \(P\) along the initial end-to-end line, with small transverse slope and negligible shear deformation. A lateral excursion \(w(x)\) creates a bending moment of magnitude \(P|w|\). Combining its restoring sign with the small-curvature law gives

\[EI w''+P w=0.\]

The difference from a laterally loaded beam is that the load itself creates a moment through the unknown displacement.

Apply two pin conditions

Write \(k^2=P/(EI)\) and integrate:

\[w=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx).\]

The first pin imposes \(w(0)=0\), so \(B=0\). The second imposes \(A\sin(kL)=0\). The zero-amplitude straight solution always exists. A nonzero amplitude is possible within this linearised model only when \(kL=n\pi\), for positive integer \(n\). The smallest compressive load admitting a bent mode is

\[P_{\mathrm{cr}}=\frac{\pi^2EI}{L^2},\]
\[w_1(x)\propto\sin(\pi x/L).\]

The proportionality leaves the amplitude undetermined. It locates a loss of stiffness, not a post-buckled deflection. The same result follows from energy: for \(w=A\sin(\pi x/L)\) the quadratic elastic energy is \(EI\pi^4A^2/(4L^3)\), while end shortening releases \(P\pi^2A^2/(4L)\). Their difference changes sign at the critical load.

Find which direction buckles first

A rectangular steel strut is 1.50 m long with section 40 mm by 12 mm and \(E=200\) GPa. Bending across the 12 mm dimension gives

\[I_{\min}=40(12)^3/12=5760\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

Across the 40 mm dimension,

\[I=12(40)^3/12=64\,000\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

Free to buckle either way, the column chooses the smaller bending stiffness. Converting \(5760\,\mathrm{mm^4}\) to \(5.76\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) gives \(P_{\rm cr}=5.05\) kN.

The corresponding mean stress is only \(5050/480=10.5\) MPa. The radius of gyration about the weak axis is

\[r_g=\sqrt{I/A}=3.46\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Writing \(P_{\rm cr}/A=\pi^2E/(L/r_g)^2\) makes the sensitivity to slenderness explicit. If a real brace prevents weak-axis deflection at midspan, a pin-like brace divides that buckling length by two. Check other unbraced axes and the brace stiffness before crediting the fourfold ideal increase.

Interpret a pre-curved strut

Suppose the unloaded column already bows with amplitude \(w_0\) in the first sine shape. Elastic bending resists the curvature change from that initial shape. Writing its total amplitude as \(w\), the mode equation now gives \(P_{\rm cr}(w-w_0)=Pw\), hence \(w=w_0/(1-P/P_{\rm cr})\). If \(w_0=1.0\) mm, the predicted amplitude is 2.0 mm at half the ideal load and 5.0 mm at 80%. The steep increase can show up in a test well before a distinct peak load.

This model assumes small slopes and a material still in its elastic range. Large deflection changes the geometry, and local yielding can reduce the tangent stiffness. End restraint also changes the eigenvalue. The often-used effective-length notation replaces \(L\) by the pin-ended length giving the same ideal load. It must reflect the actual translations and rotations permitted by the supports.

Use two readings to estimate an elastic critical load

For an imperfect elastic column dominated by its first buckling shape, write total offset \(e=e_0/(1-P/P_{\rm cr})\). Let \(y=e-e_0\) be the additional deflection measured from its unloaded shape. Rearrangement gives

\[y/P=(y+e_0)/P_{\rm cr}.\]

A graph of \(y/P\) against \(y\) should therefore be linear with slope \(1/P_{\rm cr}\). As a consistency test, measured pairs \(P=20\) kN, \(y=0.50\) mm and \(P=30\) kN, \(y=1.00\) mm give slope \((1/30-0.5/20)/(1.0-0.5)=1/60\,\mathrm{kN^{-1}}\). The implied critical load is 60 kN and the intercept implies a 1 mm initial offset. Clearance or nonlinear material response can spoil this inference before the theoretical critical load is reached.

Estimate a buckling load from an assumed lateral shape

The differential buckling equation is especially simple for uniform pinned columns. An energy method extends the calculation to cases where stiffness varies or extra bracing is present. Take a conservative compressive dead load \(P\) acting along the original axis. A small lateral displacement \(w(x)\) stores bending energy \(\tfrac12\int EI(w'')^2dx\). To the same quadratic order in slope the ends move towards one another by \(\tfrac12\int(w')^2dx\), allowing the axial load to do positive work. The straight configuration loses stiffness against a given shape when these quadratic contributions balance.

\[P_R=\frac{\int_0^L EI(w'')^2dx}{\int_0^L(w')^2dx}.\]

The trial shape must satisfy the geometric support conditions and must possess finite bending energy. Its amplitude cancels, since numerator and denominator are both quadratic in the same displacement. For this conservative model the smallest possible quotient over all admissible shapes equals the fundamental buckling load. A particular admissible shape generally yields an upper estimate. This is a potential hazard in assessment: a plausible trial shape can overestimate the true capacity and refinement should make the estimate decrease.

For the pinned uniform column choose \(w=A\sin(\pi x/L)\). Squaring and integrating the derivatives recovers \(P_R=EI\pi^2/L^2\). A deliberately less accurate trial \(w=A x(L-x)\) has constant curvature and gives

\[P_R=12EI/L^2.\]

It satisfies zero displacement at both pins, hence is admissible in this energy calculation, but it misses the exact sinusoidal curvature. Its predicted load is about 22% high.

Decide whether a central brace can suppress the lowest mode

Install a lateral spring of stiffness \(k\) at the midpoint of a 2.0 m pinned member with \(EI=40\,\mathrm{kN\,m^2}\). The spring adds \(\tfrac12 k w(L/2)^2\) to stored energy. Using the first sine shape gives

\[P_R=\pi^2 EI/L^2+2kL/\pi^2.\]

Its unbraced value is 98.7 kN. For \(k=100\,\mathrm{kN/m}\), the quotient rises by \(2(100)(2)/\pi^2=40.5\) kN, giving 139.2 kN. The value estimates the improved symmetric mode; the spring generally changes that mode’s shape.

The second sine shape has a node at midspan, so its frequency-independent elastic buckling load remains \(4\pi^2EI/L^2=394.8\,\mathrm{kN}\). Equating the two trial values would suggest a transition near \(k=731\,\mathrm{kN/m}\). This crossing identifies when a one-shape description merits scrutiny. It cannot prove the actual transition because the symmetric trial result is an upper bound. A brace added at midspan can suppress a large single bow and still leave a two-half-wave buckling mode essentially unaffected. Checking the other bending direction and the brace’s own connection stiffness is essential before applying either idealised improvement to a real member.

A midspan dial indicator responds strongly to the symmetric shape and sees zero displacement for the ideal two-half-wave shape. An experiment observing only that indicator might report successful suppression even as the second mode grows. A second indicator at quarter span has nonzero response to both shapes and reveals the change of governing mechanism.
