How a beam spreads a point load through an elastic support

A long rail rests on a strip of elastic material. An instrument foot applies a vertical load near the middle. Which part of the strip carries that load? If the rail were completely rigid the answer would depend on its full length. A flexible rail selects a local length through the competition between bending and support compression. Derive that length, calculate a displacement that can be measured by a dial gauge, and recover the answer independently by minimising the energy of a discrete beam.

Replace many small supports by a force per length

Let \(x\) measure distance along the beam and \(w(x)\) displacement positive downward from the unloaded configuration. A span \(dx\) moves downward by \(w\) and experiences an upward foundation reaction of magnitude \(kw\,dx\). The coefficient \(k\) has units N/m². If a manufacturer gives a compression modulus per area and per deflection in N/m³, multiply it by the effective contact width before using it in this one-dimensional description. An error here changes the apparent spreading length as well as the deflection. Doubling an actual pad width also changes how the contact pressure is distributed across the rail, which the line model cannot resolve.

A Winkler foundation is a set of independent local springs in a continuum limit. Adjacent springs communicate through the bending rail, while the substrate itself transmits no horizontal shear between their tops. This is appropriate for a row of distinct pads represented on length scales large compared with their spacing. A continuous elastic half-space can spread load inside the substrate as well. Treating both systems as the same foundation merely because they give the same displacement under one foot can produce different predictions for two feet placed some distance apart.

For a slender, uniform Euler–Bernoulli rail take the change of curvature to be \(w''\). The bending energy per unit length is \(EI(w'')^2/2\). The elastic support stores \(kw^2/2\) per unit length. With a point load \(P\) at the origin, the total potential energy is

\[\Pi[w]=\frac12\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\big[EI(w'')^2+kw^2\big]\,dx-Pw(0).\]

Vary the shape by adding a small test displacement \(\eta\) that vanishes sufficiently far away. The variation of the bending term is \(\int EI w''\eta''dx\). Integration by parts twice moves the derivatives onto \(EIw''\). Equating the remaining interior coefficient of \(\eta\) to the applied work gives

\[EIw''''+kw=P\delta(x).\]

The right-hand side is a load per length; its integral over any small interval containing zero is the finite force \(P\). The free equation applies everywhere away from the foot. A narrow real foot agrees with the point-load idealisation only while its footprint is short relative to the calculated spreading length.

Build the decaying deflection and determine its amplitude

The spatial response satisfies \(EI r^4+k=0\) for a trial field \(w=e^{rx}\). Define \(\beta=(k/4EI)^{1/4}\). On the positive half-line the roots with negative real part are \(-\beta\pm i\beta\). Decay and oscillation therefore have the same spatial scale. Symmetry about the load requires zero slope at the origin. Applying this condition to the combination of sine and cosine gives \(w=A e^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x+\sin\beta x)\) for \(x>0\).

To find \(A\), integrate the beam equation across the loading point. The support force over this short interval vanishes as the interval shrinks, leaving a jump in \(EIw'''\) equal to \(P\). Reflection symmetry makes the third derivative odd. Its right-hand value is \(4A\beta^3\), so \(8EI\beta^3 A=P\). Substituting the definition of \(\beta\) produces the same amplitude written as \(P\beta/(2k)\). Reflecting the one-sided shape gives the complete result:

\[w(x)=\frac{P\beta}{2k}e^{-\beta|x|}\big(\cos\beta|x|+\sin\beta|x|\big),\]
\[\ell=\beta^{-1}.\]

A units check provides a quick guard against interchanging the line and surface moduli. The ratio \(EI/k\) has units m⁴, so \(\ell\) is a length. At fixed \(k\) a stronger rail spreads the reaction more widely. At fixed \(EI\) a harder support makes the footprint narrower yet reduces the central displacement, since \(w(0)\) varies as \(k^{-3/4}\). These exponents also tell us which property can be inferred most reliably from which observable. A deflection reading alone mixes the effects of the foundation and the bending stiffness.

Integrate the footprint that a row of load cells would measure

Imagine force sensors beneath a symmetric interval \([-d,d]\). The reading is a signed integral of \(kw\). Transform it to \(s=\beta x\), integrate the product of the exponential with the sine and cosine, and use the symmetry. The load fraction becomes

\[\frac{R(d)}P=1-e^{-\beta d}\cos(\beta d).\]

Its limiting value is one, as required by vertical equilibrium. At one spreading length the interval already carries about 80.1% of the applied force. At \(d=\pi\ell/2\) it carries exactly the applied load. This does not mean every reaction beyond that position vanishes. Additional positive and negative reactions there integrate to zero. The cumulative curve reaches a maximum where \(w\) first crosses zero, at \(\beta d=3\pi/4\). These are distinct spatial locations and represent different observable events.

For an aluminium rail 40 mm wide and 20 mm deep, choose \(E=70\) GPa and \(k=1.5\) MN/m². A central 150 N load gives \(I=2.667\times10^{-8}\) m⁴, \(EI=1867\) N m², \(\ell=0.2656\) m and \(w(0)=0.1882\) mm. Sensors extending 0.266 m on each side then measure approximately 120 N. The centre support intensity is \(kw(0)=282\) N/m. This is a line load; a rough mean pressure across the 40 mm width is 7.06 kPa. Resolving a nonuniform transverse pressure requires an additional contact model.

One can estimate a local coefficient without knowing the applied force precisely. Divide a neighbouring displacement by the central displacement. The amplitude drops out, leaving an equation for \(\beta\) from the measured ratio. At \(x=\ell\) the ideal ratio is approximately 0.508. Once this length has been identified, combine it with the accurately calibrated centre displacement and the applied load to estimate \(k=P/(2\ell w(0))\). Repeating the inference on either side of the foot tests the assumed symmetry.

Reconstruct the field by minimising a grid energy

Put equally spaced nodes on a long finite interval and impose zero displacement at the end supports. Replace each curvature by the second difference of the displacement divided by \(h^2\). If \(D_2\) contains these differences on the interior nodes, minimising the resulting quadratic energy yields

\[(EI D_2^T D_2+kI_d)\mathbf w=\mathbf f.\]

Here \(I_d\) is the identity matrix for the degrees of freedom. The central component of \(\mathbf f\) is \(P/h\) because the discrete energy carries the integration weight \(h\). Setting the central component to \(P\) would make the total force disappear under refinement.

The notebook puts the end supports eight decay lengths from the centre. With 65 interior unknowns the centre differs from the infinite-beam answer by about 1.44%. With 129 it differs by 0.38%. The finer result tests both a numerical approximation and a finite-domain approximation. To separate them, first hold the length fixed while refining, then extend the interval while holding \(h\) nearly fixed. The optional OneSimulation linear solver takes the energy matrix directly. Changing the algebraic solver and changing the physical boundary condition test different questions.

Predict the interference between two instrument feet

A long optical table carries equal 150 N loads at two feet spaced one decay length apart. Mark the rail under one of them and install a displacement gauge there. The first load acting alone produced 0.1882 mm of deflection. The second acts at a distance of one decay length, so its contribution at the gauge is the central value multiplied by \(e^{-1}(\cos1+\sin1)=0.5083\). Superposing the two gives 0.2839 mm. The maximum need not occur exactly under either foot. At the midpoint each foot lies half a decay length away, so the midpoint displacement is

\[2(0.1882)e^{-1/2}(\cos0.5+\sin0.5)=0.3096\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Two locally smaller contributions produce a larger combined movement between the load points.

To reproduce the calculation on the grid, place two force entries in the load vector. If a foot lies between nodes, distribute the force linearly between its two neighbours. For a foot a fraction \(\xi\) of the way across an element of length \(h\), give the left and right entries \((1-\xi)P/h\) and \(\xi P/h\). Their weighted sum recovers the force, and their first moment recovers its position. Simply moving each foot to a nearby node changes the experimental geometry each time the mesh changes. For closely spaced loads that can masquerade as a change in stiffness. To test superposition in a real assembly, record each foot alone, sum the readings at the same gauge locations and compare with applying both weights together.

Exercises on inference, contact and material choice

1. Double the section depth at fixed width. Predict the new decay length and central deflection before running the notebook.

Work through the solution

The second moment increases by eight. The length increases by \(8^{1/4}=1.682\), to 0.447 m. Since the same total force now spreads farther, \(w(0)\) decreases by the same factor, to 0.112 mm. A simple unsupported beam with fixed length would exhibit a different stiffness exponent. Its support positions supply an independent length, while the foundation here selects the active span.

2. A free-standing rail on an unbonded pad appears to have a very small upward deflection far from the loaded foot. Can you use the negative foundation pressure predicted there?

Work through the solution

For a support that can only push, a negative total pressure is impossible. With no preload the bilateral solution first requests tension at a distance \(3\pi\ell/4\), about 0.626 m. A unilateral calculation must determine the contact set and impose zero pressure where separation occurs. With a substantial compressive preload, a negative incremental reaction may remain compatible with contact. Check the total pressure before judging that case.

3. Only the central displacement and its half-width are available. How could the observations distinguish a soft rail from a soft foundation?

Work through the solution

Fit the normalised spatial profile to obtain \(\ell\). A measured half-width corresponds to the positive root of \(e^{-s}(\cos s+\sin s)=1/2\) on the first lobe, with \(s\) close to one. The observed amplitude gives

\[k=P/(2\ell w(0)).\]

Finally obtain \(EI=k\ell^4/4\). A single displacement supplies one equation for two unknowns. A spatial measurement supplies the missing independent relation.
