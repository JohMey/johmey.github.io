    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Draw free bodies and recover support reactions. A cut exposes axial forces, shear and bending moments. A material law and displacement compatibility turn those internal actions into extensions and beam deflections.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Stress components combine on an inclined plane. Compressive load can also destabilise a whole member. Torsion and transverse shear explain why the location and direction of the largest stress depend on section geometry.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Use compatibility to solve redundant supports and strain measurements to reconstruct the stress state. Plastic redistribution and crack growth introduce two different limits to the elastic load calculation.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. From cut forces to local equilibrium

      A support reaction tells you the total force entering a part. To predict what an inclined internal surface carries, keep track of both the orientation of the cut and the direction of the force across it.

    2. How a lateral restraint changes a measured modulus

      A short compression test can report a larger stiffness than a tensile coupon cut from the same material. Derive the difference from the strains that the fixture permits.

    3. Radial displacement and hoop stress in a thick ring

      As a soft sleeve presses outward on a ring, the inside and outside radii move by different amounts. A cut through the thickness reveals why replacing the entire wall by one hoop stress loses information.

    4. Creep, relaxation and what a holding test measures

      Two polymer specimens can show the same initial force and quite different readings ten minutes later. Change the quantity held fixed and the same material reveals a different time scale.

    5. How a continuum balance becomes a finite-element stiffness matrix

      A single bar element can predict an end displacement exactly and still miss the stress field inside. The difference follows directly from the balance it is asked to satisfy.

    6. Finding a displacement when no load acts in its direction

      A displacement gauge measures the lateral travel of a truss joint under a vertical load. A force balance determines the bar forces, but cannot directly give the sideways motion. Introduce a dummy load, differentiate the stored energy and recover the gauge reading with its sign.

    7. Why a bonded pair of strips bends under uniform heating

      Two perfectly bonded strips would expand by different amounts if separated. After bonding they must share a continuous longitudinal strain field. Solve simultaneously for its mean and its curvature and recover stresses that can be present even when the entire strip is free.

    8. How a beam spreads a point load through an elastic support

      A long rail rests on a strip of elastic material. An instrument foot applies a vertical load near the middle. Which part of the strip carries that load? If the rail were completely rigid the answer would depend on its full length. A flexible rail selects a local length through the competition between bending and support compression. Derive that length, calculate a displacement that can be measured by a dial gauge, and recover the answer independently by minimising the energy of a discrete beam.

    Applied study

    Trace loads through a structure and distinguish equilibrium, stress and deformation.

    Where does a joint load go?

    A lifting frame spans 6 m between a pin and a roller. A machine hanging from the middle applies a downward 100 kN load. The frame must carry that load without yielding, buckling or deflecting too far. Each check asks for a different property of the structure.

    For the first calculation, idealise the joints as frictionless pins, the members as straight elastic bars and the load as a joint force. Neglect self-weight and out-of-plane motion. Each bar is then a two-force member. Its end forces lie along its axis. Let positive member force mean tension.

    Place bottom joints A, B and C at \(x=0,3,6\) m and \(z=0\). Top joints D and E sit at \(x=1.5,4.5\) m and \(z=2\) m. Join AB, BC and DE horizontally, then connect the zigzag A–D–B–E–C. Support A is pinned, C is a horizontal roller, and the load acts at B. Horizontal and vertical force balance and moments about A give \(A_x=0\) and \(A_z=C_z=F/2=50\) kN.

    Use a joint free-body diagram

    Each diagonal rises 2 m over 1.5 m, so its length is 2.5 m and \(\sin\theta=0.8,\ \cos\theta=0.6\). Assume that every unknown force pulls away from its joint. Vertical balance at A then gives \(50+0.8N_{AD}=0\), hence \(N_{AD}=-62.5\) kN. Horizontal balance gives

    \[N_{AB}=-0.6N_{AD}=37.5\,\mathrm{kN}.\]

    The minus sign identifies a compressed diagonal.

    At B the two inner diagonals share the downward load, each supplying \(F/2\) vertically. Each therefore carries 62.5 kN in tension. At D the horizontal components of AD and DB both point to the right and equal 37.5 kN. Member DE carries 75 kN in compression to close the joint balance. Reflection supplies the remaining forces. The horizontal support reactions vanish, yet the chords carry substantial horizontal forces inside the frame.

    From member strain to a displacement

    With \(E=200\) GPa and area \(A=1000\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) throughout, the top chord carries a nominal compressive stress of 75 MPa. To find the movement of B, store the elastic strain energy of all seven bars. A bar of length \(\ell_i\) extends by \(N_i\ell_i/(EA)\) and stores \(N_i^2\ell_i/(2EA)\). Ramp the load slowly from zero. The external work is \(F\delta/2\) because the displacement grows linearly with load. Equating the two gives

    \[\delta=\frac{1}{F}\sum_i\frac{N_i^2\ell_i}{EA}.\]

    Use two 3 m bottom chords at 37.5 kN, one 3 m top chord at 75 kN and four 2.5 m diagonals at 62.5 kN. These groups contribute 0.422 mm, 0.844 mm and 1.953 mm respectively. Joint B moves down by 3.219 mm. Compression stores positive energy just as tension does. The largest individual force and the largest group contribution to displacement occur in different members.

    For a member that carries a transverse load between joints, the bending moment also contributes to strain energy. Study bending in a clamped beam.

    Transfer problem · A shaft that carries torque

    A drive shaft transmits 5.0 kW at 600 rpm to a lifting drum. Its internal load is a torque, even when its axial force is zero. In a uniform solid circular shaft of radius \(r_o\), cross-sections rotate relative to their neighbours. A fibre at radius \(r\) acquires shear strain \(\gamma=r\,d\phi/dx\). Using \(\tau=G\gamma\) and taking moments of the shear stresses gives

    \[T=GJ\,d\phi/dx,\]

    with \(J=\int_A r^2dA=\pi r_o^4/2\).

    Take \(r_o=15\) mm, length 0.80 m and \(G=80\) GPa. The angular speed is \(600(2\pi/60)=62.8\) rad/s, so the transmitted torque is

    \[T=P/\omega=79.6\,\mathrm{N\,m}.\]

    Here \(J=7.95\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^4}\). The surface stress is \(Tr_o/J=15.0\) MPa, and the twist angle is \(TL/(GJ)=0.0100\) rad or 0.57°. Doubling the radius at the same power and speed reduces stress eightfold and twist sixteenfold. An area check alone would capture neither dependence.

    Exercises

    1. Stiffen only the top chord

    Double the area of DE and leave the other bars as they are. Find the new top stress and the displacement at B. Why do the diagonal forces stay the same?

    Show the force and flexibility calculation

    All member forces in this statically determinate geometry follow from equilibrium, which contains no area or modulus. Top stress halves to 37.5 MPa. Only the 0.844 mm energy contribution halves, so the displacement becomes

    \[3.219-0.422=2.797\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

    Stiffening one bar has a much smaller effect than doubling every area.

    2. Check the compression member

    Suppose DE has a weak-axis second moment of area \(I=3.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) and pin ends 3 m apart. Use the ideal elastic buckling load \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}=\pi^2EI/\ell^2\) to test the axial solution.

    Show the stability check

    The ideal buckling load is \(65.8\) kN, below the 75 kN compressive demand. Even if the nominal stress is below yield, the assumed straight configuration can lose stability. Setting \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}=75\) kN gives \(I=3.42\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) at the theoretical threshold. An axial truss calculation alone cannot detect this bending instability.
