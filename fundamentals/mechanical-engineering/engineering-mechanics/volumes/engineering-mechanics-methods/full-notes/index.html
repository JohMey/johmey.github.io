Stress, deformation and structural measurement · 1. Torsion in a thin-walled two-cell section

Two closed cells share one slender web. The applied couple gives only their combined torque, yet the common web can carry much less shear than either exterior wall. Derive the missing compatibility equation, calculate a twist and test which measurements distinguish the two circulations.

Obtain one torque equation from traction

Consider an initially straight prismatic member of length much greater than its section dimensions. The walls are thin relative to cell dimensions, shear modulus is uniform and Saint-Venant torsion is established away from ends. Define shear flow \(q=\tau t\), the tangential force transmitted per unit member length along the wall midline. In a closed single cell without a longitudinal body load, equilibrium makes its circulatory shear flow constant. Each tangential force around the contour contributes a torque, and the line integral of the moment arm gives twice the enclosed midline area. Consequently \(T=2Aq\).

For two cells choose positive circulation with the same sense in both. Superposition gives \(T=2A_1q_1+2A_2q_2\). On the shared wall the two contour directions oppose each other, so its actual flow is \(q_1-q_2\) when viewed along cell one's circulation. Adding the two flows there would violate the chosen orientation. This torque equation alone cannot determine two unknown circulations. Treating each as proportional to area would be a guess about stiffness, not a derivation.

Enforce equal twist on both closed contours

The tangential shear strain integrated around a closed contour is related to axial twist rate \(\theta'\). Under the thin-wall Saint-Venant assumptions, warping displacement is single-valued after a closed circuit, leaving \(2A\theta'=\oint q/(Gt)\,ds\). Let \(C_1,C_2\) denote integrals of \(ds/t\) along the two exterior contours excluding their common wall and \(C_w\) its shared-wall integral. Compatibility then requires

\[2A_1G\theta'=C_1q_1+C_w(q_1-q_2),\]
\[2A_2G\theta'=C_2q_2+C_w(q_2-q_1).\]

The common twist is a geometrical condition because the cells belong to one connected section. With different materials replace each \(ds/(Gt)\) term individually; pulling out one common \(G\) would then be invalid. For equal exterior geometry the equations give equal flows and zero net web shear, while the web can still stabilise shape and carry other load cases.

Take heights 40 mm and widths 60 and 40 mm, with 1 mm walls throughout, \(G=27\) GPa and \(T=10\) N m. The areas are 2400 and 1600 square millimetres. Exterior and common geometric coefficients are \(C_1=160,C_2=120,C_w=40\). Solving gives flows 1.287 and 1.195 kN/m, a shared difference 0.092 kN/m, and twist rate 0.001617 rad/m. The exterior wall stresses equal their flow divided by thickness and are therefore 1.287 and 1.195 MPa. Over a one-metre span the ideal rotation is only 0.0926 degrees.

Which measurement fixes which resistance?

Sweep the right width through the fixed left width. The shared flow should pass smoothly through zero while the outer walls continue transmitting the global torque. Increase the web thickness. Its \(C_w\) decreases and it can transfer a given difference at smaller shear strain. The exterior walls and compatibility still control the complete response; the common wall need not carry a large flow simply because it is thick. Measure external twist and at least one wall strain if the individual circulations are the quantities of interest.

A diagonal strain-gauge pair on a thin wall gives an approximate shear strain when the gauges sample in-plane strain at plus and minus forty-five degrees. Their difference rejects common normal strain to first order. At a predicted 0.092 MPa common-wall shear, the strain is only about 3.4 microstrain for the stated modulus. A one-axis gauge zero error can therefore overwhelm the common-wall signal even while exterior torque and twist appear accurate. Confirm the sign by reversing the applied couple.

Check the wall flows by minimising strain energy

A second derivation makes an effective independent numerical test. Under a torque constraint choose the two cell flows so that the sum of their area moments gives the required torque. Their elastic energy per unit length is the sum of three terms, one from each group's exclusive walls and one from the shared wall:

\[U'=\frac1{2G}\left(C_1q_1^2+C_2q_2^2+C_w(q_1-q_2)^2\right).\]

Perturb the first circulation while adjusting the second to keep torque constant. At the physical solution this variation cannot lower the energy. The resulting stationarity condition reproduces equal twist, including the minus sign on the common wall. A program that accidentally squares the sum on that wall would predict positive strain energy there even for two identical cells at equal flow. This unit test takes no measured twist as input and pinpoints a common implementation error. A second comparison differentiates the minimum energy with respect to applied torque and obtains twist per unit length. Because all wall stresses are linear in torque in this model, doubling torque doubles twist and quadruples stored energy. In a bench specimen, rapid departures from this scaling can reveal slip at a seam or local buckling before individual strain gauges identify which wall has changed.

Tutorial · Test a symmetry prediction numerically

Make both cells 50 mm wide with the same height and identical exterior walls. Find each circulating flow at 10 N m. Does changing only shared-web thickness affect ideal torsional twist in this exactly symmetric case?

Use symmetry before solving two equations

Each area is 0.0020 square metres. Equal flows give \(q=T/[2(A_1+A_2)]=1250\) N/m. The net web flow vanishes identically and hence its shear-compliance contribution drops out of both contour equations. With unchanged exterior geometry the ideal twist is unchanged by web thickness in this case. A measured change could reflect local distortion, end constraints, material changes during fabrication or other loads outside the thin-wall torsion model.

Tutorial · Infer torque from imperfect shear gauges

In the original unequal section calibrated exterior gauges indicate 1.30 and 1.18 MPa. Recover torque and predict shared-wall shear. Why is the torque result less sensitive to the gauge fluctuations than the difference?

Propagate the measured flows

At one millimetre thickness the flows are 1300 and 1180 N/m. Torque is \(2(0.0024\cdot1300+0.0016\cdot1180)=10.016\) N m. Predicted shared flow is 120 N/m, or 0.120 MPa. Independent small errors affect a sum of two large positive contributions only modestly in relative terms, while the shared difference is a much smaller residual. Check twist compatibility as a separate residual instead of concluding from the accurate torque alone that both gauge readings are correct.

Tutorial · Identify a missing effect at a clamped end

A very short specimen twists less than the prediction and shows high longitudinal stresses near the clamp. Which model assumptions are questionable, and how could two specimen lengths test them without changing cross-section?

Compare distributed and end contributions

Restrained warping, local load introduction and section distortion can create additional stresses and stiffness near the clamps. Fabricate geometrically similar fixtures at two or more lengths and compare differential rotation between interior stations per unit gauge length. An approximately constant interior slope matching the derived twist with endpoint departures supports a local end effect. If the slope itself varies throughout, check actual wall thickness, joint continuity, shear modulus and distortion. Increasing the number of contour integration points in an otherwise prismatic free-warping model does not recover a missing boundary mechanism.

Stress, deformation and structural measurement · 2. Contact pressure between elastic cylinders

Two polished cylindrical rollers touch along a narrow strip. Dividing load by a nominal full face area gives little indication of the actual pressure. Use the local shape of the gap and elastic half-space compliance to obtain the pressure distribution, and decide whether a measured contact width can determine material stiffness.

Replace the undeformed outlines by their local curvatures

Take two long parallel cylinders pressed together by total force \(F\) uniformly per engaged length \(L\). Ignore tangential traction, adhesion, end effects and plastic deformation. In a cross-section perpendicular to the axes the local undeformed gap grows as \(x^2/(2R^*)\), with \(1/R^*=1/R_1+1/R_2\) for two externally convex cylinders. A flat counterpart corresponds to infinite radius. For an internal conforming surface the signed curvature changes, but nearly conformal contact can violate the small-patch assumptions used here.

Normal deformations of both elastic bodies add. For isotropic half-spaces in line contact their combined modulus obeys \(1/E^*=(1-\nu_1^2)/E_1+(1-\nu_2^2)/E_2\). Replacing this by one body's modulus overestimates stiffness whenever the partner also deforms significantly. For two identical steels with \(E=210\) GPa and \(\nu=0.30\), \(E^*=115.4\) GPa. An elastically rigid counterpart would give roughly twice that value for the same first body.

Why the square-root distribution fits the quadratic gap

In two-dimensional half-space elasticity a line element of pressure produces a logarithmic normal surface displacement up to an arbitrary reference. Differentiating the displacement removes that reference and gives a singular kernel proportional to the inverse horizontal separation. In contact the two deformed faces conform, so the derivative of their combined displacement has magnitude \(x/R^*\). Using a consistent compressive sign convention, the elastic equation for \(|x|<a\) is

\[\frac{x}{R^*}=\frac{2}{\pi E^*}\operatorname{PV}\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{p(s)}{x-s}\,ds .\]

The trial shape \(p(s)=p_0\sqrt{1-s^2/a^2}\) gives a linear principal-value integral \(\pi p_0 x/a\), exactly matching the gap slope. Normal force per unit length \(W=F/L\) is the integral of this pressure, the area of a semiellipse: \(W=\pi ap_0/2\). Combining the two conditions determines both quantities,

\[a=\sqrt{\frac{4WR^*}{\pi E^*}},\]
\[p_0=\frac{2W}{\pi a}.\]

The full width is \(2a\). Central pressure exceeds the average over the strip by \(4/\pi\). The normal approach of ideal infinite cylinders has a logarithmic outer-length dependence, so absolute displacement from a frame is less cleanly predicted by this local half-space construction than pressure or width. Include whole-body and fixture compliance before using crosshead displacement to infer a local contact modulus.

Use a width series and check its scaling

For \(R^*=10\) mm, \(E^*=115\) GPa and \(W=20{,}000\) N/m the predicted half-width is 47.1 micrometres and peak pressure about 271 MPa. Doubling force at constant length increases both width and pressure by a factor of \(\sqrt2\). A nominal area built from the full millimetre-scale roller width would understate this pressure dramatically. Conversely, comparing peak hydrostatic or normal pressure directly with uniaxial yield stress is not a rigorous yield condition: evaluate the multiaxial subsurface stress and actual material strength for that separate question.

A pressure-sensitive film or optical contact image has finite resolution and can broaden the visible strip. Fit the square of measured half-width against \(W\). A nonzero intercept, load-dependent slope or hysteresis on unloading can reveal optical threshold bias, initial crowning or irreversible deformation. Surface roughness can produce sparse microcontacts inside a broader nominal strip. Compare several axial positions to detect end loading or misalignment before interpreting the width as uniform line contact.

Compare a resolved pressure trace with total applied force

The pressure texture of a two-dimensional Hertz contact gives more information than one width measurement. Predict the integrated line load by integrating the measured pressure over transverse position and compare it with a load cell divided by contact length. The model semiellipse has mean pressure across its width equal to one quarter of pi times its peak. A sensor with limited spatial resolution tends to round the central peak and move the apparent edges. If its response kernel has unit area and the full footprint is recorded, however, convolution preserves the integrated force. Disagreement in area suggests gain error, incomplete coverage or a departure from uniform loading along the roller length.

Try the selected loading and then double it while keeping radius and modulus fixed. The recorded footprint broadens, so rescale the transverse axis by each run's own calculated half-width. Also divide pressure by its own peak. The two normalised shapes should collapse. That collapse tests shape and linear elasticity, while the scale factors separately test the force and length calibration. Repeating with the load applied closer to an end changes the axial uniformity assumption; a contact trace at the middle may then give a different inferred load from a trace close to the end.

Tutorial · Infer a modulus and estimate its systematic sensitivity

A roll pair with \(R^*=12.0\) mm is loaded at 15.0 kN/m. A camera gives a full width of 96.0 micrometres. Infer \(E^*\). If optical blur makes the true full width five percent smaller than the reported value, by what factor does the inferred modulus change?

Convert full width before inverting

Using \(a=48.0\) micrometres gives \(E^*=4WR^*/(\pi a^2)=99.5\) GPa. Replacing \(a\) by \(0.95a\) raises the inferred modulus by \(1/0.95^2=1.108\), about eleven percent. Treating the quoted full width as \(a\) would instead underestimate modulus fourfold. Independent optical calibration and known line load both matter to a meaningful inverse measurement.

Tutorial · Change the engaged roller length

A fixed total force is spread uniformly over twice the original length. Predict changes in central pressure, half-width and total nominal contact area. Which change would reveal a failure of uniform load sharing?

Track load per length and the physical area

The line load halves, so width and pressure each fall to \(1/\sqrt2\) of their initial values. Total nominal area \(2aL\) rises by \(\sqrt2\), preserving total force after integrating pressure. If measured width at the centre remains nearly fixed while a large near-end maximum develops, the assumption that \(F/L\) is spatially uniform is suspect. Roller bending, tapered clearance or imposed misalignment can redistribute line load even at unchanged total force.

Tutorial · Can width identify both material moduli?

A laboratory wants to estimate \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) using the same pair at several loads. Is this possible from ideal widths alone if both Poisson ratios are known? Design a measurement that supplies additional information.

Identify the one combination present in the forward equation

Every load yields the same effective reciprocal modulus combination. A load sweep tests the square-root law and improves precision but does not separate the two material moduli. Pair one unknown with a reference body of independently measured modulus and known curvature, then solve for the first unknown. A second arrangement constrains the other. Use multiple loads to verify elasticity and repeatability in each arrangement; a permanent flat left after unloading invalidates the reversible Hertzian inversion.

Stress, deformation and structural measurement · 3. Springback and residual stress after plastic bending

After a rectangular strip has been bent beyond first yield, removing its end couple does not return it to its original curvature. Work out the loading and unloading paths fibre by fibre. Then explain why an unloaded strip can contain a substantial stress field with zero net force and zero net bending moment.

Locate the elastic core and compute the applied moment

Consider a straight rectangular strip of width \(b\), half-depth \(c\) and Young modulus \(E\), in pure bending with plane sections remaining plane. Assume symmetric elastic-perfectly plastic response with yield magnitude \(\sigma_y\), small strain, no hardening and no unloading-induced reverse yielding for the excursion being considered. Symmetry puts the neutral axis at mid-depth. Under increasing curvature \(\kappa\) the axial strain is \(\kappa y\); stress increases linearly until it reaches the signed yield plateau. First yield occurs at \(\kappa_y=\sigma_y/(Ec)\).

At greater curvature define \(y_e=\sigma_y/(E\kappa)\), the half-thickness of the remaining elastic core. Integrate stress times moment arm over the upper half and double. The core contributes \(2bE\kappa y_e^3/3\) and the yielded layer contributes \(b\sigma_y(c^2-y_e^2)\). Substituting the definition of \(y_e\) gives

\[M=b\sigma_y(c^2-y_e^2/3),\]
\[M_y=\frac23b\sigma_yc^2,\]
\[M_p=b\sigma_yc^2 .\]

The limiting fully plastic moment is one and a half times first-yield moment. It is approached as the elastic core shrinks; ideal plane-section theory does not justify arbitrary curvature once strains and geometry cease to be small. A load-controlled perfectly plastic model can also become indeterminate at a fully developed plastic hinge. A known curvature history is a cleaner basis for calculating springback in this ideal test.

Subtract an elastic stress field to satisfy moment release

During a purely elastic unloading of moment magnitude \(M\), every fibre responds with slope \(E\). The curvature decreases by \(M/(EI)\), where \(I=2bc^3/3\). The remaining curvature and signed residual stress profile are

\[\kappa_r=\kappa-\frac{M}{EI},\]
\[\sigma_r(y)=\sigma_{\rm load}(y)-\frac{M}{I}y .\]

The antisymmetric residual stresses have zero net force. Their bending resultant is zero because the second term removes exactly the original moment. A free strip can therefore remain curved even though its internal stress has neither an axial nor a bending resultant. Its plastic strain varies through the thickness and makes the unstrained reference of each fibre incompatible with one common straight shape.

Take \(E=200\) GPa, \(\sigma_y=250\) MPa, \(b=20\) mm and \(c=2.0\) mm. The yield curvature is 0.625 per metre, first moment 13.33 N m and plastic limit 20.0 N m. At curvature 1.25 per metre, \(y_e=1.0\) mm and applied moment 18.33 N m. Bending stiffness is 21.33 N square metres and elastic curvature recovery 0.859 per metre. The unloaded curvature is about 0.391 per metre. The surface formerly in tension ends up in compression of about 93.75 MPa, well below reverse yield under this idealised law.

Measure curvature and residual stress with different observables

A photograph of the unloaded centreline can determine residual curvature but cannot recover the full through-thickness stress profile. A single strain gauge bonded before loading measures total strain change at its location; separating plastic strain from elastic residual strain requires a constitutive model or an independent release measurement. If a thin surface layer is removed, the remaining section can change curvature as its previously self-equilibrated stress resultants are unbalanced. That change contains information about the removed residual stress but needs a recalculated section geometry.

Measure moment and curvature through loading and initial unloading as separate data sets. The small unloading slope gives an independent estimate of \(EI\), while the first slope departure during loading constrains yield. Friction at fixture rollers, nonuniform moment and axial tension during forming can produce an asymmetric neutral axis that the simple pure-couple solution cannot fit. Reversing the strip or comparing strains on both faces is an inexpensive diagnostic.

Look inside the strip after the applied moment vanishes

In the plot compare the stress at the surface with that on the boundary of the surviving elastic core. The applied stress there reaches yield but the subsequent elastic unloading stress is proportional to distance from the middle. The surface therefore experiences a greater stress change during unloading than the point at the edge of the core. This permits a tensile residual just inside a compressive outer skin on one side. Reflection about the middle reverses all the signs. A surface strain reading alone cannot reveal that self-equilibrated pattern.

Check the area under the residual stress over the entire depth. Antisymmetry makes the axial resultant vanish. The moment also vanishes, which follows because subtracting the elastic unloading distribution subtracts exactly the applied moment. The two cancellation tests are stronger than checking the stress at one point. They can be applied to a numerical fibre calculation even when hardening changes the shape of each branch. If an experiment instead clamps the strip straight after bending, the imposed curvature differs from free springback and a reaction moment appears. Compare the same unloading boundary condition before fitting a hardening coefficient.

Tutorial · Predict a residual profile at the elastic-core edge

For the numerical strip above calculate the residual stress at \(y=+1.0\) mm after unloading, and compare its sign with the outer surface. Where in the originally tensile outer layer is residual stress zero?

Use the loading plateau and the unloading slope

The point at the core boundary reached plus 250 MPa. Its elastic unloading change is \(E(M/EI)y=171.875\) MPa, so residual is plus 78.1 MPa. At the outer surface the unloading change is twice that and residual negative. Within the former tensile plateau the zero occurs at \(y=\sigma_y/[E(M/EI)]=1.455\) mm. The sign change is necessary for the residual field to carry no net moment, although the exact value follows from its detailed profile.

Tutorial · Recover a yield stress from first moment and depth

A second strip of width 15 mm and total thickness 3.0 mm first deviates reproducibly from linear bending near 6.00 N m. Infer yield stress assuming the same model, and estimate the consequence of a two-percent thickness calibration error.

Use the section modulus and its sensitivity

With \(c=1.5\) mm, \(\sigma_y=3M_y/(2bc^2)=267\) MPa. Yield stress scales inversely with squared thickness, so a two-percent overestimate of thickness biases stress downward by nearly four percent. A small proportionality deviation can also reflect fixture or material nonlinearity before conventional yield. A tensile specimen or repeated unload–reload cycles provide independent evidence of irreversibility.

Tutorial · Test whether elastic unloading remains admissible

How would you detect reverse yielding or a Bauschinger effect when applying this springback calculation to a previously prestrained alloy? What can and cannot be repaired by substituting a fitted effective Young modulus?

Check the stress path before reusing the formula

Compute the signed unloaded stresses throughout the section and compare the full unloading path against measured reverse yield behaviour. A reverse-yield threshold lower than the virgin value can be reached before the nominal moment is fully removed. Observe changes of unloading slope and hysteresis in cyclic bending or in a tension–compression test on similarly prestrained material. A fitted scalar modulus might reproduce one final curvature yet fail residual stresses and subsequent reverse loading. A history-dependent constitutive law with an appropriate hardening rule is then required for transferable predictions.

Stress, deformation and structural measurement · 4. Why an open channel twists under a transverse load

A channel can twist even when a force passes through its centroid. Trace the longitudinal shear flow around its flanges, find the load line that produces bending without torsion, and design a loading experiment that separates the stiffness in bending from eccentricity relative to this shear centre.

Find the internal horizontal force in each flange

Use a thin channel made of a vertical web of midline height \(h\) and two horizontal flanges of midline width \(b\) projecting to the same side. Take uniform thickness \(t\ll h,b\), ignore corner radii and flange through-thickness bending, and consider a vertical shear resultant \(V\). Symmetry places the shear centre on the horizontal axis through mid-height but does not place it on the web or centroid. Set the web midline at \(x=0\) and both flanges at positive \(x\).

For bending about the horizontal centroidal axis, the thin-wall second moment is \(I_x\simeq th^3/12+2bt(h/2)^2\). The usual beam shear-flow relation \(q=VQ/I_x\) follows from balancing the longitudinal stress change of a cut-off strip over an incremental beam length, using \(dM/dz=V\) with a consistent beam-axis orientation. At a free flange edge the cut-off area vanishes and the flow is zero. A distance \(s\) inwards from that free edge, \(Q\simeq t s h/2\). The flow increases linearly toward the web.

Integrating the flow over one flange gives a horizontal resultant magnitude \(F_f=Vthb^2/(4I_x)\). Top and bottom flange forces are opposing, so they contribute zero net horizontal force but a couple \(F_fh\) about the web line. The web carries the net vertical shear and its contribution has zero moment about its own midline in this thin-wall representation. Balance the internal couple with the moment of the external vertical force about the web. The shear centre is on the side opposite the flanges, at

\[x_{\rm sc}=-e,\]
\[e=\frac{th^2b^2}{4I_x}=\frac{3b^2}{h+6b}.\]

The sign describes the stated channel opening direction. Reflecting the channel reverses the sign in a fixed apparatus. The result is independent of uniform thickness in this thin-wall limit because both shear flow first moment and bending second moment scale with thickness. It does not imply that the torsional stiffness or stress is independent of thickness.

Measure a load line outside the material

Take \(h=60\) mm, \(b=30\) mm and \(t=2\) mm. The shear centre lies 11.25 mm behind the web. The thin-wall centroid is at \(x_c=b^2/(h+2b)=7.50\) mm on the opening side. A vertical load of 100 N applied through the centroid therefore introduces a torsional moment magnitude \(100(0.01875)=1.875\) N m about the shear centre. A cantilever channel clamped against warping can show both Saint-Venant twist and additional restrained-warping stresses, especially near the clamp.

Mount an adjustable loading arm across the tip and apply several known vertical forces at signed positions \(x\). In a sufficiently linear regime the measured twist under fixed load is proportional to \(x-x_{\rm sc}\). Fit its zero crossing; this eliminates the need to know absolute torsional rigidity in the first estimate. A force applied through an external bracket can reach a line outside the actual material. Ensure the bracket supplies a force and no unintended direct couple, and take readings away from local load introduction.

Find the same shear centre with a signed load-position sweep

Keep the vertical force fixed and move its line of application laterally while recording twist at a section a known distance from a clamp. In a member with constant torsional compliance in the test range, the slope of twist versus lateral position contains both the force and compliance. Its zero crossing locates the shear centre without requiring a separate torsional stiffness measurement. The second curve in the model shows the moment computed about the web. Its zero crossing is set by the coordinate origin. Only the other crossing predicts a vanishing twist of the section in the simple beam idealisation.

Open the live experiment

Repeat the sweep with the force reversed and the same attachment bracket. A constant apparatus torque shifts the two inferred zero positions in opposite directions because the applied torque per unit position changes sign. Their mean can remove a load-independent offset. A bracket whose compliance changes asymmetrically under reversal requires a separate correction. Measure twist with two gauges spanning a known width so that common vertical translation cancels. If the two gauges are far apart along the beam instead of across the same section, differing bending slopes can contaminate the apparent twist and shift the crossing.

Tutorial · Recover the shear-centre location from two angular readings

At fixed load an adjustable fixture gives twist minus 0.10 degrees when the load lies at \(x=-15.0\) mm and plus 0.30 degrees at \(x=0\). Infer the shear-centre coordinate and predict twist when the force passes through the centroid of the example.

Use signed interpolation and check the geometric result

The slope is \(0.40/15=0.0267\) degrees per millimetre. The zero crossing is \(-15.0+0.10/0.0267=-11.25\) mm, matching the predicted shear centre. At \(x=7.50\) mm the lever arm is 18.75 mm and the predicted twist 0.500 degrees. This inference assumes no independent angular offset. Reversing load lifts out an offset unchanged by force sign, while moving the bracket with no force checks alignment.

Tutorial · Predict the effect of making the flanges shorter

At unchanged web height and thickness, halve flange width to 15 mm. Predict both centroid and shear-centre location. Is their separation simply half its old value?

Recompute both first moments

The centroid is \(225/(60+30)=2.50\) mm, while \(e=3(225)/(60+90)=4.50\) mm. Their distance becomes 7.00 mm instead of 18.75 mm. Both formulas contain a squared flange width and a changing perimeter or second moment, so halving width does not simply halve the eccentricity. In the formal limit of vanishing flange width channel torsional resistance also becomes very small, requiring careful control of imperfection and fixture compliance.

Tutorial · Distinguish eccentric bending from torsional warping

A short channel shows nonzero mean twist even at the predicted load line and opposite longitudinal strains on the free flange tips near its clamp. Could changing shear modulus alone move the predicted shear centre? Suggest two specific measurement changes.

Test the assumptions that control the zero crossing

For this homogeneous thin elastic channel the centre follows force and moment equilibrium of bending shear and ideal geometry; changing one common shear modulus principally changes torsional compliance rather than moving that ideal geometric point. Compare two specimen lengths and measure twist slopes at several interior stations to identify restrained end warping. Reverse channel orientation while keeping the apparatus fixed to separate a channel-related eccentricity from a fixed bracket couple. If the discrepancy follows the specimen, measure actual corner radii, wall thickness asymmetry and local distortion and use a suitable open-section beam or shell model before attributing every measured longitudinal strain to pure flexure.
