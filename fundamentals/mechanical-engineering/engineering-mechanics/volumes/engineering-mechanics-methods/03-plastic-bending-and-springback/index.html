Springback and residual stress after plastic bending

After a rectangular strip has been bent beyond first yield, removing its end couple does not return it to its original curvature. Work out the loading and unloading paths fibre by fibre. Then explain why an unloaded strip can contain a substantial stress field with zero net force and zero net bending moment.

Locate the elastic core and compute the applied moment

Consider a straight rectangular strip of width \(b\), half-depth \(c\) and Young modulus \(E\), in pure bending with plane sections remaining plane. Assume symmetric elastic-perfectly plastic response with yield magnitude \(\sigma_y\), small strain, no hardening and no unloading-induced reverse yielding for the excursion being considered. Symmetry puts the neutral axis at mid-depth. Under increasing curvature \(\kappa\) the axial strain is \(\kappa y\); stress increases linearly until it reaches the signed yield plateau. First yield occurs at \(\kappa_y=\sigma_y/(Ec)\).

At greater curvature define \(y_e=\sigma_y/(E\kappa)\), the half-thickness of the remaining elastic core. Integrate stress times moment arm over the upper half and double. The core contributes \(2bE\kappa y_e^3/3\) and the yielded layer contributes \(b\sigma_y(c^2-y_e^2)\). Substituting the definition of \(y_e\) gives

\[M=b\sigma_y(c^2-y_e^2/3),\]
\[M_y=\frac23b\sigma_yc^2,\]
\[M_p=b\sigma_yc^2 .\]

The limiting fully plastic moment is one and a half times first-yield moment. It is approached as the elastic core shrinks; ideal plane-section theory does not justify arbitrary curvature once strains and geometry cease to be small. A load-controlled perfectly plastic model can also become indeterminate at a fully developed plastic hinge. A known curvature history is a cleaner basis for calculating springback in this ideal test.

Subtract an elastic stress field to satisfy moment release

During a purely elastic unloading of moment magnitude \(M\), every fibre responds with slope \(E\). The curvature decreases by \(M/(EI)\), where \(I=2bc^3/3\). The remaining curvature and signed residual stress profile are

\[\kappa_r=\kappa-\frac{M}{EI},\]
\[\sigma_r(y)=\sigma_{\rm load}(y)-\frac{M}{I}y .\]

The antisymmetric residual stresses have zero net force. Their bending resultant is zero because the second term removes exactly the original moment. A free strip can therefore remain curved even though its internal stress has neither an axial nor a bending resultant. Its plastic strain varies through the thickness and makes the unstrained reference of each fibre incompatible with one common straight shape.

Take \(E=200\) GPa, \(\sigma_y=250\) MPa, \(b=20\) mm and \(c=2.0\) mm. The yield curvature is 0.625 per metre, first moment 13.33 N m and plastic limit 20.0 N m. At curvature 1.25 per metre, \(y_e=1.0\) mm and applied moment 18.33 N m. Bending stiffness is 21.33 N square metres and elastic curvature recovery 0.859 per metre. The unloaded curvature is about 0.391 per metre. The surface formerly in tension ends up in compression of about 93.75 MPa, well below reverse yield under this idealised law.

Measure curvature and residual stress with different observables

A photograph of the unloaded centreline can determine residual curvature but cannot recover the full through-thickness stress profile. A single strain gauge bonded before loading measures total strain change at its location; separating plastic strain from elastic residual strain requires a constitutive model or an independent release measurement. If a thin surface layer is removed, the remaining section can change curvature as its previously self-equilibrated stress resultants are unbalanced. That change contains information about the removed residual stress but needs a recalculated section geometry.

Measure moment and curvature through loading and initial unloading as separate data sets. The small unloading slope gives an independent estimate of \(EI\), while the first slope departure during loading constrains yield. Friction at fixture rollers, nonuniform moment and axial tension during forming can produce an asymmetric neutral axis that the simple pure-couple solution cannot fit. Reversing the strip or comparing strains on both faces is an inexpensive diagnostic.

Look inside the strip after the applied moment vanishes

In the plot compare the stress at the surface with that on the boundary of the surviving elastic core. The applied stress there reaches yield but the subsequent elastic unloading stress is proportional to distance from the middle. The surface therefore experiences a greater stress change during unloading than the point at the edge of the core. This permits a tensile residual just inside a compressive outer skin on one side. Reflection about the middle reverses all the signs. A surface strain reading alone cannot reveal that self-equilibrated pattern.

Open the live experiment

Check the area under the residual stress over the entire depth. Antisymmetry makes the axial resultant vanish. The moment also vanishes, which follows because subtracting the elastic unloading distribution subtracts exactly the applied moment. The two cancellation tests are stronger than checking the stress at one point. They can be applied to a numerical fibre calculation even when hardening changes the shape of each branch. If an experiment instead clamps the strip straight after bending, the imposed curvature differs from free springback and a reaction moment appears. Compare the same unloading boundary condition before fitting a hardening coefficient.

Tutorial · Predict a residual profile at the elastic-core edge

For the numerical strip above calculate the residual stress at \(y=+1.0\) mm after unloading, and compare its sign with the outer surface. Where in the originally tensile outer layer is residual stress zero?

Use the loading plateau and the unloading slope

The point at the core boundary reached plus 250 MPa. Its elastic unloading change is \(E(M/EI)y=171.875\) MPa, so residual is plus 78.1 MPa. At the outer surface the unloading change is twice that and residual negative. Within the former tensile plateau the zero occurs at \(y=\sigma_y/[E(M/EI)]=1.455\) mm. The sign change is necessary for the residual field to carry no net moment, although the exact value follows from its detailed profile.

Tutorial · Recover a yield stress from first moment and depth

A second strip of width 15 mm and total thickness 3.0 mm first deviates reproducibly from linear bending near 6.00 N m. Infer yield stress assuming the same model, and estimate the consequence of a two-percent thickness calibration error.

Use the section modulus and its sensitivity

With \(c=1.5\) mm, \(\sigma_y=3M_y/(2bc^2)=267\) MPa. Yield stress scales inversely with squared thickness, so a two-percent overestimate of thickness biases stress downward by nearly four percent. A small proportionality deviation can also reflect fixture or material nonlinearity before conventional yield. A tensile specimen or repeated unload–reload cycles provide independent evidence of irreversibility.

Tutorial · Test whether elastic unloading remains admissible

How would you detect reverse yielding or a Bauschinger effect when applying this springback calculation to a previously prestrained alloy? What can and cannot be repaired by substituting a fitted effective Young modulus?

Check the stress path before reusing the formula

Compute the signed unloaded stresses throughout the section and compare the full unloading path against measured reverse yield behaviour. A reverse-yield threshold lower than the virgin value can be reached before the nominal moment is fully removed. Observe changes of unloading slope and hysteresis in cyclic bending or in a tension–compression test on similarly prestrained material. A fitted scalar modulus might reproduce one final curvature yet fail residual stresses and subsequent reverse loading. A history-dependent constitutive law with an appropriate hardening rule is then required for transferable predictions.
