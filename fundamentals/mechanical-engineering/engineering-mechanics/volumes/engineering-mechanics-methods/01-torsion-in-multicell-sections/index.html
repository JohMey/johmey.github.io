Torsion in a thin-walled two-cell section

Two closed cells share one slender web. The applied couple gives only their combined torque, yet the common web can carry much less shear than either exterior wall. Derive the missing compatibility equation, calculate a twist and test which measurements distinguish the two circulations.

Obtain one torque equation from traction

Consider an initially straight prismatic member of length much greater than its section dimensions. The walls are thin relative to cell dimensions, shear modulus is uniform and Saint-Venant torsion is established away from ends. Define shear flow \(q=\tau t\), the tangential force transmitted per unit member length along the wall midline. In a closed single cell without a longitudinal body load, equilibrium makes its circulatory shear flow constant. Each tangential force around the contour contributes a torque, and the line integral of the moment arm gives twice the enclosed midline area. Consequently \(T=2Aq\).

For two cells choose positive circulation with the same sense in both. Superposition gives

\[T=2A_1q_1+2A_2q_2.\]

On the shared wall the two contour directions oppose each other, so its actual flow is \(q_1-q_2\) when viewed along cell one's circulation. Adding the two flows there would violate the chosen orientation. This torque equation alone cannot determine two unknown circulations. Treating each as proportional to area would be a guess about stiffness, not a derivation.

Enforce equal twist on both closed contours

The tangential shear strain integrated around a closed contour is related to axial twist rate \(\theta'\). Under the thin-wall Saint-Venant assumptions, warping displacement is single-valued after a closed circuit, leaving \(2A\theta'=\oint q/(Gt)\,ds\). Let \(C_1,C_2\) denote integrals of \(ds/t\) along the two exterior contours excluding their common wall and \(C_w\) its shared-wall integral. Compatibility then requires

\[2A_1G\theta'=C_1q_1+C_w(q_1-q_2),\]
\[2A_2G\theta'=C_2q_2+C_w(q_2-q_1).\]

The common twist is a geometrical condition because the cells belong to one connected section. With different materials replace each \(ds/(Gt)\) term individually; pulling out one common \(G\) would then be invalid. For equal exterior geometry the equations give equal flows and zero net web shear, while the web can still stabilise shape and carry other load cases.

Take heights 40 mm and widths 60 and 40 mm, with 1 mm walls throughout, \(G=27\) GPa and \(T=10\) N m. The areas are 2400 and 1600 square millimetres. Exterior and common geometric coefficients are

\[C_1=160,C_2=120,C_w=40.\]

Solving gives flows 1.287 and 1.195 kN/m, a shared difference 0.092 kN/m, and twist rate 0.001617 rad/m. The exterior wall stresses equal their flow divided by thickness and are therefore 1.287 and 1.195 MPa. Over a one-metre span the ideal rotation is only 0.0926 degrees.

Background · Why does a shared wall carry the difference of the cell flows?

Trace a positive circulation counterclockwise around each closed cell. Along the common wall the two traces point in opposite directions. Give the common wall the positive direction of cell 1. Its physical shear flow is then \(q_1-q_2\); it is this flow divided by thickness that produces the local shear stress. For a common wall of length \(l\) and constant thickness \(t\) the stored shear energy per unit beam length is

\[U^{\prime}_{\rm common}=\frac{l}{2Gt}(q_1-q_2)^2.\]

For identical cells under symmetric torsion, equal circulations leave the common wall unstressed in this ideal model even though the outer walls carry torque. If the common wall is very thin, its large compliance increasingly penalises unequal circulations. This energy check fixes the relative sign without depending on how the cross-section was drawn.

Which measurement fixes which resistance?

Sweep the right width through the fixed left width. The shared flow should pass smoothly through zero while the outer walls continue transmitting the global torque. Increase the web thickness. Its \(C_w\) decreases and it can transfer a given difference at smaller shear strain. The exterior walls and compatibility still control the complete response; the common wall need not carry a large flow simply because it is thick. Measure external twist and at least one wall strain if the individual circulations are the quantities of interest.

A diagonal strain-gauge pair on a thin wall gives an approximate shear strain when the gauges sample in-plane strain at plus and minus forty-five degrees. Their difference rejects common normal strain to first order. At a predicted 0.092 MPa common-wall shear, the strain is only about 3.4 microstrain for the stated modulus. A one-axis gauge zero error can therefore overwhelm the common-wall signal even while exterior torque and twist appear accurate. Confirm the sign by reversing the applied couple.

Check the wall flows by minimising strain energy

A second derivation makes an effective independent numerical test. Under a torque constraint choose the two cell flows so that the sum of their area moments gives the required torque. Their elastic energy per unit length is the sum of three terms, one from each group's exclusive walls and one from the shared wall:

\[U'=\frac1{2G}\left(C_1q_1^2+C_2q_2^2+C_w(q_1-q_2)^2\right).\]

Perturb the first circulation while adjusting the second to keep torque constant. At the physical solution this variation cannot lower the energy. The resulting stationarity condition reproduces equal twist, including the minus sign on the common wall. A program that accidentally squares the sum on that wall would predict positive strain energy there even for two identical cells at equal flow. This unit test takes no measured twist as input and pinpoints a common implementation error. A second comparison differentiates the minimum energy with respect to applied torque and obtains twist per unit length. Because all wall stresses are linear in torque in this model, doubling torque doubles twist and quadruples stored energy. In a bench specimen, rapid departures from this scaling can reveal slip at a seam or local buckling before individual strain gauges identify which wall has changed.

Tutorial · Test a symmetry prediction numerically

Make both cells 50 mm wide with the same height and identical exterior walls. Find each circulating flow at 10 N m. Does changing only shared-web thickness affect ideal torsional twist in this exactly symmetric case?

Use symmetry before solving two equations

Each area is 0.0020 square metres. Equal flows give

\[q=T/[2(A_1+A_2)]=1250\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

The net web flow vanishes identically and hence its shear-compliance contribution drops out of both contour equations. With unchanged exterior geometry the ideal twist is unchanged by web thickness in this case. A measured change could reflect local distortion, end constraints, material changes during fabrication or other loads outside the thin-wall torsion model.

Tutorial · Infer torque from imperfect shear gauges

In the original unequal section calibrated exterior gauges indicate 1.30 and 1.18 MPa. Recover torque and predict shared-wall shear. Why is the torque result less sensitive to the gauge fluctuations than the difference?

Propagate the measured flows

At one millimetre thickness the flows are 1300 and 1180 N/m. Torque is \(2(0.0024\cdot1300+0.0016\cdot1180)=10.016\) N m. Predicted shared flow is 120 N/m, or 0.120 MPa. Independent small errors affect a sum of two large positive contributions only modestly in relative terms, while the shared difference is a much smaller residual. Check twist compatibility as a separate residual instead of concluding from the accurate torque alone that both gauge readings are correct.

Tutorial · Identify a missing effect at a clamped end

A very short specimen twists less than the prediction and shows high longitudinal stresses near the clamp. Which model assumptions are questionable, and how could two specimen lengths test them without changing cross-section?

Compare distributed and end contributions

Restrained warping, local load introduction and section distortion can create additional stresses and stiffness near the clamps. Fabricate geometrically similar fixtures at two or more lengths and compare differential rotation between interior stations per unit gauge length. An approximately constant interior slope matching the derived twist with endpoint departures supports a local end effect. If the slope itself varies throughout, check actual wall thickness, joint continuity, shear modulus and distortion. Increasing the number of contour integration points in an otherwise prismatic free-warping model does not recover a missing boundary mechanism.
