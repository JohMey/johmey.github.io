Constraints and Lagrange’s equations

Classical mechanics

Eliminate an unknown tension with a generalised coordinate and identify how a pulley’s rotational inertia changes an acceleration.

From first principles

Derive the one-coordinate Lagrange equation from virtual work

For point masses with fixed, smooth ideal constraints, describe all positions as \(\mathbf r_i(q)\). An allowed instantaneous virtual displacement is

\[\delta\mathbf r_i=(\partial_q\mathbf r_i)\delta q.\]

Assume constraint reactions do zero total virtual work. Newton's equations projected along this common displacement therefore eliminate the unmeasured reactions:

\[\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i\cdot\partial_q\mathbf r_i=\sum_i\mathbf F_i^{\rm applied}\cdot\partial_q\mathbf r_i=Q .\]

Use \(K=\sum_i m_i\mathbf v_i^2/2\) and \(\mathbf v_i=(\partial_q\mathbf r_i)\dot q\). Since \(\partial_{\dot q}\mathbf v_i=\partial_q\mathbf r_i\), differentiate \(\partial_{\dot q}K\) in time. The product term involving \(d(\partial_q\mathbf r_i)/dt\) equals \(\partial_q K\). Subtracting it leaves

\[\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i\cdot\partial_q\mathbf r_i=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial K}{\partial q}.\]

If the applied forces derive from a potential \(U(q)\) then \(Q=-\partial_qU\). Moving the potential term gives the equation for \(L=K-U\):

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0 .\]

The coordinate choice can eliminate a tension without assuming that tension vanishes. A rolling constraint requires the virtual displacements to obey its no-slip condition.

Background · Action
\[S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt\]

Compare nearby admissible paths with the specified endpoint data. Stationarity gives a local equation of motion. In SI the action has units of joule seconds.

Derivation and applications

How does a moving pulley change the inertia seen by a load?

A string constraint fixes acceleration ratios before tensions are known. Consider a mass \(M\) carried by a light movable pulley. One end of a single inextensible string is fixed to the ceiling. It passes around the movable pulley, over a fixed pulley of radius \(R\) and moment of inertia \(I\), and down to a free counterweight \(m\). Use downward coordinate \(q\) for the movable assembly and downward coordinate \(y\) for the counterweight. The fixed pulley rotates freely about its axle. Assume a massless string, no slip, and constant lengths of the wrapped arcs.

The moving pulley changes the lengths of two vertical segments. Differentiate the length constraint to determine both the counterweight speed and the rim speed of the fixed pulley:

\[2q+y=\text{constant},\]
\[\dot y=-2\dot q,\]
\[|\omega|=2|\dot q|/R .\]

Write all kinetic and potential energies in terms of the single coordinate. Downward displacement reduces gravitational potential, and raising the counterweight contributes with the opposite sign:

\[K=\tfrac12\!\left(M+4m+4I/R^2\right)\dot q^2,\]
\[U=(-M+2m)gq+\text{constant}.\]

The Lagrange equation equates the rate of change of generalized momentum to the generalized force. The velocity ratios enter the inertia squared, whereas the displacement ratios enter gravitational work linearly:

\[(M+4m+4I/R^2)\ddot q=(M-2m)g.\]

All terms multiplying acceleration have units of mass. An ideal fixed pulley with a heavy rim still slows the motion, even though its centre does not translate. At \(M=2m\) the gravitational driving force vanishes for every value of \(I\).

Worked example

Predict a descending load and an ascending counterweight

Set \(M=1.20\) kg, \(m=0.400\) kg, \(R=0.0500\) m and \(I=2.50\times10^{-4}\) kg m². The rotational contribution is \(0.400\) kg and the effective mass is \(3.20\) kg. The driving force is \(0.400g=3.924\) N, giving \(\ddot q=1.23\) m/s² downward and counterweight acceleration \(2.45\) m/s² upward. A release from rest moves the load \(0.153\) m in \(0.500\) s if sufficient travel is available.

Second application

Measure a pulley inertia from a reversed acceleration

With the same \(M\) and \(R\), change the counterweight to \(m=0.800\) kg. A camera records upward load acceleration \(0.8175\) m/s². The signed numerator is now \(-3.924\) N, so the measured effective mass is \(4.80\) kg. Subtracting \(M+4m=4.40\) kg leaves \(4I/R^2=0.400\) kg, recovering \(I=2.50\times10^{-4}\) kg m² to measurement precision. The reversal tests both the sign of gravitational work and the factor of two in the length constraint.

Which parts of a vertical loop need a two-sided constraint?

A bead of mass 100 g moves in a frictionless circular groove of radius \(R\). It starts at the bottom with speed \(v_0\). At angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical, energy conservation gives

\[v^2=v_0^2-2gR(1-\cos\theta).\]

The inward radial equation is

\[N-mg\cos\theta=mv^2/R.\]

The sign of the groove force \(N\) is measurable. A two-sided groove can reverse this force; a bead held only against the inside of a smooth rail would lose contact where the required inward force first becomes negative.

Count independent motions

Two hanging masses and a massive pulley appear to introduce two translations, a rotation and several forces. Yet an inextensible taut string and rolling contact without slip leave just one independent motion. Let mass \(m_2\) descend a distance \(q\) while mass \(m_1\) rises by the same distance. For a fixed axle and pulley radius \(R\), the rotation is \(\theta=q/R\) relative to its initial value. Assume the masses stay clear of the pulley, the string is light, the axle friction is negligible and the pulley’s moment of inertia about the axle is \(I\).

A constraint relates permitted displacements before any force balance is written. The fact that both ends move the same distance follows from length conservation. It does not require equal tensions. A massive accelerating pulley needs a net torque, so replacing the tensions by a common value would remove its angular acceleration from the model. This example is a useful test of when a constraint allows us to remove coordinates and when it also allows us to remove forces.

Background · How can an ideal constraint transfer energy?

A bead slides without friction on a rail whose shape and position can change with time. An admissible virtual displacement compares nearby positions at the same time. It is tangent to the rail, so the normal reaction does no virtual work. The actual laboratory velocity also contains the motion of the rail itself. Write a point on the rail as \(\mathbf r(q,t)\) and take its time derivative.

\[P_{\rm rail}=\mathbf N\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf N\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial q}\dot q+\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial t}\right)=\mathbf N\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf r}{\partial t}.\]

A horizontal rail lifted vertically at constant speed \(u\) gives the simplest check. The bead's vertical acceleration is zero and the normal force is \(mg\). The rail supplies power \(mgu\), exactly the rate at which gravitational potential energy rises. The constraint can be ideal while an external actuator supplies the energy.

Construct the stationarity equation

For ideal time-independent constraints the reaction forces do no total work on allowed virtual displacements. Form the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L=K-U\) using the independent coordinates. Consider two paths with the same initial and final values of \(q\), differing by a small variation \(\delta q(t)\). The first variation of the action is \(\int(\partial_q\mathcal L\,\delta q+\partial_{\dot q}\mathcal L\,\delta\dot q)dt\). Integrate the second term by parts. The endpoint term vanishes and stationarity for arbitrary interior variations requires

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q}=0.\]

This equation supplies one independent balance for each independent coordinate. If an applied nonconservative force does work \(\delta W=Q\,\delta q\), its generalised force \(Q\) enters the right-hand side. For an angular coordinate \(Q\) has units of torque; for this translational coordinate it has units of force. It must be calculated using the same sign choice as the coordinate.

Find a conserved quantity from a continuous symmetry

Constraints identify which coordinates can vary independently. A symmetry asks a different question: which small change of those coordinates leaves the action unchanged? Let \(q_i\mapsto q_i+\varepsilon\xi_i(q,t)\) at fixed time and \(p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i\). Along a solution the Euler–Lagrange equations turn the variation into a total derivative,

\[\frac{\delta L}{\varepsilon}=\sum_i\left(\dot p_i\xi_i+p_i\dot\xi_i\right)=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i p_i\xi_i .\]

If the left side vanishes for every path, \(Q=\sum_i p_i\xi_i\) is conserved on solutions. More generally, a change \(\delta L=\varepsilon\,dB/dt\) changes only endpoint terms in the action, and \(Q=\sum_i p_i\xi_i-B\) is conserved. This is Noether's theorem for this fixed-time transformation. Separate conservation of mechanical energy requires time-translation symmetry.

Consider a bead of mass \(m\) on a smooth horizontal table. A light string attached to the bead passes through a central hole. Pulling the string prescribes the radius \(r(t)\). Its tension has no azimuthal component. Rotation of the entire trajectory through a constant azimuth leaves \(L=m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)/2\) unchanged. Therefore \(mr^2\dot\phi\) is constant even when a pulling device supplies work. For \(m=0.250\) kg, \(r_i=0.400\) m and \(\dot\phi_i=3.00\) rad/s, \(Q=0.120\) kg m²/s. Halving the radius raises angular speed to \(12.0\) rad/s. With zero radial speed at each endpoint, kinetic energy rises from \(0.180\) to \(0.720\) J. A central force can do work while exerting no torque about the centre.

Add every energy contribution

Both hanging masses move at speed \(|\dot q|\). The pulley contributes rotational kinetic energy \(I(\dot q/R)^2/2\). Raising the first mass increases its gravitational potential, while lowering the second decreases it. Omitting an arbitrary constant gives

\[U=(m_1-m_2)gq.\]

The full kinetic energy is

\[K=(m_1+m_2+I/R^2)\dot q^2/2.\]

Substitution yields

\[(m_1+m_2+I/R^2)\ddot q=(m_2-m_1)g.\]

The quantity \(I/R^2\) enters like an additional translational mass. It stores kinetic energy without changing the gravitational driving force. A constant opposing axle torque of magnitude \(\tau_f\) would give a generalised resisting force \(\tau_f/R\) while the second mass descends. Applying a single constant sign across a reversal would incorrectly model dry friction.

Verify the reactions by restoring the constraints

Take \(m_1=1.00\) kg, \(m_2=1.50\) kg, \(R=0.100\) m and \(I=0.0050\) kg·m². The effective mass is 3.00 kg and the unbalanced weight is 4.905 N. Thus \(\ddot q=1.635\) m/s². From rest, a descent of 0.300 m produces a speed \(\sqrt{2(1.635)(0.300)}=0.990\) m/s. The drop in potential energy is 1.472 J, equal to the gain in translational and rotational energy together.

Now isolate each hanging body. Upward acceleration on the lighter side requires

\[T_1=m_1(g+\ddot q)=11.45\,\mathrm{N}.\]

The heavier side gives

\[T_2=m_2(g-\ddot q)=12.26\,\mathrm{N}.\]

The tension difference times radius is about 0.082 N·m. The pulley independently requires

\[I\ddot q/R=0.0818\,\mathrm{N\,m}.\]

Agreement checks both the direction convention and the contribution of pulley inertia. The energy description solved for observable motion first. The separate body balances recovered forces that a mount or a string must actually withstand.

A useful check is to let one inertia become dominant. If the pulley’s moment of inertia is made very large while its radius and both hanging masses stay fixed, the effective inertia in the denominator grows and acceleration approaches zero. The two string tensions then approach their respective hanging weights, which can remain unequal despite the tiny acceleration. The finite difference supplies torque to a very large rotational inertia. Equality of tension throughout a string that wraps around a massive pulley is therefore an additional physical assumption that must be justified.

Rolling without slipping and the force enforcing it

A constraint can remove an unknown contact force from the equation of motion while leaving a definite physical condition for its realization. Consider a cylinder of mass \(m\), radius \(R\) and moment of inertia \(I\) about its centre, rolling straight down a plane inclined at angle \(\alpha\). Let \(x\) increase down the plane, and take the spin angle \(\theta\) positive for forward rolling. A stationary contact point on the plane requires

\[\dot x=R\dot\theta.\]

In this straight-line geometry the velocity constraint integrates directly to \(x-R\theta=\mathrm{constant}\). General rolling on a surface with changing direction requires more care because the analogous constraints need not integrate to coordinate relations.

Use \(x\) as the sole coordinate. The centre falls through \(x\sin\alpha\) and kinetic energy contains both translation and rotation. The reduced Lagrangian is

\[L=\tfrac12(m+I/R^2)\dot x^2+mgx\sin\alpha .\]

The Euler–Lagrange equation gives

\[\ddot x=g\sin\alpha/(1+I/(mR^2)).\]

For a body released from rest, the speed follows by integrating \(\dot x\,d\dot x/dx=\ddot x\). Different mass distributions at fixed radius and mass therefore arrive at different times even if their initial gravitational energies are equal. Some of that energy appears in spin, whose contribution grows with \(I\).

To determine the unknown friction, return to the original translational and rotational balances. If \(f\) points up the plane,

\[m\ddot x=mg\sin\alpha-f,\]
\[I\ddot\theta=fR.\]

Combining with the rolling constraint gives

\[f=(I/R^2)\ddot x.\]

The absence of \(f\) from the reduced Lagrangian came from zero virtual work under an admissible rolling displacement. It did not imply zero force.

Decide whether a solid cylinder will actually roll

A uniform solid cylinder has \(I=mR^2/2\). Let \(m=2.00\) kg, \(R=0.100\) m and \(\alpha=30.0^\circ\). Predicted acceleration is

\[9.81(0.5)/(1.5)=3.27\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.\]

The required upward friction is \((m/2)\ddot x=3.27\) N, while the normal force is

\[mg\cos30^\circ=17.0\,\mathrm{N}.\]

Static friction can maintain rolling only for \(\mu_s\ge f/N=0.192\). This comparison tests an assumption that could not be checked from the acceleration equation alone.

After travelling 1.00 m from rest a rolling cylinder reaches \(2.56\) m/s and spins at \(25.6\) rad/s. Translational energy is \(6.54\) J and rotational energy is \(3.27\) J, accounting for the \(9.81\) J released by gravity. In the limit \(I/(mR^2)\to0\), nearly all energy goes into translation, acceleration tends to \(g\sin\alpha\) and the required friction tends to zero. A surface with a measured coefficient below the threshold instead permits slipping. The translation and rotation must then be solved independently using the sliding contact law.

Rolling tests can be run without knowing the cylinder’s mass distribution in advance. Obtain acceleration from two light gates at known positions and use \(I/(mR^2)=g\sin\alpha/\ddot x-1\) to infer a dimensionless inertia. Repeat at a shallower inclination. A genuine moment of inertia has the same inferred value in both runs, whereas slipping can appear only above a limiting angle. The most direct test tracks a painted mark on the rim as well as the centre. Admissible rolling requires the increase in centre displacement to equal radius times accumulated spin angle in each time interval.

Hamiltonian motion in phase space

Classical mechanics

Construct a Hamiltonian, read a phase portrait and distinguish bounded motion from rotation at a separatrix.

From first principles

Construct Hamilton’s equations by exchanging velocity for momentum

Begin with a smooth Lagrangian \(L(q,\dot q,t)\) for a conservative mechanical system. Assume the momentum definition \(p=\partial_{\dot q}L\) can locally be inverted to give \(\dot q\) as a function of \((q,p,t)\). This regularity is what lets position and momentum serve as independent state coordinates. Define the Hamiltonian and differentiate the product before substituting any equations of motion:

\[H=p\dot q-L,\]
\[dH=\dot q\,dp+p\,d\dot q-L_q\,dq-L_{\dot q}\,d\dot q-L_t\,dt .\]

The two \(d\dot q\) terms cancel by the momentum definition. Comparing the coefficients of the independent differentials gives

\[H_p=\dot q,\]
\[H_q=-L_q.\]

Hamilton's principle previously gave the Lagrange equation \(\dot p=L_q\), so

\[\dot q=\partial_pH,\]
\[\dot p=-\partial_qH,\]
\[\partial_tH=-\partial_tL .\]

Along a solution the \(q\) and \(p\) contributions to \(dH/dt\) cancel. For a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H\) is constant, so planar trajectories lie on its level sets. In the elementary case \(L=m\dot q^2/2-U(q)\), the transformation yields

\[H=p^2/(2m)+U(q).\]
Background · Generalised momentum
\[p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i\]

Its units depend on the coordinate. Conjugacy to an angle gives an angular momentum. Conservation requires the associated coordinate to be cyclic in the chosen Lagrangian.

Derivation and applications

How Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space area

An energy contour shows where a single trajectory may lie. To learn what the dynamics does to nearby initial conditions, follow a small patch of the canonical \((q,p)\) plane. Consider one degree of freedom and a twice continuously differentiable Hamiltonian. Let the system advance by a short time \(\delta t\). To first order the coordinate map is a displacement along the Hamiltonian velocity field:

\[q'=q+H_p\,\delta t,\]
\[p'=p-H_q\,\delta t .\]

The area of a sufficiently small parallelogram changes by the determinant of this map's derivative. Differentiate with respect to both initial coordinates. The product of off-diagonal elements enters only at second order:

\[\det\frac{\partial(q',p')}{\partial(q,p)}=(1+H_{pq}\delta t)(1-H_{qp}\delta t)+H_{pp}H_{qq}\delta t^2=1+O(\delta t^2).\]

For the exact continuous map its area rate is the divergence of the phase velocity. Equality of the mixed second derivatives gives

\[\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=\partial_q\dot q+\partial_p\dot p=H_{pq}-H_{qp}=0\]

for an infinitesimal material area \(A\). Integrating adjacent patches gives the same conclusion for a finite region. The outline can stretch and rotate. Even explicit time dependence of \(H\) does not change this volume result. A friction force imposed independently of the Hamiltonian can change it. The second-order remainder above belongs to a short expansion; repeatedly taking finite steps with an approximate rule can accumulate a spurious change.

Worked example

Track a cloud in a harmonic trap

Prepare a small rectangular set of initial states, \(2.00\) mm wide in position and \(0.0100\) kg m/s wide in momentum. Its area is \(2.00\times10^{-5}\) J s. In a trap of mass \(0.500\) kg and frequency \(\omega=4.00\) rad/s, the exact quarter-period map is \(q'=p/(m\omega)\), \(p'=-m\omega q\). The new widths are \(5.00\) mm and \(0.00400\) kg m/s. Their product retains the original area even though neither width is conserved.

Second application

Diagnose a numerical phase portrait

An explicit Euler step in the same trap uses \(q'=q+p\delta t/m\) and \(p'=p-m\omega^2q\delta t\). Its determinant is exactly \(1+\omega^2\delta t^2\). At \(\delta t=0.0500\) s it expands area by \(1.04\) per step, giving \(1.04^{50}=7.11\) times the initial area after 50 steps. A graph of the growing cloud could look like physical heating. The determinant exposes a discretization error without requiring an exact trajectory for each point.

Position needs a conjugate momentum

The position of a pendulum at one instant does not say which way it will swing. A state needs both a configuration coordinate and a momentum. In Lagrangian mechanics define the canonical momentum by \(p=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q\). It can have different physical dimensions depending on the coordinate. For a particle on a line it has units of linear momentum. For a pendulum angle it has units of angular momentum. The pair \((q,p)\) forms a two-dimensional phase space for one degree of freedom.

Provided the relation between \(p\) and \(\dot q\) can be inverted, define \(H(q,p,t)=p\dot q-\mathcal L(q,\dot q,t)\) with the velocity expressed in terms of \(q,p\). Take a differential. The terms proportional to \(d\dot q\) cancel by the definition of \(p\). Using Lagrange’s equation \(\partial_q\mathcal L=\dot p\) gives

\[dH=\dot q\,dp-\dot p\,dq-\partial_t\mathcal L\,dt.\]

Matching the coefficients produces Hamilton’s equations

\[\dot q=\partial_p H,\]
\[\dot p=-\partial_q H.\]

For a system with ordinary quadratic kinetic energy, a velocity-independent potential and fixed constraints, \(H=K+U\). Its change along a solution is \(dH/dt=\partial_tH\) because the two terms containing the derivatives with respect to \(q,p\) cancel. A time-independent Hamiltonian confines the trajectory to a level set.

Use Poisson brackets to test conservation

Hamilton's two equations specify how \(q_i\) and \(p_i\) change. To follow an observable \(A(q,p,t)\), apply the chain rule and substitute \(\dot q_i=\partial H/\partial p_i\) and \(\dot p_i=-\partial H/\partial q_i\). This defines the Poisson bracket,

\[\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+\{A,H\},\]
\[\{A,B\}=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_i}\right).\]

Since \(\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}\), a candidate change of phase-space coordinates must preserve these brackets to be canonical. A vanishing bracket with \(H\) certifies conservation for an observable having no explicit time dependence. It is a local calculation and does not require solving an orbit.

For a particle in a two-dimensional trap set \(H=(p_x^2+p_y^2)/(2m)+(k_xx^2+k_yy^2)/2\) and \(L_z=xp_y-yp_x\). The momentum derivatives in the bracket cancel the two kinetic contributions. The remaining coordinate terms give \(\dot L_z=(k_x-k_y)xy\), exactly the torque \(xF_y-yF_x\). With \(k_x=4.00\) N/m, \(k_y=6.00\) N/m and instantaneous position \((0.030,0.040)\) m, angular momentum decreases at \(2.40\times10^{-3}\) N m. At a point on either axis the instantaneous derivative vanishes even for the anisotropic trap. For a conservation law holding on every trajectory the expression must vanish throughout phase space, as it does when \(k_x=k_y\).

Draw energy contours before integrating

A rigid pendulum of mass \(m\) and length \(\ell\) has \(p=m\ell^2\dot q\) and \(H=p^2/(2m\ell^2)+mg\ell(1-\cos q)\). At the bottom \(q=0,p=0\) is a stable equilibrium. Low-energy contours are ellipses to leading order because the cosine potential is quadratic there. Each closed contour returns to its original phase point after one full oscillation. Points of greatest \(|p|\) are fastest and occur at the bottom.

The top \(q=\pi,p=0\) has energy \(2mg\ell\). Its contour separates swinging trajectories from trajectories that keep rotating in one direction. This separating curve is the separatrix. Since angles differing by \(2\pi\) label the same physical position, the full portrait lives on a cylinder. A rotation winds around that cylinder. As a trajectory approaches the separatrix, it spends a long time near the unstable top, so the small-angle period no longer estimates its return time accurately.

Compare thresholds and phase velocities

For \(m=0.50\) kg, \(\ell=0.80\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s², the potential barrier is 7.85 J. A start from the bottom at 5.0 rad/s supplies kinetic energy \(0.5(0.50)(0.80)^2(5.0)^2=4.00\) J. At the turning point \(\cos q=1-4.00/(0.50\times9.81\times0.80)=-0.0194\). The maximum angle is about 91.1°. Energy classifies this as a swing even though a small-angle expansion would be inaccurate at the turning point.

Continuous rotation requires bottom speed above \(\sqrt{4g/\ell}=7.00\) rad/s for this ideal pendulum. At precisely that value the system approaches the upper equilibrium asymptotically. This statement assumes a rigid link that can sustain compression. A pendulum made from a flexible string may lose tension before reaching the top, in which case the assumed circular constraint fails.

Check a numerical phase portrait

For any smooth one-degree Hamiltonian the divergence of the phase velocity is

\[\partial_q\dot q+\partial_p\dot p=H_{pq}-H_{qp}=0.\]

A small patch of initial states can shear and stretch while preserving its phase-space area. Viscous damping would instead contract the patch. Numerical time integration can also produce apparent contraction or growth, so refining the step is essential before interpreting a spiral as evidence of friction.

For the harmonic oscillator, ordinary forward Euler gives

\[q'=q+\Delta t\,p/m,\]
\[p'=p-\Delta t\,kq.\]

The determinant of this linear update is \(1+\Delta t^2 k/m\), larger than one at every step. The spurious outward spiral follows from the numerical map alone. Updating momentum first and then using the new momentum for the position gives determinant one. That area property preserves the geometric structure, though it does not by itself guarantee accurate phase for a coarse step. Keep both geometry and timing in view when judging numerical trajectories.

The area inside a closed harmonic-oscillator contour provides another numerical test. Its semiaxes are \(\sqrt{2E/k}\) in position and \(\sqrt{2mE}\) in momentum. Their product times \(\pi\) gives \(2\pi E/\omega\). Increasing oscillator energy therefore expands the area in direct proportion. A phase portrait plotted using velocity replaces every momentum value by its value divided by mass, changing numerical areas and their units. Specify the vertical coordinate before comparing this diagnostic between models with different masses.

A slowly changing spring transports a phase-space area

Suppose an oscillator's spring stiffness can be varied externally during the motion. Its Hamiltonian \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega(t)^2q^2/2\) now depends explicitly on time, so ordinary energy conservation does not apply. At an instantaneous point on a trajectory Hamilton's equations make the terms involving \(\dot p\) and \(\dot q\) cancel in \(dH/dt\). The remaining change is

\[dE/dt=\partial H/\partial t=m\omega\dot\omega q^2.\]

Energy is supplied when the imposed parameter raises the potential at the current displacement.

Take \(|\dot\omega|\ll\omega^2\) so that many cycles occur while the frequency changes by an appreciable fraction. Over one cycle \(\omega\) and \(E\) may then be treated as nearly fixed. Because average potential and kinetic energies of a harmonic oscillator are equal,

\[\langle q^2\rangle=E/(m\omega^2).\]

Inserting this mean into the work rate gives the averaged relation

\[\frac{dE}{dt}\simeq\frac{E}{\omega}\dot\omega,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac E\omega\right)\simeq0 .\]

The quantity \(J=E/\omega\) is the area enclosed by the instantaneous constant-energy ellipse divided by \(2\pi\). Calling it an adiabatic invariant states both the approximation and the observable. A slowly changing system migrates between contours of different energies in a way that preserves this action to leading order. This mechanical meaning of adiabatic concerns a timescale ratio. The derivation does not assume heat exchange, dissipation or a thermodynamic temperature.

Compare a slow compression with a sudden change

A \(0.500\) kg oscillator has initial angular frequency \(20.0\) rad/s and amplitude \(0.0400\) m. Its energy is \(m\omega_i^2 A_i^2/2=0.160\) J and initial action is \(0.00800\) J s. Raise the frequency smoothly to \(40.0\) rad/s over many periods. Action conservation predicts \(E_f=0.320\) J and final amplitude \(\sqrt{2E_f/(m\omega_f^2)}=0.0283\) m. The external control supplies work even as the maximum displacement decreases.

For comparison, change the stiffness instantaneously at a turning point, retaining the same initial and final frequencies. Position and momentum are continuous, but the potential energy at that position quadruples immediately. The new energy is \(0.640\) J and the amplitude remains \(0.0400\) m. Make the same switch at the zero crossing instead and the energy initially remains \(0.160\) J because the potential vanishes there. These two outcomes distinguish a rapid change tied to an oscillator phase from the nearly phase-independent slow ramp. Recording both position and velocity after the ramp allows energy, action and phase to be assessed separately.

A time-dependent Hamiltonian still carries each small phase-space area element into one of equal area, even during a sudden change of curvature. This exact conservation for an ensemble is distinct from slow conservation of the contour area assigned to a single orbit. A group of uniformly distributed initial phases illustrates the difference: immediately after a sudden spring switch all points occupy the same locations and enclose their original region, but their new energies depend on phase. The switch maps the original single-energy contour onto points that subsequently occupy many different energy contours of the new Hamiltonian.

Principal axes and torque-free precession

Classical mechanics

Use the inertia tensor to distinguish rotation about a principal axis from the precession of a freely rotating symmetric body.

From first principles

Build the inertia tensor from the momenta of particles in a rigid body

Choose the centre of mass as origin and consider a rigid body of fixed mass distribution. Its instantaneous internal velocity field is

\[\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r.\]

Starting from angular momentum as the moment of linear momentum, integrate over small masses. The vector triple-product identity separates the part parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\) from a direction-dependent subtraction:

\[\mathbf L=\int\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dm=\int[r^2\boldsymbol\omega-(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)\mathbf r]\,dm .\]

Reading off coefficients of the components of \(\boldsymbol\omega\) defines the symmetric inertia tensor. It is diagonal only in appropriately chosen principal axes:

\[L_i=\sum_j I_{ij}\omega_j,\]
\[I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_i r_j)\,dm .\]

Torque gives the laboratory time derivative of angular momentum. In a body-fixed principal basis the tensor components are constant but the basis rotates. Applying the rotating-vector derivative yields, for the first component,

\[\tau_1=I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3 .\]

Cyclic permutation gives the others. Even zero torque can change the body-frame components of angular velocity. Alignment of angular velocity and angular momentum holds about a principal direction, or about every direction for spherical inertia.

Derivation and applications

Find a pivot that gives a specified physical-pendulum period

An extended body hanging from a pivot rarely oscillates like a point at its centre of mass. Take a rigid body of mass \(M\) with centre of mass a distance \(d\) below a horizontal, frictionless axis. Let \(I_C\) be its moment of inertia about the parallel axis through the centre of mass. Measure angle \(\theta\) from vertically downward. For each mass element, decompose its perpendicular displacement from the pivot into the centre-of-mass offset and a displacement relative to the centre. The cross term integrates to zero by definition of the centre:

\[I_O=\int r_\perp^2\,dm=I_C+Md^2 .\]

Gravity is equivalent to a force \(Mg\) at the centre, so its torque depends only on \(d\). Angular momentum about the fixed pivot is \(I_O\dot\theta\) along the rotation axis. The angular equation, followed by its small-angle limit, is

\[(I_C+Md^2)\ddot\theta=-Mgd\sin\theta,\]
\[\omega^2=\frac{Mgd}{I_C+Md^2}.\]

Define the radius of gyration \(k_C=\sqrt{I_C/M}\). A simple pendulum with the same period has effective length \(\ell_{\rm eff}=g/\omega^2\). Unlike the actual distance \(d\), this length includes the cost of rotating the mass distribution about its centre:

\[T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{\rm eff}}g},\]
\[\ell_{\rm eff}=d+\frac{k_C^2}{d}.\]

Moving the pivot toward the centre eventually increases the period because the torque vanishes faster than the inertia. Differentiating the effective length gives \(d\ell_{\rm eff}/dd=1-k_C^2/d^2\), so the shortest period occurs at \(d=k_C\) if the apparatus admits that pivot.

Worked example

Compare two pivot positions on a uniform bar

A thin \(0.900\) m uniform bar oscillates in a vertical plane. Integration of \(x^2\,dm\) along its length gives

\[k_C^2=L^2/12=0.0675\,\mathrm{m^2}.\]

Pivoting at the end gives \(d=0.450\) m and \(\ell_{\rm eff}=0.600\) m, hence \(T=1.55\) s for \(g=9.81\) m/s². Pivoting \(0.150\) m from the centre also gives

\[0.150+0.0675/0.150=0.600\,\mathrm{m}.\]

The two periods agree even though the gravitational torque coefficients differ by a factor of three.

Second application

Infer an unknown inertia from equal periods

Two distinct pivot distances \(d_1=0.0800\) m and \(d_2=0.180\) m give the same measured period for a \(0.500\) kg body. Equating the effective lengths and canceling \(d_1-d_2\) yields

\[k_C^2=d_1d_2=0.0144\,\mathrm{m^2}.\]

Thus \(I_C=0.00720\) kg m², and the common effective length is

\[d_1+d_2=0.260\,\mathrm{m}.\]

A predicted period of \(1.02\) s checks the inference independently of the inertia measurement.

Describe rotation around a moving centre

Take a rigid body with a freely moving centre of mass and no applied torque about that centre. Remove the uniform translation and label material positions \(\mathbf r\) relative to the centre. Each material point has velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\). Integrating \(\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dm\) gives

\[\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega,\]

where \(I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_i r_j)dm\). The inertia tensor is symmetric, so a set of orthogonal body axes diagonalises it.

In its principal axes the rotational kinetic energy is \(\sum_i I_i\omega_i^2/2\). This expression follows by integrating the kinetic energy of individual mass elements. A body containing mass far from an axis usually has large moment of inertia about that axis. Unless the components seeing different moments vanish, angular momentum and angular velocity point in different directions. A body can therefore rotate freely even when the angular velocity changes direction relative to painted marks on its surface.

Background · Where does the parallel-axis term come from?

Compare the kinetic energies for rotation about two parallel axes, one through the centre of mass and one displaced a perpendicular distance \(d\). For a mass element write the perpendicular position about the second axis as \(\boldsymbol\rho+\mathbf d\), where \(\boldsymbol\rho\) is measured from the centre of mass. Expanding its squared distance produces a mixed term. The mass-weighted mean of \(\boldsymbol\rho\) vanishes by the definition of the centre.

\[I_{\rm shifted}=\int|\boldsymbol\rho+\mathbf d|^2\,dm=I_{\rm CM}+Md^2.\]

For a uniform thin rod of length \(L\) rotating about a transverse axis through an end, the shift adds \(ML^2/4\) to \(ML^2/12\). The result is \(ML^2/3\). If instead the new axis tilts, each mass element has a different perpendicular projection. Then apply the inertia tensor to the new axis direction before making a parallel shift.

Apply the moving-frame derivative

The laboratory angular momentum is constant in the absence of torque. Its components in the body basis satisfy \((d\mathbf L/dt)_{\rm body}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L=0\). For axial symmetry put \(I_1=I_2=I_\perp\) and call \(I_3\) the moment about the symmetry axis. Then \(\dot\omega_3=0\),

\[\dot\omega_1=-(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_2\omega_3/I_\perp,\]
\[\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_1\omega_3/I_\perp.\]

These last two equations describe uniform rotation of the pair of transverse components in body coordinates.

The signed angular rate of that rotation is \(\Omega_b=(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_3/I_\perp\). A body-mounted sensor sees its transverse spin vector execute a circle, even though a laboratory observer sees a constant angular-momentum vector. For a spherical inertia tensor the transverse components also become constant. The possible motion then simplifies because \(\mathbf L\) is parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\) for any spin direction.

Predict what two observers see

The symmetry-axis unit vector \(\mathbf e_3\) has laboratory derivative \(\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_3\). Express the angular velocity as \(\mathbf L/I_\perp\) plus a term parallel to \(\mathbf e_3\), which drops out of the cross product. The result is

\[\dot{\mathbf e}_3=(\mathbf L/I_\perp)\times\mathbf e_3.\]

Thus in the laboratory the symmetry axis sweeps around fixed \(\mathbf L\) at rate \(L/I_\perp\) and constant tilt. This rate differs from the spin-vector rotation observed in body coordinates because the two measurements track different vectors relative to different frames.

Let \(I_\perp=0.020\) kg·m², \(I_3=0.030\) kg·m², \(\omega_3=40\) rad/s and initial transverse angular speed 6.0 rad/s. The body-frame rate is 20 rad/s. The magnitude of momentum is \(\sqrt{(0.020\times6)^2+(0.030\times40)^2}=1.206\) kg·m²/s. Laboratory precession is about 60.3 rad/s. The tilt obeys \(\tan\theta=0.120/1.20=0.10\), giving 5.71°. Two cameras recording these distinct angular rates need not disagree on the mechanics.

For three distinct moments the two transverse equations no longer form this simple uniform circle. The stability of individual principal-axis spins then becomes an additional question. Recording both energy and angular momentum helps discriminate conservative tumbling from external bearing drag.

One can also infer a moment of inertia without opening the rotor. Measure angular momentum by applying a calibrated torque for a known duration and integrating the torque impulse. During free motion, track the body axis with an external camera and find its precession frequency about the fixed angular momentum. For the symmetric rotor their ratio estimates the transverse moment \(I_1\). A second measurement of the axial component of angular momentum and spin gives \(I_3\).

Balance torque and angular momentum in a heavy top

Support a symmetric top at one fixed point on its symmetry axis, a distance \(\ell\) below its centre of mass when upright. Unlike free rotation about the centre, gravity now exerts a torque about the pivot. Use principal moments about the pivot, \(I_1=I_2\) and \(I_3\), and let \(\theta\) be the tilt from upward vertical. In steady precession \(\theta\) is constant, the azimuth increases at rate \(\Omega\), and the body’s total angular-velocity component along its symmetry axis is \(\omega_3=\dot\psi+\Omega\cos\theta\).

The rotational kinetic energy in Euler angles is \(I_1(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)/2+I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2/2\). Insert \(U=mg\ell\cos\theta\) into the \(\theta\) equation, differentiating before imposing constant tilt. For \(\sin\theta\ne0\) this yields

\[I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+mg\ell=0.\]

The slower root for rapid spin tends to \(mg\ell/(I_3\omega_3)\). This is the rate required for gravity to turn the nearly axial angular momentum. The full quadratic distinguishes that rate from a second, fast precession and gives the spin threshold for a tilted steady solution when \(\cos\theta>0\). Initial conditions outside such a solution produce a varying tilt, called nutation.

For \(m=0.200\) kg, \(\ell=0.0450\) m, \(I_1=0.00180\) kg m², \(I_3=0.000800\) kg m², \(\omega_3=200\) rad/s and \(\theta=30^\circ\), the slow-root approximation is

\[0.0883/0.160=0.552\,\mathrm{rad/s}.\]

Substituting the quadratic coefficients gives \(0.555\) rad/s, a precession period of about 11.3 s. The other root is about 102 rad/s. A camera measuring only rotation of a mark painted on the body cannot generally separate axial spin, precession and nutation without also tracking the axis.

Stability of rotation about a principal axis

A symmetric rotor can precess steadily. A body with three different principal moments has a striking additional possibility. Suppose \(I_1<I_2<I_3\) and no torque acts about the centre of mass. In body coordinates Euler’s equations can be written \(I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3\), with cyclic permutations for the other components. A pure rotation about any one principal axis is an exact solution. Stability asks what happens to a small transverse disturbance of that solution, a question about neighbouring trajectories rather than the exact rotation itself.

Consider nominal rotation about axis 2 at rate \(\Omega\). Write \(\omega_1=u\) and \(\omega_3=w\), both initially small. The change of \(\omega_2\) is a product of two small quantities and can be discarded to first order. The first and third equations give

\[\dot u=-(I_3-I_2)\Omega w/I_1,\]
\[\dot w=-(I_2-I_1)\Omega u/I_3.\]

Differentiating the first and substituting the second produces

\[\ddot u=\Omega^2\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}\,u .\]

A positive coefficient gives hyperbolic solutions with an exponentially growing component for a generic disturbance. Repeating the calculation about the smallest or the largest moment reverses the sign of the product, giving bounded oscillatory disturbances. Energy and angular momentum remain conserved throughout. A nearby angular-velocity direction can change substantially while moving along the intersection of the two invariant surfaces.

Estimate the onset of a visible tumble

Take moments \(I_1=0.020\), \(I_2=0.030\) and \(I_3=0.040\) kg m² and a nominal intermediate-axis rotation \(\Omega=20.0\) rad/s. The small-perturbation growth rate is \(20.0\sqrt{0.010\times0.010/(0.020\times0.040)}=7.07\) s⁻¹. Prepare an initial transverse component of \(0.020\) rad/s in the growing eigenmode. Reaching \(0.200\) rad/s then takes \(\ln(10)/7.07=0.326\) s, still within the small-angle range. A perturbation prepared with zero initial derivative instead contains both growing and decaying contributions and follows a hyperbolic cosine initially.

These estimates predict the early departure, not the duration of a full flip. When transverse components approach the nominal spin, the nonlinear terms that preserved the dominant spin to first order can no longer be dropped. As two adjacent moments approach equality the growth rate tends to zero, recovering the absence of this isolated intermediate-axis instability in a symmetric rotor. A motion-capture experiment that records all three body components can test the exponential interval and independently check whether external torques or aerodynamic drag have changed the conserved angular momentum.

There is also a camera-independent prediction for the orientation of the early disturbance in body coordinates. A growing solution has \(\dot u=\lambda u\). From the first linearized equation this requires

\[w/u=-I_1\lambda/[(I_3-I_2)\Omega].\]

In the numerical example that ratio is \(-0.707\): the small components point in opposite signed directions. A disturbance with the reverse sign projects partly onto the decaying solution. The eventual growth rate can be the same while the visibility of the first few cycles differs greatly. Recording both components avoids assigning a disagreement in initial conditions to the underlying instability rate.

Nonlinear stiffness and amplitude-dependent period

Classical mechanics

Derive the leading frequency shift of a symmetric nonlinear oscillator and test whether a measured amplitude shift is consistent with a cubic spring.

From first principles

Derive the first amplitude correction to a spring’s frequency

A symmetric elastic restoring force has no quadratic displacement term; the first correction to a linear spring can therefore be cubic. Suppose a conservative oscillator satisfies \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\) with \(k>0\) and \(|\alpha A^2/k|\ll1\) at measured peak amplitude \(A\). Leading motion is approximately \(A\cos\Omega t\). Cube this motion to find which frequencies enter the perturbing force:

\[x^3\simeq A^3[\tfrac34\cos\Omega t+\tfrac14\cos3\Omega t] .\]

To maintain a bounded periodic solution, its fundamental component must balance at first order. Otherwise the remaining fundamental force would resonantly grow the correction. Combine the inertia and restoring-force coefficients at \(\cos\Omega t\):

\[-m\Omega^2A+kA+\tfrac34\alpha A^3\simeq0,\qquad \Omega\simeq\sqrt{k/m}\left(1+\frac{3\alpha A^2}{8k}\right).\]

The third-harmonic component also distorts the waveform, but reinserting that order-\(\alpha\) distortion into the cubic force affects the fundamental only at the next perturbative order. Positive \(\alpha\) raises frequency with amplitude; negative \(\alpha\) lowers it within the local range where the stable weakly nonlinear model applies.

Derivation and applications

Find the third harmonic generated by a weak cubic spring

A change in oscillation period is one signature of a nonlinear restoring force. The same nonlinearity also changes the shape of the recorded motion. Let an undamped mass obey \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\), with \(k>0\). Release it from rest at the measured peak displacement \(A\). Define \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\), the small signed parameter \(\epsilon=\alpha A^2/k\), and a stretched time \(\tau=\Omega t\). Write \(x/A=\cos\tau+\epsilon u(\tau)\) with \(u(0)=u'(0)=0\) so that \(A\) remains the physical turning point.

Allow the frequency to shift as \(\Omega=\omega_0(1+\epsilon\nu)\). Substitute the expansion into the equation and retain terms only to first order in \(\epsilon\). Primes below denote differentiation with respect to \(\tau\):

\[u''+u=2\nu\cos\tau-\cos^3\tau=(2\nu-3/4)\cos\tau-\tfrac14\cos3\tau .\]

A term driven at the unit frequency would grow proportionally to \(\tau\sin\tau\), contradicting a bounded, periodic correction. Choose \(\nu=3/8\) to remove it. The remaining forcing is at three times the fundamental. A trial \(C\cos3\tau\) has \(u''+u=-8C\cos3\tau\). Add the required homogeneous term to satisfy the turning-point conditions:

\[u(\tau)=\frac{\cos3\tau-\cos\tau}{32}.\]

The predicted displacement therefore contains two lines with linked amplitudes and a shifted fundamental:

\[x(t)\simeq A\!\left[(1-\epsilon/32)\cos\Omega t+(\epsilon/32)\cos3\Omega t\right],\qquad \Omega/\omega_0\simeq1+3\epsilon/8 .\]

The coefficient sign gives the third harmonic relative to the phase of a release at positive displacement. Squaring an amplitude to obtain a power spectrum erases that sign. The approximation assumes \(|\epsilon|\ll1\) and excludes appreciable dissipation during the observation.

Worked example

Estimate a micrometre-scale distortion

Take \(A=12.0\) mm, \(k=20.0\) N/m and \(\alpha=1.00\times10^4\) N/m³. Then \(\epsilon=0.0720\), so the third-harmonic displacement amplitude is

\[12.0(0.0720)/32=0.0270\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The fundamental frequency should increase by \(2.70\%\). With \(\omega_0=10.0\) rad/s, the third-harmonic line lies close to \(30.8\) rad/s.

Second application

Test whether a measured line comes from the spring

At \(A=6.00\) mm on the same apparatus, \(\epsilon\) falls by four and the third-harmonic amplitude falls by eight to \(3.38\) micrometres. The fractional frequency shift becomes \(0.675\%\). A third-harmonic signal that instead halves with amplitude could arise from an already distorted input or readout. The two amplitude scalings plus an independent frequency measurement test the cubic model more strongly than observing a line at three times the frequency.

Start from a reversible force law

A spring that looks linear under small deflection can stiffen as it is displaced further. For an oscillator symmetric under \(x\mapsto-x\), the restoring force expanded about equilibrium has no even powers. Keep the leading correction and write \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\), with \(k>0\) and \(\alpha\) measured in N/m³. Assume the displacement is small enough that neglected fifth-order terms remain unimportant. Positive \(\alpha\) gives a hardening spring. A negative cubic coefficient describes local softening and may need higher-order terms to represent large displacements safely within the model.

The potential is \(kx^2/2+\alpha x^4/4\). Without damping the total energy is conserved and a release at amplitude \(A\) fixes that energy. Unlike a harmonic oscillator, the quadratic curvature at the origin no longer fixes the period for every possible energy. We want a quantitative connection between the amplitude of the motion and its zero-crossing interval.

Obtain the first frequency correction

Try \(x\simeq A\cos\Omega t\) and substitute it into the cubic equation. The identity \(\cos^3 u=(3\cos u+\cos3u)/4\) shows that the nonlinearity introduces a component at the original frequency and a third harmonic. Balance the fundamental coefficient to get \(-m\Omega^2+k+3\alpha A^2/4\simeq0\). Expanding the square root gives

\[\Omega\simeq\omega_0\left(1+\frac{3\alpha A^2}{8k}\right),\]
\[\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}.\]

The control parameter is \(|\alpha|A^2/k\), the ratio of cubic to linear force at maximum displacement. The small additional third harmonic changes the waveform at first order but only feeds back into this leading frequency result at higher order. Fitting a perfect sinusoid to a high-precision trace may therefore show a residual even when the measured average period follows this formula.

Check the result using the conserved energy

The quarter-period is the time to move from equilibrium to \(A\). Substitution of the energy at the turning point gives an integral over \(dx/\sqrt{2[E-U(x)]/m}\). Let \(x=A\sin u\) to remove the square-root endpoint. The remaining integrand is proportional to \(\{1+[\alpha A^2/(2k)](1+\sin^2u)\}^{-1/2}\). Expanding and averaging over the interval from zero to \(\pi/2\) uses mean \(\sin^2 u=1/2\), yielding \(T\simeq(2\pi/\omega_0)[1-3\alpha A^2/(8k)]\). This agrees with the reciprocal of the harmonic-balance result.

The integral approach generalises to experimentally tabulated conservative forces by integrating them first to obtain a potential. It would need modification for strong damping, where energy varies significantly between the two turning points of one cycle.

Infer stiffness from period data

For \(m=0.50\) kg, \(k=200\) N/m and \(\alpha=4.0\times10^4\) N/m³, \(\omega_0=20.0\) rad/s. At \(A=0.020\) m the force ratio is 0.080, giving a leading frequency increase of 3.0% to 20.6 rad/s. At half this amplitude the predicted increase is 0.75%. Measure the period over several cycles at each amplitude, keeping the amplitude change due to loss small. A plot of frequency against the square of amplitude should be approximately straight near the origin. A nonzero intercept shift under a change of temperature could indicate a change in ordinary linear stiffness instead of a new nonlinear coefficient.

Compute the entire amplitude dependence of a pendulum

A cubic expansion is useful near equilibrium. For a pendulum it is possible to carry the energy calculation beyond every finite Taylor approximation. Take a point mass with fixed length \(\ell\), no friction and a release from rest at angle \(0<\theta_0<\pi\). Its conserved energy gives

\[\dot\theta^2=(2g/\ell)(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0).\]

Use \(\cos\theta=1-2\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Defining \(k=\sin(\theta_0/2)\) reduces the speed magnitude to \(2\sqrt{g/\ell}\sqrt{k^2-\sin^2(\theta/2)}\).

One quarter period is the time to travel between zero angle and the turning point. The reciprocal speed appears to diverge at the latter, but it has an integrable square-root singularity for every ordinary turning angle. To evaluate it stably, set \(\sin(\theta/2)=k\sin u\). The factor \(\cos u\) from differentiating cancels the same factor inside the square root. Four equivalent quarters give

\[T=4\sqrt{\ell/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}}=4\sqrt{\ell/g}\,K(k).\]

Here \(K\) denotes the complete elliptic integral with modulus \(k\). Expanding the integrand at small \(k\) and integrating the first correction gives

\[T/T_0=1+k^2/4+\cdots=1+\theta_0^2/16+\cdots,\]

where \(T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\). The positive correction is the familiar softening: increasing amplitude lengthens the period because the restoring torque grows more slowly than its linear extrapolation.

Decide when a small-angle clock loses accuracy

A pendulum of length \(0.800\) m has \(T_0=1.794\) s at \(g=9.81\) m/s². At a release of \(60.0^\circ\),

\[k=\sin30^\circ=0.500.\]

Evaluating the integral gives \(K(0.500)=1.68575\) and \(T/T_0=2K/\pi=1.0732\), hence \(T=1.926\) s. The weak-angle approximation gives

\[1+(\pi/3)^2/16=1.0685.\]

It misses the exact period by roughly \(0.008\) s per cycle; a many-cycle timing record resolves the accumulated discrepancy.

As \(\theta_0\) tends to \(\pi\), the integrand near \(u=\pi/2\) approaches the reciprocal of a vanishing cosine. The integral diverges logarithmically, expressing the long time spent close to the unstable upper equilibrium. No finite polynomial in amplitude can reproduce that divergence. A numerical timing experiment should therefore locate regular zero crossings rather than demand a turning-point velocity of exactly zero in sampled data. Comparing the crossing intervals with the regularized integral distinguishes numerical resolution error from the genuine increase in physical period.

A way to evaluate the elliptic integral without a special-function library is to form the arithmetic–geometric mean. Begin with \(a_0=1\) and \(b_0=\sqrt{1-k^2}\), then replace the pair by their arithmetic and geometric means. Both converge rapidly to a common \(M\), and \(K(k)=\pi/(2M)\). For the 60° release the first pair is \(1\) and \(0.86603\), the next pair is approximately \(0.93301\) and \(0.93060\), and the subsequent pair agrees near \(0.93181\). Taking the reciprocal of the common value directly gives the period ratio. This procedure supplies an accurate independent benchmark even when the small-amplitude expansion ceases to converge quickly.

Numerical quadrature and the arithmetic–geometric mean should agree at a fixed amplitude. If a period extracted from actual trajectories differs while these two evaluations agree, examine the dynamical time step, how release angle is measured, and whether the observed oscillations have suffered appreciable damping.

Read a period from the area enclosed by an orbit

Classical mechanics

A track with a V-shaped potential has straight force segments but a strongly amplitude-dependent period. Its area in phase space gives a useful way to predict both the period and a slow change of the track.

Construct the closed area

For one bound degree of freedom at energy \(E\), position runs between two turning points. In a mechanical Hamiltonian \(p^2/(2m)+U(q)\), the upper branch of the phase portrait has positive momentum and the lower branch has its negative. The directed integral around one full orbit counts the enclosed area. Define the action \(J\) using the area divided by \(2\pi\). This choice makes its conjugate phase an angle measured in radians:

\[J(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dq=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\sqrt{2m(E-U(q))}\,dq .\]

Now change the energy slightly. The integrand vanishes at each regular endpoint, so the moving-endpoint contributions to the derivative vanish. In the interior \(\partial p/\partial E=m/p=1/v\). The derivative of the upper-branch area therefore measures the time to reach the opposite turning point.

\[\frac{dJ}{dE}=\frac{T(E)}{2\pi},\]
\[\omega(E)=\frac{dE}{dJ}.\]

This argument also explains why the construction becomes delicate near a separatrix. A trajectory can spend an arbitrarily long time near an unstable equilibrium, and \(T\) can diverge even though the enclosed area stays finite.

Background · Action variable
\[J=(2\pi)^{-1}\oint p\,dq\]

This area measure is defined for a closed cycle. It can change strongly near a separatrix even under a slow parameter variation.

Carry out the integral for a constant restoring force

Use \(U(q)=F|q|\) with \(F>0\). The turning amplitude is \(A=E/F\). Integrate the positive branch from \(0\) to \(A\) and use reflection symmetry. Substitution \(s=E-Fq\) gives

\[J=\frac{4\sqrt{2m}}{3\pi F}E^{3/2},\]
\[T=\frac{4\sqrt{2mE}}{F}=4\sqrt{\frac{2mA}{F}} .\]

The answer has an independent mechanical check. From a turning point the bead accelerates uniformly with magnitude \(F/m\) until the centre. Covering a distance \(A\) from rest takes \(\sqrt{2mA/F}\), a quarter period. Doubling the amplitude increases the full period by \(\sqrt{2}\). The abrupt change of force direction at the centre leaves the velocity continuous.

Slowly steepen the track

A slowly adjusted parameter can preserve the orbit action to leading order when the motion remains periodic and many cycles fit inside the adjustment time. For this trap \(m\) stays constant, so constant \(J\) requires \(E^{3/2}/F\) to stay fixed. Therefore \(E\propto F^{2/3}\),

\[A=E/F\propto F^{-1/3},\]
\[T\propto F^{-2/3}.\]

Work done by adjusting the slope changes the energy despite the approximate conservation of action.

Start with \(m=0.25\) kg, \(F=2.0\) N and \(A=0.080\) m. The initial energy is \(0.160\) J and the period \(0.566\) s. A slow eightfold increase of \(F\) makes the energy four times larger, \(0.640\) J, and the amplitude half as large, \(0.040\) m. The period becomes \(0.141\) s. In contrast, switching slopes instantaneously at the centre leaves the kinetic energy and position unchanged at that instant. The same final slope would then give amplitude \(0.010\) m. The route by which a parameter changes matters.

Parametric resonance and Floquet growth

Classical mechanics

A pendulum can gain energy when its length varies periodically even if no additive force acts on it. A modulation near twice a natural frequency amplifies one phase of the oscillation and attenuates another. The stability test concerns the growth of a perturbation over a complete modulation period.

Linearise the timing mechanism

Consider \(\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2[1+h\cos(\Omega t)]x=0\). Here \(h\) is the fractional modulation of the stiffness and \(\gamma\) is the amplitude damping rate. If \(x=\dot x=0\) exactly, the solution stays zero. Small fluctuations or an initial condition provide the seed. Direct differentiation of \(E=[\dot x^2+\omega_0^2(1+h\cos\Omega t)x^2]/2\) yields

\[\dot E=-2\gamma\dot x^2-h\omega_0^2\Omega x^2\sin(\Omega t)/2.\]

Energy transfer depends on the phase of \(x^2\), which naturally contains a component at twice the oscillation frequency.

Near \(\nu=\Omega/2\simeq\omega_0\), write \(x=A(t)e^{i\nu t}+A^*(t)e^{-i\nu t}\) with slowly varying \(A\). Multiplication by \(\cos(2\nu t)\) mixes the negative-frequency term back into the positive-frequency term with coefficient \(h\omega_0^2/2\). There is also a component at \(3\nu\), rapidly oscillating in this first-resonance approximation. Retain terms proportional to \(e^{i\nu t}\), neglect \(\ddot A\) and products of small damping with \(\dot A\). This gives

\[2i\nu\dot A+(\omega_0^2-\nu^2+2i\gamma\nu)A+(h\omega_0^2/2)A^*=0.\]

Find the first instability tongue

Set \(\Delta=\nu-\omega_0\) and keep leading terms in the small ratios \(h,\gamma/\omega_0,|\Delta|/\omega_0\). The slow equations are \(\dot A=(-\gamma-i\Delta)A+igA^*\) and their conjugate, where \(g=h\omega_0/4\). The two-by-two characteristic equation is

\[(s+\gamma)^2=g^2-\Delta^2.\]

The faster exponent is

\[\operatorname{Re}s_+=-\gamma+\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{(h\omega_0/4)^2-\Delta^2}.\]

The oscillation grows when \(g^2>\gamma^2+\Delta^2\). Exactly on resonance this gives \(h>4\gamma/\omega_0\). With \(\omega_0=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(\gamma=0.10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(h=0.08\), \(g=0.20\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), giving an on-resonance doubling time \(\ln2/0.10=6.93\,\mathrm s\). The approximate growing interval in drive angular frequency is \(20\pm0.346\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). A large-amplitude real oscillator can saturate as its natural frequency changes with amplitude, a mechanism absent from this linear equation.

Their final columns form a matrix \(M\), which maps every initial perturbation over the same period \(T\). After \(j\) periods the map is \(M^j\); the maximum asymptotic amplitude exponent is \(\ln\max|\lambda_i(M)|/T\). Störmer–Verlet and a discrete invariant

Numerical ordinary differential equations

Derive the leapfrog update, identify its oscillatory stability interval and quantify the difference between bounded energy and correct phase.

From first principles

Derive a reversible position update from Newton’s law

For a force that depends only on position, \(m\ddot q=F(q)\), the acceleration can be evaluated at a stored position without an implicit solve. Expand the exact position one step forward and one step backward about the same time \(t_n\). The odd derivatives reverse sign and cancel when the expansions are added:

\[q(t_n+h)+q(t_n-h)=2q(t_n)+h^2\ddot q(t_n)+O(h^4).\]

Replace acceleration by force divided by mass and solve for the forward position. The resulting relation is unchanged if the labels \(n+1\) and \(n-1\) are exchanged:

\[q_{n+1}=2q_n-q_{n-1}+\frac{h^2}{m}F(q_n).\]

Introduce the staggered momentum \(p_{n-1/2}=m(q_n-q_{n-1})/h\). The same algebra becomes an impulse followed by a drift,

\[p_{n+1/2}=p_{n-1/2}+hF(q_n),\]
\[q_{n+1}=q_n+h p_{n+1/2}/m .\]

A specified momentum at \(t=0\) starts the first half step with \(p_{1/2}=p_0+h F(q_0)/2\). For smooth forces the method has second-order position accuracy. It is important to distinguish the staggered momentum from momentum at the stored position time when checking energy.

Derivation and applications

Why a stable Verlet trajectory can arrive at the wrong time

Long-term energy behaviour is important in reversible mechanics, but phase error can dominate a timing measurement. Consider the velocity-independent linear oscillator \(q''=-\omega^2q\). Use a uniform step \(h\), an accurately initialized first point and the position recurrence obtained by eliminating the half-step momenta in Verlet. Write \(z=h\omega\):

\[q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-z^2q_n .\]

Assume a discrete mode \(q_n=e^{in\vartheta}\). Divide by the mode value at \(n\) and combine the two exponentials. The angle advanced per step obeys

\[2\cos\vartheta-2=-z^2,\]
\[\sin(\vartheta/2)=z/2 .\]

For \(0<z<2\) choose the branch continuous with the exact oscillation at small \(h\). The discrete angular frequency and its expansion are

\[\widetilde\omega=\frac2h\arcsin(z/2)=\omega\left[1+\frac{z^2}{24}+O(z^4)\right].\]

The numerical oscillator runs slightly fast even though its amplitude can remain bounded indefinitely. Its phase lead grows approximately linearly with elapsed time at fixed step. Above \(z=2\) the dispersion relation permits no real phase angle, signaling a separate stability failure. The formula concerns time discretization alone; incorrect initial half-step velocities can add an initial phase offset.

Worked example

Estimate a ten-period timing error

For \(\omega=5.00\) rad/s and \(h=0.100\) s, \(z=0.500\) and \(\widetilde\omega=20\arcsin(0.250)=5.054\) rad/s. Ten true periods last \(12.57\) s. Over that duration the numerical phase gains about \((5.054-5.00)(12.57)\simeq0.674\) rad. The estimated time at which the numerical oscillator completes its tenth period is \(20\pi/5.054=12.43\) s, about \(0.13\) s too soon.

Second application

Select a step from a phase requirement

Suppose 100 periods should accumulate less than \(0.100\) rad of phase error. The leading estimate gives \(2\pi(100)z^2/24<0.100\), so \(z<0.0618\). For \(\omega=5.00\) rad/s this means \(h<0.0124\) s. A choice based only on the stability requirement \(h<0.400\) s would be far too coarse. Halving a small step should reduce the accumulated phase error by approximately four at fixed physical duration.

Position and momentum at staggered times

For unit mass and a time-independent potential \(V(q)\), the equations are

\[q'=p,\ p'=-V'(q).\]

Treat the potential force over a half step, drift with the updated momentum for a full step, then use the new position for the last half kick. This yields

\[p_{n+1/2}=p_n-\tfrac h2V'(q_n),\]
\[q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_{n+1/2},\]
\[p_{n+1}=p_{n+1/2}-\tfrac h2V'(q_{n+1}).\]

Successive half kicks at the same position share a force evaluation. Reversing the sign of \(h\) exactly undoes the steps in reverse order, in exact arithmetic. Subtracting consecutive position increments gives

\[q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-h^2V'(q_n).\]

The starting position and velocity must initialise the very first half step correctly; using the full velocity as the half-step velocity injects an initial phase error.

The oscillator has an exactly computable modified energy

For \(V=\omega^2q^2/2\) define \(z=h\omega\). The position coefficient of \(q_n\) after one step is

\[a=1-z^2/2;\]

the momentum coefficient of \(q_n\) is \(-h\omega^2(1-z^2/4)\). The full map has equal diagonal entries \(a\) and off-diagonal entries \(h,-hc\) with \(c=\omega^2(1-z^2/4)\). Since \(a^2+h^2c=1\), expanding squares shows \(p_{n+1}^2+cq_{n+1}^2=p_n^2+cq_n^2\).

When \(0<z<2\), \(c>0\) and level sets of this invariant are bounded ellipses. The eigenvalues are \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) with \(\cos\theta=a\). Beyond two, one real eigenvalue has magnitude greater than one and trajectories generically grow. At exactly two the invariant loses control of position and a repeated eigenvalue can cause linear growth. An apparently bounded run starting from specially chosen data at the threshold cannot certify stability for arbitrary perturbations.

Follow a step and its timing

For \(\omega=1,h=1,q_0=1,p_0=0\), the half kick gives \(-0.5\), the drift gives \(q_1=0.5\), and the final half kick gives \(p_1=-0.75\). The physical energy decreases from 0.5 to 0.40625 in this first step. The modified energy \((p^2+0.75q^2)/2\) equals 0.375 at both points. The discrete phase is \(\theta=\pi/3\), so the computed trajectory returns after six steps. The true oscillator returns after \(2\pi\approx6.283\) seconds. A stable trajectory with a bounded energy error can therefore accumulate a large timing error over many cycles.

For small \(z\), expanding \(\theta=2\arcsin(z/2)\) gives

\[\theta=z+z^3/24+O(z^5).\]

At a fixed final time the accumulated phase error is therefore second order in \(h\). A period estimate and an energy trace probe different aspects of long-time integration and should be reported separately.

Why the initial velocity test matters

A test with only the initial position nonzero may conceal the borderline instability at \(h\omega=2\). At that boundary the update is

\[q_{n+1}=-q_n+hp_n,\ p_{n+1}=-p_n.\]

For \(p_0=0\) the positions merely alternate sign forever. With \(q_0=0\) and \(p_0=1\), however, explicit iteration gives

\[p_n=(-1)^n,\]
\[q_n=(-1)^{n-1}nh.\]

The position magnitude grows linearly. A stability test must account for all independent starting directions or small perturbations of the chosen one, especially when an eigenvalue of modulus one is repeated.

Prove conservation of angular momentum by individual substeps

For a particle of constant mass in a radial potential \(V(\|q\|)\), the force is parallel to the position vector. Its angular momentum is \(L=q\times p\). During a kick of duration \(\tau\), momentum changes by \(\tau F(q)\) at fixed position, so the change in \(L\) is

\[\tau q\times F(q)=0.\]

During a drift, position changes by \(\tau p/m\) at fixed momentum, giving change \((\tau/m)p\times p=0\). The full kick–drift–kick map therefore preserves angular momentum exactly in arithmetic without rounding, for any step length for which these evaluations are defined.

The proof uses radial force and the relationship between velocity and momentum. An external noncentral force exerts a physical torque, while a coordinate-dependent mass matrix changes the drift subflow. Under either modification the two zero cross products need rechecking. The argument also gives no bound on energy or phase. Exact conservation of angular momentum constrains orbital geometry but leaves freedom to move from the correct energy level onto a nearby one or to return to a point on the orbit at the wrong time.

Compare three diagnostics on an elliptical oscillator trajectory

Use unit mass and two-dimensional potential \(V=(x^2+y^2)/2\). Start at \(q=(1,0)\), \(p=(0,2)\), so \(L=2\) and physical energy is

\[E=(1+4)/2=2.5.\]

At step \(h=0.5\), the first half kick gives

\[p_{1/2}=(-0.25,2).\]

The drift leads to \(q_1=(0.875,1)\). The last half kick subtracts \(0.25q_1\), giving \(p_1=(-0.46875,1.75)\). The resulting planar angular momentum is \(0.875(1.75)-1(-0.46875)=2\), exactly as proved.

Calculate energy separately. The squared position is \(0.875^2+1=1.765625\), and the squared momentum is

\[0.46875^2+1.75^2=3.2822265625.\]

Hence the physical energy after this step is 2.52392578125, about 0.96 percent above the initial value. The exact continuous position at this time is \((\cos0.5,2\sin0.5)\approx(0.87758,0.95885)\), so the position error is concentrated in the second coordinate. Angular momentum, physical energy and the individual trajectory coordinates expose three distinct properties of the same update.

Because the harmonic force is separable by coordinate, the modified invariant from the scalar oscillator adds across the two directions: \([\|p\|^2+(1-h^2/4)\|q\|^2]/2\) is constant. Substitution gives 2.46875 at both steps, independently checking the arithmetic. For a general radial potential this particular quadratic modified invariant no longer follows, while the angular momentum proof remains valid. A conservation claim should identify the symmetry responsible rather than assuming every observed invariant survives a change of potential.

If there were a small constant transverse force, the physical change in angular momentum would be the time integral of \(q\times F_{\rm ext}\). A better diagnostic would subtract the accumulated applied torque from the measured change in \(L\), rather than demand that \(L\) remain fixed. This distinction also guards against a subtle implementation error: zeroing a real torque to improve a conservation plot would change the physical problem and could hide a significant trajectory bias.

Measuring the signed torque makes its direction as well as its magnitude available for this balance check.

Implicit midpoint, reversibility and the phase of an oscillator

Numerical ordinary differential equations

Imagine integrating a mechanical trajectory forward, saving only its end state and then running the same method backward. Recovering the start is a stronger demand than obtaining a plausibly small one-step error. Even exact recovery does not guarantee that an oscillation peaked at the correct time.

Build a method from a centred difference

Integrate \(y'=f(t,y)\) over an interval of length \(h\). Midpoint quadrature approximates the integral by \(h\) times its integrand at \(t_n+h/2\). The arithmetic mean of the unknown endpoint states approximates the exact midpoint state up to order \(h^2\) on a smooth trajectory. Using this mean as the derivative's state argument gives

\[y_{n+1}-y_n=h f\!\left(t_n+h/2,(y_n+y_{n+1})/2\right).\]

Errors from midpoint quadrature and from evaluating \(f\) at the approximate midpoint both contribute \(O(h^3)\) locally, giving global order two on a fixed interval under the usual regularity and stability conditions. In general the unknown appears inside \(f\), so an implicit equation must be solved. If \(f\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L_f\), direct fixed-point iteration contracts when \(|h|L_f/2<1\). Newton iteration can converge over a wider range. Neither a failed nonlinear solve nor loose tolerances inherit the exact algebraic properties of the method.

Exchange \(y_n,y_{n+1}\) and replace \(h\) by \(-h\). The left side changes sign, the arithmetic midpoint is identical and the midpoint time is identical if the reverse step starts at \(t_{n+1}\). Thus, on the same locally unique solution branch,

\[\Phi_{-h}=\Phi_h^{-1}.\]

A round trip using variable steps must repeat the accepted steps in reverse order. Independently choosing a new adaptive mesh on the way back need not retrace the states.

Solve the step for a harmonic oscillator

Write \(q'=\omega p,\ p'=-\omega q\), with scaled momentum \(p\) in the same units as \(q\), and \(z=q+ip\). The ODE becomes

\[z'=-i\omega z.\]

Solving the linear midpoint equation gives the scalar multiplier

\[M(u)=\frac{1-iu/2}{1+iu/2}=\exp[-2i\arctan(u/2)],\]
\[u=\omega h.\]

Its numerator and denominator are complex conjugates. Hence it preserves \(q^2+p^2\), proportional to the oscillator energy, exactly in real arithmetic. For any radius or step size \(M(-u)M(u)=1\). The rotation per step is

\[\theta=2\arctan(u/2)=u-u^3/12+O(u^5).\]

The numerical phase lags by \(u-\theta\) per step. After \(N\) steps the accumulated lag is \(N(u-\theta)\), increasing linearly in elapsed time even while the energy remains exactly constant.

Compare explicit midpoint Runge–Kutta. A half Euler predictor leads to

\[M_{\rm RK2}=1-iu-u^2/2.\]

It also has order two, but \(|M_{\rm RK2}|^2=1+u^4/4\). The amplitude grows slowly at every nonzero step. A forward and reverse pair multiplies the starting amplitude by \(1+u^4/4\). In a conservative problem these two second-order procedures can produce visibly different long records.

A time is an implicitly defined output

\[\dot g=\partial_tg+\nabla g\cdot f\]

Virtual work and the forces hidden by a constraint

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A bead follows a wire, a pendulum remains the same length, and a rolling wheel obeys a relation between translation and rotation. Their reduced equations describe the motion but can conceal the largest forces in the apparatus. Determine which forces disappear under a virtual displacement, recover their values, and decide when the assumed motion ceases to be physically admissible.

Hold the clock fixed when defining a virtual displacement

A system of particles has Cartesian positions \(\mathbf r_i\) and applied forces \(\mathbf F_i\). A holonomic constraint is an equation \(g_\alpha(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N,t)=0\). At a fixed instant an admissible infinitesimal change of configuration obeys \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\). The physical velocities obey a different relation, \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\partial_tg_\alpha=0\). A virtual displacement compares geometrically allowed configurations at the same time. It has no prescribed duration and makes no claim that the system's momentum actually points along it.

The distinction becomes measurable for a bead on a moving horizontal table. Every allowed virtual displacement has zero vertical component, so the table's upward normal force gives zero virtual work. An upward-moving table can nevertheless raise the bead and perform positive ordinary work. Ordinary power involves the actual velocity, which includes the table motion. A careless statement that constraint forces do no work confuses two different operations. For a fixed ideal frictionless surface the real velocity is tangent as well, and vanishing virtual work then does imply vanishing ordinary power.

Let \(\mathbf R_i\) be the reaction forces and use \(m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i+\mathbf R_i\). Multiply by an admissible \(\delta\mathbf r_i\) and sum over particles. We call the constraints ideal when \(\sum_i\mathbf R_i\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\) for every allowed virtual displacement. Eliminating those reactions gives d'Alembert's principle,

\[\sum_i(\mathbf F_i-m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0.\]

This is a dynamical equation expressed through a static-looking virtual-work test. It applies instantaneously along the motion. It does not assert that the total force vanishes or that the acceleration is an independent virtual displacement. One advantage is that the detailed construction of a rigid support need not be known to determine the admissible tangential acceleration; its force can be calculated afterwards when designing the support.

Pass from virtual work to equations for coordinates

Choose independent coordinates \(q_a\) with \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r_i(q,t)\) and independent virtual increments \(\delta q_a\). The velocity is \(\sum_a\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i\,\dot q_a+\partial_t\mathbf r_i\). With kinetic energy \(T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i^2/2\), differentiation at fixed \(q,t\) gives

\[\partial_{\dot q_a}T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Differentiate in time. The product rule produces an acceleration term and a second term involving \(d(\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i)/dt\). The latter equals \(\partial_{q_a}\dot{\mathbf r}_i\), with the generalised velocities held fixed, and supplies \(\partial_{q_a}T\). Hence

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_a}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_a}=\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf r_i}{\partial q_a}.\]

The projected applied force is

\[Q_a=\sum_i\mathbf F_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Independence of the coordinate variations in d'Alembert's principle gives one equation \(d(\partial_{\dot q_a}T)/dt-\partial_{q_a}T=Q_a\) per coordinate. When \(\mathbf F_i\) comes from a potential \(V(q,t)\), the conservative contribution is \(-\partial_{q_a}V\). Other contributions such as a measured resisting torque remain explicit. A coordinate expressed as an angle has a generalised force in joules per radian; a coordinate expressed as a length has one in newtons. Checking \(Q_a\,dq_a\) as work avoids mistaking a generalised force for a Cartesian component.

For a simple pendulum of length \(l\) and mass \(m\), choose \(\theta\) measured from downward vertical. Then \(T=ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\) and \(V=mgl(1-\cos\theta)\). The equation \(ml^2\ddot\theta=-mgl\sin\theta\) follows at once. The string tension vanished under the angular displacement. It has not been shown to vanish physically. Projecting Newton's equation onto the direction from the bead toward the pivot gives

\[T_{\rm string}=mg\cos\theta+ml\dot\theta^2.\]

This second observable is sensitive to speed even when the reduced angular acceleration depends only on angle.

Recover multipliers before choosing coordinates

Cartesian coordinates keep reaction directions explicit. Put all coordinates into a vector \(x\), let \(M\) be the mass matrix and let \(G\) contain the rows \(\nabla g_\alpha\). The tangent virtual displacements are precisely those satisfying \(G\delta x=0\). A reaction doing zero virtual work must be a linear combination of the normal covectors, \(R=G^T\lambda\), when the constraints are independent. Newton's equation becomes

\[M\ddot x=F+G^T\lambda.\]

Differentiate the constraints twice along the trajectory: \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\), where \(\gamma\) includes the known velocity, curvature and explicit time terms. Substituting for \(\ddot x\) determines reactions from a much smaller system,

\[(GM^{-1}G^T)\lambda=-\gamma-GM^{-1}F.\]

For any vector \(c\), \(c^TGM^{-1}G^Tc=(G^Tc)^TM^{-1}(G^Tc)\) is positive if \(G\) has independent rows and particle masses are positive. Dependent constraint equations make the individual multipliers ambiguous. For instance writing both \(g=0\) and \(2g=0\) declares the same geometrical restriction twice. Only the combined reaction is then determined. The magnitude of a raw multiplier also depends on the units and normalisation of \(g\). Reporting the force \(G^T\lambda\) makes comparisons between alternative constraint descriptions meaningful.

Use \(g=(x^2+y^2-l^2)/2=0\) for a planar pendulum, with \(y\) upward, so the gradient is the radius vector \(\mathbf r\). Newton's equation reads \(m\ddot{\mathbf r}=-mg\hat y+\lambda\mathbf r\), where the symbol \(g\) in the gravitational term denotes the acceleration magnitude. The acceleration constraint \(\mathbf r\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}=-v^2\) gives

\[\lambda=(mgy-mv^2)/l^2.\]

The inward tension is \(-\lambda l=mv^2/l-mgy/l\). Since \(y=-l\cos\theta\), this is the radial-force result already obtained. The multiplier in this convention has units of force per length. Using the radial-distance constraint instead would make its magnitude a force.

Background · What if the support can push but cannot pull?

Replacing a surface by an equality constraint assumes the object stays in contact. A particle sliding along the inside of a smooth vertical circular track illustrates the additional physical test. At the top, gravity and the inward normal reaction together must supply the centripetal acceleration.

\[N=\frac{mv^2}{R}-mg,\qquad N\geq0.\]

At a top speed below \(\sqrt{gR}\) the equality-constrained solution requires negative \(N\). An ordinary track cannot exert that pull. Its normal force then falls to zero and the particle must follow free flight until it meets the track again. A bead threaded on a wire could receive the oppositely directed force and would follow a different trajectory. Both models have the same geometrical path during contact.

Recognise an inequality at a contact

A bead threaded on a stiff loop can experience reaction in either direction, whereas a string can pull only. A surface without adhesion can push only. Derive the constrained motion, calculate its reaction, and compare the required sign with the physical mechanism. When a calculated tension turns negative, replace the constrained trajectory by free flight until a new contact event. Continuing to integrate the pendulum equation would retain a mathematically valid circle and a physically unavailable pushing string.

A mass starts at the bottom of a frictionless vertical loop with speed \(v_b\). Energy conservation gives \(v_{\rm top}^2=v_b^2-4gl\), since the top is \(2l\) higher. A string just remaining taut at the top requires \(T_{\rm top}=mv_{\rm top}^2/l-mg\ge0\), giving \(v_b^2\ge5gl\). The energy-only criterion for reaching the elevation is \(v_b^2\ge4gl\); the interval between these two values illustrates why a trajectory test must include reactions. With \(l=0.80\,{\rm m}\), the threshold bottom speed is \(6.26\,{\rm m/s}\). At this threshold the bottom tension is \(6mg\), six times the weight, even though the tension is about to vanish at the top.

Recover the forces on a wedge that is free to slide

Place a wedge of mass \(M\) on a smooth horizontal floor and a block of mass \(m\) on its smooth upper face. Let the slope descend to the right at angle \(\alpha\). Choose \(X\) as the wedge's horizontal position and \(s\) as distance down the face. The block's coordinates apart from fixed offsets are \(x=X+s\cos\alpha,\ y=-s\sin\alpha\). Its relative velocity points down the slope but its absolute acceleration need not. The kinetic energy contains a mixed term \(m\dot X\dot s\cos\alpha\). Dropping that term is equivalent to holding the wedge stationary while allowing it to recoil, two incompatible mechanical models.

The reduced equations are \((M+m)\ddot X+m\ddot s\cos\alpha=0\) and \(\ddot s+\ddot X\cos\alpha=g\sin\alpha\). The first is conservation of total horizontal momentum, since the block–wedge normal force is internal and the floor is frictionless. Elimination gives

\[\ddot s=(M+m)g\sin\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha),\]
\[\ddot X=-mg\sin\alpha\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

The acceleration down the moving face exceeds \(g\sin\alpha\) because the wedge moves left as the block slides right. Nevertheless the absolute vertical acceleration of the block remains less than or equal to free fall because the upper face pushes upward.

To find the block's normal force, project its absolute acceleration onto the outward unit normal \((\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)\). The part proportional to \(\ddot s\) cancels, leaving \(\ddot X\sin\alpha\). Newton's equation along that direction gives

\[N=m(g\cos\alpha+\ddot X\sin\alpha)=mMg\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

With \(M=3.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=1.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(\alpha=30^\circ\), one finds \(\ddot s=6.04\,{\rm m/s^2}\),

\[\ddot X=-1.31\,{\rm m/s^2},\]
\[N=7.84\,{\rm N}.\]

The normal based on a stationary wedge would be \(8.50\,{\rm N}\). A force sensor under the moving face could distinguish the two models even when a short record of relative slope displacement cannot resolve the difference.

Which reaction does no virtual work here? The normal force on the block has virtual work \(N\hat n\cdot(\delta X\hat x+\delta s\hat t)=N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\). The opposite normal force on the wedge gives \(-N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\), so the sum cancels. Considering only the block and asserting zero work from the normal for arbitrary \(\delta X\) would be incorrect. At the real velocities the same reaction transfers energy between the bodies even though its net internal power on their pair is zero. The floor's normal reaction balances the vertical momentum change of the whole system. During the slide it is \((M+m)g-m\ddot s\sin\alpha\), less than total weight while the block accelerates downwards.

Take the limits as an additional test. At fixed \(m,\alpha\), increasing \(M\) to infinity returns the familiar fixed-wedge results. At a nearly horizontal face, downslope acceleration and wedge recoil vanish, restoring the full block weight as normal force. At a vertical face the normal vanishes and the block falls. These limits hold because the contact has no friction and can be maintained with a nonnegative normal. A moving wedge of independently prescribed acceleration would replace the horizontal momentum equation by a control input, changing both the relative acceleration and the support force.

Distinguish rolling constraints from purely geometric restrictions

A cylinder of radius \(R\), mass \(m\) and axial inertia \(I\) rolls without slip down a fixed rough incline. Choose coordinate \(s\) down the incline and spin angle \(\phi\) in the corresponding sense. The constraint \(\dot s=R\dot\phi\) is integrable in this one-dimensional motion, so it can be inserted as \(s-R\phi=\mathrm{constant}\). The resulting kinetic energy is \((m+I/R^2)\dot s^2/2\). The reduced equation gives

\[\ddot s=g\sin\alpha/[1+I/(mR^2)].\]

Reconstruct the static friction from translation, \(f=g m\sin\alpha-m\ddot s\), directed up the slope, and compare it with the available maximum \(\mu_s mg\cos\alpha\).

For a homogeneous solid cylinder \(I=mR^2/2\), the speed-up is \(2g\sin\alpha/3\) and friction magnitude \(mg\sin\alpha/3\). A ring of the same mass and radius has \(I=mR^2\), half the gravitational downslope acceleration and half the downslope weight component as friction. At \(30^\circ\) the minimum static friction coefficients are respectively \(\tan30^\circ/3=0.192\) and \(\tan30^\circ/2=0.289\). Two objects on the same surface can therefore diverge qualitatively because the ring slips while the cylinder still rolls. Treating an assumed no-slip relation as exact without testing that inequality can produce an unphysical result despite apparently consistent energy conservation.

The friction on a rigid body rolling without slip on a fixed surface has zero power because its instantaneous point of contact is stationary. The force still changes both linear and angular momentum. On a moving conveyor the contact velocity is no longer zero in the laboratory; friction can supply mechanical energy even while the relative contact velocity vanishes. For a wheel steering on a plane the restrictions \(\dot x=R\dot\phi\cos\psi,\ \dot y=R\dot\phi\sin\psi\) depend on heading \(\psi\). In general they cannot be integrated into two configuration equations relating \(x,y,\phi,\psi\) globally. d'Alembert's fixed-time allowed variations still project out ideal rolling reactions, but replacing those velocity constraints by invented holonomic coordinates gives the wrong dynamics. The distinction can be seen geometrically by rolling and steering through a closed sequence of steering operations that leaves a net displacement.

Find loads on two supports of a suspended bar

A rigid horizontal bar of mass \(m\) and length \(2a\) is supported at its ends by two vertical inextensible light cords. At one instant both cords are vertical and the bar is horizontal. An extra downward load of magnitude \(F\) is applied at horizontal coordinate \(s\) relative to the centre. Ask first for a static experiment. The two vertical reaction forces \(T_L,T_R\) obey force balance \(T_L+T_R=mg+F\) and moment balance \(a(T_R-T_L)=sF\). This gives

\[T_R=(mg+F+sF/a)/2,\]
\[T_L=(mg+F-sF/a)/2.\]

The lever arms encode geometry that the vertical balance alone cannot provide.

The same result can be obtained from virtual variations if the reactions are kept as multipliers until the end. For small vertical translation \(y\) of the centre and small tilt \(\theta\), the endpoint vertical displacements are \(\delta y-a\delta\theta\) on the left and \(\delta y+a\delta\theta\) on the right. The load point displacement is \(\delta y+s\delta\theta\). Before eliminating the endpoint constraints, project all applied and reaction forces against arbitrary independent \(\delta y,\delta\theta\); vanishing of both coefficients gives the preceding balances. After imposing both endpoint constraints directly, these two variations vanish and the reduced model for vertical motion contains no equation from which the support loads can be read. Removing a degree of freedom can remove precisely the observable of interest unless reactions are reconstructed.

For \(m=2.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.40\,{\rm m}\), \(F=12\,{\rm N}\) and \(s=0.25\,{\rm m}\), the total load is \(31.62\,{\rm N}\) and the load difference \(7.50\,{\rm N}\). Hence \(T_R=19.56\,{\rm N}\), \(T_L=12.06\,{\rm N}\). Exchanging the load from right to left reverses the difference while preserving the sum. This symmetry is a calibrating manoeuvre for two load cells with different offsets: subtract readings from mirrored configurations to isolate the lever-arm response, and compare their sum to the known total weight and applied force.

Now imagine three vertical supports at \(-a,0,+a\). Their three tensions enter only two independent balances. Perfectly rigid constraints determine no unique allocation to the centre support. The ambiguity is physical; the stiffnesses, preloads and small height differences of real supports decide the result. With three identical unstrained linear springs and a centred load \(F\), each spring extends equally if the bar stays level, so each carries one third of the total weight. If the middle spring is twice as stiff and all have the same unloaded height, it carries one half and each outer spring one quarter. Both answers satisfy exactly the same force and moment balances. Reporting a unique value from a singular ideal multiplier system would manufacture an unmeasured constitutive assumption.

Test an acceleration constraint against drift of the position itself

Solving \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\) guarantees the correct instantaneous normal acceleration at a state lying on the constraint manifold with tangent velocity. It does not magically repair earlier position or velocity errors in approximate time integration. For a particle constrained to a circle, monitor \(g=(r^2-l^2)/2\), \(\dot g=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\), and the computed force. A small tangential displacement can give tiny instantaneous force error while the radius gradually drifts. Integrating an acceleration-level constraint twice without a position or velocity correction can cause substantial mismatch over many revolutions.

A simple projection illustrates what each correction does. Normalise an approximate position vector to length \(l\), then remove the radial component of velocity by \(\mathbf v\leftarrow\mathbf v-\hat r(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)\). The corrected point and tangent satisfy the constraints, but the velocity correction removes kinetic energy \(m(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)^2/2\), and the position change can alter potential energy. Projection is therefore a numerical operation whose physical invariant errors must be measured. A structure-preserving constrained integrator builds the position and momentum conditions into the step and often gives better long-time behaviour. Compare convergence of reaction, constraint residual and energy separately instead of treating a rounded radius as proof of a correct force.

A pendulum on a free cart tests the complete mass matrix

Mount a pendulum pivot on a cart of mass \(M\) moving horizontally on a smooth track. The bob has mass \(m\), length \(l\) and downward angle \(\theta\). Choose the cart position \(X\) and use bob coordinates \(X+l\sin\theta,-l\cos\theta\). The kinetic energy is \((M+m)\dot X^2/2+ml\dot X\dot\theta\cos\theta+ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\). Two different off-diagonal mass terms would contradict its origin as a quadratic form; the same factor \(ml\cos\theta\) multiplies the companion acceleration in each equation. This is a useful check on a manually assembled multibody model.

The equations are

\[(M+m)\ddot X+ml(\ddot\theta\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2\sin\theta)=0,\]
\[l\ddot\theta+\ddot X\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0.\]

In the small-angle regime the horizontal centre of mass moves uniformly. Choosing zero total momentum gives

\[\dot X\simeq-ml\dot\theta/(M+m).\]

Substitute its derivative into the angular equation to obtain

\[\ddot\theta+[(M+m)g/(Ml)]\theta=0.\]

Releasing the support raises the oscillation frequency because the effective angular inertia falls while the gravitational stiffness stays the same. The result is a statement about the relative pendulum angle, measured from an inertial vertical, with an unforced horizontally moving support.

For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=0.20\,{\rm kg}\) and \(l=0.50\,{\rm m}\), the small-angle period is

\[2\pi\sqrt{Ml/[(M+m)g]}=1.229\,{\rm s}.\]

Fixing the cart with a clamp gives \(1.419\,{\rm s}\) instead. At zero total horizontal momentum the cart displacement from its equilibrium is approximately \(-0.125\,\theta\) metres, so a \(0.10\,{\rm rad}\) angle corresponds to \(12.5\,{\rm mm}\) of recoil. Simultaneous position measurements on cart and bob should reconstruct a horizontal centre of mass that is stationary to within tracking uncertainty. A missing cart inertia or a wrongly filled cross term can alter both period and recoil; checking just one number may not identify which error occurred.

The reaction on the moving bob can again be recovered. Its radial acceleration along the outward string direction is \(\ddot X\sin\theta-l\dot\theta^2\). Thus the tension is \(T=m[g\cos\theta-\ddot X\sin\theta+l\dot\theta^2]\). The acceleration of the support appears explicitly. Reusing the fixed-pivot formula at all angles would omit a term that vanishes accidentally at the bottom. To test the sign, observe a rightward tilted pendulum whose pivot accelerates right: the support's acceleration has an outward projection along the tilted string, reducing the inward tension needed for the same relative motion. Substituting a directly measured cart acceleration provides an independent load-cell prediction.

Decide which force a sensor actually reports

Reaction forces can be reported as tension along a member, normal traction at a contact, total support load on a floor, or a single Cartesian component read by a load cell. A rigid-body idealisation alone does not specify a sensor's orientation or the weight of its housing. For a fixed pendulum, for example, the string carries tension \(T\), while a vertical force channel at its pivot reads the vertical string contribution \(T\cos\theta\). Euler–Poinsot motion, torque-free nutation and the intermediate axis

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A freely rotating body has fixed angular momentum in space, yet the components recorded by three body-mounted gyroscopes can vary periodically or linger near an unstable rotation. Solve the nonsymmetric top from its invariants, distinguish its body-component period from its spatial orientation, and extract a stability time scale from a measured inertia tensor.

Derive the body equations from a spatial conservation law

Choose principal axes fixed in a rigid body and label its moments \(I_1<I_2<I_3\). In the co-rotating basis the angular-momentum components are

\[L_i=I_i\omega_i.\]

For any vector expressed in that basis, its inertial derivative is its component derivative plus \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L\). With no external torque the inertial derivative is zero. Taking components gives

\[I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\]
\[I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\]
\[I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2 .\]

Multiply these equations by the corresponding \(\omega_i\) and sum: the coefficients of \(\omega_1\omega_2\omega_3\) cancel, giving constant \(E=\sum I_i\omega_i^2/2\). Multiplying instead by \(I_i\omega_i\) gives constant \(L^2=\sum I_i^2\omega_i^2\). In body angular-momentum space the motion lies on the intersection of a sphere and an energy ellipsoid. At fixed \(L\), the possible energies range from \(L^2/(2I_3)\) to \(L^2/(2I_1)\). The intermediate value \(L^2/(2I_2)\) divides two families of closed curves and contains the unstable principal-axis orientations.

In Poinsot's construction the inertia ellipsoid in angular-velocity space rolls on an invariable plane normal to the fixed spatial angular momentum. The ellipsoid corresponding to a given energy is

\[\sum I_i\omega_i^2=2E.\]

Its normal at the instantaneous angular velocity is proportional to \(\mathbf L\); the tangent plane obeys \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf x=2E\). In space that plane therefore has fixed distance \(2E/L\) from the origin. Its instantaneous point in the body along \(\boldsymbol\omega\) has zero instantaneous rotational velocity because the vector cross product of angular velocity with itself is zero. These facts underlie the rolling construction. A photographed body axis traces a different curve from the moving contact point or from a body-frame gyro trace.

Test all three principal rotations locally

For rotation \((0,0,\Omega)\), linearise the first two Euler equations in transverse perturbations. Eliminating one component gives

\[\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega^2[(I_3-I_2)(I_3-I_1)/(I_1I_2)]\omega_1.\]

Small transverse deviations oscillate. Performing the analogous process about \(I_1\) also gives a restoring sign. About \((0,\Omega,0)\) the sign reverses and the growing exponent is \(\lambda=|\Omega|\sqrt{(I_2-I_1)(I_3-I_2)/(I_1I_3)}\). The unstable rotation is still an exact solution when both perturbations vanish identically. Instability describes the evolution of arbitrarily small disturbances, not a spontaneous violation of the torque-free equations.

For moments proportional to \(1,2,3\) and intermediate-axis rotation \(\Omega=6.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(\lambda=3.46\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and the linear e-folding time is \(0.289\,{\rm s}\). A perturbation with a growing-mode component \(10^{-3}\) of the spin reaches roughly \(10^{-1}\) in \(\ln(100)/\lambda=1.33\,{\rm s}\). Its nonlinear fate remains bounded by the momentum sphere and energy ellipsoid. An exponential growth fit carried past the first large reorientation would therefore misrepresent the global motion. Initial perturbations projected entirely on the decaying linear eigenvector can delay the first visible flip.

Solve the low-energy family with elliptic functions

For \(L^2/(2I_3)<E<L^2/(2I_2)\) the trajectory surrounds the largest-inertia axis. Start at a crossing with \(\omega_2=0,\omega_1=A>0,\omega_3=C>0\). Solving the two invariants gives

\[A^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_1(I_3-I_1)],\]
\[C^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_3(I_3-I_1)].\]

When \(\omega_1\) reaches zero the other transverse component has maximum \(B^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_2(I_3-I_2)]\). Use \(s=\omega_2/B\). Eliminating the other squares with the invariants gives

\[\omega_1^2=A^2(1-s^2),\]
\[\omega_3^2=C^2(1-k_e^2s^2).\]

Euler's second equation now reduces to

\[\dot s^2=\nu^2(1-s^2)(1-k_e^2s^2),\]
\[\nu^2=\frac{(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)}{I_1I_2I_3},\]
\[k_e^2=\frac{(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)}{(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)} .\]

Separating variables defines \(u=\nu t=\int_0^s dw/\sqrt{(1-w^2)(1-k_e^2w^2)}\). Its inverse is Jacobi's \({\rm sn}(u,k_e)\). The continuously signed square roots are \({\rm cn}\) and \({\rm dn}\), giving \((\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3)=(A\,{\rm cn},B\,{\rm sn},C\,{\rm dn})\). This construction defines the special functions from the mechanical quadrature instead of assuming them as a table of answers. At \(k_e=0\) the inverse is a sine and the stabilising motion is harmonic. As \(k_e\to1\), the integral to \(s=1\) diverges logarithmically and the period grows without bound.

The body-frame period is

\[T_b=4K(k_e)/\nu,\]

with \(K\) the same integral evaluated at one. Some software defines elliptic functions in terms of parameter \(m=k_e^2\) rather than modulus \(k_e\). Supplying one to an implementation expecting the other produces a plausible curve with the wrong period. Check the derivative at the zero crossing \(\dot\omega_2(0)=B\nu\) and the time of its first maximum \(K/\nu\) before fitting a long trace. The third component \({\rm dn}\) repeats after half the full period, so a single principal-axis gyro can report a dominant frequency twice that of the full body vector.

Reconstruct the observed nutation angle

Orient the inertial vertical along \(\mathbf L\). The angle between body axis three and this fixed direction obeys \(\cos\theta=L_3/L=I_3\omega_3/L\). For \(I_1,I_2,I_3=(1,2,3)\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) and initial \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0.50,0,1.00)\,{\rm rad/s}\), the invariants give \(L^2=9.25\), \(2E=3.25\) in SI, \(A=B=0.50\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(C=1.00\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(\nu=1.00\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), and \(k_e^2=1/12\). Thus the nutation angle changes from \(\arccos(3/\sqrt{9.25})\approx9.46^\circ\) to \(\arccos[3\sqrt{11/12}/\sqrt{9.25}]\approx19.2^\circ\). A torque is unnecessary for this change in the angle of a body axis relative to fixed \(\mathbf L\).

The principal-axis components alone determine body orientation only when supplemented with an initial attitude and the kinematic equation. For the Euler-angle convention with polar axis \(\mathbf L\), the azimuthal rate of body axis three is \(\dot\phi=L(L_1^2/I_1+L_2^2/I_2)/(L_1^2+L_2^2)\) away from the polar singularity. It follows by differentiating the body-axis unit vector in space and projecting onto its azimuthal direction. A complete period of the body components therefore leaves a finite spatial rotation about fixed \(\mathbf L\). Return of the three gyro readings does not imply that a painted mark has returned to its starting position in the laboratory.

Solve the high-energy family and join both sides at the separatrix

For \(L^2/(2I_2)<E<L^2/(2I_1)\) the closed curves on the angular-momentum sphere surround axis one. The component \(\omega_1\) now keeps its sign while \(\omega_3\) changes sign through a cycle. As in the first family, choose a phase with \(\omega_2=0\) and both other components positive. The two invariants give the same initial \(A^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_1(I_3-I_1)]\) and \(C^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_3(I_3-I_1)]\). Now the maximum of \(\omega_2\) occurs when \(\omega_3=0\), so

\[B^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_2(I_2-I_1)].\]

Which component reaches zero is the physical branch choice that determines the arrangement of elliptic functions.

Let \(s=\omega_2/B\). The invariant equations give

\[\omega_3^2=C^2(1-s^2),\]
\[\omega_1^2=A^2(1-k_h^2s^2).\]

Substitution in the second Euler equation yields the same elliptic quadrature but with \(\nu_h^2=(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)/(I_1I_2I_3)\) and \(k_h^2=(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)/[(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)]\). All components are given by \((A\,{\rm dn},B\,{\rm sn},C\,{\rm cn})(\nu_h t,k_h)\), where the notation means each function is evaluated at the same time and modulus. On this energy side \(0<k_h<1\). The low-energy modulus formula evaluated here would exceed one, signalling that the original choice of component scaling and physical extrema must be changed.

For the same inertias \(1,2,3\), choose initial \((1,0,0.20)\,{\rm rad/s}\). Then \(L^2=1.36,\ 2E=1.12\), and the high-energy inequality holds since \(2E>L^2/I_2=0.68\). The formulas give

\[A=1,\ B=\sqrt{0.12}=0.3464,\ C=0.20,\ \nu_h=\sqrt{1/3}=0.5774\,{\rm s^{-1}},\ k_h^2=0.12.\]

The minimum \(\omega_1\) is \(A\sqrt{1-k_h^2}=0.9381\,{\rm rad/s}\), so that principal gyro never reverses even though the third one does. A body-marking aligned with axis three can undergo a conspicuous reversal while the dominant axis-one spin changes only modestly. These component predictions are more specific than an energy or angular-speed trace.

At \(2E=L^2/I_2\), both moduli approach one. The definitions give

\[{\rm sn}(u,1)=\tanh u,\]
\[{\rm cn}(u,1)={\rm dn}(u,1)={\rm sech}\,u.\]

One separatrix solution therefore has \(\omega_2=B\tanh(\nu t)\) with \(\omega_1=A\,{\rm sech}(\nu t)\) and \(\omega_3=C\,{\rm sech}(\nu t)\), up to signs and time shifts consistent with Euler's equations. It connects opposite steady orientations along axis two as \(t\) goes from negative to positive infinity. Its asymptotic approach is exponential, with \(\nu\) equal to the small-disturbance growth exponent at the intermediate-axis equilibrium after inserting \(B=L/I_2\). A nearby periodic orbit spends much of its time near the unstable spin and flips more rapidly between those intervals.

Reconstruct spatial attitude without a singular azimuth

Let \(R(t)\) map body components into a laboratory basis. For body angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega_b\), its kinematic equation is

\[\dot R=R[\boldsymbol\omega_b]_\times,\]

where the skew matrix acts by a cross product. Starting from measured \(R(0)\), propagate by multiplying by the rotation appropriate to each short time interval. Exponentiating the skew matrix for an interval with constant estimated body angular velocity preserves orthogonality and determinant exactly. Applying a naive forward-Euler matrix addition \(R+hR[\omega]_\times\) generally stretches the basis, even if the angular velocity components came from an exact elliptic solution.

For a constant rotation about axis three at one radian per second, a single Euler step of duration \(0.10\,{\rm s}\) takes a transverse unit vector to one of length \(\sqrt{1+0.10^2}=1.00499\). After one hundred such steps the transverse length would grow by \((1.01)^{50}\approx1.64\). A quaternion or matrix exponential avoids that artificial stretch. Normalising each axis separately is insufficient if the axes lose orthogonality; check \(R^TR\) against identity and \(\det R\) against one. Also compute \(R(I_1\omega_1,I_2\omega_2,I_3\omega_3)^T\) at every time. It should give the same spatial vector in a torque-free run.

A measured attitude time series allows a different inference from fitting Euler angles separately. Use relative rotations between successive images to estimate angular velocity in the correct body or spatial frame. A camera can momentarily lose sensitivity to yaw when a marked axis points towards it; simultaneous orthogonal views or multiple non-collinear marks avoid that degeneracy. The gyro and attitude measurements together distinguish a true physical precession from an Euler-angle wrap by \(2\pi\). Synchronise their clocks before comparing the phase of a nutation extremum, since even a small time delay produces a plausible-looking mismatch near a fast flip.

Infer inertia ratios from a free-rotation record

A calibrated triad records \(\omega_i(t)\) in axes thought to be principal. Euler's equations predict that \(\dot\omega_1/(\omega_2\omega_3)\) is the constant \((I_2-I_3)/I_1\) wherever the denominator is appreciable, and similarly for cyclic indices. Instead of dividing noisy derivatives near zeros, integrate each component over a time interval. For example \(\omega_1(t_2)-\omega_1(t_1)=a_1\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\omega_2\omega_3\,dt\). Fit \(a_1\) over several intervals and repeat for \(a_2,a_3\). Select intervals with different angular configurations so that products do not all average nearly to zero. The ratios depend on inertia shape; multiplying every moment by a common constant leaves a torque-free angular-velocity record unchanged.

For a record giving \(a_1=-1.0\), \(a_2=1.0\) and \(a_3=-1/3\), solve the homogeneous equations \(I_2-I_3=-I_1\), \(I_3-I_1=I_2\), \(I_1-I_2=-I_3/3\). They give \(I_1:I_2:I_3=1:2:3\). An independent angular-momentum or torque calibration is needed to set the absolute scale. A known short applied torque impulse that changes one principal angular velocity by a measured amount can do this, provided the other gyroscopic contributions over that impulse are small or accounted for. A fit that estimates all absolute inertias from angular velocity alone can have an apparently excellent residual and still be structurally underdetermined.

If the sensors are not along principal axes the inertia matrix contains off-diagonal entries. The coordinate-free body equation is

\[I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega)=0.\]

Each measured time sample gives equations linear in the constant entries of the symmetric inertia tensor. With sufficiently rich motion one may estimate these entries up to scale, then diagonalise the fitted tensor to recover principal directions. Pure rotation along one axis gives insufficient excitation to reveal three distinct directions. Comparing a family of initial spins with a single tensor provides a stronger validity test than fitting a different set of effective principal inertias to each record.

Relate a body-frame period to a measured geometric phase

After a closed polhode in body angular-momentum space the laboratory orientation differs by a rotation about the fixed spatial momentum. That phase can be separated into a term accumulated at a dynamical rate and a term depending on the oriented solid angle of the body momentum path. For a fixed \(L\), the angular-velocity component along the momentum is \(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\hat L=2E/L\), since \(\mathbf L\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=2E\). Over one body period it contributes \(2ET_b/L\). Parallel transport of a transverse reference direction along the unit momentum curve contributes minus the oriented solid angle enclosed, up to whole turns and the chosen orientation of the sphere. The resulting net attitude phase is \(2ET_b/L-\Omega_{\rm solid}\pmod{2\pi}\).

The geometric part can be visualised by carrying a local tangent basis along the closed curve. Its two basis vectors are constrained to stay perpendicular to the moving unit momentum and are transported with no imposed twist around that direction. A return to the initial unit momentum need not return that basis to its starting orientation. For a latitude circle on the unit sphere the enclosed cap area is \(2\pi(1-\cos\theta)\); shrinking the path to a point makes the geometric phase vanish modulo \(2\pi\). This is why comparing a painted mark after a nutation only to an integrated scalar spin speed can give a discrepancy even with exact torque-free motion.

In practice the total phase can be measured without choosing a cap convention. Fit the attitude at two times separated by the full gyro period, form \(R(t+T_b)R(t)^T\), and extract the relative rotation around the known fixed spatial momentum. Independently integrate \(2E/L\) over that interval and compare the difference modulo \(2\pi\) with the signed spherical area traced by normalised body momentum. A factor-of-two error in the identified period, common if only \(\omega_3\) is recorded in the low-energy family, generally leaves the relative attitude rotation axis different from \(\mathbf L\). The axis check can reveal the error before assigning a numerical geometric phase.

Identify which inertias can exist in an actual rigid body

Three positive principal moments inserted into Euler's equations define a mathematical top, but the moments of a physical nonnegative mass distribution also satisfy triangle inequalities. In principal coordinates \(I_1=\int(y^2+z^2)\,dm\),

\[I_2=\int(z^2+x^2)\,dm,\]
\[I_3=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm.\]

Therefore \(I_1+I_2-I_3=2\int z^2\,dm\ge0\), and likewise by cyclic permutation. Equality means all mass is confined to the corresponding coordinate plane. The convenient example \(1,2,3\) lies on that boundary and can be realised by an ideal planar lamina. A fitted ratio \(1,2,3.5\) would imply negative mean squared distance along one direction and should prompt a calibration or modelling check.

A uniform thin rectangular plate of mass \(M\) and side lengths \(b\) along body axis one and \(a\) along body axis two has \(I_1=Ma^2/12,\ I_2=Mb^2/12,\ I_3=M(a^2+b^2)/12\). Choosing \(b=\sqrt2\,a\) gives exactly \(1:2:3\). For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(b=0.283\,{\rm m}\), the moments are approximately \(0.0020,0.0040,0.0060\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). The component equations for these moments give the same time histories as the scaled numerical example for identical initial angular velocities, while the actual angular momentum and energy are smaller by a factor 500. This gives a physical specimen for checking principal-axis stability without requiring a massive rotor.

Adding a small mass at a known location changes both inertia and possibly the centre of mass. If a symmetric pair of equal small masses is attached at \(\pm d\hat e_3\), their combined mass \(m_a\) adds \(m_a d^2\) to \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), nothing to \(I_3\), and leaves the centre fixed. For the plate above and \(m_a=0.10\,{\rm kg}, d=0.10\,{\rm m}\), the new moments are \(0.0030,0.0050,0.0060\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). The intermediate-axis growth exponent at fixed \(\Omega\) changes from \(|\Omega|/\sqrt3\) to \(|\Omega|\sqrt{(0.005-0.003)(0.006-0.005)/(0.003\cdot0.006)}=|\Omega|/3\). A reversible mass-placement experiment tests the inertia dependence of instability, not merely the existence of a visually dramatic flip.

The heavy symmetric top, precession branches and the sleeping state

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

Gravity supplies a torque to a top pivoted away from its centre of mass. Its axis may precess steadily, nutate between two turning angles, or trace loops. Derive all three from the same angular kinetic energy and determine when an upright spinning top can remain stable.

Project gravity onto a body with one fixed point

Let \(I_1=I_2=I\) and \(I_3\) be moments about the pivot, with the centre of mass a distance \(l\) along the symmetry axis. Use Euler angles \(\theta,\phi,\psi\), where \(\theta\) measures the tilt from upward laboratory vertical, \(\phi\) measures the azimuth of the symmetry axis, and \(\psi\) spins the body about that axis relative to the nodal line. The centre-of-mass height is \(l\cos\theta\), so gravity contributes \(V=Mgl\cos\theta\). In the body the spin component of angular velocity is \(\omega_3=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta\). The squared transverse component is \(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta\). Consequently

\[L=\tfrac12 I(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)+\tfrac12 I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2-Mgl\cos\theta .\]

The angles \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) are absent themselves. Their momenta \(L_z=I\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta\) and \(L_3=I_3\omega_3\) are constants. Gravity torque about the pivot is perpendicular to the vertical and to the body symmetry axis, consistent with these two conservation laws. The entire vector \(\mathbf L\) need not be constant. Distinguish the axial spin \(\omega_3\) measured by a body gyro from the Euler rate \(\dot\psi\). A precessing top can have appreciable \(\dot\phi\cos\theta\); using the painted-mark rate in a spin estimate without specifying the reference can bias a precession prediction.

Eliminate cyclic velocities to find the nutation potential

Solve \(\dot\phi=(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)/(I\sin^2\theta)\) and \(\dot\psi=L_3/I_3-\dot\phi\cos\theta\). The energy can now be written \(E=L_3^2/(2I_3)+I\dot\theta^2/2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta)\), with

\[V_{\rm eff}(\theta)=Mgl\cos\theta+\frac{(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}.\]

The second term is not an additional gravitational interaction. It is the transverse rotational energy required by the two fixed angular momenta at each tilt. A root of \(E-L_3^2/(2I_3)-V_{\rm eff}=0\) is a polar turning angle; a simple root is generally reached and reversed in finite time. A minimum gives stable regular precession under sufficiently small nutations at fixed conserved momenta. The apparent divergence at a pole disappears if the numerator vanishes in the same limit. The possibility of the upright sleeping state therefore requires

\[L_z=L_3.\]

Putting \(u=\cos\theta\) gives a polynomial first integral. With \(E'=E-L_3^2/(2I_3)\), one obtains

\[I^2\dot u^2=2I(E'-Mglu)(1-u^2)-(L_z-L_3u)^2=P_3(u).\]

The accessible range lies within \([-1,1]\) where \(P_3\ge0\). The physical turning points can be found without assigning an Euler azimuth at a pole. A nutation period is \(2I\int_{u_{\min}}^{u_{\max}}du/\sqrt{P_3(u)}\). Computing the azimuthal advance over the same cycle requires averaging \(\dot\phi(u)\) with the same time weight. An arithmetic average of the two turning-point precession rates misses the longer dwell times near the endpoints.

Find slow and fast regular precession

For constant tilt and constant \(\dot\phi=\Omega\), the \(\theta\) equation at a nonzero tilt gives

\[I\Omega^2\cos\theta-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0.\]

This relation follows directly by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to \(\theta\), using \(\partial_\theta[\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta]=-\dot\phi\sin\theta\). It is quadratic in precession speed, so the often-used \(\Omega\simeq Mgl/L_3\) is only one limit. For a top above the horizontal plane and \(L_3>0\), two positive precession branches exist if \(L_3^2\ge4IMgl\cos\theta\). Their sum is \(L_3/(I\cos\theta)\), and their product is \(Mgl/(I\cos\theta)\).

Take \(I=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(I_3=0.010\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(Mgl=0.200\,{\rm N\,m}\),

\[L_3=0.200\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s},\]
\[\theta=60^\circ.\]

The quadratic is \(0.010\Omega^2-0.200\Omega+0.200=0\), with roots \(1.056\) and \(18.94\,{\rm rad/s}\). Both have the same material spin \(\omega_3=20\,{\rm rad/s}\), but their vertical angular momenta are different. The slow branch has \(L_z=0.020(1.056)(0.75)+0.200(0.5)=0.1158\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\); the fast branch has about \(0.384\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\). Preparing a tilt and a spin alone therefore does not select a unique regular precession. The initial angular velocity of the tilted axis also matters.

Distinguish looping, cusped and monotone azimuth traces

During nutation the sign of \(\dot\phi\) is the sign of \(L_z-L_3u\). If \(u_*=L_z/L_3\) lies strictly between the turning points, the azimuth reverses along each nutation and the axis can trace loops on the unit sphere. If \(u_*\) equals a turning point, both \(\dot\theta\) and \(\dot\phi\) can vanish together there, producing a cusp in the ideal trace. If the numerator keeps one sign the azimuth advances monotonically even as tilt oscillates. Seeing an apparent loop in a two-dimensional camera projection is insufficient by itself; recover the three-dimensional orientation or check the signed azimuthal rate.

One classic release starts with a tilted axis that initially has no angular velocity except spin about its own direction. Then \(L_z=L_3\cos\theta_0\), \(\dot\phi=0\), \(\dot\theta=0\). The gravitational torque starts tilting the axis down; on returning to that initial turning point the projected angular speed again momentarily vanishes. By contrast, giving the top the correct nonzero precession velocity at release puts it on a regular precession branch with no initial nutation. Identical tilt and spin measurements can thus yield dramatically different trajectories if the release mechanism imparts a different transverse angular velocity.

Derive the upright stability threshold

At \(L_z=L_3\), reduce the ratio \((1-\cos\theta)^2/\sin^2\theta\) to \((1-\cos\theta)/(1+\cos\theta)\). Near zero tilt the effective potential is \(Mgl+[L_3^2/(8I)-Mgl/2]\theta^2+O(\theta^4)\). A positive coefficient gives an energy minimum and requires \(L_3^2>4IMgl\). For the preceding \(I,Mgl\) this critical angular momentum is \(0.1265\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), or an axial spin speed \(12.65\,{\rm rad/s}\) for the chosen \(I_3\). A high spin stabilises the upper position by coupling tilt to conservation of angular momentum. The purely gravitational potential by itself has a maximum there.

The fixed-\(L_z=L_3\) quadratic energy curvature describes a polar disturbance within that momentum class. Near the axis \(\phi,\psi\) individually become coordinate singularities. To analyse arbitrary small perturbations, use horizontal components of the unit axis and linearise their coupled gyroscopic equations. The two circular precession frequencies at the pole solve \(I\Omega^2-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\), giving the same discriminant. Exactly at threshold the linear frequencies coalesce, and nonlinear terms or dissipation can dominate a finite disturbance. Distinguishing a stable ideal top from an experimentally long-lived slowly slowing top requires monitoring spin over the entire observation interval.

Measure the nutation frequency of a regular precession branch

Take any regular precession tilted away from the poles, with prescribed constants \(L_z,L_3\). A small perturbation of the polar angle gives \(I\ddot\eta+V_{\rm eff}''(\theta_0)\eta=0\), so the nutation frequency at fixed momenta follows from the second derivative of the effective potential. Differentiating while holding the instantaneous \(\dot\phi\) fixed would give a different result, because a displaced top with the same momenta must change its precession rate. This distinction matters when preparing a perturbation: a small tap that adds a transverse angular impulse can change the constants as well as the tilt.

A convenient explicit formula follows by writing \(F(\theta)=L_z-L_3\cos\theta\),

\[w(\theta)=F/(I\sin^2\theta).\]

Then \(V_{\rm eff}'=\sin\theta[-Mgl+L_3w-Iw^2\cos\theta]\). At a regular solution the bracket vanishes. Differentiate \(w\) as \(w'=(L_3/I-2w\cos\theta)/\sin\theta\), substitute into the derivative of the bracket, and obtain

\[\omega_{\rm nut}^2=\frac{V_{\rm eff}''(\theta_0)}{I}=\left(\frac{L_3}{I}-2\Omega\cos\theta_0\right)^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta_0.\]

For the \(60^\circ\) example,

\[L_3/I=10.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

On the slow branch \(\Omega=1.056\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), this gives \(\omega_{\rm nut}\approx8.99\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), or a nutation period of \(0.699\,{\rm s}\). On the fast branch \(\Omega=18.94\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), it gives approximately \(18.7\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), or \(0.336\,{\rm s}\). Both tilted regular solutions have positive fixed-momentum curvature in this example. Comparing just the precession rates would miss the different small oscillations around their two reduced equilibria.

Suppose a small angular displacement conserves \(L_z,L_3\) on the slow branch. The correlated rate shift is \(w'\delta\theta\). At the example point \(w'=(10-1.056)/\sin60^\circ=10.33\,{\rm s^{-1}/rad}\). A nutation of \(0.020\,{\rm rad}\) therefore modulates the precession rate by about \(0.207\,{\rm rad/s}\), a substantial fraction of its slow steady value. A low-resolution video that measures azimuth per frame can show a conspicuous modulation even when the nutation angle itself is visually small. Filtering only one angle and fitting it with constant angular speed would hide this dynamical correlation.

Recover a nutation trajectory from energy and two turn angles

Suppose a measured top has \(I=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(Mgl=0.20\,{\rm J}\), \(L_3=0.20\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), and is released without precession or nutation at \(\cos\theta_0=0.50\). Its constants are \(L_z=0.10\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and \(E'=0.10\,{\rm J}\). The polar polynomial becomes

\[P_3(u)=0.04(0.10-0.20u)(1-u^2)-(0.10-0.20u)^2.\]

Factoring out \(0.10-0.20u\) leaves \(0.04(1-u^2)-(0.10-0.20u)\). The other roots satisfy \(0.04u^2-0.20u+0.06=0\), giving \(u\simeq0.3206\) and \(u\simeq4.68\). Only the first lies within the physical interval. Thus the tilt runs from \(60^\circ\) to about \(71.3^\circ\), and the unphysical cubic root must be discarded.

At the initial \(u=0.50\), the azimuthal numerator vanishes and the top momentarily has no precession. At the lower turning \(u\simeq0.3206\), the precession rate is \((0.10-0.20u)/[0.020(1-u^2)]\approx2.0\,{\rm rad/s}\). These two measurements together discriminate the cusped release from regular slow precession. A detector reporting only a mean precession rate near one radian per second could confuse them. Timing the first turning angle after release offers an independent check of \(Mgl/I\), while a direct gyro constrains \(L_3/I_3\).

To evaluate the nutation quadrature numerically without endpoint difficulties, substitute \(u=u_{\min}+(u_{\max}-u_{\min})\sin^2\alpha\). As \(\alpha\) approaches either endpoint, \(du\) cancels the simple square-root singularity from \(P_3\). Integrate once with numerator one for time and once with the extra factor \(\dot\phi(u)\) for azimuth. Repeat after halving the quadrature spacing. An exactly repeated root signals a limiting regular solution or a separatrix; the simple-turning substitution alone cannot regularise an actual logarithmically divergent period. In the cusped trajectory above the angular trace is continuous at the polar turning, even though the tangent to that trace can vanish.

Use horizontal tilt coordinates at the sleeping pole

Near the upright orientation the separate Euler angles \(\phi,\psi\) are ill conditioned. Instead let the symmetry-axis unit vector be \(\mathbf n=(x,y,1)+O(x^2+y^2)\) in an inertial frame with \(z\) upward. Here \(x,y\) are small dimensionless tilts, not translations. The instantaneous transverse angular velocity is \(\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n}\approx(-\dot y,\dot x,0)\), because \((\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n})\times\mathbf n=\dot{\mathbf n}\). The laboratory angular momentum to first order is \(I\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n}+L_3\mathbf n\). Its derivative equals the gravitational torque \(Mgl\,\hat z\times\mathbf n\). Equating the two horizontal components yields

\[I\ddot x+L_3\dot y-Mgl\,x=0,\]
\[I\ddot y-L_3\dot x-Mgl\,y=0.\]

Let \(w=x+iy\). These combine into \(I\ddot w-iL_3\dot w-Mgl\,w=0\). Substituting \(w\propto e^{i\Omega t}\) returns \(I\Omega^2-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\) without ever assigning azimuth to the exact pole. For spin strictly above threshold, a general small tilt is a superposition of two circular motions of the two real frequencies. Their vector sum traces a wobble. For spin below threshold, the two frequencies form a complex conjugate pair and one mode grows exponentially. A perfectly vertical initial state can remain exactly vertical in an ideal simulation on either side, so stability requires a nonzero test perturbation.

Take a below-threshold spin \(L_3=0.100\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) with the earlier \(I=0.020\) and \(Mgl=0.200\). The real precession rate is \(L_3/(2I)=2.50\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and the exponential growth rate is

\[\sqrt{4IMgl-L_3^2}/(2I)=1.94\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

If the growing-mode component starts at \(1\,{\rm mrad}\), a \(20\,{\rm mrad}\) excursion takes about \(\ln20/1.94=1.55\,{\rm s}\) within the linear model. Below threshold the top need not collapse immediately after release. A short video can show several degrees of apparently orderly precession before growing tilt becomes unmistakable.

Just above threshold the mode frequencies are nearly degenerate. Decomposing noisy initial conditions into two circular amplitudes then involves division by their small frequency separation. A finite tilt impulse can produce a large beat envelope even though both ideal modes remain bounded. At exactly the repeated root the general linear solution contains \(t e^{i\Omega t}\) as well as \(e^{i\Omega t}\); neutral-looking roots alone do not ensure boundedness. This distinction matters when inferring a critical spin from finite observation windows. Report the initial perturbation and the tilt threshold used to define loss of stability.

Infer the centre-of-mass offset from controlled precession

If regular precession can be prepared at a known tilt, the quadratic balance gives

\[Mgl=L_3\Omega-I\Omega^2\cos\theta.\]

Neglecting the second term is customary only on a sufficiently slow branch. With the slow \(60^\circ\) case the neglected term is about \(0.0112\,{\rm N\,m}\), or \(5.6\%\) of the true gravitational torque scale. On the fast branch the two terms nearly cancel and a small error in \(I\) or \(\Omega\) can be magnified substantially in the inferred \(Mgl\).

For a top of measured mass \(0.50\,{\rm kg}\) and \(Mgl=0.200\,{\rm N\,m}\), the offset is \(0.0408\,{\rm m}\). Verify the torque law with a horizontal gyroscope

An instructive limiting experiment supports one end of a spinning wheel axle at a pivot, leaving its symmetry axis nearly horizontal. Choose the centre of mass along the free end of the axle and define the wheel's axial angular momentum by the right-hand rule. The gravitational torque is \(\boldsymbol\tau=Mgl\,\hat z\times\mathbf n\). If \(\mathbf L\) is approximately \(L_3\mathbf n\), a regular precession at laboratory angular speed \(\Omega\) gives

\[\dot{\mathbf L}\approx L_3\Omega\,\hat z\times\mathbf n.\]

Equating the torque gives

\[\Omega=Mgl/L_3.\]

At exactly horizontal tilt this agrees with the finite root of the full quadratic; slightly away from horizontal the small transverse angular momentum affects the quantitative result.

For a wheel with \(I_3=0.012\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), axial spin \(40\,{\rm rad/s}\), mass \(0.40\,{\rm kg}\) and pivot offset \(0.060\,{\rm m}\),

\[L_3=0.480\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s},\]
\[Mgl=0.235\,{\rm N\,m}.\]

Horizontal precession is \(0.490\,{\rm rad/s}\), one revolution in \(12.8\,{\rm s}\). Reversing the spin reverses the precession direction. Moving the pivot to the other side of the centre also reverses the gravitational torque for a fixed axis convention. Testing each reversal separately helps distinguish the true gyroscopic response from a static torque due to a tilted support or a twisting suspension lead.

The phrase that gravity causes the wheel to stay up hides a vertical support force and a changing horizontal angular momentum. The pivot supplies most or all of the vertical force required to support the wheel's weight. With steady horizontal precession, the centre of mass also accelerates horizontally toward the precession axis with magnitude \(l\Omega^2\). Thus the pivot must supply a horizontal radial reaction \(Ml\Omega^2\). For the numerical wheel this is only \(0.0058\,{\rm N}\), much smaller than the \(3.92\,{\rm N}\) vertical support. A torque signal can be easy to see through slow precession while the corresponding horizontal reaction is weak. A load cell measuring only vertical force would not directly measure the gravitational torque acting about the pivot.

Spin friction alters the interpretation of a long run. Suppose the measured axial spin falls from \(40\) to \(35\,{\rm rad/s}\) over one minute and the axis remains close to horizontal regular precession. The predicted precession speed rises from \(0.490\) to \(0.561\,{\rm rad/s}\), even though mechanical energy is being dissipated. This is not negative damping. Less axial angular momentum must change direction under essentially the same gravitational torque, so its direction turns more rapidly. If the spin-down time becomes comparable with a nutation period, an instantaneous regular-precession approximation is no longer sufficient; include dissipative torques in the full equations and measure both tilt and angular momentum.

Canonical perturbations, small denominators and resonant capture

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants

A weak periodic force can produce a bounded ripple on most trajectories and a large change on a narrow band of initial conditions. Derive the averaging transformation away from resonance, construct the slow resonant dynamics where that transformation fails, and estimate the physically observable width and time scale of a resonance.

Separate the unperturbed clock from a weak correction

Take an integrable Hamiltonian \(H(I,\theta,t)=H_0(I)+\epsilon H_1(I,\theta,t)\) in action–angle variables. With \(\epsilon=0\), actions are constants and angles advance at \(\boldsymbol\omega(I)=\partial_I H_0\). A perturbation with Fourier component \(h_{\mathbf n}(I)\cos(\mathbf n\cdot\theta-\Omega t)\) drives action changes proportional to its sine. If the phase sweeps quickly through all angles, positive and negative contributions largely cancel. If \(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\omega-\Omega\) is small, the perturbation acts with a coherent sign over a long time. We want a transformation that makes both statements quantitative.

Use a near-identity type-two generating function \(F_2(\theta,J,t)=\theta\cdot J+\epsilon S_1(\theta,J,t)\). Old actions are \(I=J+\epsilon\partial_\theta S_1\), and new angles are \(\psi=\theta+\epsilon\partial_JS_1\). Expand \(H_0(I)=H_0(J)+\epsilon\boldsymbol\omega(J)\cdot\partial_\theta S_1+O(\epsilon^2)\). At first order the new Hamiltonian is \(K=H_0(J)+\epsilon[H_1+\partial_t S_1+\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\partial_\theta S_1]\). Replacing old by new angles inside that bracket produces only higher-order errors. To leave the averaged part \(\langle H_1\rangle\), solve the homological equation

\[(\partial_t+\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\partial_\theta)S_1=-\big(H_1-\langle H_1\rangle\big).\]

For an oscillating cosine with detuning \(\Delta=\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\omega-\Omega\), choose \(S_1=-h_{\mathbf n}\sin(\mathbf n\cdot\theta-\Omega t)/\Delta\). The transformation is useful while the resulting action displacement \(\epsilon|\partial_\theta S_1|\) is small on the relevant action scale and the derivatives entering the angle transformation remain controlled. Small \(\epsilon\) alone is insufficient if \(\Delta\) is even smaller. A singular denominator says that this choice to remove the harmonic is unsuitable, not that the underlying mechanical force becomes infinite.

Obtain a nonlinear frequency shift from the angle average

A free oscillator with mass \(m\), small-amplitude frequency \(\omega_0\), and quartic correction \(\beta q^4/4\) has unperturbed coordinates \(q=\sqrt{2I/(m\omega_0)}\sin\theta\). Substituting gives the correction \(\beta I^2\sin^4\theta/(m^2\omega_0^2)\). Since \(\langle\sin^4\theta\rangle=3/8\), the first-order averaged Hamiltonian is \(K=\omega_0J+3\beta J^2/(8m^2\omega_0^2)\). Its derivative gives

\[\omega=\omega_0+3\beta J/(4m^2\omega_0^2).\]

Expressing \(J\simeq m\omega_0 A^2/2\) in terms of leading oscillation amplitude yields the frequency shift \(3\beta A^2/(8m\omega_0)\). Oscillatory harmonics at \(2\theta,4\theta\) change the waveform but do not contribute directly to this averaged first-order clock.

For \(m=0.20\,{\rm kg}\), \(\omega_0=10.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), \(\beta=80\,{\rm N/m^3}\), and \(A=0.10\,{\rm m}\), the predicted shift is \(0.150\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), about \(1.5\%\). The nonlinear-to-linear force ratio at maximum displacement is \(\beta A^2/(m\omega_0^2)=0.040\), consistent with the expansion. At twice the amplitude the shift quadruples and the expansion parameter grows to \(0.16\). Measuring period over several amplitudes and plotting the frequency against \(A^2\) tests both the slope and the onset of curvature from higher orders. A positive \(\beta\) stiffens the spring; a negative quartic term is only a local approximation unless higher powers restore global confinement.

Solve the slow motion in the resonant frame

Consider a rotor \(H=I^2/(2{\cal I})-\epsilon V\cos(\theta-\Omega t)\), where \({\cal I}\) is moment of inertia and \(\epsilon V>0\) has energy units. Choose \(F_2=(\theta-\Omega t)P\), so \(P=I\),

\[\phi=\theta-\Omega t,\]
\[K=P^2/(2{\cal I})-\Omega P-\epsilon V\cos\phi.\]

Completing the square gives \(K=(P-P_*)^2/(2{\cal I})-\epsilon V\cos\phi+\mathrm{constant}\) with \(P_*={\cal I}\Omega\). This is an exact pendulum in the rotating frame. It retains the resonant cosine instead of attempting to average it away. Phase locking means libration of \(\phi\) around zero; outside its separatrix the relative phase circulates.

The stable fixed point has \(P=P_*,\phi=0\); its small libration frequency is

\[\omega_{\rm lib}=\sqrt{\epsilon V/{\cal I}}.\]

The saddle at \(\phi=\pi\) has energy \(+\epsilon V\) relative to the same constant. At \(\phi=0\) that energy is reached for \(|P-P_*|=2\sqrt{{\cal I}\epsilon V}\), the resonance half-width in action. Its complete width is twice that number. For \({\cal I}=0.050\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(\Omega=8.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(\epsilon V=0.0020\,{\rm J}\), the central action is \(0.40\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\); the half-width is \(0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and small libration period \(31.4\,{\rm s}\). The rotor makes many laboratory turns while its slow relative phase completes one libration.

An initial action inside the quoted half-width is necessary but not sufficient for trapping at every phase. At \(\phi=\pi\), any nonzero \(P-P_*\) is above the saddle energy. Compute the actual rotating-frame energy of the initial state and compare with the saddle. For \(\phi=0,P=0.415\), the kinetic detuning energy is \(0.00225\,{\rm J}\); subtracting \(0.0020\,{\rm J}\) gives \(0.00025\,{\rm J}\), below the saddle. With the same action at \(\phi=\pi\) the value is \(0.00425\,{\rm J}\), above the saddle. Selecting trajectories by mean frequency alone can therefore mix trapped and circulating populations.

Retain a resonant harmonic in a general integrable system

For one action with \(H_0'(I_*)=\Omega\), use the rotating frame and expand \(H_0(I)-\Omega I\) about \(I_*\). The linear term vanishes, leaving \(H_0''(I_*)(I-I_*)^2/2\). Retain the slowly varying resonant cosine and average the rapidly varying harmonics. When \(H_0''\ne0\), this gives the same pendulum normal form with an effective inertia and possibly a reversed sign of the stable phase. The action half-width scales as \(2\sqrt{\epsilon|V/H_0''|}\). Thus a weak perturbation can influence a band of width proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\), parametrically larger than a typical nonresonant action ripple proportional to \(\epsilon\).

For an exactly harmonic oscillator \(H_0=\omega_0 I\) the curvature vanishes. One cannot replace \(H_0''\) with zero in the width formula. An exactly tuned additive drive in a linear oscillator instead produces secular growth until damping, detuning, nonlinearity or a finite drive interval intervenes. In multiple actions, one first transforms to the resonant phase \(\mathbf n\cdot\theta-\Omega t\) and complementary angles, preserving the canonical one-form. Other near-resonances can prevent isolation of a single pendulum. Apparent chaos inferred from a short irregular time trace alone needs additional tests, including resolved phase portraits, convergence under time refinement and identification of competing resonances.

Calculate a phase-locking threshold for a drive that sweeps in frequency

The exact rotating rotor also allows a tractable time-dependent drive frequency. Write the pattern angle as \(\alpha(t)\), with \(\Omega(t)=\dot\alpha\). In its frame \(K=P^2/(2{\cal I})-\Omega(t)P-\epsilon V\cos\phi\). Hamilton's equations give

\[\dot\phi=P/{\cal I}-\Omega(t),\]
\[\dot P=-\epsilon V\sin\phi.\]

Define the detuning momentum \(p=P-{\cal I}\Omega(t)\). Even when \(\dot\phi=0\) at one instant, phase locking throughout a ramp requires \(\dot p=0\) and hence \(\sin\phi_0=-{\cal I}\dot\Omega/(\epsilon V)\). The restoring torque must be sufficient to accelerate the rotor along with the changing pattern.

For constant \(\dot\Omega\) and \(|{\cal I}\dot\Omega|<\epsilon V\), a stable locked phase with \(\cos\phi_0>0\) exists. Perturbing the phase by \(\eta\) gives \(\ddot\eta+(\epsilon V/{\cal I})\cos\phi_0\,\eta=0\), so the libration softens as the required torque approaches its maximum. At threshold the stable and unstable stationary phases meet. Above it no fixed phase can provide the torque needed by the ramp. The mere existence of a stable locked solution still does not imply that an arbitrary initial phase and momentum will be captured in it. In a conservative system initial phase-space location and separatrix crossing are essential.

With the previous \({\cal I}=0.050\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) and \(\epsilon V=0.0020\,{\rm J}\), the largest trackable constant acceleration in this model is \(0.040\,{\rm rad/s^2}\). At half that value the stable phase lags by \(\pi/6\), and the small relative-phase frequency is

\[0.20\sqrt{\cos(\pi/6)}=0.186\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

Increasing the drive frequency by \(1.0\,{\rm rad/s}\) at this rate takes \(50\,{\rm s}\), only about one and a half libration periods. Adiabatic capture estimates that additionally require many libration periods may therefore be unconvincing even though the elementary torque threshold is satisfied. A measured lag versus sweep rate assesses the static torque condition, while a full phase trajectory tests dynamic capture.

The same equations admit a tilted effective washboard potential in the accelerating frame, \(U_{\rm rel}=-\epsilon V\cos\phi+{\cal I}\dot\Omega\,\phi\), as long as \(\dot\Omega\) is constant. Its potential difference over a whole \(2\pi\) turn is nonzero, so unwrap the relative phase before using its energy. Mapping every phase back to \([-\pi,\pi)\) hides this difference and produces artificial jumps in the inferred energy. In the original variables the physical motor does work. There is no contradiction between a conserved local accelerating-frame energy for a constant ramp and the changing laboratory kinetic energy of a locked rotor.

Derive secular energy exchange in a pair of weakly coupled oscillators

Take two equal masses \(m\) with individual natural frequency \(\omega_0\) and potential coupling \(k_c(q_1-q_2)^2/2\). The normal coordinates \(Q_\pm=(q_1\pm q_2)/\sqrt2\) have frequencies \(\omega_+=\omega_0\),

\[\omega_-=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+2k_c/m}.\]

Release mass one at displacement \(A\) with both velocities and \(q_2\) initially zero. Superposition gives

\[q_1=A[\cos\omega_+ t+\cos\omega_-t]/2,\]
\[q_2=A[\cos\omega_+t-\cos\omega_-t]/2.\]

The slowly varying difference phase transfers oscillation amplitude between the masses over a time much longer than their rapid periods.

For \(\omega_0=10\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(k_c/m=1.0\,{\rm s^{-2}}\), the exact separation is

\[\sqrt{102}-10=0.0995\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

The first near-complete exchange occurs at \(t=\pi/|\omega_--\omega_+|\approx31.6\,{\rm s}\). Over a single fast period the displacements can look almost like uncoupled oscillations, despite large later energy redistribution. Perturbing two isolated oscillators while trying to average the coupling over both independent fast angles would discard a term depending on their slow difference phase. Retaining the resonant combination predicts the exchange; diagonalising the linear model supplies a reference solution for that approximation.

Detune the uncoupled springs so their natural frequencies have a separation large compared with \(k_c/(m\omega_0)\). The coupled normal modes then align more closely with the original oscillators, and the energy exchange cannot reach its former depth. In a nonlinear problem the individual frequencies also depend on action, so the energy transfer can detune itself. To interpret an observed beating envelope, measure both normal frequencies and relative phases. An apparent loss of amplitude in oscillator one can be energy stored in oscillator two, distinguishable from damping through recovery at later times and a balance of the total Hamiltonian.

Estimate the accuracy of averaging over a finite observation time

For a nonresonant perturbation with initially slow action change, one can integrate the first-order drive along the unperturbed trajectory over the actual observation window instead of assuming an infinite-time average. For \(H_1=A\cos(\theta-\Omega t)\), \(\dot I=A\sin(\Delta t+\phi_0)\) to leading order with \(\Delta=\omega_0-\Omega\). Thus \(\Delta I(T)=A[\cos\phi_0-\cos(\Delta T+\phi_0)]/\Delta\). For \(\Delta\ne0\) its absolute value remains at most \(2|A/\Delta|\), and its largest possible value over initial phase is \(2|A\sin(\Delta T/2)/\Delta|\). For \(\Delta\to0\) the phase-dependent result tends to \(AT\sin\phi_0\). The finite-time expression is regular right at resonance and shows exactly when a secular term emerges.

For \(A=0.001\,{\rm J}\), \(T=10\,{\rm s}\) and \(\Delta=0.20\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), the maximum-amplitude change in action over initial phase is \(0.00841\,{\rm J\,s}\). At zero detuning the leading largest change is \(0.0100\,{\rm J\,s}\), only modestly larger because this observation window resolves a frequency band of order \(1/T\). For \(\Delta=2\pi/T\) the change after exactly \(T\) vanishes, although the action swung during the interval. A final-minus-initial measurement alone can mistake a completed nonresonant excursion for an entirely unperturbed trajectory. Recording at an additional end time tests that cancellation.

At leading order a force of finite duration produces this sine-over-detuning envelope even in a linear system. It is an observation-time bandwidth, distinct from the dynamical resonance width proportional to \(\sqrt{\epsilon}\) in the nonlinear rotor. Repeat the experiment with doubled drive duration at fixed amplitude. Features that narrow inversely with the duration are evidence of finite-time spectral selection. A persistent bounded island in a stroboscopic phase portrait, with the predicted amplitude-dependent width and libration frequency, is evidence of the phase-locking dynamics. If drive amplitude is large enough to change the natural frequency appreciably over the interval, the unperturbed phase-integration formula ceases to describe the trajectory.

Construct a stroboscopic section with the right clock

For a time-periodic Hamiltonian sample \((I,\theta)\) every period of the external drive at a fixed drive phase. This creates a two-dimensional area-preserving map for one periodically forced degree of freedom. A phase-locked solution can become a fixed point of this map, and librating trajectories around it can form an island. Sampling at each crossing of a moving coordinate threshold instead can produce a different return map and a different phase weight. A return plot can look irregular simply because the drive phase was not held fixed at the samples.

Consider a rigid rotor commanded at \(\Omega=8\,{\rm s^{-1}}\). Its drive period is

\[2\pi/8=0.7854\,{\rm s}.\]

An acquisition system sampling nominally every \(0.7900\,{\rm s}\) accumulates approximately \(8(0.7900-0.7854)=0.0368\,{\rm rad}\) of phase error per sample. After one hundred samples the sampling phase has moved about \(3.68\,{\rm rad}\), enough to smear a narrow island across a substantial fraction of the plot. Interpolating trajectories to timestamps from a synchronised drive encoder controls that effect. Keep unwrapped relative phase as a separate channel to count actual slips.

A numerical area test on the stroboscopic map should use canonical action and angle or an equivalent canonical coordinate pair. A scatter plot of polar angle against angular velocity for a state-dependent inertia can distort area. Changes of pixel density, trajectory dwell time and resolution near the separatrix further affect visual appearance. To identify a resonance quantitatively, measure the action extent at the stable phase, the local libration period and the saddle crossing energy on independently prepared trajectories. Agreement among those three observations is less easily mimicked by a sampling artefact than the existence of a visually closed loop alone.

Recover a frequency shift by removing a secular term

Canonical averaging yields a frequency correction for the quartic oscillator. An independent derivation makes clear why a small difference in frequency can become a large trajectory error at long times. Write the equation as \(\ddot q+\omega_0^2q+(\beta/m)q^3=0\). Put \(q=A x\), \(\tau=\omega t\), \(\lambda=\beta A^2/(m\omega_0^2)\) and seek \(\omega=\omega_0(1+c\lambda+\cdots)\),

\[x=x_0+\lambda x_1+\cdots.\]

Leading initial conditions \(x(0)=1,x'(0)=0\) give

\[x_0=\cos\tau.\]

At first order one finds

\[x_1''+x_1=2c\cos\tau-\cos^3\tau.\]

Using \(\cos^3\tau=(3\cos\tau+\cos3\tau)/4\), the right-hand side contains a term \((2c-3/4)\cos\tau\) resonant with the leading motion. Choosing \(c=3/8\) removes the forcing that would otherwise generate a term proportional to \(\tau\sin\tau\). The frequency then agrees with the action-angle calculation. The remaining particular solution is \(\cos3\tau/32\). Including the homogeneous term required by \(x_1(0)=x_1'(0)=0\) gives

\[x_1=(\cos3\tau-\cos\tau)/32.\]

This separates the leading frequency shift from an actual third-harmonic distortion of the orbit. Fitting only frequency and overlooking the small third harmonic cannot generally reproduce a precise waveform.

In the earlier example \(\lambda=0.040\), so the displacement amplitude at the third harmonic is \(A\lambda/32=0.125\,{\rm mm}\) at \(A=0.10\,{\rm m}\). The frequency shift is about \(0.15\,{\rm s^{-1}}\). After twenty seconds the uncorrected harmonic prediction is nearly three radians out of phase, far larger than the instantaneous third-harmonic displacement. A small-amplitude correction to the right-hand side can therefore cause an order-one error in the long-time phase. Measuring both the third harmonic and frequency gives two independent checks of the same inferred quartic stiffness. A drive electronics harmonic can mimic the former without producing the amplitude-dependent free-oscillation frequency.

Test overlap of nearby resonances without declaring chaos from one curve

Take a rotor driven by two weak periodic patterns with distinct angular speeds \(\Omega_1,\Omega_2\). For an isolated pattern \(j\), the earlier calculation gives a resonant centre \(P_j={\cal I}\Omega_j\) and half-width \(\Delta P_j=2\sqrt{{\cal I}\epsilon V_j}\). Comparing the sum of estimated half-widths with the separation \(|P_2-P_1|\) is a practical indication of when the isolated-island approximation may fail. From first principles

Why cantilever stiffness scales as the inverse cube of span

Take a straight slender beam clamped horizontally, with a downward end force \(P\). The empirical inputs are small-strain linear elasticity and the beam assumption that plane sections remain normal to the deflected centreline. Denote its centroidal bending rigidity by \(EI\). With constant section and modulus the stress moment of the stretched and compressed fibres is proportional to curvature. With downward deflection positive, equilibrium of a cut at \(x\) from the clamp supplies

\[M(x)=P(L-x),\]
\[w''(x)=M(x)/(EI).\]

Curvature is the rate at which slope changes. Integrating it once gives the slope, and integrating slope gives deflection. Combine both integrations into a weighted curvature integral: curvature occurring near the clamp rotates a longer remaining segment than curvature near the tip. The clamp fixes initial slope and displacement to zero, hence

\[w(L)=\int_0^L(L-x)w''(x)\,dx=\frac{P}{EI}\int_0^L(L-x)^2dx=\frac{PL^3}{3EI}.\]

This is a displacement prediction under small slopes, not just a stress limit. Its input \(M=EIw''\) is established from individual fibre strains and the neutral-axis condition in the detailed derivation. Short beams can also require shear deflection.

Background · Second moment of area
\[I=\int_A y^2\,dA\]

Measure the transverse coordinate from the stated bending axis. This section property has units of length to the fourth power and enters the flexural rigidity.

Derivation and applications

From the extension of a fibre to cantilever curvature and displacement

Why does section depth enter a bending calculation through a second moment of area? Take a straight prismatic cantilever with small slopes, uniform Young’s modulus \(E\), linear elastic response and slender dimensions. Euler–Bernoulli kinematics assumes that cross-sections remain plane and normal to the deflected centreline. Measure \(x\) from the clamp and transverse deflection \(w\) downward. Measure cross-section coordinate \(y\) downward from an initially arbitrary reference line. Sections separated by \(dx\) rotate relative to one another by \(d\theta\); a fibre at \(y\) shortens relative to the reference line by \(y\,d\theta\).

\[\epsilon_x(y)=\epsilon_0-y\,d\theta/dx\simeq\epsilon_0-yw''(x),\]
\[\sigma_x=E\epsilon_x .\]

With no imposed axial resultant, the integral of this normal stress must vanish. Writing \(\bar y=A^{-1}\int_A y\,dA\), we obtain the strain of the reference line, and thereby identify the zero-strain fibre:

\[0=N=\int_A E(\epsilon_0-yw'')\,dA,\]
\[\epsilon_0=\bar y w'',\]
\[\sigma_x=-E(y-\bar y)w''.\]

Define bending moment positive for this downward curvature. Each fibre contributes its force times its lever arm about the neutral axis. Integrating introduces the centroidal second moment \(I\):

\[M=-\int_A(y-\bar y)\sigma_x\,dA=EIw'',\]
\[I=\int_A(y-\bar y)^2\,dA.\]

A downward tip load \(P\) gives \(M(x)=P(L-x)\) by equilibrium of the portion beyond the cut. For constant \(EI\), integrate twice and enforce the actual clamp conditions \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\):

\[EI w'=P(Lx-x^2/2),\]
\[EI w=P(Lx^2/2-x^3/6),\]
\[w(L)=PL^3/(3EI).\]

The slope and displacement constants follow from compatibility at the support. The zero moment at the free tip follows independently from the load on the right-hand portion. Plane sections can also remain plane under pure bending of a short specimen, but shear deformation near loads and supports can matter in short cantilevers.

When a beam is strong enough but too flexible

A tool rail may stay below its elastic limit while its cutting head moves outside a position tolerance. The maximum bending stress depends on the largest moment at one section. Deflection accumulates the effect of curvature over a length. A displacement sensor near an unloaded end can therefore move even if the nearby bending moment is zero. To predict it, identify both the point and direction of the required displacement.

Consider a straight, slender Euler–Bernoulli beam. Cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the deformed centreline. Take \(w\) positive upward and \(M\) positive in sagging. For small slopes the signed curvature is \(w''\), with \(\kappa=M/(EI)\). Integrate once to find the slope and once more to find the displacement. Each constant of integration has physical meaning. A clamp fixes displacement and rotation. A simple support fixes its vertical displacement while permitting rotation. At an ordinary join without a hinge, both displacement and slope are continuous.

Work through a tip reading

Clamp a beam at \(x=0\). Apply a downward point load \(P\) at \(x=a\) on a beam of total length \(L>a\). The signed moment in \(0<x<a\) is \(-P(a-x)\). For \(x>a\) the free segment has no external force and no moment. Taking constant stiffness and using the clamp conditions gives

\[EI w'(x)=-P(ax-x^2/2),\]
\[EI w(x)=-P(ax^2/2-x^3/6),\quad 0\le x\le a.\]

At the load point the displacement is \(-Pa^3/(3EI)\) and the slope is \(-Pa^2/(2EI)\). The free extension beyond it is straight but tilted. Use those values as initial conditions for the unloaded segment:

\[w(L)=w(a)+(L-a)w'(a)=-Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI).\]

This expression correctly vanishes when the force is applied at the clamp and reduces to the standard end-load expression when \(a=L\).

Calculate both position and slope

Let \(EI=8400\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}\), \(L=1.20\) m, \(a=0.80\) m and \(P=100\) N. Under the load the downward displacement is 2.03 mm and the rotation magnitude is

\[100(0.80)^2/[2(8400)]=0.00381\,\mathrm{rad}.\]

The 0.40 m overhang contributes another 1.52 mm at the tip, for a total downward reading of 3.56 mm. The slope is small compared with a radian, consistent with the linear curvature assumption. Obtain one displacement directly

There is another way to compute a single sensor reading. Introduce a fictitious force \(Q\) at the sensor in its desired positive direction. The moment becomes \(M_P+Qm\), where \(m\) is the moment per unit sensor force. The bending energy is

\[U=\int(M_P+Qm)^2/(2EI)\,dx.\]

Differentiating with respect to \(Q\) gives the displacement conjugate to that force. Set \(Q=0\) afterwards to recover the original beam.

\[\delta=\int_0^L\frac{M_P(x)m(x)}{E(x)I(x)}\,dx.\]

For a downward unit force at the free tip, \(m=-(L-x)\). Only the portion up to \(a\) contributes because \(M_P=0\) further along. The integral gives \(Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI)\), the positive downward reading obtained above. This form is especially useful for stepped sections: split the integral where either stiffness or loading changes. Its positive self-flexibility for a load and a sensor pointing the same way is a useful sign check.

Exchange the load and the gauge

The unit-load formula supplies a test that can be made on the same beam without knowing its exact stiffness distribution. Put the same 100 N force at the tip and move the displacement sensor to \(x=0.80\) m. The new integral has exactly the previous pair of moment fields with their roles exchanged. Since their product is unchanged, the new reading is also 3.56 mm downwards. This Maxwell–Betti reciprocity applies to these work-conjugate forces and displacements in a linear elastic structure with unchanged restraints. A slipping clamp, a support that lifts off or motion large enough to change the load geometry can cause discrepancies between reciprocal tests. Swapping load and sensor positions is therefore also a practical check of the assumed support behaviour.

Quantify the deformation omitted by a bending-only model

The straight-normal assumption of Euler–Bernoulli theory omits transverse shear distortion. For a slender beam this is often small, but for a deep beam or a material with a small effective shear modulus it can dominate a displacement measurement. In a simple Timoshenko description let \(\kappa_s GA\) be the effective shear stiffness. The dimensionless shear correction factor \(\kappa_s\) accounts for the nonuniform cross-sectional shear field when a single transverse shear resultant \(V\) is used. For the illustration use \(\kappa_s=5/6\) for a homogeneous rectangular section.

For an end-loaded cantilever the bending moment magnitude is \(P(L-x)\) and the shear force magnitude is \(P\) throughout the span. Add the elastic shear energy to the bending energy. Differentiating with respect to the end force yields the total deflection under that force:

\[U=\int_0^L\left[\frac{P^2(L-x)^2}{2EI}+\frac{P^2}{2\kappa_sGA}\right]dx,\]
\[\delta=\frac{PL^3}{3EI}+\frac{PL}{\kappa_sGA}.\]

The shear contribution grows linearly with span while the bending contribution grows cubically. Their ratio is \(3EI/(\kappa_s GA L^2)\). Writing \(I/A=h^2/12\) for a rectangle gives \([E/(4\kappa_sG)](h/L)^2\). This form identifies both the geometric slenderness and material stiffness ratio required for a sensible model choice. A fixed rule based on span-to-depth alone can be poor for a sandwich section with a compliant core.

From first principles

Derive the torque–twist relation from local shear deformation

Consider a straight circular shaft in small elastic torsion. In Saint-Venant torsion of a circular section, each cross-section can rotate as a rigid plane relative to the next. Let its rotation be \(\phi(x)\) and take two sections separated by \(dx\). At radius \(r\) their relative tangential displacement is \(r\,d\phi\). Dividing by the axial spacing gives engineering shear strain and, through the measured shear modulus \(G\), shear stress:

\[\gamma_{x\theta}=r\,d\phi/dx,\]
\[\tau_{x\theta}=Gr\,d\phi/dx .\]

Each patch of area \(dA\) carries tangential force \(\tau\,dA\) with moment arm \(r\). Sum these moments over the section and integrate the resulting twist rate along the shaft:

\[T=\int_A r\tau\,dA=GJ\phi',\]
\[J=\int_A r^2\,dA,\]
\[\Delta\phi=\int \frac{T(x)}{G(x)J(x)}dx .\]

For a homogeneous solid circular shaft \(J=\pi R^4/2\), so doubling radius strongly increases torsional stiffness. Noncircular sections generally warp and their torsion constant need not equal this polar area moment. The circular-shaft kinematics should not be used unchanged for an open thin-walled section.

Derivation and applications

How two bonded shaft materials share one applied torque

Two shafts in series carry the same torque; a bonded core and sleeve instead undergo the same twist per length. Consider a circular core of radius \(a\) and shear modulus \(G_c\), bonded to a concentric sleeve with outer radius \(b\) and modulus \(G_s\). Use a long prismatic region away from load introduction, small elastic deformations, perfect bond and no interfacial slip. Let \(\phi(x)\) describe section rotation. Two cross-sections separated by \(dx\) acquire a relative tangential motion \(r\,d\phi\) at radius \(r\):

\[\gamma_{x\theta}=r\phi',\]
\[\tau_i(r)=G_i r\phi' .\]

Compatibility makes \(\phi'\) common. The shear stress may change at the material boundary because the moduli differ. The torque contribution from a thin annulus is its area \(2\pi r\,dr\) times shear stress times radius. Integrating in each material gives

\[T_c=G_cJ_c\phi',\]
\[T_s=G_sJ_s\phi',\]
\[J_c=\frac{\pi a^4}{2},\]
\[J_s=\frac{\pi(b^4-a^4)}{2}.\]

The applied torque \(T\) is their sum. Solving first for the common twist and then for the load split yields

\[\phi'=\frac{T}{G_cJ_c+G_sJ_s},\]
\[\frac{T_c}{T}=\frac{G_cJ_c}{G_cJ_c+G_sJ_s}.\]

The denominator has units of N m². Setting both shear moduli equal recovers the homogeneous solid shaft independently of the arbitrary division at \(a\). If the sleeve is unbonded and torque is applied only to the core, equal twist is no longer a valid compatibility condition.

How much torque does the sleeve actually carry?

A steel core and an aluminium sleeve extend the full length of a circular shaft. They are bonded along their cylindrical interface, and the end fittings transmit the resultant torque to the complete section. A student dividing the torque in proportion to cross-sectional area obtains a plausible answer that misses both lever arm and material stiffness. We can reconstruct the division from the displacement at the interface before writing any torque balance.

Let \(a\) denote the core radius and \(b\) the outside radius. A cross section rotating by angle \(\varphi(x)\) moves a point at radius \(r\) tangentially by \(r\varphi\). \[T=[G_cJ_c+G_sJ_s]\varphi',\]
\[J_c=\pi a^4/2,\]
\[J_s=\pi(b^4-a^4)/2.\]

The missing equation is twist compatibility: because the cells are bonded along the common web, their twist per unit length must agree. Assume a long, thin-walled prismatic box of one material carrying Saint-Venant torsion with negligible distortion. Use median-line areas and length-to-thickness ratios throughout.

Assign positive clockwise circulations \(q_1,q_2\) to both cells. They oppose each other on the common web, where physical flow becomes \(q_1-q_2\) measured in the first cell’s direction. Let \(S_1,S_2\) denote \(\int ds/t\) over the exterior walls of each cell and \(S_w\) that quantity along their common web. The torque and the two contour compatibility relations become

\[T=2A_1q_1+2A_2q_2,\]
\[2A_1G\theta'=(S_1+S_w)q_1-S_wq_2,\]
\[2A_2G\theta'=(S_2+S_w)q_2-S_wq_1 .\]

These equations express the same shear-strain compatibility that leads to \(J_t=4A_m^2/\oint ds/t\) for one cell. If both cells and exterior wall thicknesses are mirror images, symmetry requires equal circulations and zero common-web flow under pure torque. The common web may still carry transverse shear from a separate load case. A missing web flow under pure torque is therefore a property of that loading and symmetry.

Compute the stress in an internal dividing web

Take adjacent median rectangles 40 mm high, 60 and 30 mm wide, with every wall 2 mm thick. Then

\[A_1=2400,\ A_2=1200\,\mathrm{mm^2}.\]

Their three exterior walls give \(S_1=(60+40+60)/2=80\) and \(S_2=(30+40+30)/2=50\), while \(S_w=20\). Equal twist requires \((100q_1-20q_2)/2400=(70q_2-20q_1)/1200\), giving \(140q_1=160q_2\). Thus \(q_2=0.875q_1\), larger than one might expect from the smaller area alone.

Applying \(T=345\,\mathrm{N\,m}=345000\,\mathrm{N\,mm}\) gives \(345000=4800q_1+2400(0.875)q_1\), so \(q_1=50.0\) and \(q_2=43.75\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). The dividing web carries only 6.25 N/mm, or 3.13 MPa. The primary exterior walls carry 25.0 and 21.9 MPa. Using \(G=27000\,\mathrm{MPa}\), either contour gives \(\theta'=(100\times50-20\times43.75)/(2\times2400\times27000)=3.18\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/mm}\), about 1.82° per metre. Checking the other contour independently catches a reversed sign on the shared wall. If a weld along that wall fails, equal twist and the closed paths that led to these flows can no longer be assumed.

Individual cell torque contributions are informative when interpreting a section cut. The larger cell contributes \(2(2400)(50)=240\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), and the smaller contributes \(2(1200)(43.75)=105\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Their 345 N m sum is independent of the coordinate origin. These are bookkeeping circulations rather than two torques applied independently to separate tubes. Cutting through the common web changes the compatibility and available flow paths, so using the original partition after a cut is not justified. Surface strain measurements on the upper exterior walls and the shared web would distinguish the predicted 50, 43.75 and 6.25 N/mm flows more effectively than measuring a single outer-wall strain alone.

A comparison with a thin unpartitioned box is possible without changing outer dimensions. For the outer median rectangle 90 by 40 mm, \(A_m=3600\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) and \(\oint ds/t=2(90+40)/2=130\). Its single-cell torsion constant is

\[4A_m^2/130=398769\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

The partitioned solution implies

\[J_t=T/(G\theta')=345000/[27000(3.183\times10^{-5})]\simeq401500\,\mathrm{mm^4}.\]

Under this specific pure torque the additional web therefore raises torsional stiffness by less than one percent. It experiences little shear because the neighbouring circulations are similar. Its contribution to transverse load sharing, panel buckling resistance or local attachment stiffness can nevertheless be important and needs separate calculations.

Move the web to the centre, producing identical cells of median width 45 mm. Pure torque then gives equal circulations, zero shear flow on the common web and precisely the single outer-cell twist in this idealisation. This limiting case is a demanding model check. A numerical section calculation that predicts double the torsional stiffness simply because there are now two cells has likely treated the common web as two independent exterior walls.

Creep, relaxation and what a holding test measures

Engineering mechanics

Two polymer specimens can show the same initial force and quite different readings ten minutes later. Change the quantity held fixed and the same material reveals a different time scale.

Connect a spring to a dashpot and keep track of the variables

An ideal spring obeys \(\sigma=E\epsilon_s\). A linear dashpot obeys \(\sigma=\eta\dot\epsilon_d\), where the viscosity \(\eta\) has units Pa s. These elements describe recoverable storage and dissipative flow. Start with a Maxwell element by putting them in series. Both carry the same stress and their strains add, so differentiating total strain gives

\[\dot\epsilon=\frac{\dot\sigma}{E_1}+\frac{\sigma}{\eta}.\]

A fixed strain sets the left side to zero. Stored spring deformation slowly transfers to the dashpot while total extension stays fixed, producing a decaying stress. Under a constant nonzero stress the dashpot instead accumulates strain indefinitely. This second prediction makes the fluid-like long-time behaviour of the Maxwell element easy to test.

Add an equilibrium load path

Place a spring of modulus \(E_0\) parallel to the Maxwell element and call the Maxwell stress \(s\). Both branches now have the same total strain. The measured stress is \(E_0\epsilon+s\). Replace \(s\) by \(\sigma-E_0\epsilon\) in the series equation and gather the derivatives:

\[\dot\sigma+\frac{E_1}{\eta}\sigma=(E_0+E_1)\dot\epsilon+\frac{E_0E_1}{\eta}\epsilon .\]

In a sudden small strain step of magnitude \(\epsilon_*\), a finite dashpot cannot change strain instantaneously, so both springs respond at first. Holding the extension then allows the Maxwell stress to relax to zero. Conversely, a sudden stress step loads both springs at first but ends with the equilibrium spring carrying the complete applied stress. Solving the constant-coefficient equations with these initial and final conditions gives

\[\sigma(t)=\epsilon_*[E_0+E_1 e^{-t/\tau_r}],\]
\[\tau_r=\eta/E_1 ,\]
\[\epsilon(t)=\frac{\sigma_*}{E_0}+\left[\frac{\sigma_*}{E_0+E_1}-\frac{\sigma_*}{E_0}\right]e^{-t/\tau_c},\]
\[\tau_c=\frac{\eta(E_0+E_1)}{E_0E_1}.\]

The two exponential times differ because under load control the equilibrium spring gradually takes a different share of the force. At \(t=0\) the effective modulus is \(E_0+E_1\) in either test.

Worked example · identify parameters from one record

Impose 1% strain. Stress starts at 0.90 MPa and eventually settles at 0.30 MPa. The final value gives \(E_0=30\) MPa and the drop gives \(E_1=60\) MPa. Suppose the excess above the plateau falls to \(1/e\) of its initial value after 40 s. The viscosity is then \(60(40)=2400\) MPa s. After 80 s the stress prediction is

\[0.30+0.60e^{-2}=0.3812\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

Subtracting the plateau before fitting a logarithm is essential: the logarithm of the total stress has a changing slope.

Worked example · make a prediction the fit did not use

In an independent test impose a stress of 0.45 MPa. Strain jumps to \(0.45/90=0.005\) and approaches \(0.45/30=0.015\). The predicted creep time is

\[40(90/30)=120\,\mathrm{s}.\]

After one minute the prediction is

\[0.015-0.010e^{-0.5}=0.00893.\]

A model that used the 40 s relaxation time for creep would yield 0.01277, readily distinguishable if the load has settled. Both experiments should use the same temperature because molecular mobility, and therefore the viscosity, can be highly temperature dependent.

Double viscosity while leaving both spring moduli fixed. Predict which curve reaches its original one-minute value after two minutes. Next change the parallel spring alone and use the normalized curves to distinguish the two characteristic times.

A moving-coil galvanometer as an electrical and mechanical system

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A scale division records an angle, yet the quantity marked on the dial is electric current. A spring, a magnetic field and the resistance of the external measuring circuit connect the two. Derive the calibration, predict how a pointer approaches its reading, and find which experiment measures the charge of a short pulse.

Convert the force on two straight sides into a torque

A light rectangular coil has \(N\) turns, active length \(l\), width \(w\), and area per turn \(A=lw\). It pivots about an axis parallel to its long sides. Shaped pole pieces confine an approximately radial magnetic field of magnitude \(B\) to an air gap. Over the working angular range the magnetic field is perpendicular to the active wire and lies approximately in the plane of the coil. An elementary length carrying conventional current \(i\) experiences \(d\mathbf F=i\,d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B\). The two long sides carry current in opposite directions and experience forces of equal magnitude \(N ilB\). Acting with lever arms \(w/2\), the forces make a couple whose torque is \(NiAB\). Their net force is approximately zero. The short ends supply negligible torque about the pivot under the ideal geometry.

Write \(K=NAB\), and choose the positive sense of current to give positive torque. Its units are \({\rm N\,m/A}\). In a uniform Cartesian field an ordinary rigid loop instead gives torque \(NiAB\sin\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the angle between its normal and the field. The radial gap maintains the factor equivalent to \(\sin\alpha\) close to unity as the pointer turns. This is a design choice that produces a nearly linear scale. If a measured calibration curve flattens at a large angle, field nonuniformity and a nonlinear torsion spring are candidate causes that can be tested separately.

For a soft torsion spring with stiffness \(\kappa\), the return torque is \(-\kappa\theta\) at small deflections from the zero-current setting. At static equilibrium \(\theta=Ki/\kappa\). With \(N=200\), \(A=1.00\,{\rm cm^2}\), \(B=0.250\,{\rm T}\) and \(\kappa=8.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm N\,m/rad}\), the torque constant is \(0.0050\,{\rm N\,m/A}\) and the current sensitivity is \(6.25\,{\rm rad/A}\). A \(5.0\,{\rm mA}\) current turns the pointer through \(0.03125\,{\rm rad}\), about \(1.79^\circ\). A mirror mounted on the same axle deflects a reflected laser through twice this angle. At a screen distance of \(0.50\,{\rm m}\) the spot shifts \(0.50\tan(2\theta)=31.3\,{\rm mm}\), making the small mechanical rotation easy to resolve.

Recover the reciprocal voltage from work

Now rotate the coil through a small positive angle while it carries positive current. The electromagnetic torque does mechanical work \(Ki\,d\theta\). The voltage associated with the motion must therefore draw electrical work \(i e_{\rm back}\,dt\) of equal magnitude when the motor torque assists that motion. This gives the back-emf relation \(e_{\rm back}=K\dot\theta\), measured as a positive drop in the motor sign convention. Its coefficient has units \({\rm V\,s/rad}\), numerically the same SI coefficient as the torque constant. One can also derive it from the motional emf on the two active sides. Each moves at speed \((w/2)\dot\theta\), crosses field \(B\), and generates a voltage \(Bl(w/2)\dot\theta\) along its wire. The two contributions add over every turn.

Let \(J\) denote the rotational inertia, \(b\) the mechanical viscous damping coefficient, \(L\) the coil inductance and \(R\) the sum of winding plus external series resistance seen by a voltage source \(v(t)\). The electromechanical equations are

\[J\ddot\theta+b\dot\theta+\kappa\theta=Ki,\]
\[L\dot i+Ri+K\dot\theta=v(t).\]

Multiplying the first equation by \(\dot\theta\) and the second by \(i\) eliminates the coupling power \(Ki\dot\theta\). The result says that source power \(vi\) equals the rate of increase of \(J\dot\theta^2/2+\kappa\theta^2/2+Li^2/2\), plus \(b\dot\theta^2+Ri^2\). This identity fixes the relative signs of the torque and back emf. If a sign mistake turns a passive closed coil into a self-accelerating pointer, the same mistake produces a residual coupling term in this power balance. Mechanical friction and winding heat represent distinct sinks even though both can damp the observed displacement.

Predict overshoot and find a critical external resistance

If the electrical relaxation time \(L/R\) is short compared with the oscillation time \(1/\sqrt{\kappa/J}\), set \(i\simeq(v-K\dot\theta)/R\). Substituting into the mechanical equation increases its effective damping to \(b_{\rm eff}=b+K^2/R\). Set \(v=0\) when discussing free relaxation with the electrical terminals connected through a resistor. A moving shorted coil generates a current that opposes its motion. Opening the circuit instead suppresses that current and leaves the mechanical damping. An ideal current source holding specified current through all pointer motions also changes the back-emf response because its output voltage adjusts to enforce that current.

The undamped angular frequency is \(\omega_0=\sqrt{\kappa/J}\) and the damping ratio is

\[\zeta=b_{\rm eff}/(2\sqrt{\kappa J}).\]

Take \(J=2.0\times10^{-6}\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(b=1.6\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), and the preceding \(K,\kappa\). Then \(\omega_0=20.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(2\sqrt{\kappa J}=8.0\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), and an open circuit gives \(\zeta=0.20\). With \(R=1.00\,\Omega\) the electric contribution to damping is \(2.50\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), giving \(\zeta=0.5125\). For a voltage step the ideal fractional overshoot in the reduced model is

\[\exp[-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}]=0.153.\]

The first peak occurs after \(\pi/(\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})=0.183\,{\rm s}\).

Critical damping occurs when \(b+K^2/R=2\sqrt{\kappa J}\), which requires a total resistance of about \(0.391\,\Omega\). A winding resistance of \(0.300\,\Omega\) therefore leaves \(0.091\,\Omega\) for external source and leads. If the winding alone exceeds the target resistance, a simple passive external series resistor cannot reach this damping in the reduced model. A finite \(L\) must also be checked: with \(L=5.0\,{\rm mH}\) and \(R=1.0\,\Omega\), \(\omega_0L/R=0.10\), so the approximation provides a useful starting estimate. Reducing \(R\) indefinitely eventually invalidates the elimination of current. The full pair of equations then predicts the damping and frequency dependence.

Distinguish a charge impulse from a slow current reading

Suppose a brief imposed current pulse ends long before the pointer has turned appreciably. Integrating the torque equation over that interval gives

\[J\Delta\dot\theta\simeq K\int i\,dt=Kq.\]

The initial signal is now angular velocity proportional to total transmitted charge. For weak subsequent damping, the first excursion is approximately \(Kq/(J\omega_0)\). A dose of \(20\,\mu{\rm C}\) gives \(2.50\,{\rm mrad}\) with the instrument above, corresponding to about \(2.50\,{\rm mm}\) on the mirror scale. Two current pulses of identical charge but different shapes give nearly the same reading if both are short on the mechanical time scale. Their peak currents can be entirely different.

For appreciable damping, use the impulse solution \(\theta(t)=Kq\,e^{-\zeta\omega_0t}\sin(\omega_dt)/(J\omega_d)\), with \(\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) for an underdamped pointer. Its maximum obeys \(\tan(\omega_dt_p)=\omega_d/(\zeta\omega_0)\) in the first quarter cycle. Substitution corrects the ballistic calibration instead of treating the damped peak as an undamped amplitude. The state of the external circuit after the pulse matters because it determines \(\zeta\) during the throw. A calibration pulse should leave the measuring circuit connected in the same way as the unknown pulse.

Measure the torque constant without assuming the magnetic field

The preceding design calculation multiplies turn count, active area and a nominal air-gap induction. An independent calibration can measure the same constant without knowing the field distribution along every wire. Move the pointer through a small controlled angle while measuring its open-circuit voltage with a high-impedance instrument. In the same working interval the voltage is \(K(\theta)\dot\theta\) up to a polarity choice. Integrating the voltage over the sweep gives \(\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}K(\theta)\,d\theta\). If \(K\) is constant this is simply \(K\Delta\theta\), independent of sweep speed. It is often easier to integrate a recorded voltage pulse accurately than to differentiate the angle trace to obtain instantaneous speed.

For a sweep through \(0.0800\,{\rm rad}\), suppose the measured voltage integral is \(0.401\,{\rm mV\,s}\). The generator constant is \(5.01\times10^{-3}\,{\rm V\,s/rad}\). Independently add a known small torque to the spring and measure its static angle. A torque \(32.0\,\mu{\rm N\,m}\) rotates the pointer by \(0.0400\,{\rm rad}\), giving \(\kappa=8.00\times10^{-4}\,{\rm N\,m/rad}\). Combining the two measurements predicts a current sensitivity of \(6.27\,{\rm rad/A}\). Splitting the sweep into smaller equal angular intervals tests whether the measured \(K\) depends on angle. Sweeping backwards should reverse the integrated voltage. A voltage offset instead contributes its offset times duration, so repeating at two sweep speeds helps separate it.

A dynamic cross-check uses free decay under two load conditions. Measure the exponential decay rate \(\alpha_{\rm open}\) with a high-impedance voltage probe and \(\alpha_R\) with a known total resistance in the closed circuit. In the short-electrical-time limit, subtraction of the two damping laws yields

\[K^2=2JR(\alpha_R-\alpha_{\rm open}).\]

With \(J=2.0\times10^{-6}\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(R=1.00\,\Omega\), and decay rates \(4.0\) and \(10.25\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), this gives

\[K=0.0050\,{\rm N\,m/A}.\]

The method needs two identifiable decay rates and independent inertia. If the loaded response is overdamped, the envelope of two oscillation peaks is no longer available; fit the full two-real-pole response to the measured displacement.

Keep the third dynamical variable in a frequency-response experiment

For a sinusoidal voltage at angular frequency \(\omega\) the exact linear transduction law follows by eliminating the complex current after transforming both differential equations. The mechanical impedance in angular-displacement form is \(\kappa-J\omega^2+i\omega b\); the winding impedance is \(R+i\omega L\). Using phasors with time dependence \(e^{i\omega t}\) gives

\[\frac{\Theta}{V}=\frac{K}{(R+i\omega L)(\kappa-J\omega^2+i\omega b)+i\omega K^2}.\]

At zero frequency the gain is \(K/(R\kappa)\) and the phase tends to zero, as the static calibration requires. At a sufficiently high frequency both the electrical and inertial impedances grow. For \(L>0\) the magnitude approaches \(K/(LJ\omega^3)\). The reduced model with the current eliminated algebraically falls only as \(1/\omega^2\). The change of the asymptotic slope provides an experimental way to distinguish finite inductance from an incorrectly adjusted viscous damping constant. Beyond the working band of a real instrument, elastic modes of the pointer or parasitic capacitance can enter before that asymptote is resolved.

At the nominal undamped frequency \(20\,{\rm rad/s}\), with \(R=1.0\,\Omega\) and \(L=5.0\,{\rm mH}\), the stiffness and inertia terms cancel. The denominator becomes \(-\omega^2 L b+i\omega(Rb+K^2)\), or approximately \(-0.000032+i\,0.000820\) in consistent SI units. Its magnitude gives about \(6.09\,{\rm rad/V}\). A sinusoid of \(1.0\,{\rm mV}\) peak produces about \(6.09\,{\rm mrad}\) peak, whereas the static response to \(1.0\,{\rm mV}\) is \(6.25\,{\rm mrad}\). The phases differ radically despite their similar amplitudes: near this frequency the pointer lags by about \(92.2^\circ\). A gain-only calibration at a single frequency would miss the phase lag.

Change the electrical load and compare two step models

In the live graph the source voltage is chosen anew at each resistance to give a final current of \(5.0\,{\rm mA}\) after the pointer settles. That convention keeps the final angle common to all curves and isolates the transient response of the load. The full model retains \(i,\theta,\dot\theta\); the second curve sets \(L\dot i=0\). Predict first which trace should start flatter. With finite inductance the current initially vanishes and increases at a finite rate. The initial torque and angular acceleration therefore vanish, while the reduced model applies a finite torque immediately. Increasing the inductance makes that distinction easier to see, especially when the electrical response time becomes comparable to the period of mechanical oscillation.

Eddy-current braking, magnetic penetration and the heat left behind

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A conducting loop moves above a row of magnetic poles. The drag first increases with speed, but a rapid crossing need not generate proportionally greater braking. Trace the work done against the Lorentz force into circuit energy, heat and a measurable temperature change. Then decide which features of a solid plate the single-loop model can capture.

Generate a voltage from a spatially periodic field

Let a rectangular loop of height \(h\) along \(y\) and width \(w\) along \(x\) translate to the right above a stationary magnetic pattern. At the loop let the normal induction be \(B_z(x)=B_0\cos(kx)\), with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). Its two active sides are centred symmetrically about the coordinate \(x_c\). Assume the field varies negligibly along \(y\) over this height. Integrating through the loop gives

\[\Phi=h\int_{x_c-w/2}^{x_c+w/2}B_0\cos(kx)\,dx=[2hB_0\sin(kw/2)/k]\cos(kx_c).\]

A uniform translation \(x_c=ut\) therefore produces an external emf with peak amplitude \(E_{\rm pk}=K u\), where \(K=2hB_0|\sin(kw/2)|\).

This spatial averaging explains an initially surprising design feature. A loop spanning precisely one full spatial period always links zero net flux from the sinusoidal field and therefore generates no emf in this ideal geometry. A loop of half a period has maximum coupling at a specified height. If \(w\) is much smaller than the period,

\[\sin(kw/2)\simeq kw/2,\]
\[K\simeq h w B_0 k.\]

This is exactly the small-loop result from using its area times the local gradient of induction. Reversing velocity reverses the emf at a fixed position. At a fixed positive speed the temporal frequency is \(f=u/\lambda\), so the same conductor experiences different electrical frequencies at different mechanical speeds.

Include finite inductance and derive the drag curve

Model the current path by a constant resistance \(R\) and self-inductance \(L\). Its steady periodic current solves \(L\dot i+Ri=\mathcal E(t)\). In phasor notation its magnitude is \(E_{\rm pk}/\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L)^2}\), lagging the emf by \(\arctan(\omega L/R)\), where \(\omega=ku\). The current develops field energy \(Li^2/2\) and dissipates \(Ri^2\). Multiplying the circuit equation by \(i\) and averaging over a period eliminates the derivative of stored energy. The average mechanical power delivered against the drag is therefore the mean Joule heating \(P=E_{\rm pk}^2R/[2(R^2+\omega^2L^2)]\). Dividing by the speed gives the drag magnitude

\[F(u)=\frac{K^2u}{2R[1+(u/u_c)^2]},\]
\[u_c=\frac{R}{kL}.\]

At low speed the drag is proportional to velocity. At the crossover \(u=u_c\) it reaches \(K^2u_c/(4R)\), and at much larger speeds it falls approximately as \(1/u\) within the assumed lumped model. The incoming mechanical power then tends to a constant rather than growing without bound. Why is there a maximum? Increasing speed raises the emf, but it also raises the reversal frequency. An inductive current increasingly lags the driving emf, and its component in phase with the emf does the net dissipative work. A measured turnover therefore tests both an electrical time scale and the spatial scale of the magnet pattern.

Use \(h=50\,{\rm mm}\), \(w=25\,{\rm mm}\), \(\lambda=0.100\,{\rm m}\), \(B_0=0.20\,{\rm T}\), \(R=0.050\,\Omega\) and \(L=0.150\,{\rm mH}\). Then \(K=0.01414\,{\rm T\,m}\), \(u_c=5.31\,{\rm m/s}\), and maximum mean drag is \(5.31\,{\rm mN}\). At \(u=2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) the field alternates at \(20\,{\rm Hz}\), giving an emf of \(28.3\,{\rm mV}\) peak. The current is \(0.529\,{\rm A}\) peak and its phase lag \(20.7^\circ\). The resistor receives approximately \(7.00\,{\rm mW}\), and the motor maintaining translation supplies a mean force of \(3.50\,{\rm mN}\). Using peak current in \(I^2R\) without its factor one half would overstate the mechanical force by a factor two.

Determine whether the current penetrates a real plate

A continuous metal plate contains many possible current paths, unlike a preformed loop. Their resistances, inductances and mutual couplings depend on the plate boundary, magnet geometry and frequency. For a good homogeneous conductor the magnetic diffusion equation gives a skin depth \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\) under sinusoidal excitation. At a depth \(\delta\) a field entering a locally planar half-space has lost a factor \(e\) in amplitude and acquired a phase delay of one radian. The frequency to use is the frequency experienced by the moving material; the fact that the magnets are stationary in the laboratory does not make this frequency zero.

For copper with \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\,{\rm S/m}\) and permeability close to \(\mu_0\), the skin depth is about \(14.8\,{\rm mm}\) at \(20\,{\rm Hz}\) and \(1.48\,{\rm mm}\) at \(2.0\,{\rm kHz}\). A \(2.0\,{\rm mm}\) plate therefore sees little through-thickness attenuation in the first experiment but may need a diffusion calculation in the second. The plate's lateral current pattern can still matter when the through-thickness current density is nearly uniform. Slots interrupt wide current loops and usually reduce drag even when the mass and the bulk conductivity change little. Comparing a slotted and an unslotted plate directly tests the importance of current closure.

If a plate experiences sharply separated poles, its applied field in the moving frame contains a fundamental and higher harmonics. Harmonic \(n\) has a skin depth smaller by \(\sqrt n\), so checking only the fundamental can miss surface concentration associated with narrow features. A Hall scan along the track supplies the actual spatial field spectrum. Multiplying each spatial wavenumber by the speed converts it into a temporal angular frequency for a constant-speed pass. Once the speed changes significantly over one pole spacing, a time-domain calculation using the measured position trace is preferable to this fixed-frequency conversion.

Close a mechanical and thermal energy budget

Take a rotating brake as a second experiment. A flywheel of known inertia \(J_{\rm rot}=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) slows from \(120\) to \(80\,{\rm rad/s}\) while no drive supplies power. The released rotational kinetic energy is

\[J_{\rm rot}(120^2-80^2)/2=80.0\,{\rm J}.\]

A separate run with the magnet removed measures bearing and air losses. Suppose the corresponding speed-dependent losses integrated over the magnetic braking trajectory total \(8.0\,{\rm J}\). Then the electromagnetic channel receives \(72.0\,{\rm J}\). Using the reference run's total energy over an arbitrary equal time could give a different answer, since the two speed histories affect the parasitic losses.

A calorimeter enclosing the conducting rotor has effective heat capacity \(C=60\,{\rm J/K}\) and a thermal link of conductance \(G=0.12\,{\rm W/K}\) to an environment at constant temperature \(T_a\). Its balance is

\[C\,dT/dt=P_{\rm deposited}-G(T-T_a).\]

During a \(20\,{\rm s}\) run, suppose the time-averaged rise above ambient is \(0.50\,{\rm K}\). Then \(1.20\,{\rm J}\) flowed through the thermal link. If all electromagnetic heat entered the calorimeter, its final rise from an ambient start is

\[(72.0-1.20)/60=1.18\,{\rm K}.\]

Find the spatial features that dominate a measured braking signal

A single Fourier component of field already produces an informative turnover. In a tracked experiment the field often has a measurable third harmonic. Let \(B(x)=B_1\cos kx+B_3\cos(3kx+\varphi)\), with constant translation speed. Flux integration across the finite loop multiplies each harmonic by its own width factor. The corresponding emf peak is \(2h|B_n|u|\sin(nkw/2)|\), and its temporal angular frequency is \(nku\). The linear \(RL\) equation treats the two drives independently. Orthogonality over a complete fundamental period makes the mean of their product vanish, so total mean heat can be found by adding the individual resistive powers. Within one period the interference terms do affect instantaneous torque and power.

Suppose \(B_3/B_1=0.20\) and keep the preceding \(w=\lambda/4\). Here \(\sin(kw/2)\) and \(\sin(3kw/2)\) both have magnitude \(1/\sqrt2\). At low speed the third harmonic therefore adds about four percent to mean drag. At \(u=u_c\), however, its angular frequency sees inductive impedance \(3R\) while the fundamental sees \(R\). The third-harmonic share relative to the fundamental becomes \(0.20^2(1+1)/(1+9)=0.008\), only \(0.8\%\). A peak-to-peak field reading can change visibly without a comparable change in mean drag. Conversely, if the loop width happens to null one spatial harmonic, another smaller harmonic can supply most of the signal. A field map and the loop geometry together determine what source drives the circuit.

In a solid plate, predicting force from field and speed needs a field solution that enforces current closure. In the slowly moving conductor, \(\mathbf J=\sigma[-\nabla\phi+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B]\) when the imposed magnetic pattern is effectively time independent in the laboratory and the induced magnetic field can be neglected. Charge conservation then gives

\[\nabla\cdot[\sigma(-\nabla\phi+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)]=0.\]

On an electrically insulated plate edge, enforce \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J=0\). Solving this potential problem accounts for charge accumulation that redirects currents at a boundary. Simply setting \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf u\times\mathbf B\) can predict current flowing straight out of an insulating edge. The force follows from integrating \(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B\) through the conducting volume, and Joule heat follows from \(\int J^2/\sigma\,dV\).

A useful check on such a field calculation is a finite plate translating wholly inside an exactly uniform static magnetic field. Charges can rearrange until a uniform electrostatic field cancels \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B\). An isolated plate in this steady limit then has zero transport current and zero drag. Motion alone does not guarantee braking. At an entry edge, the moving conductor spans a spatial change of induction, and one scalar potential generally cannot cancel the motional drive around every closed path. Slits, thickness variations, a conducting contact at a boundary, and the induced field at higher speeds each change this test problem in an identifiable way.

Coupled optical and mechanical resonances

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A vibrating mirror can imprint its position on an optical field, while the stored light pushes back on the mirror. An apparent pair of peaks can indicate two hybrid modes, interference at a readout port or two unrelated resonances. Starting from energy in a moving cavity, derive the coupled response and learn which measurements establish a coupling strength and its uncertainty.

From a moving boundary to a force per photon

Take a nearly empty Fabry–Pérot cavity of optical length \(L\) with one moving end. A longitudinal resonance has \(\omega_c=m\pi c/L\) for integer \(m\), neglecting frequency-dependent mirror phase. For a small extension \(x\), \(\delta\omega_c=(\partial\omega_c/\partial x)x\) with signed slope \(G_x=-\omega_c/L\). At fixed photon number \(n\) the optical energy is \(n\hbar\omega_c(x)\). Its derivative gives the radiation-pressure force on the coordinate. For this mirror \(F=-n\hbar G_x>0\) pushes outward. The derivative must be at fixed photon number. Differentiating a driven steady-state photon number as though it were a closed-system energy would incorrectly include work exchanged with the pump.

For a 10 mm cavity resonant near 1064 nm the frequency slope magnitude in ordinary frequency is about 28.2 MHz per nanometre. A static shift of 10 pm therefore changes the optical resonance by approximately 282 kHz. One photon exerts a mean force \(\hbar\omega_c/L\simeq1.87\times10^{-17}\) N. An equivalent classical derivation counts a momentum change twice per normal reflection for each relevant round trip. The two descriptions yield the same mean force. The mirror displacement is the properly normalised mode coordinate; in a flexural device the optically sampled position and the total physical mass are generally different from centre-of-mass displacement and mass.

Introduce a single mechanical eigenmode with effective mass \(m_{\rm eff}\), angular frequency \(\Omega_m\) and displacement scale \(x_{\rm zpf}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m_{\rm eff}\Omega_m)}\). The single-photon coupling magnitude is \(g_0=|G_x|x_{\rm zpf}\). This normalisation is convenient even when the observed vibrations are entirely classical. For \(m_{\rm eff}=10\) ng and \(\Omega_m/(2\pi)=1.0\) MHz, \(x_{\rm zpf}=9.16\times10^{-16}\) m and \(g_0/(2\pi)=25.8\) Hz for the stated optical geometry. The small displacement scale explains why a coherent pump is normally used to make the interaction measurable.

How a laser couples frequencies that are otherwise far apart

An optical carrier oscillates at hundreds of terahertz, whereas the mirror may vibrate at only megahertz. Direct comparison of these two bare frequencies would never suggest a resonant exchange. A strong coherent pump supplies a phase reference and converts a mechanical displacement into optical sidebands. Move into a frame rotating at the pump frequency and write the cavity amplitude as its large steady part plus a small perturbation. The leading frequency-shift interaction is proportional to photon number times displacement. Expanding the intensity around the large pump keeps terms linear in both the probe field and displacement. The resulting coupling is \(G=g_0\sqrt{n_c}\), where \(n_c\) is mean intracavity pump photon number.

Tune the pump about one mechanical frequency below the optical cavity resonance. In the resolved-sideband and weak-fluctuation limit a near-resonant process moves one excitation between the mechanical mode and an upper optical sideband. The rapidly rotating opposite-sideband terms average out. Now the two coupled frequencies are an optical sideband offset and the mechanical frequency, measured relative to a common rotating reference. The large carrier frequency has already been accounted for by the pump. The pump phase can be absorbed into the mode definitions, so \(G\) may be chosen positive for this isolated pair.

If instead the pump is placed near the upper sideband, the surviving interaction creates or annihilates excitations in pairs. The passive exchange equations below cease to apply and strong pumping can produce amplification or instability. A bare two-oscillator machine driven mechanically near a shared natural frequency often has analogous exchange equations without a carrier pump. Identical matrices can describe different energy supplies; identify the drive and rotating reference before assigning a literal photon-to-phonon energy conversion.

Derive complex poles and the effect of unequal damping

Let \(a,b\) be small energy-normalised optical and mechanical complex amplitudes. In a frame where the uncoupled optical sideband has zero frequency, let the mechanical detuning be \(\delta\). Let \(\kappa,\Gamma\) denote energy linewidths in angular frequency, so amplitude decay rates are half these values. With an optical probe \(s\) at offset \(\omega\), the linear system is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\kappa/2-i\omega&iG\\ iG&\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\delta)\end{pmatrix}\binom{a}{b}=\binom{s}{0}.\]

Zeros of the determinant give autonomous complex mode frequencies. In the lossless case the eigenvalues are \(\delta/2\pm\sqrt{G^2+(\delta/2)^2}\). Far off resonance each branch approaches a bare mode. At zero detuning the two eigenvectors have equal optical and mechanical weights and the splitting is \(2G\). The eigenvectors rotate continuously through the avoided crossing, an important discriminator from two uncoupled lines simply passing through one another.

At zero detuning with losses, completing the square in the determinant gives

\[\omega_\pm=-i(\kappa+\Gamma)/4\ \pm\sqrt{G^2-(\kappa-\Gamma)^2/16}.\]

The real parts separate once \(G>|\kappa-\Gamma|/4\). The shared imaginary part above that threshold corresponds to a hybrid energy linewidth \((\kappa+\Gamma)/2\). Resolving two intensity features on a given instrument is a stronger, readout-dependent condition. The dimensionless cooperativity \(C=4G^2/(\kappa\Gamma)\) is yet another measure, comparing interaction against the product of dissipation scales. A value greater than one does not automatically prove two resolved normal-mode poles in a device with very unequal linewidths.

Use frequency numbers divided by \(2\pi\) consistently from here for examples. With optical linewidth 240 kHz, mechanical linewidth 40 kHz and coupling 160 kHz, the real pole separation is

\[2\sqrt{160^2-50^2}=304\,\mathrm{kHz}.\]

Both hybrid energy linewidths are 140 kHz, and cooperativity is 10.7. If coupling falls to 45 kHz, cooperativity is still about 0.844 and no real zero-detuning pole separation appears. At 50 kHz the square root reaches zero. Near that threshold even a small linewidth or detuning calibration error has a large effect on a coupling estimate inferred from a single splitting.

Compute what an optical or mechanical receiver actually sees

Invert the matrix. With \(D=(\kappa/2-i\omega)[\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\delta)]+G^2\), the small amplitudes are \(a=s[\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\delta)]/D\) and \(b=-iGs/D\). The poles are shared. Their numerators are different, so the two channels can show different apparent maxima. A port in reflection also has a directly reflected field that interferes with the leaking intracavity amplitude. Its intensity can display a narrow dip or asymmetric feature without a matching resonance-height pattern inside the cavity. A legitimate forward model must describe the measured port.

Watch energy exchange in time and identify the linewidth convention

In an ideal equal-linewidth system starting with optical perturbation \(a(0)=a_0\), \(b(0)=0\) at zero detuning, change to symmetric and antisymmetric modes. Recombination gives

\[a(t)=a_0e^{-\kappa t/2}\cos Gt,\]
\[b(t)=-ia_0e^{-\kappa t/2}\sin Gt.\]

Their normalised total energy decays as \(e^{-\kappa t}\), while energy moves back and forth between optical sideband and vibration. The first full transfer occurs at \(t=\pi/(2G)\) if the rotating-wave approximation holds. For \(G/(2\pi)=100\) kHz, this is 2.50 microseconds. With a shared energy linewidth of 20 kHz in ordinary frequency the surviving energy fraction then is

\[e^{-2\pi(20\,{\rm kHz})(2.50\,\mu{\rm s})}=0.730.\]

A gated excitation shorter than the exchange period but long enough to avoid unwanted modes can prepare an approximately optical initial perturbation. Independent detection of an optical revival and a delayed mechanical response tests coherent exchange more strongly than the presence of two peaks alone. Infer a coupling with propagated measurement errors

When the two hybrid pole frequencies have been obtained from a justified fit and zero bare detuning is established, the coupling follows from the measured real separation \(S\):

\[G=\sqrt{S^2/4+(\kappa-\Gamma)^2/16}.\]

All quantities here can be expressed in the same ordinary-frequency units. If \(S=300\pm8\) kHz, \(\kappa=240\pm10\) kHz and \(\Gamma=40\pm4\) kHz, the estimate is 158.1 kHz. Independent small errors give

\[\partial G/\partial S=S/(4G)=0.474,\]
\[\partial G/\partial\kappa=-\partial G/\partial\Gamma=(\kappa-\Gamma)/(16G)=0.0791.\]

Combining in quadrature gives about 3.9 kHz uncertainty. A common frequency calibration can correlate all three inputs, and a fitted detuning and splitting from one scan may also be correlated. Use their covariance rather than multiplying independent error bars blindly.

A second inference varies intracavity pump power. Since \(G^2=g_0^2 n_c\), plot the squared fitted coupling against independently calibrated \(n_c\). A nonzero intercept could indicate an additional direct coupling, baseline fitting bias or a mode incorrectly assigned at low power. The linewidths themselves can vary with pump power because of heating or backaction, so fit or measure them across the scan. Incident optical power is not identical to stored photon number; cavity coupling, detuning and loss determine their relation.

A measured frequency shift and linewidth change reveal optical feedback

The same instrument can be operated below the resolved splitting regime and read out a dispersive shift of the mechanical response. Algebraically eliminate the optical amplitude in the two-mode equations while driving the mechanical mode weakly. Its inverse susceptibility acquires a self-energy contribution proportional to the optical susceptibility. Let \(x\) be probe frequency measured from the bare optical sideband and use the same linewidth convention as above. In the mechanical denominator the additional term is

\[\Sigma(x)=\frac{G^2}{\kappa/2-ix}=\frac{G^2(\kappa/2+ix)}{(\kappa/2)^2+x^2}.\]

Its real part adds to the amplitude damping, while its imaginary part shifts the resonant condition. At very weak coupling one can evaluate it at the bare mechanical frequency \(x=\delta\), because variation across the narrow mechanical peak is small. Near an avoided crossing or when the mechanical peak samples a large change in optical susceptibility, solve the coupled poles directly; simply adding a linewidth and shift then neglects the very hybridisation being tested.

In ordinary-frequency units choose \(G=10\) kHz, optical linewidth 200 kHz, mechanical linewidth 2 kHz and detuning \(+100\) kHz. At that offset the optical amplitude half-width is 100 kHz, giving \(\operatorname{Re}\Sigma=0.50\) kHz and \(\operatorname{Im}\Sigma=0.50\) kHz. The full mechanical linewidth increases from 2.0 to approximately 3.0 kHz. Its centre moves upwards by about 0.50 kHz with the adopted matrix signs. Moving to minus 100 kHz reverses the shift and preserves the added damping in this symmetric model. At zero detuning the predicted shift vanishes but the added linewidth is largest.

A scan on both sides is more discriminating than measuring broadening alone. A background rise in mechanical temperature changes integrated thermal noise and may change intrinsic loss, but need not reproduce this odd shift and even broadening pattern. Measure intrinsic linewidth far from the optical response and subtract that baseline. An unknown constant frequency offset can be removed with the two symmetric detunings, but an optical power dependence of cavity frequency can move the actual symmetry centre. Monitor the optical resonance during the scan.

The effective susceptibility describes classical feedback through the delayed optical field. It does not by itself specify the final mechanical temperature. A bath occupation or noise spectrum is also required to convert a damping change into an energy distribution. A driven response peak that broadens and lowers in amplitude is evidence for a changed susceptibility; thermal cooling additionally requires a calibrated measurement of the fluctuating mechanical displacement and its optical transduction.

Estimate a cavity linewidth from a finite, biased record

Numerical field laboratory

Connect the time and frequency descriptions of one damped mode, then diagnose why a weak detector background systematically biases the inferred quality factor.

Know whether the detector reads field or energy

After a drive switches off, let a single cavity mode have complex analytic amplitude \(a(t)=a_0e^{-\kappa t/2}e^{-i\omega_0t}\) for \(t\ge0\). The energy is proportional to \(|a|^2\), giving \(U=U_0e^{-\kappa t}\). Its instantaneous decay rate \(-U'/U=\kappa\) implies

\[Q=\omega_0 U/P_\mathrm{loss}=\omega_0/\kappa.\]

A phase-sensitive receiver measuring field amplitude sees a time constant twice as long as the energy lifetime. Before fitting the data, ask how the measurement transducer converts the field into a reported number.

Fourier transforming the causal decaying amplitude gives an integral proportional to \(1/[\kappa/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)]\), up to the time convention. Its squared magnitude has half-power points at \(|\omega-\omega_0|=\kappa/2\). Therefore the full power linewidth in hertz is \(\Delta f=\kappa/(2\pi)\). A measurement of either the energy decay time \(\tau_U=1/\kappa\) or the spectral width can test the other without having to calibrate the absolute detector gain.

At \(f_0=2.0\) GHz and \(\tau_U=0.50\ \mu\mathrm s\), we obtain \(\kappa=2.0\times10^6\) s⁻¹, \(\Delta f\simeq318.3\) kHz and \(Q\simeq6283\). A field receiver would report an amplitude time constant of 1.0 μs. Substituting that time directly into \(Q=2\pi f_0\tau_U\) would double the apparent quality.

Derive the two-point fit with a detector offset

Let a power detector report \(S(t)=A e^{-\kappa t}+B\), with a constant background \(B>0\). In the absence of background two samples separated by \(\Delta t\) give

\[\widehat\kappa=\log[S(t)/S(t+\Delta t)]/\Delta t.\]

With background, the logarithmic derivative is

\[-\frac{d}{dt}\log S=\kappa\frac{Ae^{-\kappa t}}{Ae^{-\kappa t}+B}.\]

It starts below the true rate and tends to zero in the tail. A straight line fitted to late log data can therefore look impressively precise while overestimating the lifetime. Increasing the acquisition duration can worsen this bias if the entire record is forced into a straight-line model.

In the experiment set \(b=B/A=0.04\). A two-point fit from one true lifetime to two has dimensionless rate \(\log[(e^{-1}+0.04)/(e^{-2}+0.04)]\), approximately 0.844. Its apparent lifetime is about 18.5% high. The source, walls and coupling have not changed. Only the interpretation of detector output has changed. Move the start of the fit towards three lifetimes and inspect the increasing gap from the ideal log line.

Multiplying the measured field by a rectangular window of duration \(T\) convolves its spectrum with a sinc-like response of width of order \(1/T\). If two nearby modes reach the same detector, their fields add before squaring. The result contains an interference term oscillating at their frequency difference on top of a decaying envelope. A scalar positive background cannot explain that alternating residual. An honest linewidth report specifies the measured quantity, acquisition window, background treatment, coupling state, reference frequency and uncertainty. A fit to stored energy measures total loss. Inferring internal absorption alone additionally requires a known port coupling or a measurement that changes it separately, as in the course's cavity project.

Measure gravity with a pendulum of finite amplitude

Mechanics in the laboratory

A metre-long pendulum is an appealing clock because its small-angle period depends only on length and gravity. A precise clock can nevertheless give a precisely wrong value of gravity. Derive the full period, trace the systematic error to an identifiable approximation and design a measurement that distinguishes a length error from a release-angle error.

Define the measured length and time

Use a point bob on a massless, inextensible string attached to a fixed pivot. Let \(L\) be the distance from pivot to the bob's centre of mass, and \(\theta\) the signed departure from the downward vertical. For a rigid extended bob, rotational inertia about its own centre adds to \(mL^2\); a length measured to the bottom of the bob is neither of these dynamical lengths. Measure the elapsed time between successive crossings of a photogate in the same direction. Crossings in opposite directions give half of the period in a symmetric oscillation. A stationary turning point is a poor timing marker because the position error there produces a large time error.

At rest at the release angle \(\theta_0\), the energy is \(mgL(1-\cos\theta_0)\). At a later angle the speed is \(L|\dot\theta|\). Equating kinetic plus potential energy to the release value gives

\[\dot\theta^2=(2g/L)(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0).\]

Only a quarter cycle is needed. Inverting the angular speed and integrating from zero to \(\theta_0\) produces a square-root singularity at release. The period is finite since integrating the inverse square root over a small interval gives a square root.

Resolve the turning point before approximating

Write \(k=\sin(\theta_0/2)\) and substitute \(\sin(\theta/2)=k\sin u\). The vanishing square root cancels a factor \(\cos u\) from the differential. One obtains the complete elliptic integral of the first kind with modulus \(k\).

\[T=4\sqrt{\frac Lg}\,K(k),\]
\[K(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}}.\]

Expanding its integrand yields

\[K(k)=(\pi/2)(1+k^2/4+9k^4/64+\cdots).\]

Since \(k\simeq\theta_0/2\) at small amplitude, the first fractional period correction is \(\theta_0^2/16\) with angle in radians. It is positive. At larger amplitude the tangential component of gravity rises more slowly with angle than the linear restoring law predicts. The pendulum spends longer near the turns. Passing degrees directly into the quadratic correction would give nonsense even if the timer and recorded angle were perfect.

Infer the bias in gravity

Suppose the true acceleration is \(9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the length is 1.000 m and the release angle is 30 degrees. The small-angle period is about 2.0061 s and the finite-amplitude period about 2.0410 s. Interpreting the longer measured period by the linear formula would give about \(9.48\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The fractional gravity error is about twice the fractional period error with opposite sign because gravity is inferred from \(T^{-2}\). Timing a hundred periods reduces random timing error but leaves this systematic shift.

The numerical curve evaluates the complete elliptic integral. It uses the arithmetic–geometric mean identity \(K(k)=\pi/[2\,\mathrm{AGM}(1,\sqrt{1-k^2})]\), so no sampled point sits on the turning point. The single-correction curve truncates the amplitude series after its first nonconstant term. Vary angle and length, comparing both the predicted period and the result a linear analysis would infer. Which change rescales both period curves together? Which changes their relative discrepancy?

Design a two-release check

For the same pendulum the ratio \(T(\theta_a)/T(\theta_b)=K(k_a)/K(k_b)\) cancels the unknown length and local gravity. Measure at two amplitudes before deciding whether to recalibrate the length. If the ratio agrees but both absolute times differ from prediction, check the effective length, clock rate and model of the bob. If the ratio changes during a decaying swing, record initial and final amplitudes for the timed sequence. The period associated with a long sequence is an average over its slowly changing amplitude. Substituting the initial angle throughout would overcorrect the measurement.

Measure inertia and gravity with two suspension axes

Mechanics in the laboratory

A rigid pendulum can oscillate from either of two knife edges. If the periods agree at distinct distances from the centre of mass, the spacing of the edges determines gravity. Derive the cancellation, identify a second way equal periods can arise and find why a poorly chosen geometry magnifies timing errors.

Torque about an offset pivot

Take a rigid body of mass \(m\) with parallel suspension axes perpendicular to its plane of oscillation. Its moment of inertia about the centre of mass is \(I_C=m\rho^2\); \(\rho\) is the radius of gyration. For a pivot a distance \(a>0\) from the centre, the parallel-axis theorem gives

\[I_a=m(\rho^2+a^2).\]

Gravity exerts torque \(-mga\sin\theta\). In the small-angle limit,

\[T_a^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{g}\frac{\rho^2+a^2}{a},\]
\[L_a=a+\frac{\rho^2}{a}.\]

Here \(L_a\) is the length of a point pendulum with the same period. It can lie beyond the physical centre of mass. A tap applied at this distance from a pivot gives the centre of percussion when the ideal instantaneous pivot reaction is to vanish. The same combination of mass distribution and geometry appears in an impulsive and in an oscillatory measurement because both depend on angular momentum about that pivot.

Match the periods and keep both algebraic branches

Put the second knife edge on the opposite side of the centre at distance \(b\). Equal periods require

\[a+\rho^2/a=b+\rho^2/b.\]

Rearrange before dividing by \(a-b\):

\[(a-b)\left(1-\frac{\rho^2}{ab}\right)=0.\]

Two equal distances always give equal periods. That branch provides no information about \(\rho\). If the distances are distinct, equality implies

\[\rho^2=ab.\]

Both equivalent lengths then equal \(a+b\), the directly measurable edge separation. For \(a=0.200\,\mathrm m,\ b=0.450\,\mathrm m,\ \rho=0.300\,\mathrm m\), the equivalent length is 0.650 m and both periods at standard laboratory gravity are about 1.62 s. A small value of \(a-b\) is an experimental warning sign, even if the observed equality is exceptionally clean.

Background · Can unequal release amplitudes imitate a mismatch between pivots?

A rigid pendulum about either knife edge obeys the same sine nonlinearity as a simple pendulum. To leading order in its release angle \(\theta\) in radians, the measured period exceeds the small-amplitude period by the fraction \(\theta^2/16\). At matched small-amplitude periods, measurements with amplitudes \(\theta_a,\theta_b\) therefore give

\[\frac{T_a-T_b}{T_0}\simeq\frac{\theta_a^2-\theta_b^2}{16}.\]

Releasing at 6° about the first edge and 2° about the other creates a fractional mismatch of about \(6.1\times10^{-4}\). For a two-second period this is 1.2 ms. Record the two amplitudes along with the timing data and reduce both periods to the same limit before moving the trim mass. Otherwise the trim can compensate a preparation error.

Extract gravity when the two measured periods differ

Multiply the first period equation by \(a\) and the second by \(b\). Subtract to eliminate \(\rho^2\). The exact small-angle result for unequal distances is

\[g=4\pi^2\,\frac{a^2-b^2}{aT_a^2-bT_b^2}.\]

At matched periods this reduces to the simple expression in terms of the edge spacing. The general formula involves a difference in its denominator. If \(a\) and \(b\) nearly coincide, the denominator is small and two independent timing errors subtract with poor relative precision. This is a property of the inverse measurement, even if computing each individual period is perfectly well conditioned.

Once \(g\) is known, use \(\rho^2=gaT_a^2/(4\pi^2)-a^2\) to recover the body inertia per unit mass. A negative value may indicate a wrong distance measured to the centre or a timing observable that is actually a half period. Verify the result using the second pivot and recover a separately predicted period after shifting a suspension point. A single successful cancellation is weaker than reproducing an additional measurement.
