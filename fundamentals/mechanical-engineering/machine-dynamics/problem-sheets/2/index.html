Frequency response and modes

Use complex dynamic stiffness and modal symmetry to predict what a force and a sensor can observe.

1. Inspect a forced oscillator

A 2.0 kg mass on a 200 N/m spring and 4.0 N·s/m damper is driven at 10 rad/s by a 1.0 N peak sinusoidal force. (a) Find the displacement amplitude and phase. (b) Find average power. (c) Repeat the amplitude calculation at 5 rad/s with the same force.

Solve the phasor balance

The natural angular frequency is 10 rad/s. At this speed the real dynamic stiffness vanishes, giving \(|X|=1/(4\cdot10)=0.0250\) m and 90° displacement lag. The velocity peak is 0.250 m/s and average input power is

\[1(0.25)/2=0.125\,\mathrm{W}.\]

At 5 rad/s the dynamic stiffness is \(150+20i\) N/m, hence

\[|X|=1/\sqrt{150^2+20^2}=6.61\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Proximity to resonance has a larger effect than the static-force displacement alone suggests.

2. Invert measured modal frequencies

Two identical 4.0 kg masses have equal ground springs and a shared coupling spring. Their measured angular frequencies are 10 rad/s in phase and 16 rad/s in opposition. (a) Infer both stiffnesses. (b) Apply displacements +3 mm and −1 mm. Decompose this state into the two modes. (c) Predict the average displacement over time after release from rest.

Separate symmetric and antisymmetric coordinates

The first mode gives

\[k=m\omega_s^2=400\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

The other gives

\[k_c=(4(16)^2-400)/2=312\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

The initial mean is 1 mm and the initial half-difference is 2 mm, corresponding to \(1(1,1)+2(1,-1)\) mm. With zero initial velocities both modes start as cosines. Averaging the two signals removes the antisymmetric term, leaving \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\cos(10t)\). A single average sensor would miss a mode that is actually populated.

3. Choose the force pattern

Use the preceding system. Two actuators apply harmonic force amplitudes \(F\) and \(-F\) simultaneously. (a) Find the drive of the mean coordinate and half-difference coordinate. (b) Which frequency is resonant? (c) If one actuator fails, which resonances can now appear?

Project the load into each mode

Adding the two physical equations and dividing by two gives forcing \((F_1+F_2)/2\) for the mean, which is zero. Subtracting gives \((F_1-F_2)/2=F\) for the half-difference. Only the antisymmetric mode is driven, at 16 rad/s. With one actuator operating, the force pattern decomposes as \((F,0)=(F/2)(1,1)+(F/2)(1,-1)\), so both 10 and 16 rad/s can produce resonant responses. Real damping limits amplitudes in a steady sweep.

Estimate how a frame mode distributes its kinetic energy

For the highest tabulated mode, doubling tip translation mass lowers its frequency from 44.645 to 42.379 Hz. Doubling its rotary inertia instead lowers it to 34.791 Hz. Freeze the baseline mode shape for an estimate. Derive the fraction of its original modal mass associated with each changed contribution. Compare the estimates and state what the approximation omits.

Work through the solution

The Rayleigh quotient is \(\omega^2=K_{\rm mode}/M_{\rm mode}\). For an unchanged shape, doubling one contribution \(M_i\) replaces the denominator with \(M_{\rm mode}+M_i\). Thus

\[M_i/M_{\rm mode}=(f_0/f_i)^2-1.\]

Translation gives 0.110 and rotation 0.647. The much stronger rotary sensitivity is observable from the computed frequencies even if the frozen-shape estimates are imperfect. A finite change in the mass matrix can rotate the eigenvectors, so these fractions are estimates rather than an exact decomposition of modal kinetic energy.
