Chapter 1 · Coordinates, energy and equivalent inertia

Reduce a constrained drive to one coordinate and use a power balance to check its equation of motion.

Follow the motion through a mechanism

A motor accelerating a load feels inertia from components that turn at different speeds. Simply adding their physical moments of inertia would treat all of those speeds as equal. Instead, choose one independent coordinate and express every kinetic energy in terms of it. No-slip belts, rigid gears and inextensible cables give useful constraints when their compliance and backlash are negligible over the motion of interest.

Consider a drum of radius \(R\) winding a cable carrying a vertically guided mass \(m\). Choose the drum angle \(\theta\) positive when the mass rises. If the effective winding radius is constant, displacement, velocity and acceleration satisfy \(x=R\theta\), \(\dot x=R\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot x=R\ddot\theta\). These equalities are kinematic. A torque relation requires force balance or work in addition. When the cable builds successive layers on a spool, the effective radius varies with angle and the constant-ratio assumption must be revisited.

Reflect mass and forces to a common shaft

Let \(J_d\) be the drum inertia. Its rotational kinetic energy and the load’s translational kinetic energy sum to \(K=(J_d+mR^2)\dot\theta^2/2\). The equivalent inertia seen at the drum is \(J_{\rm eq}=J_d+mR^2\). A small positive angle raises the mass through \(R\,d\theta\), changing its potential energy by \(mgR\,d\theta\). Thus gravity contributes a resisting torque \(mgR\). A positive applied torque \(T_d\) contributes power \(T_d\dot\theta\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12J_{\rm eq}\dot\theta^2+mgR\theta\right)=T_d\dot\theta,\qquad J_{\rm eq}\ddot\theta=T_d-mgR.\]

The second expression also holds at zero speed by force balance. It separates steady effort, needed to hold or raise the load against gravity, from the extra effort needed to accelerate the two inertias. With the motor disconnected, an ideal light cable does not make the mass accelerate quite at \(g\), since the drum must gain rotational kinetic energy too.

Calculate a motor torque

A drum with \(R=0.10\) m and \(J_d=0.15\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) lifts 25 kg. A reduction gear gives \(\dot\theta_m=4\dot\theta\). The rotor has inertia \(J_m=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Refer the train to the motor coordinate. The drum-plus-load inertia becomes \((0.15+25(0.10)^2)/4^2=0.025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), so the total at the motor is 0.045 kg·m². Gravity contributes \(25(9.81)(0.10)/4=6.13\) N·m at that shaft.

Demand upward acceleration 0.80 m/s². The drum must accelerate at 8.0 rad/s² and the motor at 32 rad/s². The motor supplies \(6.13+0.045(32)=7.57\) N·m. A separate check at load speed 1.0 m/s gives motor speed 40 rad/s. Its 303 W then divide into about 245 W raising the weight and 57.6 W increasing kinetic energy. Ideal gearing changes torque and angular speed inversely while preserving transmitted power.

Identify which approximation controls the answer

At constant lifting speed the inertial torque vanishes. At constant angular acceleration, doubling the transmission ratio decreases reflected load inertia by four but also doubles the motor acceleration for the same load motion. The rotor’s own inertia remains at the motor and eventually dominates. A gear ratio therefore cannot reduce every part of acceleration torque indefinitely. Bearings, cables and gears also dissipate energy or store it elastically. Include their effects when a measured transient shows a repeatable lag, oscillation or loss that an ideal rigid constraint cannot explain.

Is the largest reduction always preferable?

For a prescribed drum acceleration \(\alpha_d\) and reduction \(n=\omega_m/\omega_d\), collect gravity and load-side acceleration into the drum demand \(T_d=mgR+(J_d+mR^2)\alpha_d\). The motor torque becomes \(T_m=T_d/n+J_mn\alpha_d\). The first term falls as reduction rises, while rotor acceleration grows. Differentiating with respect to \(n\) gives an ideal minimum at \(n=\sqrt{T_d/(J_m\alpha_d)}\). For the stated lift \(T_d=27.73\) N m and \(\alpha_d=8\) rad/s², giving \(n=13.2\) approximately. This torque minimum assumes the motor can achieve the associated speed. At a drum speed of 10 rad/s it would turn at 132 rad/s, over three times its speed with the original reduction. Shaft-speed and voltage limits can therefore select a different practical ratio.

Derive speed ripple from a torque diagram

A crank-driven machine can require very different torque at different angles even when the average load is constant. A flywheel stores kinetic energy when driving torque exceeds resisting torque, then releases it when the load peaks. Refer all inertias and torques to the same shaft and suppose the equivalent inertia \(J\) is constant. The signed difference \(M(\theta)=T_{\rm drive}-T_{\rm load}\) gives \(J\dot\omega=M\). For continuous positive rotation write \(\dot\omega=\omega\,d\omega/d\theta\). Integration between two angular positions gives an exact work–energy relation.

\[\tfrac12 J[\omega_b^2-\omega_a^2]=\int_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}M(\theta)\,d\theta .\]

A periodic steady sequence requires zero net area under the torque-difference curve over a whole revolution. Its cumulative area nevertheless has a maximum and a minimum. Their difference \(\Delta E\) is the energy that must swing into and out of the rotating inertia. Define mean speed in the ripple specification as the arithmetic mean of the two extreme speeds \(\bar\omega=(\omega_{\max}+\omega_{\min})/2\) and fluctuation coefficient \(C_s=(\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})/\bar\omega\). Factoring the difference of squared speeds gives \(J=\Delta E/(\bar\omega^2 C_s)\). A time-average tachometer reading approximates this mean for small ripple, but the definitions are distinct for a strongly pulsating shaft.

Size a flywheel for a repeating work demand

A laboratory indexing machine runs at a nominal 300 rpm. A motor supplies approximately constant 30 N m. The resisting torque is 70 N m for the first quarter revolution and a lower constant value for the remaining three quarters. Equate total input and output work over each revolution: \(30(2\pi)=70(\pi/2)+T_l(3\pi/2)\), giving \(T_l=16.67\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). The first quarter produces a 40 N m deficit, so the cumulative energy falls by \(40(\pi/2)=62.83\,\mathrm J\). The remaining 13.33 N m surplus restores exactly that energy.

For a maximum peak-to-peak speed variation of 2% and \(\bar\omega=31.42\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), total inertia required is \(62.83/[31.42^2(0.02)]=3.18\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). If the machine already has 1.00 kg m² on this shaft, an extra 2.18 kg m² is required. A thin-ring estimate at mean radius 0.30 m gives mass \(2.18/0.30^2=24.2\,\mathrm{kg}\). A solid disc of the same radius needs twice the mass because its material lies closer to the shaft. At half speed the same torque-versus-angle work deficit requires four times the total inertia for the same fractional ripple. Startup torque or bearing load may therefore limit an inertia-based solution even when steady motor power suffices.

The torque balance also predicts where a shaft encoder should record the two speed extremes. The energy derivative with respect to angle equals the torque difference, so the maximum occurs just before the high resisting plateau begins and the minimum where that plateau ends. The maximum torque itself persists over a finite angular interval, while minimum speed occurs only at its end. This distinction matters when timing a torque pulse from a speed record: the sign of the speed derivative, rather than the absolute value of speed, identifies when the flywheel is discharging.

Chapter 2 · Free vibration, energy loss and ring-down data

Infer stiffness and damping from a released machine mount and check the implications of a viscous damping model.

Displace a machine from its static position

A pump on a spring mount settles under its weight. Pull it slightly farther down and release it. The weight still acts, but the extra motion is driven by the change in spring force. Define \(x\) as displacement from the loaded equilibrium, take constant local spring stiffness \(k\), and represent dissipation by a force opposing velocity of magnitude \(c|\dot x|\). Newton’s law becomes \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0\). This model describes one isolated mode over an amplitude range where inertia, stiffness and damping are approximately constant.

Multiply the equation by \(\dot x\). The mass term differentiates kinetic energy, and the spring term differentiates stored elastic energy. Consequently \(d(m\dot x^2/2+kx^2/2)/dt=-c\dot x^2\). Energy cannot grow for positive damping. Displacement can increase temporarily as kinetic energy converts back into spring energy, so the instantaneous displacement alone is not an energy meter.

Obtain the decay and oscillation rates

Trying \(x=e^{st}\) gives \(ms^2+cs+k=0\). Define \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n)\). The roots are \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). For \(0<\zeta<1\) their imaginary part gives the damped angular frequency \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). The real part gives a decaying exponential. A general underdamped trace is

\[x(t)=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\left[A\cos(\omega_dt)+B\sin(\omega_dt)\right].\]

At \(\zeta=1\) the repeated root gives \((A+Bt)e^{-\omega_nt}\). Above critical damping both roots are negative and real. A lack of visible oscillation therefore does not by itself determine the stiffness. For an underdamped trace, use two peaks of the same sign: their separation is \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_d\), and their amplitude ratio depends only on the envelope decay.

Infer parameters from four cycles

A 20.0 kg mounted component rings down from a positive peak of 8.0 mm to a positive peak of 2.0 mm four cycles later, after 1.60 s. The period is \(T_d=0.400\) s and \(\omega_d=15.71\) rad/s. The log decrement per cycle is \(\delta=\ln(8/2)/4=0.3466\). Since \(x_j/x_{j+1}=\exp(\zeta\omega_n T_d)\), the envelope decay rate is \(\alpha=\delta/T_d=0.8664\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Now \(\omega_n=\sqrt{\omega_d^2+\alpha^2}=15.73\) rad/s and \(\zeta=\alpha/\omega_n=0.0551\). The inferred stiffness is \(m\omega_n^2=4.95\) kN/m, while \(c=2m\alpha=34.7\) N·s/m. After eight cycles the prediction is 0.50 mm. Using peaks separated by several cycles reduces sensitivity to noise in any one amplitude or timestamp.

Challenge the damping assumption

Subtract the equilibrium sensor reading before taking amplitude ratios. A fixed offset changes the ratio of small late peaks more strongly than large early peaks. Plot \(\ln |x_j|\) against time for peaks whose signal clearly exceeds the noise floor. A straight trend supports the viscous envelope. Approximately constant loss of amplitude per cycle suggests a different mechanism such as dry friction. Two nearby natural frequencies may produce a beat envelope that grows and shrinks even though total energy decays.

A second experiment can separate stiffness from added inertia. If a small known mass is bolted to the same moving assembly and the support stiffness is unchanged, \(1/\omega_n^2\) should increase in proportion to effective mass. This check assumes that the added mass participates fully in the chosen coordinate. A flexible appendage that moves out of phase changes the system being identified.

Correct a biased displacement signal

Suppose a displacement sensor reads 0.50 mm high relative to the true equilibrium. The first and fifth positive peaks then appear as 8.50 and 2.50 mm. Their raw log ratio over four cycles gives \(0.25\ln(8.5/2.5)=0.306\), underestimating the true 0.347 decrement. The error grows as the decaying signal approaches the offset. One remedy is to measure equilibrium independently when excitation has stopped. Another is to use the difference between consecutive positive and negative extrema and compare such differences one whole period apart. The constant offset cancels while, for a single underdamped mode, successive peak-to-trough ranges retain the same exponential ratio. Late peaks below the sensor resolution still carry little reliable damping information.

Derive the repeated-root response and its crossing condition

The oscillatory ring-down described above assumes \(\zeta<1\). For a return mechanism holding a fragile component, the question can instead be whether it reaches equilibrium without crossing it. At critical damping, \(c=2\sqrt{mk}\), the characteristic equation is \((s+\omega_n)^2=0\). The repeated root gives two independent free solutions \(e^{-\omega_nt}\) and \(te^{-\omega_nt}\). Imposing \(x(0)=x_0,\dot x(0)=v_0\) yields

\[x(t)=[x_0+(v_0+\omega_n x_0)t]e^{-\omega_nt}.\]

For release from rest at positive displacement, the bracket stays positive and \(\dot x=-\omega_n^2 x_0t e^{-\omega_nt}\) stays negative for \(t>0\). If a mechanism reaches the same \(x_0\) with negative incoming velocity, the bracket can cross zero once even at critical damping. In particular \(v_0<-\omega_n x_0\) gives crossing time \(-x_0/(v_0+\omega_n x_0)\). Critical damping rules out repeated free oscillations, but the initial velocity still determines whether the target is overshot.

For overdamping the distinct real decay rates are \(\omega_n[\zeta\pm\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}]\). Their product is \(\omega_n^2\). Increasing viscous resistance makes the fast root decay more rapidly but the slow root approach zero. Simply adding damping can consequently prolong the final approach to a sensing threshold.

Select a damper for a spring-return slide

A slide of mass 3.0 kg has a return spring of 300 N/m, so \(\omega_n=10\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(c_{\rm cr}=60\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). Released from rest at \(x_0=50\) mm with critical damping, its normalised position is \((1+10t)e^{-10t}\). To enter a 5% band, solve \((1+s)e^{-s}=0.05\) with \(s=10t\). Testing 4.7 and 4.8 gives 0.0518 and 0.0477; the crossing is about 4.74, or \(t=0.474\) s.

The same slide at the same 50 mm location might arrive with \(v_0=-0.80\,\mathrm{m/s}\) after an actuator disconnects. The bracket is now \(0.050-0.30t\), which crosses at 0.167 s. Differentiating gives its negative peak at \(t=0.267\,\mathrm s\), with an overshoot magnitude about 2.08 mm. An optical stop positioned only 1 mm beyond equilibrium would register a crossing even though the damper is critical. Compare that with a slow overdamped return from rest: for \(\zeta=2\) its dominant decay rate falls to \(10(2-\sqrt3)=2.68\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). An assessment based simply on whether the motion oscillates would overlook both the clearance requirement and this much slower return.

The return time depends on which event the instrumentation calls completion. Some switches trigger when the position first enters a band and remain latched even if it later leaves. For the critical release with negative entering speed, first crossing the equilibrium plainly does not mean motion has stopped. A position-plus-velocity criterion is stricter. At the first zero crossing, the bracket in \(x\) vanishes, so the velocity is its constant derivative multiplied by the exponential, \(-0.30e^{-10(0.167)}=-0.0567\,\mathrm{m/s}\). A slide with a maximum docking speed of 0.020 m/s would fail that condition despite its position being exactly correct at that instant. The appropriate response test must state both allowed path excursion and the speed at any mechanical contact.

Checking only the first displacement peak cannot identify this velocity-limited docking event.

Distinguish sliding-friction decay from a viscous ring-down

Suppose a slide moves on a dry horizontal guide under a spring force \(-kx\). While the slide moves left, kinetic friction \(F_c\) acts right, and \(m\ddot x+kx=+F_c\). The solution is harmonic about the shifted equilibrium \(+F_c/k\). During rightward sliding the equilibrium switches to \(-F_c/k\). A complete undisturbed sliding half-cycle still lasts \(\pi\sqrt{m/k}\), but friction dissipates energy in proportion to distance travelled. At zero speed, static friction can take any value up to \(F_s\), so checking the spring force before starting the next half-cycle is essential.

Let a leftward excursion start at \(x=A>0\) and reach a turning point at \(x=-B\), with \(A,B>0\). The spring energy drop equals friction work, \(k(A^2-B^2)/2=F_c(A+B)\). Cancellation leaves \(B=A-2F_c/k\). Same-side peak magnitudes fall by \(4F_c/k\) each complete sliding cycle. This is a constant absolute decrement. A viscous exponential gives a constant ratio instead. If a late half-cycle does not cross zero, use its signed turning-point coordinate \(x_{\rm next}=2F_c/k-x_{\rm start}\) for leftward motion and apply the static criterion there.

A 2 kg slide has \(k=800\,\mathrm{N/m}\), \(F_c=2.0\) N and \(F_s=2.4\) N. Release it from 18 mm. Its initial spring force is 14.4 N, so it moves. Each half-cycle reduces the alternating peak magnitude by 5.0 mm, giving signed positions \(+18,-13,+8,-3\) mm. At −3 mm the spring pull is 2.4 N, equal to the static threshold. Under an ideal static-friction law it can remain there. It took \(3\pi\sqrt{2/800}=0.471\) s to reach that position. The final displacement need not be zero. Interpreting its first two positive peaks with a logarithmic decrement would give a damping estimate that fails to predict the finite stop.

Problem sheet 1 · Equivalent inertia and ring-down

Refer all energy terms to one coordinate, then use measured peaks to identify a vibrating support.

1. Accelerate a conveyor

A roller of inertia 0.080 kg·m² and radius 0.12 m drives an effective translating belt mass of 30 kg without slip. A constant resisting belt force of 40 N acts. (a) Find the roller-referred inertia. (b) Find the torque for belt acceleration 0.60 m/s². (c) At belt speed 1.20 m/s, check the input power against friction and the kinetic-energy increase.

Reduce and check the drive

The inertia is \(0.080+30(0.12)^2=0.512\) kg·m². Angular acceleration is \(0.60/0.12=5.0\) rad/s² and the resistance torque is \(40(0.12)=4.80\) N·m. The total torque is \(4.80+0.512(5)=7.36\) N·m. At 10 rad/s this supplies 73.6 W. Resistance consumes 48.0 W and kinetic energy increases at \(0.512(10)(5)=25.6\) W, giving the same total.

2. Identify an unknown damper

A 12 kg instrument rings down from 6 mm to 3 mm over exactly three full cycles taking 0.75 s. (a) Infer \(\omega_d\) and envelope rate \(\alpha\). (b) Obtain \(k,c,\zeta\). (c) Predict when the envelope will reach 1.5 mm and state one measurement that could falsify the viscous model.

Fit the measured peaks

The period is 0.25 s, so \(\omega_d=25.13\) rad/s. The envelope satisfies \(\alpha=\ln(6/3)/0.75=0.924\) s⁻¹. Thus \(c=2m\alpha=22.2\) N·s/m, \(k=m(\omega_d^2+\alpha^2)=7.59\) kN/m and \(\zeta=0.0367\). Another 0.75 s halves the envelope again, so 1.5 mm is expected 1.50 s after the first peak. If successive same-sign peak ratios change substantially above the noise floor, a constant viscous damper does not explain the trace by itself.

3. Identify an effective mass with an added mass

A nearly undamped mount oscillates at 5.00 Hz. A compact added mass of 4.50 kg lowers the frequency to 4.00 Hz. (a) Infer the original effective mass. (b) Find the spring stiffness. (c) Estimate the static additional sag and identify the assumption needed to compare with a ruler.

Solve the two frequency equations

If the stiffness does not change, \((5/4)^2=(m+4.50)/m\), giving \(m=8.00\) kg. Therefore \(k=8(2\pi5)^2=7.90\) kN/m. The extra weight gives sag \(4.5(9.81)/7896=5.59\) mm for a vertical mount. This comparison requires the measured displacement and added mass to participate in the same coordinate and the spring to remain linear over the extra static travel.

Identify a dry guide from three same-side peaks

A slide with stiffness 1.20 kN/m has successive positive maxima at 21, 15 and 9 mm before sticking occurs. Find the kinetic friction and predict its following negative turning point. Would it restart from there if static friction can supply 8 N?

Show the worked solution

The constant full-cycle decrement is 6 mm, hence \(F_c=k(0.006)/4=1.80\) N. The half-cycle decrement is 3 mm, so a release from +9 mm reaches −6 mm. There the spring force is \(1200(0.006)=7.2\) N, within the 8 N static limit. It remains at −6 mm. The 9 mm positive peak can start this last half-cycle because its 10.8 N spring force exceeds the same static bound.

Chapter 3 · Harmonic forcing, phase and dissipated power

Predict the steady vibration under a rotating force and relate the phase lag to the power absorbed by damping.

Extract one sinusoidal component

A slightly eccentric rotating part exerts a periodic force on its housing. Measure the housing displacement from static equilibrium and first represent a single transverse force component by \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Linear response then satisfies \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t\). Its total solution contains the free transient plus a steady periodic part. Wait several decay times before comparing measured peak amplitudes with a harmonic prediction.

Write the steady part as \(\mathrm{Re}\{Xe^{i\omega t}\}\), taking the real force phasor \(F_0\) as phase reference. Each differentiation contributes \(i\omega\). The displacement phasor therefore satisfies

\[(k-m\omega^2+i c\omega)X=F_0.\]

The factor in parentheses is dynamic stiffness in N/m. At low speed the phasor is close to \(F_0/k\). At high speed the inertia term dominates and displacement is almost opposite the applied force. A phase measurement can distinguish those regimes even if their displacement amplitudes happen to be equal.

Read magnitude and phase separately

Introduce \(r=\omega/\omega_n\), \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n)\). Take the magnitude and argument of the dynamic stiffness to obtain \(|X|/(F_0/k)=[(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2]^{-1/2}\). The displacement lags by \(\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(2\zeta r,1-r^2)\). At \(r=1\) elastic and inertia forces cancel in phase and the remaining quadrature force is viscous. The displacement lags by 90 degrees and has amplitude \(F_0/(c\omega_n)\).

For constant force amplitude, maximum displacement occurs at \(r=\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}\) if \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2\). This follows by differentiating the squared denominator with respect to \(r\). Thus the frequency of greatest displacement need not coincide exactly with the undamped natural frequency. A rotating imbalance also changes force amplitude with speed, so its peak cannot be inferred from a constant-force plot alone.

Check a resonant measurement by energy

Let \(m=10\) kg, \(k=4000\) N/m, \(c=40\) N·s/m and \(F_0=8.0\) N. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and \(\zeta=0.10\). At resonance the predicted displacement amplitude is \(8/[40(20)]=10.0\) mm. The velocity amplitude is 0.200 m/s. The time-average input power of a sinusoidal force and velocity is half the product of their peak magnitudes times the cosine of their phase difference. Since velocity is in phase with force at this frequency, the average is 0.800 W.

Independently, the damper dissipates \(\overline P=c\,\overline{\dot x^2}=c\omega^2|X|^2/2=0.800\) W. The spring returns the energy it stores each cycle and the mass has no net change in kinetic energy. If force and displacement peaks are simply multiplied and reported as watts, the dimensions and the missing phase both reveal the error.

Apply the result to a rotor

A rotating eccentric mass \(m_e\) displaced a radius \(e\) generates force amplitude \(m_e e\omega^2\) when its circular motion is prescribed. With \(m_e e=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m}\) and \(\omega=20\) rad/s, this is the 8.0 N used above. At 40 rad/s the applied force grows to 32 N. The dynamic stiffness magnitude becomes \(\sqrt{(4000-10(40)^2)^2+(40(40))^2}=12.1\) kN/m, giving about 2.64 mm displacement. The force grew fourfold while the response fell because the system moved away from resonance. A speed sweep therefore needs both the changing excitation and the changing dynamic stiffness.

Estimate damping from a frequency sweep

A useful second identification uses the vibration velocity at constant force amplitude. At resonance the velocity amplitude is \(F_0/c\). At the two frequencies where mean dissipated power has fallen to half its maximum, the velocity amplitude is \(F_0/(\sqrt2 c)\). Inverting the velocity response gives \(|k/\omega-m\omega|=c\). Solving the two sign cases and subtracting gives the bandwidth \(\omega_2-\omega_1=c/m\) for this viscous single-mode system. In the numerical example the difference is 4 rad/s; the predicted limits are about 18.10 and 22.10 rad/s. This result applies to the power or velocity response at constant force amplitude. A sweep driven by a growing rotor imbalance would also require correcting the measured amplitudes for their changing excitation.

Decompose a nonsinusoidal periodic machine force

A cam, pump or reciprocating actuator can generate a nearly rectangular force over each cycle. For a linear system in periodic steady operation, expand this force into Fourier components and apply the dynamic stiffness separately at each harmonic. The construction assumes linear coefficients over the motion range and that the free transient has decayed. A periodic input does not imply that the fundamental component dominates the measured displacement; a higher harmonic near a natural frequency can be amplified strongly.

For a symmetric square force \(F(t)=F_s\,\operatorname{sgn}(\sin\Omega t)\), the mean vanishes and half-wave symmetry cancels all even harmonics. Its sine coefficient over one period is \(b_n=(2/\pi)\int_0^\pi F_s\sin(n\theta)d\theta\) for the half-wave-symmetric terms, giving \(4F_s/(n\pi)\) for odd \(n\). Each odd harmonic has its own displacement amplitude and phase:

\[X_n=\frac{4F_s/(n\pi)}{\sqrt{[k-m(n\Omega)^2]^2+(cn\Omega)^2}},\qquad \phi_n=\operatorname{atan2}(cn\Omega,k-m(n\Omega)^2).\]

Reconstruct the time history by adding \(X_n\sin(n\Omega t-\phi_n)\). Adding the magnitudes gives a peak upper bound, not generally the actual peak because phase shifts differ. Orthogonality gives an exact mean-square contribution \(X_n^2/2\) for each distinct harmonic, providing a convenient comparison of vibration levels without finding coincident maxima.

Identify the dominant vibration in an indexing machine

Take \(m=5.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(k=4500\,\mathrm{N/m}\), and \(c=9.0\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\), so \(\omega_n=30\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(\zeta=0.03\). The force alternates between ±12 N at fundamental \(\Omega=10\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). The first-harmonic force is \(48/\pi=15.28\,\mathrm N\). Its dynamic stiffness magnitude is \(\sqrt{4000^2+90^2}=4001\,\mathrm{N/m}\), giving \(X_1=3.82\,\mathrm{mm}\) with a small lag. The third harmonic has force 5.09 N but falls exactly at the natural frequency, giving \(X_3=5.09/(9\times30)=18.9\,\mathrm{mm}\) at 90° lag.

At 50 rad/s the fifth component has force 3.06 N and stiffness magnitude \(\sqrt{8000^2+450^2}\), giving only 0.381 mm. The first three nonzero harmonics give RMS displacement \(\sqrt{(3.82^2+18.9^2+0.381^2)/2}=13.6\,\mathrm{mm}\). The largest measured spectral peak would appear at three times the operating frequency. It could arise from the drive’s waveform rather than a separate three-times-speed rotating fault. Altering the rise time of the periodic force can reduce high-order Fourier coefficients, while adding damping around this resonance reduces the mechanical amplification. Which intervention addresses the observed peak can be tested by measuring the force spectrum as well as the motion.

The energy entering the damping element gives a further spectral check because high frequencies carry more velocity for the same displacement. The mean dissipated power of harmonic \(n\) is \(c(n\Omega X_n)^2/2\). In the example the first harmonic dissipates about \(9(10\times0.00382)^2/2=0.0066\) W, while the third dissipates \(9(30\times0.0189)^2/2=1.45\) W. The third harmonic therefore dominates heating even more strongly than it dominates displacement. A sensor attached near a nodal point could suppress a local displacement reading, but the overall energy dissipation still has to be provided by the drive. Synchronous force and velocity measurements over a whole period can independently check this average input power.

A centred force–velocity recording also excludes a false average power component introduced by a sensor’s DC offset.

Chapter 4 · Coupled oscillators and normal coordinates

Derive and interpret the two modes of a coupled machine, then decide which mode an applied load can excite.

Two masses allow two independent shapes

Two identical instruments sit on separate spring mounts and are connected by a flexible link. Pull both by the same amount and the link keeps its natural length. Pull them in opposite directions and the link stretches twice as much as either displacement. The two tests reveal distinct restoring mechanisms. A model with one equivalent spring and the sum of both masses would discard one of them.

Let \(x_1,x_2\) be horizontal displacements from equilibrium. Each mass is \(m\), each ground spring has stiffness \(k\), and the coupling spring has stiffness \(k_c\). With light springs, small displacements and no damping, the kinetic energy is \(m(\dot x_1^2+\dot x_2^2)/2\) and the elastic energy is \(k(x_1^2+x_2^2)/2+k_c(x_1-x_2)^2/2\). The coupling energy contains the difference of displacements because it is the extension between its ends.

Separate space from time

Taking the derivative of elastic energy with respect to each coordinate gives \(m\ddot x_1+(k+k_c)x_1-k_cx_2=0\) and the symmetric second equation. Look for \(\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\phi\cos\omega t\). Nonzero modal shapes solve \((\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)\boldsymbol\phi=0\). For this pair the determinant equation is

\[(k+k_c-m\omega^2)^2-k_c^2=0.\]

Taking both signs of the square root gives \(\omega_1^2=k/m\) and \(\omega_2^2=(k+2k_c)/m\). Substitution back yields shapes proportional to \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\). For the first, the connector transmits no incremental force. For the second, the connector acts strongly. Reversing both signs of a plotted eigenvector or multiplying it by a nonzero number describes the same physical mode.

Predict a release test at both instruments

Take \(m=5.0\) kg, \(k=2000\) N/m and \(k_c=1500\) N/m. The two angular frequencies are 20.0 and 31.62 rad/s, or 3.18 and 5.03 Hz. Pull only the first instrument 4.0 mm and release from rest. The initial displacement vector equals \(2(1,1)+2(1,-1)\) mm. Each modal amplitude starts at its corresponding value with zero initial velocity. Hence

\[x_1(t)=2\cos(20t)+2\cos(31.62t),\qquad x_2(t)=2\cos(20t)-2\cos(31.62t)\]

in millimetres. At zero time the second displacement vanishes while the connector exerts 6.0 N on its mass. Its initial acceleration is 1.2 m/s² toward the first instrument’s displacement. This apparent transfer of motion needs neither forcing nor damping: modal displacements add and cancel differently at the two observation points.

Distinguish available modes from visible modes

Define average and relative coordinates \(q_s=(x_1+x_2)/2\) and \(q_a=(x_1-x_2)/2\). Adding or subtracting the original equations decouples them. Equal external forces excite only \(q_s\); equal and opposite forces excite only \(q_a\). Taking the signal \((x_1+x_2)/2\) likewise hides the antisymmetric mode. A missing spectral peak can therefore reveal the symmetry of a load or sensor arrangement, even though the corresponding eigenmode still exists.

If the two masses differ, the simple sum and difference cease to diagonalise the equations. The general route is to form the physical mass and stiffness matrices and solve the symmetric generalised eigenproblem. Distinct modes of an undamped conservative system are orthogonal with respect to mass, \(\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_j=0\). This weighted orthogonality is what permits a measured motion or distributed load to be projected into separate modal coordinates.

Predict which mode responds to adding rotary inertia

For a unit mode shape \(\phi\), the quotient \(\omega^2=\phi^TK\phi/(\phi^TM\phi)\) compares stored elastic energy with inertia. Increasing inertia at a coordinate matters most when that coordinate has appreciable motion in the mode. A frame with three tip coordinates offers a way to check this reasoning beyond the symmetric two-mass illustration.

Compare two changes to the same frame: double only the tip mass from 200 to 400 kg, then restore it and double only the tip rotary inertia from 60 to 120 kg m². Predict whether either change can raise a natural frequency for the same stiffness and compute the fractional shift of the third mode in each case. Use the negligible-member-mass row throughout. Check the frame frequency measurements.

Derive bounce and pitch modes of a supported machine

A long machine supported on two mounts can translate and rotate as a nearly rigid body even if there is negligible frame bending. Let the mass centre move vertically by \(z\) and rotate by small angle \(\theta\), with positive rotation raising a point to the right. Vertical spring deflections relative to ground are \(z-a\theta\) on the left and \(z+b\theta\) on the right. Measure displacements from static equilibrium. The kinetic energy is \((m\dot z^2+I_G\dot\theta^2)/2\), and the spring energy is \([k_L(z-a\theta)^2+k_R(z+b\theta)^2]/2\).

Differentiating potential energy gives stiffness terms \(K_{zz}=k_L+k_R,\ K_{\theta\theta}=k_La^2+k_Rb^2,\ K_{z\theta}=-k_La+k_Rb\). The mass matrix is diagonal because translation was measured at the centre of mass. A vertical translation produces a moment whenever the weighted mount locations fail to balance, and a pure rotation then also produces a vertical force by reciprocity. If \(k_La=k_Rb\) the modes are pure translation and pure pitch. Equal spring rates alone do not guarantee decoupling when the lever arms differ.

For free motion solve \((K-\omega^2 M)\phi=0\). In mixed coordinates, metres and radians label different displacement entries, so a numerical component ratio needs units or a stated length scale. Measured vertical motions at two actual sensor locations provide an unambiguous observable mode shape.

Separate a heaving mode from a rocking mode

Let \(m=200\) kg, \(I_G=40\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), \(a=b=0.50\) m and mount rates \(k_L=30000,\ k_R=50000\,\mathrm{N/m}\). The stiffness entries are 80000 N/m, 20000 N m/rad and 10000 N in the mixed term. Setting the determinant to zero gives \((80000-200\lambda)(20000-40\lambda)-10000^2=0\) with \(\lambda=\omega^2\). Dividing through by 8000 yields \(\lambda^2-900\lambda+187500=0\). The squared frequencies are 327.5 and 572.5 s⁻², giving 2.88 and 3.81 Hz.

For the lower mode the first row implies \(\theta/z=-(80000-200\times327.5)/10000=-1.45\,\mathrm{rad/m}\). If mass-centre motion has 1 mm amplitude, the mount-point displacements are 1.725 and 0.275 mm, in phase but strongly unequal. The higher mode gives \(\theta/z=+3.45\,\mathrm{rad/m}\), so the left point now moves oppositely to the right. An accelerometer placed close to a mode’s nodal point can scarcely detect it although that mode remains physically present. For the low mode the vertical node lies about \(1/1.45=0.69\) m to the right of the centre, beyond the right mount. Two well-separated measurement points make the rotational contribution identifiable.

A static load experiment can identify the stiffness coupling before a modal test. Apply a known vertical force \(F\) at the centre and no external moment. The pitch-equilibrium equation gives \(\theta=-K_{z\theta}z/K_{\theta\theta}=-0.50z\) rad per metre for these mounts. Substitution into the vertical balance leaves effective static stiffness \(80000-10000^2/20000=75000\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Under 150 N centre load, predicted motion is 2.00 mm and pitch −0.0010 rad, giving left and right mount readings of 2.50 and 1.50 mm. A centre gauge alone would only indicate an apparent softening, while the unequal pair exposes the rotational coupling that later appears in the two mode shapes.

Solve the phasor balance

The natural angular frequency is 10 rad/s. At this speed the real dynamic stiffness vanishes, giving \(|X|=1/(4\cdot10)=0.0250\) m and 90° displacement lag. The velocity peak is 0.250 m/s and average input power is \(1(0.25)/2=0.125\) W. At 5 rad/s the dynamic stiffness is \(150+20i\) N/m, hence \(|X|=1/\sqrt{150^2+20^2}=6.61\) mm. The initial mean is 1 mm and the initial half-difference is 2 mm, corresponding to \(1(1,1)+2(1,-1)\) mm. With zero initial velocities both modes start as cosines. Chapter 5 · Base excitation and vibration isolation

Distinguish absolute motion, mount travel and transmitted force when choosing a support for a moving base.

A moving foundation is a different input

Place a microscope on a spring-and-damper mount above a vibrating floor. An accelerometer on its body reports absolute acceleration, whereas a displacement gauge connected across the mount reports relative displacement. These can be very different. First write both coordinates in the same inertial frame. Let \(y(t)\) be the motion of the floor and \(x(t)\) the motion of the instrument. The spring force on the mass is \(-k(x-y)\) and the damper force is \(-c(\dot x-\dot y)\).

For mass \(m\), Newton’s law therefore becomes \(m\ddot x+c(\dot x-\dot y)+k(x-y)=0\). A floor that moves sufficiently slowly carries the mass along with almost no relative motion. A floor moving very rapidly can move substantially beneath a nearly stationary mass. Whether the design is successful depends on which motion is constrained by the application.

Derive two transfer functions

For harmonic input \(y=\mathrm{Re}(Ye^{i\omega t})\), substitute \(x=\mathrm{Re}(Xe^{i\omega t})\). Collect terms in \(X\) and \(Y\). Writing \(r=\omega/\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2\sqrt{km})\) gives

\[\frac{X}{Y}=\frac{1+i2\zeta r}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r},\qquad \frac{X-Y}{Y}=\frac{r^2}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r}.\]

Subtract the complex quantities before taking magnitudes. In general the magnitude of the relative displacement is not the difference of the absolute amplitudes, since the motions can have different phase angles. The acceleration ratio for harmonics equals \(|X/Y|\) because both displacements acquire the same factor \(-\omega^2\). That shortcut is inapplicable to arbitrary time records without decomposition into frequencies.

Compare the squares of numerator and denominator in the first ratio. Transmissibility falls below one when \((1-r^2)^2>1\), or \(r>\sqrt2\) for positive frequency. The crossing is independent of damping in this ideal model. Damping reduces the peak around resonance but provides a force path at high frequency, increasing the transmitted motion there compared with a lighter damper.

Choose an isolation range and a clearance

Take \(m=80\) kg and desired undamped frequency \(f_n=4.0\) Hz. Then \(k=m(2\pi f_n)^2=50.5\) kN/m. With \(\zeta=0.15\), the required viscous coefficient is \(c=2m(2\pi f_n)\zeta=603\) N·s/m. Effective isolation begins above \(4\sqrt2=5.66\) Hz. A floor excursion of 0.30 mm amplitude at 12 Hz gives \(r=3\) and \(2\zeta r=0.90\). The absolute ratio is \(\sqrt{1.81/64.81}=0.167\), hence instrument motion is 0.050 mm. The relative amplitude is \(9(0.30)/\sqrt{64.81}=0.335\) mm. The smaller number is appropriate for motion performance and the larger for mount clearance.

At 4 Hz the same base displacement gives relative amplitude \(0.30/(2\zeta)=1.00\) mm. Before checking that a stop is far enough away, add the static spring compression \(mg/k=15.5\) mm if the mount is vertical and its available stroke is specified from the unloaded position.

Ask what a sensor can distinguish

Measure floor and instrument motion simultaneously with a shared clock. An isolated mass at high frequency can seem quiet despite substantial relative movement. Conversely, close to resonance both acceleration and support force may be amplified. For harmonic motion the force on the base is the spring-plus-damper force. Its magnitude equals \(m\omega^2|X|\) when no other dynamic forces act on the mass. Comparing it with the measured acceleration is an independent check on force calibration and on the assumption of one moving coordinate.

Calculate what an accelerometer would report

A sensor bolted to the instrument measures absolute acceleration. With harmonic motion its amplitude is \(\omega^2|X|\), even when the clearance requirement is governed by \(|X-Y|\). For the 12 Hz condition, the 0.050 mm instrument displacement gives \( (2\pi12)^2(0.000050)=0.284\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). The floor acceleration is 1.71 m/s², so the acceleration transmissibility is the same 0.167 as the absolute displacement ratio at this frequency. Inferring spring force from the instrument’s absolute displacement would instead be wrong: the extension of the spring is the relative motion. The damper force also depends on relative velocity, and the two transmitted force components differ in phase.

Derive the response of a mounted accelerometer

An accelerometer is itself a base-excited oscillator. Let its casing follow the machine displacement \(y(t)\) while an internal proof mass moves at \(x(t)\). A spring and viscous element connect the two, and a transducer detects relative motion \(z=x-y\). Because damping and stiffness act on relative movement, Newton’s law gives \(m\ddot z+c\dot z+kz=-m\ddot y\). The instrument manufacturer may scale the output as estimated acceleration \(\hat a=-\omega_n^2z\). This scale is exact at zero frequency in the linear model, but both gain and phase change as frequency approaches the sensor resonance.

For harmonic casing acceleration with phasor \(A\), the transfer from actual to indicated acceleration is

\[\frac{\hat A}{A}=\frac{1}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r},\qquad r=\omega/\omega_n .\]

At small \(r\), the proof mass is almost in force equilibrium with the spring and the output tracks acceleration. At large \(r\), the proof mass is nearly inertially stationary and the relative displacement approximates negative casing displacement. This is the regime exploited by certain displacement-based seismic instruments. The very same equation permits two measurement principles, but their useful frequency ranges differ.

For \(\zeta=1/\sqrt2\) the squared magnitude denominator simplifies to \(1+r^4\). The leading magnitude error then appears at fourth order, although the phase still changes at first order. A sensor can consequently have an excellent amplitude calibration and a material timing error in the same frequency range.

Select a transducer for a base-isolation measurement

A vibration test uses an accelerometer with undamped frequency 100 Hz and \(\zeta=1/\sqrt2\). At a 30 Hz machine component, \(r=0.30\). The indicated magnitude is \(1/\sqrt{1+0.3^4}=0.9960\) times the true acceleration, whereas the lag is \(\arctan[\sqrt2(0.30)/(1-0.30^2)]=25.0^\circ\). An amplitude requirement of 1% is met, but a phase comparison between this sensor and an independently calibrated force transducer would carry a large offset. For an actual acceleration amplitude of \(4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\), proof-mass travel is \(0.996(4)/(2\pi100)^2=10.1\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), which also has to fit the transducer’s linear travel range.

For the same damping, an amplitude error below 1% requires \(1/\sqrt{1+r^4}\ge0.99\), or \(r\le0.378\). A record extending to 50 Hz would thus require a resonance of at least about 132 Hz by magnitude alone. A 5° phase specification is much stricter: solving \(\sqrt2r/(1-r^2)=\tan5^\circ\) gives \(r\simeq0.0616\), or resonance above about 812 Hz for the 50 Hz component. If two identical, well-matched sensors measure base and payload at the same frequency, much of their common transfer cancels in a ratio. It does not cancel in a phase comparison to another instrument with a different dynamics.

An instrument range may be specified in acceleration rather than proof-mass stroke. Near resonance the displacement per unit base acceleration increases according to the same frequency response. A sensor that remains linear at a given low-frequency acceleration can therefore approach its internal stop at a lower applied acceleration near resonance. Mechanical range and dynamic calibration must be checked together.

Chapter 6 · Impulse response and finite-duration loading

Construct the response to a short pulse and determine when an impulse approximation preserves the peak motion.

A short event and a long response

A latch strikes a mounted instrument for only a few milliseconds, but its motion persists after contact has ended. Peak force alone gives an incomplete description of that motion. Duration, impulse and the system’s natural time scale all matter. Define impulse \(J=\int F(t)\,dt\), with units N·s. Integrating \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F\) over an ideal instantaneous event leaves \(m[\dot x(0^+)-\dot x(0^-)]=J\). The position remains continuous since no finite mass can jump a finite distance in zero time under a finite impulse.

For an initially resting underdamped oscillator, the subsequent response follows from the two initial conditions \(x(0^+)=0\) and \(\dot x(0^+)=J/m\). With \(\alpha=c/(2m)\) and \(\omega_d=\sqrt{k/m-\alpha^2}\), the displacement is \(x=Je^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega_dt)/(m\omega_d)\) for \(t>0\). The response to a unit impulse is the same expression with \(J=1\). Its units are displacement per unit impulse.

Build a solution from elementary impulses

Divide a general forcing record into short intervals at times \(s\). Each interval contributes impulse \(F(s)\,ds\) and creates a response delayed by \(s\). Adding all contributions in a linear time-invariant system with zero initial state produces the convolution

\[x(t)=\int_0^t F(s)\,\frac{e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\sin[\omega_d(t-s)]}{m\omega_d}\,ds.\]

This construction makes causality explicit: later parts of the force record cannot influence the current response. Nonzero initial conditions add the free solution. In a multi-mode system each mode has its own impulse response, and the load’s location determines the force projection into it. Replacing the structure by its first mode may describe long-wave displacement well while missing a short, local acceleration peak.

Work a pulse of known duration

Neglect damping and apply constant force \(F_0\) for \(0<t<t_p\). During the pulse integration gives \(x=(F_0/k)[1-\cos(\omega_n t)]\) and \(\dot x=F_0\omega_n\sin(\omega_n t)/k\). After unloading the mass oscillates freely. Compute its amplitude from the end displacement and velocity:

\[A=\sqrt{x(t_p)^2+\big[\dot x(t_p)/\omega_n\big]^2}=\frac{2F_0}{k}\left|\sin(\omega_n t_p/2)\right|.\]

Let \(m=4.0\) kg, \(k=1600\) N/m, \(F_0=20\) N and \(t_p=0.050\) s. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and the impulse is 1.0 N·s. The formula predicts \(A=25\sin(0.50)=12.0\) mm after unloading. Replacing the whole pulse by an instantaneous impulse gives \(A_{\rm imp}=J/(m\omega_n)=12.5\) mm. Some restoring force developed during the real pulse, so the impulse approximation is slightly high.

Find the criterion and its limitations

Divide the finite-pulse amplitude by the impulse approximation to obtain \(|\sin z/z|\) with \(z=\omega_nt_p/2\). Its small-\(z\) expansion is \(1-z^2/6+\cdots\). At fixed impulse, a sufficiently short pulse therefore gives about the same low-mode amplitude regardless of duration. A shorter event has a broader frequency content, potentially increasing the importance of higher modes.

A pulse lasting exactly one natural period gives zero residual amplitude in the ideal undamped oscillator, although the displacement during the pulse peaks at \(2F_0/k\). Checking only the post-event motion would miss that maximum. In experiments record the force and response during contact as well as after it. Damping, uncertain pulse shape or nearby modes make exact cancellation unlikely, but the example shows why pulse duration must accompany a quoted peak force.

Compare pulses of equal impulse

A force transducer may report identical total impulse for a sharp tap and a broader pulse. For an undamped mode, the amplitude after the force has ended is the modulus of its frequency-weighted impulse divided by \(m\omega_n\): \(A=|\int F(t)e^{-i\omega_nt}dt|/(m\omega_n)\). This follows by expanding the sine kernel of the convolution into two quadratures. For a rectangular pulse the ratio to the ideal instantaneous-impulse amplitude is \(|\sin(\omega_nt_p/2)/(\omega_nt_p/2)|\). At a duration equal to half the natural period that factor is \(2/\pi=0.637\). Two force records with equal signed area can therefore have visibly different post-event amplitudes at one sensor if their durations differ. A short force pulse can consequently calibrate a low-frequency mode while underexciting a faster one in the same structure.

Derive an energy-consistent time update for a force record

A measured force history may be irregular enough that a closed-form convolution is inconvenient. The constant-average-acceleration Newmark update advances a linear oscillator one time step at a time. Let \(x_n,v_n,a_n\) be displacement, velocity and acceleration at \(t_n\). Approximate the acceleration over the step through its endpoint average in the velocity update, and use the corresponding trapezoidal velocity update for displacement. With step \(h\) the relations are

\[v_{n+1}=v_n+\tfrac h2(a_n+a_{n+1}),\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n+\tfrac{h^2}4(a_n+a_{n+1}).\]

Enforce the actual equation \(ma_{n+1}+cv_{n+1}+kx_{n+1}=F_{n+1}\) at the new time. Gathering unknown acceleration terms gives effective mass \(m+ch/2+kh^2/4\). The right side is \(F_{n+1}-c(v_n+ha_n/2)-k(x_n+hv_n+h^2a_n/4)\). Solve that scalar equation and back-substitute into both kinematic updates. For several coupled linear coordinates the same derivation gives a linear system with an effective matrix.

For undamped unforced systems, multiply the average endpoint equilibrium by \(x_{n+1}-x_n=h(v_{n+1}+v_n)/2\). Differences of squares show that the discrete change in kinetic energy plus potential energy vanishes exactly. This property detects sign or unit errors in an implementation. It does not guarantee accurate phase at a coarse time step.

Check one step and distinguish stability from temporal resolution

Take \(m=2\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(c=0\), \(k=200\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Begin from \(x_0=0.010\,\mathrm m,\ v_0=0\) and release without applied force. Initial acceleration is \(-kx_0/m=-1.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). With \(h=0.10\,\mathrm s\), effective mass is \(2+200(0.10)^2/4=2.50\,\mathrm{kg}\). Its right side is \(-200[0.010+0.10^2(-1)/4]=-1.50\,\mathrm N\), giving \(a_1=-0.600\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). The updates give \(v_1=-0.0800\,\mathrm{m/s}, x_1=0.00600\,\mathrm m\).

The initial energy is \(200(0.010)^2/2=0.0100\,\mathrm J\); after one step it is \(2(0.080)^2/2+200(0.006)^2/2=0.0100\,\mathrm J\). The exact displacement at 0.10 s is nevertheless \(0.010\cos(1)=0.00540\,\mathrm m\). The numerical oscillator advances through angle \(2\arctan(\omega_nh/2)=0.9273\) rad per step instead of 1.00 rad. Over many cycles this small period error accumulates. Halving the step is a direct temporal convergence test. For a measured force pulse, a second check is that its sampled impulse and timing converge as well; an accurate oscillator update cannot recover a force feature missed by the acquisition system.

It is possible to predict the amount of step refinement needed for a phase-sensitive comparison. For small \(z=\omega_n h/2\), expand \(2\arctan z=2z-2z^3/3+\cdots\). Dividing the numerical angular advance by the true advance gives \(1-(\omega_n h)^2/12+\cdots\). The leading relative frequency error therefore falls by a factor of four when the step is halved. A one-percent target suggests \(\omega_nh\lesssim\sqrt{0.12}=0.346\) for this single undamped mode. At 10 rad/s that means roughly 0.035 s or less, before checking the sampling of the force itself. This criterion addresses the fundamental frequency, not the fastest contributing mode in a multi-degree model. If a high-frequency mode contributes significantly at the measurement station, use its frequency to assess temporal phase resolution or test convergence of the complete measured signal directly.

Once these conditions are met, comparing the computed displacement and velocity at the end of loading also tests the predicted initial condition for the subsequent free ring-down.

Separate floor motion from mount travel, then distinguish peak response during and after an impact.

1. Choose an isolation frequency

A 100 kg platform must have \(|X/Y|<1\) for floor excitation at all frequencies from 8 Hz upward. (a) Find the upper limit on its natural frequency. (b) Choose 5.0 Hz and calculate \(k\). (c) At 10 Hz and \(\zeta=0.20\), find absolute and relative amplitudes for a 0.50 mm base motion.

Apply the displacement transfer function

For the specified inequality \(f_n<8/\sqrt2=5.66\) Hz. At 5.0 Hz the stiffness is \(100(2\pi5)^2=98.7\) kN/m. At \(r=2\) and \(2\zeta r=0.8\), the common denominator magnitude is \(\sqrt{9+0.64}=3.105\). The absolute amplitude is \(0.50\sqrt{1.64}/3.105=0.206\) mm, while relative amplitude is \(0.50(4)/3.105=0.644\) mm. Size the allowable mount travel using the latter value.

2. Compare impacts with the same impulse

An undamped 5 kg component has \(\omega_n=40\) rad/s. Two rectangular forces each provide 1.0 N·s. Pulse A lasts 0.010 s and pulse B 0.100 s. (a) Calculate each force. (b) Calculate the two residual amplitudes. (c) Compare them with the ideal impulse limit.

Keep impulse fixed while changing duration

Chapter 7 · Velocities and accelerations in planar mechanisms

Use the rigid-body velocity and acceleration relations to connect an instantaneously known crank speed to a slider acceleration.

What can change between two points on a link?

The point at which a connecting rod drives a slider moves along a straight line although its other end moves around a crank circle. Writing every trajectory as a function of crank angle is possible, but often only one configuration matters. The rigidity constraint transfers instantaneous velocity and acceleration between points on a link. Choose a fixed inertial frame and use the positive out-of-plane direction for counterclockwise angles. Every vector component below refers to these fixed axes.

Write \(r_B=r_A+r_{B/A}\). The vector between two body points keeps its length but changes direction. Its derivative is \(\omega\times r_{B/A}\). Differentiating a second time introduces both the changing magnitude of angular velocity and the changing direction of the relative vector:

\[v_B=v_A+\omega\times r_{B/A},\qquad a_B=a_A+\alpha\times r_{B/A}+\omega\times(\omega\times r_{B/A}).\]

The last term is \(-\omega^2r_{B/A}\) for planar motion. It points along the rod towards \(A\), even when the rod has constant angular speed. Enforce constraints on the actual point \(B\); a slider constrained horizontally has zero vertical velocity and zero vertical acceleration if its guide is straight and fixed.

A crank at its uppermost position

Place crank centre \(O\) at the origin and the slider along the positive horizontal axis. The crank length is 50 mm and the connecting rod length is 130 mm. At the configuration considered its pin is \(A=(0,50)\,\mathrm{mm}\) and slider pin \(B=(120,0)\,\mathrm{mm}\), since \(120^2+50^2=130^2\). The crank rotates counterclockwise at constant 20 rad/s. Accordingly \(v_A=(-1,0)\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(a_A=(0,-20)\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

The rod vector from \(A\) to \(B\) is \((0.120,-0.050)\,\mathrm m\). Its relative velocity is \(\omega_r(0.050,0.120)\). The vertical velocity constraint gives \(0=0+0.120\omega_r\), so \(\omega_r=0\) at this instant and \(v_B=(-1,0)\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The rod is instantaneously translating. That description concerns velocity only. A moment later its two ends no longer have the same velocity.

Recover the slider force input

Since \(\omega_r=0\) here, the relative centripetal term for the rod vanishes. The angular-acceleration term gives \(0=-20+0.120\alpha_r\), hence \(\alpha_r=166.7\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}\). The slider’s horizontal acceleration is \(a_{Bx}=0+0.050(166.7)=8.33\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). At this instant the slider is moving to the left while accelerating to the right. A bare slider mass of 3 kg needs a net force of 25.0 N to the right before guide friction or process forces are considered.

A useful independent check follows from the exact geometry \(x_B=r\cos\theta+\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\theta}\). At \(\theta=\pi/2\) the first derivative with respect to angle is \(-r\); multiplication by crank speed gives −1 m/s. A numerical second derivative must resolve the curvature of this path. Differentiating noisy measured positions twice can produce a large fictitious force, so comparing observed positions with this geometric constraint is often a better starting point.

Change the drive speed

Repeating the same configuration at twice the constant crank speed doubles every point velocity. Its centripetal terms and the constraint-induced angular acceleration scale with the square of that speed, so the predicted slider acceleration becomes 33.3 m/s². This scaling is one reason mechanism inertia loads can grow rapidly with production rate.

Differentiate a coordinate measured along a rotating guide

The rigid-body formula above assumes that the position vector joining the two points is fixed in their common body. A slider moving along a rotating guide violates that assumption. Introduce a guide-fixed unit vector \(e_r\) along the slot and its positive quarter-turn \(e_\theta\). At an inertially fixed pivot the slider’s position is \(r(t)e_r\); the basis changes according to \(\dot e_r=\omega e_\theta,\ \dot e_\theta=-\omega e_r\). Differentiate both the scalar radial position and the changing basis at every step.

\[v=\dot r\,e_r+r\omega\,e_\theta,\qquad a=(\ddot r-r\omega^2)e_r+(r\dot\omega+2\dot r\omega)e_\theta .\]

The factor two in the transverse contribution has two origins. The outward relative velocity vector rotates with the guide, and the speed of the circumferential transport changes as the slider moves outwards. Each contributes \(\dot r\omega\). This Coriolis term is an actual part of the acceleration measured in an inertial frame. In a rotating-frame force balance an apparent Coriolis force of opposite sign must be introduced only if the inertial acceleration has been removed from that balance. Counting both would double the effect.

If the guide’s pivot itself translates, add its inertial acceleration to the expression. For an instantaneously stationary guide \(\omega=0\) eliminates centripetal and Coriolis terms, but a nonzero angular acceleration can still produce transverse acceleration \(r\dot\omega\). This distinction parallels the translating rod in the first worked mechanism.

Determine drive and guide reactions on a rotating table

A 2.5 kg carriage slides outwards along a straight radial slot in a horizontal table. At the given instant \(r=0.30\,\mathrm m\), \(\dot r=0.20\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ddot r=0.50\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\), \(\omega=4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) counterclockwise, and \(\dot\omega=1.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}\). The radial acceleration is \(0.50-0.30(4)^2=-4.30\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) and the transverse acceleration is \(0.30(1)+2(0.20)(4)=1.90\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). A frictionless slot supplies a 4.75 N transverse reaction in the positive rotational direction. The radial actuator must exert a net 10.75 N inward, even though the carriage is moving outwards and accelerating relative to the slot in that direction.

The transverse reaction produces a 1.425 N m moment about the centre. Independently, differentiating the angular momentum \(mr^2\omega\) gives \(m(2r\dot r\omega+r^2\dot\omega)=1.425\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). A table drive that assumes fixed radius would include only \(mr^2\dot\omega=0.225\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), missing most of the required moment in this manoeuvre. Holding angular speed constant would remove this last term but leave the 1.20 N m moment associated with increasing inertia. A torque sensor at the turntable shaft therefore detects radial carriage motion even when no angular acceleration is commanded.

Mechanical power supplies an independent sign check. The rail reaction transmits positive power \(4.75(r\omega)=5.70\) W through the transverse motion at this instant. The inward radial actuator supplies \(-10.75(0.20)=-2.15\) W. Their sum, 3.55 W, equals the instantaneous change of the point mass’s translational energy, \(m(\dot r\,a_r+r\omega a_\theta)=2.5[0.20(-4.30)+1.20(1.90)]\). The rail can supply power because it moves transversely with the table even though the contact has no work in the sliding direction.

Construct angular momentum when the rotation axis is not principal

A planar moment of inertia determines one scalar angular momentum. In three dimensions a balanced body can have angular momentum tilted relative to its angular velocity. About its centre of mass a particle at \(r\) has velocity \(\omega\times r\). Integrate \(r\times(\omega\times r)\) over mass and use the vector triple product. The result is \(H_G=I_G\omega\), with tensor \(I_G=\int[(r^Tr)\mathbf1-rr^T]\,dm\). Its \(x\)-diagonal element is \(\int(y^2+z^2)dm\) and its \(xy\) element is \(-\int xy\,dm\). Equal and opposite points cancel their first moments but add their products of inertia. Symmetry about a coordinate plane, rather than symmetry only about the centre, is sufficient to make the associated product vanish.

In a body-fixed basis rotating at \(\omega\), the external moment components obey \(M_G=I_G\dot\omega+\omega\times(I_G\omega)\). This follows from differentiating \(H_G\) in an inertial frame: the body basis vectors rotate as well as the components. Even with constant body components of angular velocity, a transverse moment can be required to keep a nonprincipal axis fixed.

Model a compact instrument carrier as two equal 0.50 kg masses at \((0.20,0.10,0)\) m and its negative, neglecting the frame mass. Its tensor in kg m² has entries \(I_{xx}=0.010,\ I_{yy}=0.040,\ I_{zz}=0.050,\ I_{xy}=-0.020\). At \(\omega=(20,0,0)\) rad/s, \(H_G=(0.20,-0.40,0)\) kg m²/s. Maintaining that rotation requires \(M_G=(0,0,-8.0)\) N m. There is no offset centre of mass to correct with a single static counterweight. Repositioning the masses to remove the product of inertia, or reorienting the shaft along a principal axis, eliminates this transverse couple in the model. A bearing reaction can reverse the sign convention for the load read by its support. Always state whether the reported moment acts on the rotor or on the frame.

Chapter 8 · Impulse, angular momentum and impact

Find the motion immediately after a short off-centre impact by combining angular impulse with a local restitution condition.

Select a time interval and a reference point

For a short collision, a finite change of velocity occurs over a time in which positions change very little. Integrate Newton’s law over that interval. A force that is harmless in an ordinary static calculation can deliver a large impulse if sustained long enough. Conversely, gravity often contributes very little over a millisecond. Compare its estimated impulse \(mg\Delta t\) with the contact impulse before neglecting it.

For planar rigid-body motion the impulse–momentum balances about the mass centre \(G\) and about an inertially fixed point \(O\) are

\[m(v_G^+-v_G^-)=\sum J,\qquad H_O^+-H_O^-=\sum r_{i/O}\times J_i .\]

The superscripts denote states just before and after contact. A hinge can deliver an unknown impulse but exerts no moment impulse about its own centre when modelled as an ideal pin. Choosing that point can eliminate unknowns when calculating angular speed. Kinetic energy is generally lost to deformation, contact vibration and other processes. It cannot supply a conservation equation without an additional elastic-impact assumption.

Apply the contact condition at the points that meet

For a smooth frictionless collision choose the normal from the incoming body towards the target. The separation speed along that normal equals the specified coefficient of restitution \(e\) times the approach speed. Evaluate velocities of the actual points in contact; the target’s mass-centre speed can be different if the force also rotates it. For a contact point at distance \(a\) along a pinned bar and normal perpendicular to the bar, the normal contact velocity immediately after impact is \(a\omega^+\).

Take a small slider of mass 1.0 kg moving at 3.0 m/s into a stationary slender uniform bar of mass 3.0 kg, length 0.60 m, pinned at one end. It strikes at \(a=0.40\,\mathrm m\), perpendicular to the bar. The bar inertia about the pin is \(I_O=m_bL^2/3=0.360\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Let \(J\) be the positive impulse delivered to the bar. The slider exits at \(3-J/1\), and the bar receives angular speed \(Ja/I_O\).

Close the collision and audit its energy

With \(e=0.50\), the restitution condition is \(a(Ja/I_O)-(3-J)=0.50(3)\), giving \(J=4.5/[1+0.4^2/0.36]=3.115\,\mathrm{N\,s}\). Thus \(\omega^+=3.46\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and the slider rebounds slowly at −0.115 m/s. The bar’s contact point recedes at 1.385 m/s, exactly 1.50 m/s faster than the slider after separation.

The initial kinetic energy was 4.50 J. The final energy is \(\tfrac12(1)(0.115)^2+\tfrac12(0.360)(3.46)^2=2.16\,\mathrm J\). The energy loss is physically consistent with the specified restitution. The pin impulse need not vanish although its moment impulse vanished. The bar’s centre moves at \(0.30(3.46)=1.04\,\mathrm{m/s}\); its linear momentum gain happens to equal about 3.12 N s for this impact position, very close to the contact impulse. Indeed \(a=I_O/(m_bL/2)=0.40\,\mathrm m\) is the centre-of-percussion location, for which the ideal transverse pin impulse vanishes exactly.

Determine whether a rough impact sticks or slides

The smooth collision model leaves tangential velocities unaffected. A rough planar impact can also exchange tangential impulse and rotational momentum. Consider a solid disc of radius \(R\) striking a fixed horizontal surface at its lowest point. Take horizontal right and vertical up as positive; angular speed is counterclockwise positive. Just before impact let centre velocities be \(u^-,v^-\) and angular speed \(\omega^-\). The horizontal speed of its contact point is \(g_t^-=u^-+R\omega^-\). A rightward tangential impulse \(J_t\) produces both a centre velocity increase \(J_t/m\) and a positive angular change \(RJ_t/I_G\).

The post-impact contact velocity is \(g_t^+=g_t^-+(1/m+R^2/I_G)J_t\). For the horizontal surface the normal impulse passes through the centre; restitution requires \(J_n=-m(1+e)v^-\) for \(v^-<0\). The tangential impulse needed to eliminate slip is

\[J_t^{\rm stick}=-g_t^-/(1/m+R^2/I_G).\]

Under a simple Coulomb impulse approximation compare this required magnitude with \(\mu J_n\). If it lies inside the bound the contact can finish without tangential slip. Otherwise take the limiting friction impulse opposing the pre-impact relative slip. This elementary model assumes no change in slip direction before the limiting impulse is reached, negligible external impulse, and no tangential elastic restitution. Using only the disc centre’s velocity to form the stick condition would miss its rotational compliance.

Compare two surfaces with the same normal restitution

A solid disc has \(m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(R=0.10\,\mathrm m\), \(I_G=mR^2/2=0.010\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Its centre approaches at \(u^-=3.0,\ v^-=-2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), with zero initial spin. For \(e=0.50\), \(J_n=6.0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and \(v^+=1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The inverse tangential effective mass is \(1/2+0.10^2/0.010=1.50\,\mathrm{kg^{-1}}\). Thus \(J_t^{\rm stick}=-2.0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and the friction coefficient needed is at least \(2/6=0.333\).

On a surface with \(\mu=0.20\), \(J_t=-1.2\,\mathrm{N\,s}\), giving \(u^+=2.4\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\omega^+=-12\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). The contact-point speed remains \(2.4-1.2=1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}\) to the right, consistent with the chosen sliding direction. On a surface with \(\mu=0.50\), the sticking impulse is achievable. It gives \(u^+=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\omega^+=-20\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), and zero instantaneous tangential contact speed. The disc still leaves the surface vertically because \(v^+=1.0\). Here sticking means zero relative tangential velocity at separation and does not mean a permanently attached body. The original 13 J of kinetic energy drops to 7.48 J in the sliding case and 7.00 J in the sticking case, consistent with frictional dissipation and a normal restitution below unity.

A high-speed camera that measures only the centre trajectory would determine the change in centre momentum and hence the tangential impulse, provided other horizontal impulses are negligible. Marking the disc edge permits an independent angular check: in the sliding case the ratio \(I_G|\Delta\omega|/(R m|\Delta u|)\) is \(0.010(12)/[0.10(2)(0.60)]=1\). A substantial measured deviation could indicate an additional contact couple, a second impact point or uncertainty in inertia. Measuring spin is particularly important near the transition to sticking because centre velocity by itself cannot establish that the tangential contact speed has vanished.

A marked rim therefore provides an observable that a centre-tracking experiment alone cannot supply.

Chapter 9 · Static and couple balance of rotors

Represent rotating imbalance as a vector and determine the corrections needed to cancel both net force and rocking moment.

Translate a mass offset into a periodic bearing load

A rotor can have its total mass centre on the shaft axis and still shake its housing. The reason is spatial: two equal and opposite centrifugal force vectors in separate transverse planes form a rotating couple. First describe one small excess mass \(m\) at radial offset \(e\) and clock angle \(\phi\). At constant angular speed \(\omega\), its required radial acceleration has magnitude \(e\omega^2\). The equal and opposite reaction transmitted to the rotating assembly has magnitude \(me\omega^2\).

Represent its radial unbalance by a complex number \(U=me(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)\). This is shorthand for two transverse components at a reference shaft orientation, not a complex physical force. Multiplication by \(e^{i\omega t}\) rotates all vectors together. Since both \(\omega^2\) and this rotation are common to all masses, cancellation can be carried out using \(U\) alone.

\[\sum U_j=0,\qquad \sum z_jU_j=0.\]

Here \(z\) is axial location from a chosen reference plane. The second condition cancels the rotating transverse moment; its vector direction includes a common quarter-turn from the cross product that drops out of the zero condition. If the first condition holds, shifting the axial origin leaves the second condition unchanged.

Split one known unbalance between two planes

A rigid rotor has an 18 g excess mass at radius 40 mm, angle 0°, and axial location 120 mm between two correction planes at \(z_A=0\) and \(z_B=300\,\mathrm{mm}\). Its unbalance is \(720\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) along the reference direction. Moment balance gives \(300 U_B=-120(720)\), hence \(U_B=-288\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). Force balance then gives \(U_A=-432\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). Both corrections point at 180° because the original mass lies between the planes.

At correction radius 60 mm these vectors require added masses 4.80 g in plane B and 7.20 g in plane A. If material is removed instead, the equivalent removed masses lie at the original 0° clock position. At 1800 rpm the original force amplitude was \(0.000720(188.5)^2=25.6\,\mathrm N\). Doubling speed quadruples that force, yet the same mass corrections still balance the assumed rigid rotor.

Identify what a single-plane test can miss

Consider instead two unbalances \(+400\) and \(-400\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) in planes 200 mm apart. Their resultant force is zero at every speed. On ideal low-friction supports there is no preferred heavy side in a static test. Their couple magnitude at 1800 rpm is \(0.000400(0.200)(188.5)^2=2.84\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), sufficient to generate equal opposing reactions at bearings. Measurements in two support planes can distinguish this pattern from a net shaking force. Near a flexible rotor’s bending critical speed, shaft deflection changes the mass distribution and response phase; the rigid-body correction formulas then require a modal or measured influence-coefficient model.

Interpret correction accuracy

An angular placement error also leaves a residual vector. A correction of the correct magnitude set one degree away leaves a residual approximately \(U\pi/180\) for small angle, since the tip of the vector follows a circle. At large operating speed even this residual produces a force scaling with the square of speed.

Derive a balance correction from a trial mass

An assembled rotor rarely reveals the angular location of its excess mass directly. A once-per-revolution vibration measurement supplies an amplitude and phase referred to a shaft mark. At fixed operating speed and linear support conditions, represent the measured complex vibration \(V\) by \(V=\alpha U\), where \(U\) is the complex unbalance in one plane and \(\alpha\) is the response per unit unbalance. This coefficient includes bearing compliance, dynamic amplification and the sensor’s phase lag. The phase of largest displacement is therefore not necessarily the angle where correction mass should be added.

Record the original vibration \(V_0\), add a known trial unbalance \(U_t\) and measure \(V_t\) at the same speed with the same phase reference. Subtraction cancels the original signal and gives

\[\alpha=(V_t-V_0)/U_t,\qquad U_c=-V_0/\alpha .\]

Remove the trial before installing the calculated total correction. If the trial remains attached, install only \(U_c-U_t\) as further correction. Subtract complex vectors, not their magnitudes. A trial too small to resolve above measurement noise makes the inferred phase and correction unreliable; an unnecessarily large trial can take the rotor outside the measured linear range. A repeat baseline after removing the mass can expose a changing thermal or mounting condition.

Convert measured shaft phase into a physical correction

At fixed speed a laser reports an original synchronous displacement \(V_0=(30+40i)\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). Add a 5.0 g trial mass at a 40 mm radius at the reference angle, so \(U_t=200\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). The new reading is \(V_t=(50+60i)\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The influence coefficient is \((20+20i)/200=0.10+0.10i\) micrometres per g mm. The required correction is

\[U_c=-\frac{30+40i}{0.10+0.10i}=-(350+50i)\,\mathrm{g\,mm}.\]

Its magnitude is \(353.6\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) and angle is 188.1° from the trial reference in the same positive rotational convention. With a correction radius of 50 mm, add about 7.07 g after removing the trial. Multiplying the correction vector by the measured coefficient gives \(-30-40i\) micrometres, cancelling the baseline in the linear model. If the trial must stay, the extra vector is \(-550-50i\) g mm. Installing the full correction in addition to the trial would leave its trial response as a predictable residual.

Single-plane correction minimises one measured response at one speed. For a long rotor with a separate couple unbalance, a response measured at one support may vanish while the opposite support continues to vibrate. Measuring both supports under trial masses in two planes produces a two-by-two complex influence matrix. Nearly proportional measured columns mean that the two trial planes cannot be distinguished reliably at those sensor locations, a conditioning issue that no amount of arithmetic precision can cure.

The initial amplitude in the example is 50 micrometres; the trial amplitude is 78.1 micrometres. Their scalar difference, 28.1 micrometres, happens to be close to the actual complex response increment’s magnitude of 28.3 micrometres, but the scalar comparison contains no correction angle. Even apparently favourable numbers cannot replace the shaft reference.

A balanced rotor can load its bearings during a turn

After a rotor is balanced, its spin angular momentum still has to change direction whenever its supporting structure turns. Consider an axisymmetric flywheel rotating rapidly at \(\omega_s\) about unit vector \(e_s\), with polar inertia \(I_s\). If its axis is steered at angular rate \(\Omega\), then \(\dot e_s=\Omega\times e_s\). Isolate the spin part of angular momentum and differentiate in an inertial frame:

\[H_s=I_s\omega_s e_s,\qquad M_s=I_s\dot\omega_s e_s+\Omega\times H_s.\]

The first term spins the rotor up or down. The second turns its axis without changing its spin energy to leading order. For steady orthogonal steering the moment magnitude is \(I_s|\omega_s\Omega|\). Components of \(\Omega\) parallel to the spin axis do not turn the angular momentum. For slow regular precession of a fast rotor this spin-dominated relation also gives \(\Omega\simeq M/H_s\) for a transverse applied moment. Rapid steering acceleration, strong nutation or significant transverse angular momentum requires the full tensor equations.

A horizontally mounted flywheel on a turntable has \(I_s=0.12\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), spins in the positive \(x\) direction at 3000 rpm and is turned about positive \(z\) at 0.40 rad/s. Angular momentum is \(0.12(3000)(2\pi/60)=37.7\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) along \(x\). Since \(e_z\times e_x=e_y\), the bearings must exert 15.1 N m along positive \(y\) on the rotor. A 0.30 m support separation along \(x\) requires a pair of equal opposite vertical bearing forces of 50.3 N to deliver this moment, in addition to weight and ordinary radial loads. Reversing the steering reverses the couple immediately at a given speed. A rigid imbalance force at fixed shaft speed does not reverse just because steering changes direction. That distinction is a practical way to diagnose the origin of a load measured on a moving platform.

Chapter 10 · Energy coordinates and Lagrange’s equation

Derive a coupled translation–rotation equation with a rolling constraint and identify which reaction disappears from the energy balance.

Let geometry remove a reaction

A cylinder rolls along a horizontal rail while a spring attached at its centre pulls it towards equilibrium. The rail friction is essential for rolling, but an ideal contact point is instantaneously at rest and the friction force does no work. One can write separate translational and rotational equations with an unknown friction force. Alternatively choose a coordinate that satisfies rolling from the beginning. The reduced energy then contains the rotational inertia without introducing a contact reaction.

Take the rightward displacement \(x\) of the cylinder centre as independent coordinate and clockwise angle as positive. On the fixed surface the no-slip condition gives \(x=R\theta\), up to a reference offset. A body of total mass \(m\) and centre inertia \(I_G\) has kinetic energy from both translation and rotation. A centre spring with stiffness \(k\) has elastic potential \(kx^2/2\).

\[T=\tfrac12(m+I_G/R^2)\dot x^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12kx^2 .\]

Connect energy to a dynamical equation

The virtual work of a horizontal force \(F\) applied at the centre is \(F\delta x\). In this coordinate its generalised force is simply \(F\). A torque \(M\) acting clockwise would do \(M\delta\theta=(M/R)\delta x\) and would enter as a force \(M/R\). With a centre damper \(c\), the dissipative force is \(-c\dot x\). Lagrange’s equation for this system becomes

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=F-c\dot x,\qquad (m+I_G/R^2)\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F .\]

For coordinates whose inertia depends on position, retain both derivatives involving \(T\). The differentiation of \(\partial T/\partial\dot x\) then generates a term proportional to \(\dot x^2\), part of which cancels \(\partial T/\partial x\). Simply multiplying a configuration-dependent effective mass by acceleration would miss the remaining term.

Predict a frequency and check rolling

A solid cylinder has \(m=8.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(R=0.10\,\mathrm m\), and \(I_G=mR^2/2=0.040\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). The effective mass seen by the spring is \(8+0.040/0.10^2=12.0\,\mathrm{kg}\). With \(k=480\,\mathrm{N/m}\) and negligible damping the natural frequency is \(\sqrt{480/12}/(2\pi)=1.007\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Release it from rest at \(x=+0.060\,\mathrm m\). Its initial acceleration is \(-480(0.060)/12=-2.40\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). Writing only the centre’s translation and ignoring rotation would overpredict the acceleration magnitude.

Reconstruct the previously eliminated contact force from translation. The spring pulls leftwards with 28.8 N and the centre accelerates leftwards with \(m\ddot x=-19.2\,\mathrm N\). Friction must therefore point rightwards with 9.6 N to supply the appropriate angular acceleration. The coefficient of static friction must be at least \(9.6/(8\times9.81)=0.122\) at release. For a smaller coefficient, the assumed rolling constraint fails and the model needs separate rotational and translational coordinates together with a slipping contact law.

Compare two cylinders with identical mass

A thin-walled hoop of the same mass and rolling radius has \(I_G=mR^2\). With the original spring its effective mass doubles the translational mass, giving natural frequency \(\sqrt{480/16}/(2\pi)=0.872\,\mathrm{Hz}\). A no-slip ring-down can therefore distinguish two bodies whose masses and outer dimensions are identical but whose internal mass distributions differ.

Use a cyclic coordinate to reduce coupled motion

Mount a pendulum on a cart that can roll horizontally on a level frictionless guide. Let cart displacement be \(x\), bob angle \(\theta\) from downward vertical, length \(l\), bob mass \(m\) and remaining cart mass \(M\). The bob position is \((x+l\sin\theta,-l\cos\theta)\). Differentiating and squaring its speed gives a cross term \(2l\dot x\dot\theta\cos\theta\) because cart and pendulum velocities need not be perpendicular. The full energy terms are

\[T=\tfrac12(M+m)\dot x^2+ml\cos\theta\,\dot x\dot\theta+\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2,\qquad V=mgl(1-\cos\theta).\]

Horizontal position itself does not occur in either expression. It is a cyclic coordinate, so \(\partial T/\partial\dot x=(M+m)\dot x+ml\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\) is conserved if no external horizontal force acts. This is horizontal momentum written in generalised coordinates. The pendulum equation from Lagrange’s equation is \(l\ddot\theta+\ddot x\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0\). For small swings from rest, differentiate the momentum relation and linearise to obtain \(\ddot x=-ml\ddot\theta/(M+m)\). Substituting gives \([lM/(M+m)]\ddot\theta+g\theta=0\).

Free horizontal motion makes the small-swing frequency larger than for a clamped cart, by \(\sqrt{(M+m)/M}\). This result reflects motion about the system’s horizontally constrained centre of mass. The extra degree of freedom also permits uniform horizontal translation at zero frequency. Its presence does not imply an additional oscillatory mode.

Predict opposing cart travel in a release test

Let \(M=3.0\) kg, \(m=1.0\) kg and \(l=0.60\) m. Clamp the cart: the oscillation period would be \(2\pi\sqrt{0.60/9.81}=1.55\,\mathrm s\). Release its horizontal guide and the period becomes \(2\pi\sqrt{0.60(3)/(9.81\times4)}=1.35\,\mathrm s\). At release the bob is at \(\theta_0=10^\circ\) and both bodies are at rest. Because horizontal momentum remains zero, integrate the exact relation to obtain \((M+m)x+ml\sin\theta=\text{constant}\). As the bob first crosses the vertical, the cart has shifted \(ml\sin10^\circ/(M+m)=0.0260\,\mathrm m\) to the right from its starting point.

At the vertical the potential-energy decrease is \(mgl(1-\cos10^\circ)=0.0894\,\mathrm J\). Substituting \(\dot x=-ml\dot\theta/(M+m)\) into the kinetic energy at this instant gives effective angular inertia \(ml^2M/(M+m)=0.270\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Thus the angular speed magnitude is \(\sqrt{2(0.0894)/0.270}=0.814\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), and the cart speed magnitude is \(0.15(0.814)=0.122\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The frequency estimate used a small-angle approximation; the centre-of-mass and energy results at the vertical used the actual release angle. A horizontal cable pulling on the cart or measurable wheel friction would change the conserved momentum and should be measured before attributing deviations solely to pendulum nonlinearity.

The two-sensor experiment can measure conservation without estimating acceleration. Track the cart position and bob angle from video, then plot \(x+[ml/(M+m)]\sin\theta\). For the chosen masses its value should stay constant at the initial offset despite the cart and pendulum both moving. A steadily sloping line indicates nonzero initial horizontal momentum; a curved departure suggests an external force or tracking bias. This separation is useful because rolling resistance can be weak enough to leave the first measured period nearly unchanged while producing visible drift over several cycles. The conserved combination is specific to the inertial laboratory coordinate, so a camera fixed on the moving cart needs a separate record of cart displacement.

Chapter 11 · Modal participation and absorber tuning

Project external forcing onto vibration modes and determine when a vibration absorber suppresses displacement at a selected frequency.

Recover motion from mode amplitudes

Knowing a structure’s natural frequencies does not yet tell you which frequencies a particular actuator will excite. For a linear undamped system write \(M\ddot x+Kx=f(t)\) and let \(\phi_j\) be a real mode shape solving \(K\phi_j=\omega_j^2M\phi_j\). Assume a complete set of independent modes and expand the displacement \(x=\sum_j\phi_j q_j\). Multiplying the equation by \(\phi_i^T\) uses mass and stiffness orthogonality to remove other modal coordinates.

\[m_i\ddot q_i+k_iq_i=\phi_i^Tf(t),\qquad m_i=\phi_i^TM\phi_i,\quad k_i=\omega_i^2m_i .\]

Multiplying a mode shape by a constant changes the numerical modal mass and generalised force, while the reconstructed physical motion remains invariant. Thus a tabulated mode shape needs its normalisation if it is used to compute a physical response. A forcing pattern whose projection vanishes excites none of that mode from rest, even if the excitation frequency equals its natural frequency.

Select a mode by choosing the actuator pattern

Suppose two identical masses 2 kg each have mode shapes \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\), with frequencies 10 and 20 rad/s. Both modal masses are 4 kg. A 12 N harmonic force on the first mass projects 12 N onto each mode in this normalisation. At a forcing frequency of 5 rad/s the undamped steady modal amplitudes are \(12/[4(10^2-5^2)]=0.0400\,\mathrm m\) and \(12/[4(20^2-5^2)]=0.00800\,\mathrm m\). Recombination gives first and second physical amplitudes 0.0480 and 0.0320 m.

Now distribute the same total force as 6 N on each mass in phase. The first projection stays 12 N and the second becomes \(6-6=0\). The masses now both move 0.0400 m. Relocating or phasing actuators can therefore change observed vibration without changing any mass, spring or natural frequency.

Cause the primary displacement to vanish

Attach an auxiliary mass \(m_a\) by spring \(k_a\) to a primary mass \(m_p\) grounded by spring \(k_p\). A harmonic force of amplitude \(F\) acts on the primary mass. Seek displacement amplitudes \(X_p,X_a\). The absorber equation is \(-k_aX_p+(k_a-m_a\omega^2)X_a=0\). At \(\omega^2=k_a/m_a\) this enforces \(X_p=0\). The primary equation then gives \(X_a=-F/k_a\). The absorber’s spring force balances the excitation while the primary mass is stationary in this ideal steady response.

For \(m_a=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\), a measured forcing frequency of 20 Hz requires \(k_a=m_a(2\pi20)^2=7896\,\mathrm{N/m}\). An 8 N forcing amplitude gives absorber travel amplitude 1.01 mm. The two coupled resonances bracket this tuned antiresonance; startup or a change of shaft speed can pass through them. Damping limits the resonant peaks but generally prevents the exact cancellation obtained from these undamped equations.

Choose where to observe cancellation

At the tuned absorber frequency a sensor on the primary reports zero ideal steady displacement, while a sensor on the attached mass records finite motion. A force transducer between the bodies measures the full applied excitation in the opposing phase. Moving the sensor can therefore turn the same test from an apparent null into a readily measurable response, without altering the underlying eigenfrequencies.

Project a spatially distributed load onto a flexible member

A beam or string has motion distributed along its length. Its modal forcing is determined by a spatial integral, analogous to the vector dot product for discrete coordinates. For a taut uniform string of length \(L\), linear density \(\mu\) and constant tension \(H\), small transverse displacement \(w(x,t)\) obeys \(\mu w_{tt}-H w_{xx}=p(x,t)\). The tension term follows from the difference in vertical tension components on a short segment. Assume both ends fixed in displacement and neglect bending stiffness, damping and the change of tension caused by stretching. The modes are \(\phi_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)\), with frequencies \(\omega_n=(n\pi/L)\sqrt{H/\mu}\).

Expand \(w=\sum\phi_n q_n\), multiply by \(\phi_j\) and integrate over the length. The mass integral is \(m_j=\mu\int_0^L\phi_j^2dx=\mu L/2\). Integration by parts of the tension term gives the modal stiffness \(k_j=m_j\omega_j^2\), with the fixed-end boundary terms vanishing. The modal input becomes \(Q_j=\int_0^Lp\phi_jdx\). Under a uniform harmonic load amplitude \(p_0\),

\[Q_j=\frac{p_0L}{j\pi}[1-(-1)^j].\]

Even modes have zero generalised force because their positive and negative portions cancel exactly. A point load at \(x_0\) instead yields \(Q_j=F\phi_j(x_0)\). Moving an actuator or changing a load footprint can thus suppress a resonance by symmetry without changing the material, tension or natural frequency.

Estimate a cable’s displacement from its first few modes

A laboratory cable spans 2.0 m under 200 N tension and has linear density 0.50 kg/m. Its wave speed is \(20\,\mathrm{m/s}\), so \(f_1=20/(2L)=5.0\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(m_j=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\). Apply a distributed harmonic pressure-equivalent line load \(p_0=0.40\,\mathrm{N/m}\) at 2.0 Hz. For mode one, \(Q_1=2(0.40)(2)/\pi=0.509\,\mathrm N\), and its dynamic stiffness is \(0.50[(2\pi5)^2-(2\pi2)^2]=414.5\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Its modal amplitude is 1.23 mm.

The third mode has \(Q_3=0.170\,\mathrm N\), \(f_3=15\) Hz and amplitude \(0.170/\{0.50[(2\pi15)^2-(2\pi2)^2]\}=0.0390\,\mathrm{mm}\). At the midpoint \(\phi_1=+1\) while \(\phi_3=-1\), so the two-term prediction is approximately 1.19 mm. The next odd mode contributes with the opposite sign again, but is far stiffer at this drive frequency. In the static limit an independent check follows from the parabolic string solution \(w(L/2)=p_0L^2/(8H)=1.00\,\mathrm{mm}\). If a strong measured response appears at the symmetric load’s even-mode resonance, investigate load asymmetry, asymmetric support stiffness or a disturbed initial condition before assigning a nonzero ideal modal projection.

The spatial convergence pattern can be estimated before adding many terms. For odd mode number \(n\) well above the forcing frequency, the uniform-load projection decreases as \(1/n\) while modal stiffness increases as \(n^2\). Each displacement contribution therefore decreases roughly as \(1/n^3\). A point actuator at midspan instead gives modal projections of essentially alternating constant magnitude for odd modes, producing only a \(1/n^2\) displacement decay. Hence a truncation that accurately predicts the response to a distributed flow load can be less accurate for an impulsive local tap. Strain or slope predictions may require still more terms because a spatial derivative introduces an additional factor of mode number. Choose the retained modes for the actual load and observed quantity.

The uniform cable problem admits an independent harmonic calculation without modal truncation. Set \(w(x,t)=W(x)\cos\Omega t\). The governing equation gives \(W''+\kappa^2 W=-p_0/H\), \(\kappa=\Omega\sqrt{\mu/H}\). Symmetry about midspan and fixed ends select \(W(x)=[p_0/(\mu\Omega^2)]\{\cos[\kappa(x-L/2)]/\cos(\kappa L/2)-1\}\), provided the denominator is nonzero. For the test above \(\kappa L/2=0.6283\) and \(W(L/2)=0.40[1/\cos(0.6283)-1]/[0.50(2\pi2)^2]=1.196\,\mathrm{mm}\). This direct boundary-value result confirms both the signs and the scale of the first two odd-modal contributions.

As the excitation approaches the first natural frequency, \(\kappa L/2\) approaches \(\pi/2\) and the undamped steady expression becomes singular. An actual time record may instead show progressively growing amplitude before damping or geometric effects intervene. At very low frequency expand \(\sec q\simeq1+q^2/2\); substituting \(q=\Omega L\sqrt{\mu/H}/2\) recovers the static midpoint displacement without subtracting two nearly equal numerical values. The modal and spatial calculations thus furnish independent checks in the static, off-resonant and resonant limits.

Chapter 12 · Finite-amplitude oscillations and model validity

Use conservation of energy and a frequency approximation to quantify when a small-angle pendulum model loses timing accuracy.

Retain the actual restoring moment

A pendulum gives a practical test of a linear model because its length and release angle are easy to set while its period can be measured over many cycles. Approximate the moving body first by a point mass \(m\) at distance \(l\) from a frictionless pivot. With \(\theta\) measured from vertically downwards, gravity produces torque \(-mgl\sin\theta\). Dividing the rotational balance by \(ml^2\) leaves \(\ddot\theta+(g/l)\sin\theta=0\). The linear small-angle frequency \(\omega_0=\sqrt{g/l}\) follows only after replacing \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) in radians.

The leading correction has a definite sign: \(\sin\theta\simeq\theta-\theta^3/6\). At a large positive angle the restoring force magnitude is smaller than the linear extrapolation. The pendulum should take longer to complete its swing. A sign test like this is useful before calculating a period correction.

Derive an integral valid at finite amplitude

For release from rest at amplitude \(A<\pi\), conservation of mechanical energy gives \(\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl(1-\cos\theta)=mgl(1-\cos A)\). During one quarter of the orbit the angle travels between zero and \(A\). Substitute \(\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(A/2)\sin\psi\) into the time integral. Cancelling common square roots gives

\[T=4\sqrt{l/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\psi}},\qquad k=\sin(A/2).\]

For modest amplitudes expand the integrand as \(1+\tfrac12 k^2\sin^2\psi+\cdots\). The average of \(\sin^2\psi\) over the quarter interval is one half. Therefore \(T/T_0\simeq1+k^2/4\simeq1+A^2/16\) through second order in amplitude. The oscillation period depends on energy, while in the linear spring model all amplitudes share the same period.

Estimate a measurable timing error

Take \(l=0.80\,\mathrm m\), \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) and release at \(A=30^\circ=0.5236\,\mathrm{rad}\). The small-angle period is \(T_0=2\pi\sqrt{0.80/9.81}=1.794\,\mathrm s\). The leading correction is 1.71%, giving about 1.825 s. Over ten full oscillations the predicted delay relative to the small-angle model is roughly 0.31 s. Reducing the release angle to 15° reduces the leading relative correction by a factor of four.

A timing series from a damped experimental pendulum may show the period drifting towards \(T_0\) as its amplitude decays. Measuring only the first release angle while averaging many later periods mixes different amplitudes. To test the finite-amplitude law, use consecutive same-direction crossings to measure each local period and estimate its associated amplitude from neighbouring turning points. The correction above is an expansion around zero amplitude. As \(A\) approaches the inverted equilibrium, the exact integral grows without bound and a low-order polynomial is no longer an adequate approximation.

Check the scale of the neglected term

Retaining one further term in the integrand gives \(1+\tfrac12 k^2\sin^2\psi+\tfrac38 k^4\sin^4\psi\). The quarter-interval average of the fourth power of sine is \(3/8\), so \(T/T_0\simeq1+k^2/4+9k^4/64\). At 30° this gives 1.01738, while the leading expansion in amplitude gave 1.01713. The small difference is measurable only if the uncertainty in pendulum length, release angle and crossing times supports the comparison.

Derive an amplitude-dependent resonance relation

A pendulum softens as its amplitude grows because the sine restoring law rises more slowly than its linear tangent. A mechanical flexure can instead harden through geometry. A simple modal model is \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx+\beta x^3=F\cos\omega t\). For positive \(\beta\) the restoring stiffness increases with excursion. Assume one dominant harmonic and take \(x\approx A\cos(\omega t-\phi)\). Expanding the cubic gives \(x^3=A^3[3\cos(\omega t-\phi)+\cos(3\omega t-3\phi)]/4\). Keeping only the fundamental turns the cubic term into an amplitude-dependent in-phase force.

Balance in-phase and quadrature components against the input, square and add to eliminate \(\phi\). The first-harmonic amplitude estimate satisfies

\[F^2=A^2\left[\left(k+\tfrac34\beta A^2-m\omega^2\right)^2+(c\omega)^2\right].\]

In a lightly damped free motion the same approximation gives a backbone frequency \(\omega_b^2\approx[k+(3/4)\beta A^2]/m\). This is an amplitude-dependent estimate of conservative oscillation frequency, not a new eigenvalue of the linearised equilibrium. At very small amplitude it recovers \(\sqrt{k/m}\), while a negative cubic coefficient tilts the relation towards lower frequencies. If the cubic approximation is only a local expansion, do not extrapolate it until it predicts an unphysical loss of global stiffness.

Identify a hardening spring from two release amplitudes

A test component has moving mass 1.0 kg and small-amplitude stiffness 400 N/m. Low-level ring-down gives \(\omega_0=20\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). At measured oscillation amplitude 20 mm the dominant free frequency is approximately 22 rad/s. Under the weakly nonlinear one-harmonic model, \(\beta\simeq4m(22^2-20^2)/[3(0.020)^2]=2.80\times10^5\,\mathrm{N/m^3}\). At 10 mm the inferred backbone is \(\sqrt{400+0.75(2.8\times10^5)(0.010)^2}=20.52\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), a direct third reading against which to test the fit.

Now drive near 22 rad/s with viscous coefficient \(c=0.80\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). At the 20 mm backbone amplitude the spring and inertia fundamentals cancel, so maintaining this motion takes forcing \(F=c\omega A=0.352\,\mathrm N\) in quadrature with displacement. The neglected cubic third-harmonic force amplitude is \(\beta A^3/4=0.56\,\mathrm N\); its displacement effect can nevertheless be small because the oscillator is driven far from its fundamental resonance at 66 rad/s. A separate mode near that frequency would invalidate this simplification. In a forced frequency sweep the amplitude equation can admit several positive roots near resonance. Its algebra does not establish their stability; slow upward and downward sweeps, each with sufficient settling time, can reveal different stable responses and jump transitions.

An initial static measurement tests the sign of the fitted coefficient independently. At a displacement of 20 mm the linear restoring force is \(400(0.020)=8.0\,\mathrm N\); the cubic contribution is \(2.8\times10^5(0.020)^3=2.24\,\mathrm N\). The predicted static force is therefore 10.24 N, and the tangent stiffness is \(400+3(2.8\times10^5)(0.020)^2=736\,\mathrm{N/m}\). This tangent stiffness applies to small vibrations around a held biased equilibrium. Using it directly for an oscillation centred at zero would overestimate the measured amplitude-dependent frequency because the instantaneous stiffness changes during each cycle. Static bias and free oscillation amplitude are physically different tests of the same nonlinear restoring law.

Determine whether a force excites a mode and relate an oscillator’s amplitude to the accuracy of a period measurement.

Excite an antisymmetric mode

Two masses of 1.5 kg have symmetric and antisymmetric mode shapes (1,1) and (1,−1) at natural frequencies 12 and 18 rad/s. Apply harmonic forces (+6,−6) N at 6 rad/s. Find both modal amplitudes and the physical displacement amplitudes with their relative phase.

Reveal the worked solution

The physical amplitudes are +13.89 and −13.89 mm in a common phase convention. The cancellation of the symmetric force projection determines the response symmetry.

Set a working stroke

An auxiliary mass moves against a spring attached to a primary machine. An undamped absorber must cancel an 18 N harmonic force at 25 Hz while its own amplitude stays at or below 1.5 mm. Find minimum spring stiffness and corresponding tuned mass. Does tuning ensure a small response at all speeds?

Reveal the worked solution

At exact cancellation \(X_p=0\) and \(|X_a|=F/k_a\). Thus \(k_a\ge18/0.0015=12000\,\mathrm{N/m}\). At the limiting stiffness the tuned mass is \(12000/(2\pi25)^2=0.486\,\mathrm{kg}\). The coupled modes give resonances away from the cancellation frequency. A varying-speed drive can cross these frequencies.

Estimate a release-angle limit

A pendulum experiment tolerates an amplitude-dependent period change of 0.5%. Use the leading finite-amplitude correction to estimate its largest release angle. For a 1.0 m pendulum released at half that angle, estimate the accumulated timing shift over 40 cycles.

Specify an isolation mount from two measurements

A 40.0 kg instrument is mounted on springs. Free-vibration peaks of the same sign are 8.0 and 4.0 mm four periods apart. The four periods take 1.60 s. Estimate the equivalent stiffness and damping. A bench beneath the instrument moves sinusoidally at 10 Hz with 0.50 mm amplitude. Predict absolute instrument motion and relative mount travel.

Worked solution

The logarithmic decrement per period is ln(2)/4 = 0.1733 and the damping ratio is δ/√(4π²+δ²) = 0.0276. The damped period is 0.40 s, giving ωd = 15.71 rad/s. The undamped frequency ωn = ωd/√(1−ζ²) is 15.71 rad/s to the displayed precision. The equivalent k = mωn² ≈ 9.88 kN/m and c = 2ζmωn ≈ 34.7 N s/m.

At r = ω/ωn ≈ 4.00 the absolute-motion transmissibility is √[1+(2ζr)²]/√[(1−r²)²+(2ζr)²] ≈ 0.0683. The instrument thus moves about 0.034 mm. The base-relative displacement amplitude instead uses r² in the numerator and is about 1.067 times the input, or 0.533 mm.

These two answers size different things. The instrument sees the small absolute vibration while the spring must accommodate roughly the bench excursion. Near resonance the measured low damping would permit much larger travel; a mount chosen only from the 10 Hz test could bottom during startup. Applied study

Can a motor support resonate at running speed?

A compact motor sits on the horizontal arm of a steel L-frame. After a disturbance it can move horizontally, bounce vertically and rock. A spring scale measures how far the mount yields under static load. To predict the frequencies of those three motions, we also need to know where the mass sits.

Start with one undamped coordinate \(q\), measured from equilibrium. A spring with compliance \(c\) moves \(cP\) under force \(P\), so its restoring force is \(-q/c\). Newton’s law gives \(m\ddot q+q/c=0\) and \(f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{mc})\). Gravity sets the static equilibrium. For small vibrations about it, constant gravity drops out of the linear equation.

Find the frame’s vertical compliance

The frame has a clamped column of height \(H\) and a horizontal arm of length \(L\), rigidly joined. Both have area \(A\), bending second moment \(I\) and modulus \(E\). Under a vertical tip force \(P\), the arm bends with moment \(P(L-x)\). The column carries axial force \(P\) and constant bending moment \(PL\). Neglect shear deformation and store the bending and axial energies. A slowly applied load performs work \(P^2c_{zz}/2\), so

\[c_{zz}=\int_0^L\frac{(L-x)^2}{EI}\,dx+\int_0^H\frac{L^2}{EI}\,dz+\int_0^H\frac{1}{EA}\,dz=\frac{L^3/3+L^2H}{EI}+\frac{H}{EA}.\]

Take \(H=2.0\) m, \(L=1.5\) m, \(A=0.0050\,\mathrm{m^2}\), \(I=8.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) and \(E=210\) GPa. This gives \(c_{zz}=3.35\times10^{-6}\) m/N. A 1 kN vertical force displaces the tip by 3.35 mm. The column-bending term alone contributes 2.68 mm. Calling the arm a cantilever with a perfectly fixed root would miss the dominant compliance.

Translation and rocking share the restoring energy

If only vertical inertia were retained, a 200 kg motor would give \(f=6.15\) Hz. In fact the column sways and the arm rotates when the motor moves vertically. In a light-frame approximation collect the tip coordinates in \(\mathbf q=(u_x,u_z,\theta)\), measured at the machine’s centre of mass. Apply a horizontal force, a vertical force and an end moment separately to obtain the three columns of the flexibility matrix \(\mathbf C\). Its off-diagonal entries measure the movement in one coordinate caused by a load in another. For linear elastic members reciprocity makes this matrix symmetric.

The stiffness matrix is \(\mathbf K=\mathbf C^{-1}\). With motor mass \(m\) and rotary inertia \(J\), let \(\mathbf M=\mathrm{diag}(m,m,J)\). Substituting harmonic motion into \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf K\mathbf q=0\) gives \(\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi=\omega^2\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi\). A mode shape \(\boldsymbol\phi\) describes a ratio of motions. The frequency \(f=\omega/(2\pi)\) is independent of the scale used to draw that shape.

For \(m=200\) kg and \(J=60\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), the three light-frame frequencies are 5.048, 15.449 and 44.645 Hz. If the steel frame’s distributed mass is included they fall to 4.701, 13.550 and 42.41 Hz. The first light-frame mode corresponds to about 303 rpm for an excitation that repeats once per revolution. Whether it produces a large vibration also depends on damping and on the projection of the applied force onto that mode. Explore how inertia affects the rotation of a rigid body.

Transfer problem · Isolate a machine from floor vibration

A 50 kg instrument on a spring and viscous damper sits on a floor moving as \(y(t)\). The mount extends by \(x-y\), where \(x\) is the instrument’s absolute displacement. Force balance gives \(m\ddot{x}+c(\dot{x}-\dot{y})+k(x-y)=0\). For sinusoidal floor motion the ratio of instrument to floor amplitude follows by replacing each derivative by \(i\omega\):

\[\frac{|X|}{|Y|}=\sqrt{\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}},\quad r=\omega/\omega_n,\quad \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\quad \zeta=\frac{c}{2m\omega_n}.\]

Choose \(k=20\,000\) N/m and \(c=200\) N·s/m. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and \(\zeta=0.10\). At 40 rad/s, \(r=2\) and the ratio is \(\sqrt{1.16/9.16}=0.356\). A 1.0 mm floor oscillation gives 0.356 mm of instrument motion. At 20 rad/s the same mount amplifies motion by \(\sqrt{1.04/0.04}=5.10\). A mount selected for stable operation at one speed can amplify vibration as a machine runs up through resonance.
