    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Choose a coordinate and compute the kinetic and elastic energy it carries. Free and driven vibration follow from inertia, stiffness and damping. Coupled coordinates permit several natural frequencies and mode shapes.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Distinguish base motion from a directly applied force and a transient from a steady response. Rigid-body kinematics and impulse balances tell you how an initial motion is created before vibration begins.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      An unbalanced rotor provides a periodic load. Lagrange’s equations assemble a multibody model, modal forces identify which vibrations a load excites, and nonlinear stiffness can move the resonances with amplitude.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. How a longitudinal pulse reflects from a change in rod section

      A tap on one end of a rod reaches a receiver after a finite delay. Two rods joined end to end can even return a pulse before the receiver sees a far-end reflection. Derive propagation speed and distinguish the displacement amplitude from the energy carried by a pulse.

    2. Why the frequencies of a bending beam are not equally spaced

      A distributed beam can bend into infinitely many shapes. Derive the relation between wavelength and oscillation frequency, then compare the measured response of a beam with the mode family predicted by its supports.

    3. Estimating a cantilever frequency from energy

      A clamped beam carries a small sensor at its tip. Its precise mode shape is unknown. Use the static deflection shape to approximate the fundamental frequency and see why concentrating a small mass at the tip has a large effect.

    4. Why a radial slide presses sideways on a rotating guide

      A low-friction carriage moves along a radial slot in a horizontal turntable. It can move at constant speed relative to the guide and still require a changing force. Keeping track of the moving basis exposes both acceleration components.

    5. Steady gyroscopic precession and its two possible rates

      A spinning axisymmetric rotor supported at one point can keep a constant tilt under gravity. Predict the rate at which its axis turns, examine the fast-spin approximation and determine when slow steady precession is possible.

    6. Why Coulomb damping loses a fixed amplitude each half-cycle

      A sliding carriage on a spring can stop away from zero displacement. Determine each turning point without time stepping, then identify the experimentally observable difference between Coulomb and viscous damping.

    7. When the third harmonic matters more than the fundamental

      A machine applies a short force once per cycle. Its repetition rate looks safely below a structural resonance, but one of its harmonics can lie close to that resonance. Build the forcing spectrum from a pulse and apply a complex response coefficient to each term.

    8. What a passenger and a tyre feel on the same road

      Two vehicles encounter the same nearly sinusoidal series of road undulations. In one vehicle the occupant feels little vibration, yet its contact force varies substantially. The other transmits a more noticeable body acceleration. Build a two-degree-of-freedom model from force balances, calculate the two responses to a prescribed road geometry, and test which changes in suspension damping improve one measurement at the expense of another.

    Applied study

    Move from a single oscillator to the natural frequencies and mode shapes of a machine on a flexible frame.

    Can a motor support resonate at running speed?

    A compact motor sits on the horizontal arm of a steel L-frame. After a disturbance it can move horizontally, bounce vertically and rock. A spring scale measures how far the mount yields under static load. To predict the frequencies of those three motions, we also need to know where the mass sits.

    Start with one undamped coordinate \(q\), measured from equilibrium. A spring with compliance \(c\) moves \(cP\) under force \(P\), so its restoring force is \(-q/c\). Newton’s law gives \(m\ddot q+q/c=0\) and \(f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{mc})\). Gravity sets the static equilibrium. For small vibrations about it, constant gravity drops out of the linear equation.

    Find the frame’s vertical compliance

    The frame has a clamped column of height \(H\) and a horizontal arm of length \(L\), rigidly joined. Both have area \(A\), bending second moment \(I\) and modulus \(E\). Under a vertical tip force \(P\), the arm bends with moment \(P(L-x)\). The column carries axial force \(P\) and constant bending moment \(PL\). Neglect shear deformation and store the bending and axial energies. A slowly applied load performs work \(P^2c_{zz}/2\), so

    \[c_{zz}=\int_0^L\frac{(L-x)^2}{EI}\,dx+\int_0^H\frac{L^2}{EI}\,dz+\int_0^H\frac{1}{EA}\,dz=\frac{L^3/3+L^2H}{EI}+\frac{H}{EA}.\]

    Take \(H=2.0\) m, \(L=1.5\) m, \(A=0.0050\,\mathrm{m^2}\), \(I=8.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) and \(E=210\) GPa. This gives \(c_{zz}=3.35\times10^{-6}\) m/N. A 1 kN vertical force displaces the tip by 3.35 mm. The column-bending term alone contributes 2.68 mm. Calling the arm a cantilever with a perfectly fixed root would miss the dominant compliance.

    Translation and rocking share the restoring energy

    If only vertical inertia were retained, a 200 kg motor would give \(f=6.15\) Hz. In fact the column sways and the arm rotates when the motor moves vertically. In a light-frame approximation collect the tip coordinates in \(\mathbf q=(u_x,u_z,\theta)\), measured at the machine’s centre of mass. Apply a horizontal force, a vertical force and an end moment separately to obtain the three columns of the flexibility matrix \(\mathbf C\). Its off-diagonal entries measure the movement in one coordinate caused by a load in another. For linear elastic members reciprocity makes this matrix symmetric.

    The stiffness matrix is \(\mathbf K=\mathbf C^{-1}\). With motor mass \(m\) and rotary inertia \(J\), let \(\mathbf M=\mathrm{diag}(m,m,J)\). Substituting harmonic motion into \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf K\mathbf q=0\) gives

    \[\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi=\omega^2\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi.\]

    A mode shape \(\boldsymbol\phi\) describes a ratio of motions. The frequency \(f=\omega/(2\pi)\) is independent of the scale used to draw that shape.

    For \(m=200\) kg and \(J=60\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), the three light-frame frequencies are 5.048, 15.449 and 44.645 Hz. If the steel frame’s distributed mass is included they fall to 4.701, 13.550 and 42.41 Hz. The first light-frame mode corresponds to about 303 rpm for an excitation that repeats once per revolution. Whether it produces a large vibration also depends on damping and on the projection of the applied force onto that mode. Explore how inertia affects the rotation of a rigid body.

    Transfer problem · Isolate a machine from floor vibration

    A 50 kg instrument on a spring and viscous damper sits on a floor moving as \(y(t)\). The mount extends by \(x-y\), where \(x\) is the instrument’s absolute displacement. Force balance gives

    \[m\ddot{x}+c(\dot{x}-\dot{y})+k(x-y)=0.\]

    For sinusoidal floor motion the ratio of instrument to floor amplitude follows by replacing each derivative by \(i\omega\):

    \[\frac{|X|}{|Y|}=\sqrt{\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}},\]
    \[r=\omega/\omega_n,\]
    \[\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\]
    \[\zeta=\frac{c}{2m\omega_n}.\]

    Choose \(k=20\,000\) N/m and \(c=200\) N·s/m. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and \(\zeta=0.10\). At 40 rad/s, \(r=2\) and the ratio is

    \[\sqrt{1.16/9.16}=0.356.\]

    A 1.0 mm floor oscillation gives 0.356 mm of instrument motion. At 20 rad/s the same mount amplifies motion by \(\sqrt{1.04/0.04}=5.10\). A mount selected for stable operation at one speed can amplify vibration as a machine runs up through resonance.

    Exercises

    1. Infer a frequency from measured sag

    A light support with one vertical coordinate deflects 4.0 mm under a machine’s weight. Infer its natural frequency directly from the sag. Compare it with the forcing frequency of a once-per-revolution imbalance at 450 rpm.

    Show the estimate

    From \(mg=k\delta\), \(k/m=g/\delta\). Hence

    \[f=(2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{9.81/0.0040}=7.88\,\mathrm{Hz}.\]

    The imbalance frequency is

    \[450/60=7.50\,\mathrm{Hz}.\]

    The forcing is about 5% below the estimated frequency. Estimating a response amplitude would additionally require the forcing magnitude and damping.

    2. Add a geometrically identical payload

    For the light L-frame double both \(m\) and \(J\) while holding the support stiffness fixed. Predict the change in all three frequencies and the first critical speed. Does the same factor apply to a frame whose own mass stays fixed?

    Show the modal scaling

    The entire tip mass matrix doubles. The same eigenvectors then solve the equation with each \(\omega^2\) halved. The three frequencies become 3.570, 10.924 and 31.569 Hz. The first once-per-revolution critical speed is about 214 rpm. With distributed frame mass only part of the assembled mass matrix doubles, so there is no common frequency scaling factor.
