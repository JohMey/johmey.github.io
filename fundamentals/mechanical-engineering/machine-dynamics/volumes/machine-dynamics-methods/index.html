Vibration and identification in machinery

Connect modes, moving components and measured frequency responses. Investigate how forcing, damping and the observation point change an inferred dynamic model.

    Contents

    1. Critical speed, synchronous whirl and two different rotor centres

      A stationary probe observes motion of a rotating shaft surface, while a balance calculation follows an eccentric mass centre. Derive both trajectories from one flexible-rotor model. Their difference explains the apparent recentring of a rotor at high speed and why a single vibration amplitude cannot identify unbalance and damping.

    2. Parametric resonance under periodic stiffness modulation

      A carriage supported by a spring whose stiffness changes periodically can gain oscillation energy with no additive force at its natural frequency. Compare this mechanism with an ordinary forced resonance, estimate the first instability band and learn how to measure growth before nonlinear saturation invalidates the prediction.

    3. Random base motion, spectral density and rms response

      A support vibration is described by a power spectral density rather than a single sine-wave amplitude. Derive the relative-motion filter for a mounted instrument, calculate mean-square motion from a measured spectrum and distinguish a stationary rms prediction from a peak displacement guarantee.

    4. Why a local damper couples otherwise separate modes

      Undamped mode shapes diagonalise a symmetric mass and stiffness model. Adding a damper at one attachment does not generally leave modal equations separate. Derive the resulting coupling, test its magnitude and learn when a diagonal modal-damping approximation can hide an observable phase error.

    5. Which damping law produced the free-decay trace?

      A carriage slides on a guide and a spring pulls it back to its resting position. Two dampers can be adjusted to give the same first peak reduction. Predict the following turning points and decide which measurement separates viscous loss from dry sliding friction.

    6. From beam curvature to a mass matrix and a frequency measurement

      A coarse cantilever model often predicts its first resonance accurately while putting the third far too high. Derive both matrices from the same displacement field and inspect how each mode converges.

    7. Which approximation shifted the resonance?

      A simulation of a pulsed cantilever contains three separate approximations. Measure the effect of spatial interpolation, retaining finitely many modes and representing a changing force on a time grid.
