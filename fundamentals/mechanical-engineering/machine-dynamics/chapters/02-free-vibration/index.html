From first principles

Determine the frequency and decay of a released mount

Displace a mass \(m\) from its static equilibrium on a linear spring of stiffness \(k\) and release it. A viscous damper exerts force opposite to velocity with coefficient \(c\). Measuring displacement \(x\) relative to static equilibrium removes any constant force already balanced by the spring. Newton's second law gives the free-motion equation.

\[m\ddot x=-kx-c\dot x .\]

An exponential trial \(e^{st}\) turns differentiation into multiplication by \(s\), so nonzero motion requires a root of the characteristic polynomial. Its two roots depend on the undamped natural frequency \(\omega_n\) and damping ratio \(\zeta\).

\[ms^2+cs+k=0,\]
\[\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\]
\[\zeta=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}.\]

For \(0<\zeta<1\) the roots are a conjugate pair. Their oscillatory contributions combine to give a real damped sinusoid with envelope \(e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\).

\[s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm i\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}.\]

The initial position and velocity determine amplitude and phase, while the parameters set decay and period. Same-sign peaks one period apart therefore measure the exponential envelope without requiring an accurately known release time.

Derivation and applications

Infer damping from the loss of mechanical energy per cycle

A lightly damped mount sheds a measurable fraction of stored energy on each oscillation. Consider a single translating mass \(m\) with linear stiffness \(k\), viscous damping \(c\), and no forcing after release. Measure displacement from static equilibrium. Multiply the equation of motion by velocity to convert inertia and spring terms into total time derivatives.

\[m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0,\]
\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac12kx^2\right)=-c\dot x^2 .\]

For weak damping, amplitude \(A\) changes little during one period and \(x\simeq A\cos\omega_nt\), with \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\). Integrating viscous power over the cycle then gives the leading loss. Divide by the stored maximum spring energy evaluated at the same amplitude.

\[\Delta E_{\rm loss}\simeq c A^2\omega_n^2\frac{\pi}{\omega_n}=\pi c\omega_nA^2,\]
\[\frac{\Delta E_{\rm loss}}{E}\simeq4\pi\zeta,\]
\[\zeta=\frac{c}{2m\omega_n}.\]

For appreciable damping use two same-sign turning points directly. At each peak the kinetic energy vanishes, so the exact energy ratio equals the square of the amplitude ratio. The measured logarithmic decrement relates to viscous damping for an underdamped oscillator.

\[\frac{E_{j+1}}{E_j}=\left(\frac{A_{j+1}}{A_j}\right)^2=e^{-2\delta},\]
\[\zeta=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}},\]
\[\delta=\ln\frac{A_j}{A_{j+1}} .\]

The energy fraction is independent of starting amplitude for linear viscous damping. Amplitude dependence in repeated runs is therefore a direct reason to examine extra friction or nonlinear damping.

Worked example

Determine heat release from two peak readings

For \(k=800\) N/m, consecutive positive peaks are 10.0 and 9.00 mm. The spring energies are 40.0 mJ and 32.4 mJ, so 7.60 mJ is dissipated. The decrement is \(\ln(10/9)=0.1054\), giving \(\zeta=0.0168\). If the first amplitude were doubled and the same linear damper retained, the energy lost in that cycle would quadruple.

Second application

Test a damper from its measured cycle work

A separate mount has \(k=2000\) N/m, \(\omega_n=50.0\) rad/s and small oscillations of amplitude 2.00 mm. A damper dynamometer measures 0.250 mJ of work per cycle. The leading estimate gives \(c=\Delta E/(\pi\omega_nA^2)=0.398\) N s/m. From \(m=k/\omega_n^2=0.800\) kg, \(\zeta=0.00497\). The estimated loss fraction \(4\pi\zeta=0.0625\) supports the nearly constant-amplitude approximation for a single cycle.

When does a released mass cross equilibrium?

Release a 1 kg mass from rest 10 mm to the right of equilibrium on a spring of stiffness 4 N/m. A viscous damper exerts a force opposite to the velocity. In a driven machine this free response is the transient left by the initial displacement. Here the external drive is zero, so the transient can be measured on its own.

Predict a crossing. With displacement measured from static equilibrium, the weight has already been balanced. Newton’s second law gives one restoring term for displacement and one for velocity. Write the trial motion as an exponential. Dividing the resulting equation by the nonzero exponential leaves a quadratic:

\[m\ddot x=-kx-c\dot x,\qquad x=Ae^{st}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^2+2\zeta\omega_n s+\omega_n^2=0,\qquad \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\quad\zeta=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{mk}}.\]

Here \(\omega_n=2\) rad/s. At \(\zeta=0.2\) the roots are \(-0.4\pm1.960i\) s⁻¹. The imaginary part permits a zero crossing. At critical damping the double root is \(-2\) s⁻¹. Applying the initial conditions to the two independent solutions gives \(x=10(1+2t)e^{-2t}\) mm. At \(\zeta=2\) the slowest root is about \(-0.536\) s⁻¹. Which of the last two releases is closer to equilibrium at three seconds?

Problem sheet 2 · Estimate how a frame mode distributes its kinetic energy

Read the model setup and preparatory problems

Displace a machine from its static position

A pump on a spring mount settles under its weight. Pull it slightly farther down and release it. The weight still acts, but the extra motion is driven by the change in spring force. Define \(x\) as displacement from the loaded equilibrium, take constant local spring stiffness \(k\), and represent dissipation by a force opposing velocity of magnitude \(c|\dot x|\). Newton’s law becomes \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0\). This model describes one isolated mode over an amplitude range where inertia, stiffness and damping are approximately constant.

Multiply the equation by \(\dot x\). The mass term differentiates kinetic energy, and the spring term differentiates stored elastic energy. Consequently \(d(m\dot x^2/2+kx^2/2)/dt=-c\dot x^2\). Energy cannot grow for positive damping. Displacement can increase temporarily as kinetic energy converts back into spring energy, so the instantaneous displacement alone is not an energy meter.

Obtain the decay and oscillation rates

Trying \(x=e^{st}\) gives \(ms^2+cs+k=0\). Define \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n)\). The roots are \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). For \(0<\zeta<1\) their imaginary part gives the damped angular frequency \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). The real part gives a decaying exponential. A general underdamped trace is

\[x(t)=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\left[A\cos(\omega_dt)+B\sin(\omega_dt)\right].\]

At \(\zeta=1\) the repeated root gives \((A+Bt)e^{-\omega_nt}\). Above critical damping both roots are negative and real. A lack of visible oscillation therefore does not by itself determine the stiffness. For an underdamped trace, use two peaks of the same sign: their separation is \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_d\), and their amplitude ratio depends only on the envelope decay.

Infer parameters from four cycles

A 20.0 kg mounted component rings down from a positive peak of 8.0 mm to a positive peak of 2.0 mm four cycles later, after 1.60 s. The period is \(T_d=0.400\) s and \(\omega_d=15.71\) rad/s. The log decrement per cycle is \(\delta=\ln(8/2)/4=0.3466\). Since \(x_j/x_{j+1}=\exp(\zeta\omega_n T_d)\), the envelope decay rate is \(\alpha=\delta/T_d=0.8664\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Now \(\omega_n=\sqrt{\omega_d^2+\alpha^2}=15.73\) rad/s and \(\zeta=\alpha/\omega_n=0.0551\). The inferred stiffness is \(m\omega_n^2=4.95\) kN/m, while \(c=2m\alpha=34.7\) N·s/m. After eight cycles the prediction is 0.50 mm. Using peaks separated by several cycles reduces sensitivity to noise in any one amplitude or timestamp.

Challenge the damping assumption

Subtract the equilibrium sensor reading before taking amplitude ratios. A fixed offset changes the ratio of small late peaks more strongly than large early peaks. Plot \(\ln |x_j|\) against time for peaks whose signal clearly exceeds the noise floor. A straight trend supports the viscous envelope. Approximately constant loss of amplitude per cycle suggests a different mechanism such as dry friction. Two nearby natural frequencies may produce a beat envelope that grows and shrinks even though total energy decays.

A second experiment can separate stiffness from added inertia. If a small known mass is bolted to the same moving assembly and the support stiffness is unchanged, \(1/\omega_n^2\) should increase in proportion to effective mass. This check assumes that the added mass participates fully in the chosen coordinate. A flexible appendage that moves out of phase changes the system being identified.

Correct a biased displacement signal

Suppose a displacement sensor reads 0.50 mm high relative to the true equilibrium. The first and fifth positive peaks then appear as 8.50 and 2.50 mm. Their raw log ratio over four cycles gives \(0.25\ln(8.5/2.5)=0.306\), underestimating the true 0.347 decrement. The error grows as the decaying signal approaches the offset. One remedy is to measure equilibrium independently when excitation has stopped. Another is to use the difference between consecutive positive and negative extrema and compare such differences one whole period apart. The constant offset cancels while, for a single underdamped mode, successive peak-to-trough ranges retain the same exponential ratio. Late peaks below the sensor resolution still carry little reliable damping information.

Derive the repeated-root response and its crossing condition

The oscillatory ring-down described above assumes \(\zeta<1\). For a return mechanism holding a fragile component, the question can instead be whether it reaches equilibrium without crossing it. At critical damping, \(c=2\sqrt{mk}\), the characteristic equation is \((s+\omega_n)^2=0\). The repeated root gives two independent free solutions \(e^{-\omega_nt}\) and \(te^{-\omega_nt}\). Imposing \(x(0)=x_0,\dot x(0)=v_0\) yields

\[x(t)=[x_0+(v_0+\omega_n x_0)t]e^{-\omega_nt}.\]

For release from rest at positive displacement, the bracket stays positive and \(\dot x=-\omega_n^2 x_0t e^{-\omega_nt}\) stays negative for \(t>0\). If a mechanism reaches the same \(x_0\) with negative incoming velocity, the bracket can cross zero once even at critical damping. In particular \(v_0<-\omega_n x_0\) gives crossing time \(-x_0/(v_0+\omega_n x_0)\). Critical damping rules out repeated free oscillations, but the initial velocity still determines whether the target is overshot.

For overdamping the distinct real decay rates are \(\omega_n[\zeta\pm\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}]\). Their product is \(\omega_n^2\). Increasing viscous resistance makes the fast root decay more rapidly but the slow root approach zero. Simply adding damping can consequently prolong the final approach to a sensing threshold.

Select a damper for a spring-return slide

A slide of mass 3.0 kg has a return spring of 300 N/m, so \(\omega_n=10\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(c_{\rm cr}=60\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). Released from rest at \(x_0=50\) mm with critical damping, its normalised position is \((1+10t)e^{-10t}\). To enter a 5% band, solve \((1+s)e^{-s}=0.05\) with \(s=10t\). Testing 4.7 and 4.8 gives 0.0518 and 0.0477; the crossing is about 4.74, or \(t=0.474\) s.

The same slide at the same 50 mm location might arrive with \(v_0=-0.80\,\mathrm{m/s}\) after an actuator disconnects. The bracket is now \(0.050-0.30t\), which crosses at 0.167 s. Differentiating gives its negative peak at \(t=0.267\,\mathrm s\), with an overshoot magnitude about 2.08 mm. An optical stop positioned only 1 mm beyond equilibrium would register a crossing even though the damper is critical. Compare that with a slow overdamped return from rest: for \(\zeta=2\) its dominant decay rate falls to \(10(2-\sqrt3)=2.68\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). An assessment based simply on whether the motion oscillates would overlook both the clearance requirement and this much slower return.

The return time depends on which event the instrumentation calls completion. Some switches trigger when the position first enters a band and remain latched even if it later leaves. For the critical release with negative entering speed, first crossing the equilibrium plainly does not mean motion has stopped. A position-plus-velocity criterion is stricter. At the first zero crossing, the bracket in \(x\) vanishes, so the velocity is its constant derivative multiplied by the exponential, \(-0.30e^{-10(0.167)}=-0.0567\,\mathrm{m/s}\). A slide with a maximum docking speed of 0.020 m/s would fail that condition despite its position being exactly correct at that instant. The appropriate response test must state both allowed path excursion and the speed at any mechanical contact.

Checking only the first displacement peak cannot identify this velocity-limited docking event.

Distinguish sliding-friction decay from a viscous ring-down

Suppose a slide moves on a dry horizontal guide under a spring force \(-kx\). While the slide moves left, kinetic friction \(F_c\) acts right, and \(m\ddot x+kx=+F_c\). The solution is harmonic about the shifted equilibrium \(+F_c/k\). During rightward sliding the equilibrium switches to \(-F_c/k\). A complete undisturbed sliding half-cycle still lasts \(\pi\sqrt{m/k}\), but friction dissipates energy in proportion to distance travelled. At zero speed, static friction can take any value up to \(F_s\), so checking the spring force before starting the next half-cycle is essential.

Let a leftward excursion start at \(x=A>0\) and reach a turning point at \(x=-B\), with \(A,B>0\). The spring energy drop equals friction work, \(k(A^2-B^2)/2=F_c(A+B)\). Cancellation leaves \(B=A-2F_c/k\). Same-side peak magnitudes fall by \(4F_c/k\) each complete sliding cycle. This is a constant absolute decrement. A viscous exponential gives a constant ratio instead. If a late half-cycle does not cross zero, use its signed turning-point coordinate \(x_{\rm next}=2F_c/k-x_{\rm start}\) for leftward motion and apply the static criterion there.

A 2 kg slide has \(k=800\,\mathrm{N/m}\), \(F_c=2.0\) N and \(F_s=2.4\) N. Release it from 18 mm. Its initial spring force is 14.4 N, so it moves. Each half-cycle reduces the alternating peak magnitude by 5.0 mm, giving signed positions \(+18,-13,+8,-3\) mm. At −3 mm the spring pull is 2.4 N, equal to the static threshold. Under an ideal static-friction law it can remain there. It took \(3\pi\sqrt{2/800}=0.471\) s to reach that position. The final displacement need not be zero. Interpreting its first two positive peaks with a logarithmic decrement would give a damping estimate that fails to predict the finite stop.
