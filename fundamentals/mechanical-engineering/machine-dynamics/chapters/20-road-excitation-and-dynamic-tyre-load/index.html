What a passenger and a tyre feel on the same road

Two vehicles encounter the same nearly sinusoidal series of road undulations. In one vehicle the occupant feels little vibration, yet its contact force varies substantially. The other transmits a more noticeable body acceleration. Build a two-degree-of-freedom model from force balances, calculate the two responses to a prescribed road geometry, and test which changes in suspension damping improve one measurement at the expense of another.

Choose coordinates around a loaded equilibrium

Represent one vehicle corner by a sprung mass \(m_s\) and an unsprung mass \(m_u\). The first collects the share of the body supported at this corner. The second collects the wheel assembly and the portion of the suspension moving with it. Let \(x_s\) and \(x_u\) be upward displacements from static equilibrium. A suspension spring \(k_s\) and damper \(c\) connect the masses. A tyre spring \(k_t\) connects the wheel coordinate to the road height \(y(t)\). The increments in the tyre force describe deviations about a substantial static force.

Draw the two masses separately. When the body sits momentarily above the wheel relative to its equilibrium separation, the suspension force on the body pulls down. The same force acts upward on the wheel. The tyre pushes upward on the wheel when the road rises more than the wheel. Applying Newton's law to each free body gives

\[m_s\ddot x_s=-k_s(x_s-x_u)-c(\dot x_s-\dot x_u),\]
\[m_u\ddot x_u=k_s(x_s-x_u)+c(\dot x_s-\dot x_u)-k_t(x_u-y).\]

Gravity has disappeared because the coordinates measure changes from an equilibrium that already carries weight. To test continued tyre contact, restore the static force at the end. Replacing the input by a force \(k_ty\) on the wheel is an algebraic convenience. The tyre still extends between two moving positions, and its incremental force still depends on their difference. Overlooking this distinction predicts a changing contact load even for an arbitrarily slow rigid translation of the whole vehicle.

For fixed road height, multiply the equations by their respective velocities and sum. The spring terms form the time derivatives of \(k_s(x_s-x_u)^2/2\) and \(k_t(x_u-y)^2/2\). The remaining internal power is \(-c(\dot x_s-\dot x_u)^2\). With a moving road the contact point additionally supplies \(k_t(y-x_u)\dot y\). This check detects a damper force accidentally applied with the same sign to both masses. It also identifies the mechanical source of energy in the driven oscillation.

Determine the bounce and wheel modes before adding damping

Set \(c=0\) and \(y=0\). For a displacement vector \(\hat x\) varying as \(e^{i\omega t}\), the homogeneous equations become

\[(K-\omega^2M)\hat x=0.\]

Here \(M=\mathrm{diag}(m_s,m_u)\) and the stiffness matrix has diagonal entries \(k_s,k_s+k_t\) and off-diagonal entry \(-k_s\). Nonzero motion requires the determinant to vanish. Expanding the determinant produces a quadratic in \(\omega^2\):

\[m_sm_u\omega^4-\big[m_s(k_s+k_t)+m_uk_s\big]\omega^2+k_sk_t=0.\]

The two roots are not determined by assigning one spring rigidly to each mass. In the lower mode the wheel follows part of the body motion and the tyre deflects slightly. In the upper mode the wheel moves strongly, while the much heavier body has only a small counter-motion. The approximate estimates \(\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\) and \(\sqrt{(k_s+k_t)/m_u}\) are reasons to look in two frequency bands. The exact eigenvectors determine whether the chosen sensor has enough motion in each band to reveal the mode.

Use \(m_s=250\) kg, \(m_u=35\) kg, \(k_s=15\) kN/m and \(k_t=160\) kN/m. Mass-normalising the stiffness by \(M^{-1/2}\) gives an ordinary real symmetric eigenproblem. Its eigenvectors must be mapped back by \(M^{-1/2}\) to recover displacement ratios. The frequencies are 1.178 and 11.26 Hz. The ratios \(x_s/x_u\) are approximately 11.54 and \(-0.012\). The opposite sign in the second ratio does not imply that the body strikes the wheel. It identifies a phase difference of half a cycle in a small-displacement eigenmode.

Project spatial geometry onto a temporal response

Suppose the road profile is \(y(s)=Y\cos(2\pi s/\lambda)\), where \(s\) is the distance travelled along the road. At constant speed \(v\), substitute \(s=vt\). The resulting temporal angular frequency is

\[\omega=2\pi v/\lambda.\]

A road with 4 m spacing at 12 m/s therefore supplies a 3 Hz harmonic excitation. Comparing two travel speeds on the same road involves moving along a frequency-response curve with a fixed height amplitude. This differs from holding the input acceleration amplitude fixed while sweeping frequency on a shaker.

Introduce \(S=k_s+i\omega c\), \(A=S-m_s\omega^2\) and \(B=S+k_t-m_u\omega^2\). The complex amplitudes satisfy \(AX_s-SX_u=0\) and \(-SX_s+BX_u=k_tY\). Invert this two-by-two system once and retain the phase of each result:

\[\frac{X_s}{Y}=\frac{k_tS}{AB-S^2},\]
\[\frac{X_u}{Y}=\frac{k_tA}{AB-S^2}.\]

The body accelerometer reads the amplitude \(\omega^2|X_s|\). The dynamic tyre force amplitude is \(k_t|Y-X_u|\). Subtract the complex displacements before taking an absolute value. The difference between their magnitudes cannot in general recover the extension of the tyre. For example, two signals of equal amplitude and opposite phase differ by twice that amplitude at the peak. Treating them as equal simply because their magnitudes match would predict no force change.

For \(Y=3\) mm and \(c=1200\) N s/m, the 3 Hz input produces an acceleration amplitude of 0.376 m/s² and a dynamic tyre force amplitude of 90.8 N. An instrument reporting root mean square acceleration for this pure sinusoid will show 0.266 m/s². When comparing an experimentally averaged spectrum with the notebook, identify the amplitude convention before interpreting a factor of \(\sqrt2\) as a model error.

Ask three separate questions about damping

Near the lower resonance, relative suspension motion is substantial. A damper dissipates energy there and reduces the high resonant response of a lightly damped body. Well above that first mode, the connecting damper also transmits a force proportional to the relative velocity. Increasing \(c\) can therefore increase the body acceleration in a band where the spring alone would isolate motion reasonably well. Near wheel hop the same change can again reduce a peak. Nobody needs to decide which damping is universally best to interpret these effects. A specification must identify the input spectrum, the observables and the frequency range.

At the slow end both displacements follow the road, so the dynamic contact increment tends to zero. At very high frequency the wheel scarcely follows the rapidly varying road height and the dynamic force approaches \(k_tY\) for fixed \(Y\). For a mathematical harmonic input this limit is finite. A real tyre averages a sufficiently short spatial wavelength over its contact patch; the ideal point input will then overestimate the transmitted excitation. This is one reason to keep a spatial description of the road alongside the temporal response.

The notebook evaluates the transfer functions over a frequency sweep, and an independent complex matrix solve checks the selected point. It also offers the mass-normalised eigensolve through OneSimulation when installed. Run the low and high frequency limits before using a visually plausible peak as evidence of a mode. Sensor placement, a numerator zero and damping can all shift or suppress an observed maximum.

Combine two measured spatial wavelengths

A single sinusoid is rarely a complete description of a test track. Imagine that a survey identifies one displacement component of amplitude 3 mm at a wavelength of 4 m and a second component of amplitude 1 mm at a wavelength of 1 m. At a steady speed of 12 m/s these components excite the model at 3 and 12 Hz, respectively. The smaller road displacement is close to the upper mode. Its contribution to the dynamic tyre load can therefore exceed that of the larger displacement component. Amplitude alone gives no ordering until the spatial wavelengths and speed are known.

Apply the complex frequency response separately to each component. To obtain a time trace, multiply each response by its measured road phase factor, add the complex oscillations with their own time dependence, and take the real part. For a record containing an integer number of periods of both harmonics the cross term integrates to zero. If the two body acceleration amplitudes are \(A_1,A_2\), the root mean square reading is \(\sqrt{(A_1^2+A_2^2)/2}\). Peak acceleration requires the relative phase as well. This distinction also matters for a minimum contact force because the most negative extrema of the two components may occur at different times. A short record starting at an arbitrary road position loses the exact cancellation of the cross term. For such a record, compute the time history and average its square over the actual measurement window.

Exercises in measurement and scaling

1. At what speed does a 4 m spatial wavelength excite each undamped natural frequency of the example?

Work through the solution

Multiply \(v=\lambda f\) by each frequency. The speeds are 4.71 and 45.0 m/s. These values locate the modal frequencies on a speed axis. The maximum of the damped body acceleration need not occur at exactly either speed because the response has a frequency-dependent numerator and the ordinate includes an acceleration factor.

2. How large a road amplitude would drive the minimum tyre force to zero at 3 Hz in this linear calculation?

Work through the solution

The static corner force is

\[(250+35)9.81=2796\,\mathrm{N}.\]

The force increment is proportional to road amplitude; a 3 mm input produces 90.8 N. Extrapolation gives

\[3(2796/90.8)=92.4\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

This is a diagnostic extrapolation. Large motion can reach travel limits, change the effective spring rates or invalidate the small-displacement tyre model well before that value.

3. A data logger reports very little upper-mode body displacement. Is that evidence that the wheel remains in steady contact?

Work through the solution

Use the upper-mode displacement ratio. A 1 mm wheel modal amplitude corresponds to only about 0.012 mm of body motion. The contact force involves the wheel displacement relative to the road, and therefore cannot be bounded well by this single body displacement measurement. Measure the wheel or contact force directly and include the static load when interpreting the minimum.
