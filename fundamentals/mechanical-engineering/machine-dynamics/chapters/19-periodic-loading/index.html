When the third harmonic matters more than the fundamental

A machine applies a short force once per cycle. Its repetition rate looks safely below a structural resonance, but one of its harmonics can lie close to that resonance. Build the forcing spectrum from a pulse and apply a complex response coefficient to each term.

Integrate over the part of the period where force is present

Let an even rectangular force have peak P, period T and on-time dT centred on zero, where \(0<d<1\). Use an even periodic continuation. Its mean is Pd. Sine coefficients vanish by symmetry. With \(\Omega=2\pi/T\), integrate the cosine terms over the active interval and exploit periodicity:

\[F(t)=Pd+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos(n\Omega t),\]
\[a_n={2\over T}\int_{-dT/2}^{dT/2}P\cos(n\Omega t)\,dt={2P\over n\pi}\sin(n\pi d).\]

The coefficients are signed. A negative coefficient adds a phase reversal; taking its magnitude before summing the time response loses that information. The mean drives a static offset. When \(nd\) is an integer, that harmonic vanishes even though the force still has abrupt edges. At a jump the Fourier series converges to the mean of the adjacent values. These details matter when reconstructing force but can have much smaller effects on the filtered displacement.

Solve each harmonic separately and keep phase

For \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F(t)\) the steady particular solution follows from \(H(i\omega)=1/(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)\). Define \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\),

\[\zeta=c/(2\sqrt{km}),\]
\[r_j=j\Omega/\omega_n.\]

The jth response contribution is the real part of \(a_jH(ij\Omega)e^{ij\Omega t}\). Thus

\[x(t)={Pd\over k}+\sum_{j=1}^\infty {a_j\over k}\,{(1-r_j^2)\cos(j\Omega t)+2\zeta r_j\sin(j\Omega t)\over(1-r_j^2)^2+(2\zeta r_j)^2}.\]

Far above resonance each force coefficient is attenuated roughly by another \(j^{-2}\) in displacement. Acceleration weights a displacement coefficient by \(j^2\) again, so an adequate displacement truncation can be inadequate for acceleration. Start-up transients are omitted because the expression is the settled periodic response for positive damping.

Compare harmonics before selecting an operating speed

Take \(P=10\) N, \(d=0.25,\ k=1000\) N/m, natural frequency 12 Hz, damping ratio 0.04, and repetition frequency 4 Hz. The input mean is 2.50 N and \(a_1=4.50\) N while \(a_3=1.50\) N. The first harmonic has \(r=1/3\), so its displacement amplitude is about 5.06 mm. The third is exactly resonant and gives

\[1.50/(1000\cdot0.08)=18.8\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

A single harmonic based on the largest force coefficient therefore misses the dominant response.

Choose a new duty \(d=1/3\) while leaving peak force and repetition frequency unchanged. Now \(\sin(3\pi d)=0\), so the resonant third harmonic disappears. The static offset rises from 2.50 to 3.33 mm, and the first two harmonics remain. Holding mean force fixed instead would require rescaling P. A small timing error can reintroduce the third harmonic, especially important when damping is low. Move the duty around one third and compare the full signal with its fundamental-only prediction.

Use 4 Hz and tune the duty as close as the slider allows to one third. Read the residual third harmonic and compare the waveform to the fundamental-only trace. Next shift repetition frequency and distinguish removing the forcing coefficient from moving away from resonance.

Tutorial · estimate sensitivity to an on-time error

Near \(d=1/3\) let the duty increase by 0.010 while keeping P fixed. Estimate the newly excited third force harmonic to first order and the resulting displacement near exact resonance. Can this signed contribution be added directly to the static peak displacement?

Differentiate the coefficient, then use the transfer function

From \(a_3=2P\sin(3\pi d)/(3\pi)\), its derivative is \(2P\cos(3\pi d)=-20\) N per unit duty at \(d=1/3\). A change of 0.010 gives about −0.20 N. At resonance the displacement amplitude is

\[0.20/[1000(0.08)]=2.50\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The sign sets its phase, and the dynamic transfer adds a quarter-cycle lag at resonance. The largest combined displacement requires summing instantaneous contributions with their correct phases, rather than adding every harmonic magnitude as if their crests coincided.
