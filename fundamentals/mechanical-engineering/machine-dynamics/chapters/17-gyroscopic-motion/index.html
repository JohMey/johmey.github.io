Steady gyroscopic precession and its two possible rates

A spinning axisymmetric rotor supported at one point can keep a constant tilt under gravity. Predict the rate at which its axis turns, examine the fast-spin approximation and determine when slow steady precession is possible.

Identify the changing direction of angular momentum

Let the centre of mass be a distance l along the symmetry axis from a fixed pivot. The symmetry axis is tilted by \(\theta\) from the upward vertical. The moment of inertia about that axis is \(I_3\), and about either transverse axis through the pivot it is \(I_1\). Define \(\omega_3\) as the total component of angular velocity along the symmetry axis. When the spin angular momentum dominates,

\[\mathbf H\simeq I_3\omega_3\mathbf e_3.\]

Under uniform precession at rate \(\Omega\), its tip traces a horizontal circle of radius \(I_3\omega_3\sin\theta\).

\[|\dot{\mathbf H}|\simeq\Omega I_3\omega_3\sin\theta,\]
\[|\boldsymbol\tau_g|=mgl\sin\theta,\]
\[\Omega_{\rm slow}\simeq {mgl\over I_3\omega_3}.\]

The tilt factors cancel for nonvertical orientations. Gravity changes the direction of angular momentum while doing zero work in a steady precession at fixed height. Reversing the spin changes the direction of slow precession. The support force has zero moment about the pivot.

Keep the angular momentum due to precession

For a regular precession at fixed tilt the transverse angular velocity is the part of \(\Omega\mathbf e_z\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf e_3\). Thus \(\mathbf H=I_1\Omega\mathbf e_z+(I_3\omega_3-I_1\Omega\cos\theta)\mathbf e_3\). The vertical first term is constant. The rotating second term has derivative \(\Omega\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf H\). Equating it to the gravitational torque yields

\[I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+mgl=0 .\]

For \(0<\theta<90^\circ\) and positive spin, two positive real rates exist only if \(D=(I_3\omega_3)^2-4I_1 mgl\cos\theta\ge0\). The smaller root is most accurately evaluated as \(2mgl/(I_3\omega_3+\sqrt D)\), avoiding subtraction of nearly equal numbers at high spin. The larger root corresponds to a different initial angular velocity. Releasing a rotor without providing an appropriate initial precession generally adds nutation.

A tabletop rotor as its spin slows

Take \(m=0.50\) kg, \(l=0.060\) m, \(I_1=0.0030\) kg m², \(I_3=0.0020\) kg m² and \(\theta=60^\circ\). At \(\omega_3=100\) rad/s the first estimate gives 1.47 rad/s. The quadratic gives 1.49 rad/s for the slow branch and 131.8 rad/s for the fast branch. The 1% discrepancy in the slow result is consistent with a small transverse angular momentum.

At \(\omega_3=30\) rad/s the approximation gives 4.91 rad/s and the exact slow rate is 5.72 rad/s. The two rates meet at a spin of roughly 21.0 rad/s. Below this value there is no constant-tilt solution for the prescribed angle and inertias. Plotting the discriminant or using the real branches avoids reporting a fictitious real rate after the branches disappear.

Compare the approximation and exact branch near the minimum admissible spin, then at high spin. Raise the tilt toward a right angle. Predict whether the minimum spin increases or falls before checking the new domain of the curve.

Tutorial · infer a moment arm from observed motion

For the same inertias and mass, measure \(\omega_3=50\) rad/s, \(\Omega=3.0\) rad/s and \(\theta=60^\circ\). Estimate l from the full relation. Calculate the bias if transverse angular momentum is omitted.

Show the torque estimate

The full torque parameter is \(mgl=I_3\omega_3\Omega-I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2=0.300-0.0135=0.2865\) N m. The arm is

\[l=0.2865/(0.50\cdot9.81)=58.4\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Keeping only the first term gives 61.2 mm, overestimating the arm by about 4.7%. Measuring steady precession over several cycles helps distinguish that systematic error from fluctuations caused by nutation.
