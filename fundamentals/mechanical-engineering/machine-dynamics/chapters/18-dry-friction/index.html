Why Coulomb damping loses a fixed amplitude each half-cycle

A sliding carriage on a spring can stop away from zero displacement. Determine each turning point without time stepping, then identify the experimentally observable difference between Coulomb and viscous damping.

Write a different equation for each direction of motion

A mass m slides horizontally against a linear spring k. During motion take friction magnitude \(F_k>0\), with force opposing velocity. At rest allow any friction up to the static limit \(F_s\ge F_k\). The moving equation is

\[m\ddot x+kx=-F_k\,{\rm sgn}(\dot x).\]

Release the mass at \(x=A>0\) with zero speed. If \(kA\le F_s\) static friction can hold it there. If \(kA>F_s\) it starts moving left. While it moves left, friction points right. Shift the coordinate by \(a=F_k/k\) to obtain a homogeneous oscillator:

\[m\ddot x+k(x-a)=0,\]
\[x(t)=a+(A-a)\cos(\omega_n t),\]
\[\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}.\]

At \(t=\pi/\omega_n\) the next turning point is \(x_1=2a-A\). Test static force balance there before starting another sliding segment. If the mass continues, the sign of friction changes and the next cosine is centred on \(-a\). The time for each complete sliding half-cycle is constant despite the decreasing amplitudes. An integration routine that uses only \({\rm sgn}(0)=0\) can generate spurious chatter near the final state.

Recover the amplitude drop from work done by friction

For a full crossing from a positive peak A to a negative peak \(-B\), with \(A,B>0\), friction dissipates \(F_k(A+B)\). The change in spring energy is \(\tfrac12 k(A^2-B^2)\). Cancel the travelled distance to obtain

\[A-B={2F_k\over k},\]
\[A_j-A_{j+2}={4F_k\over k}\]

for successive magnitudes on opposite sides and successive positive peaks, respectively, while sliding continues. An ordinary viscous oscillator instead has an approximately fixed ratio between successive positive peaks. A mixed friction-damping experiment can exhibit both effects. An ideal Coulomb model also permits a final equilibrium anywhere in the static-friction band \([-F_s/k,F_s/k]\).

Predict a finite stopping time and reconstruct a friction force

Take \(m=1.0\) kg, \(k=100\) N/m, \(F_k=0.50\) N, \(F_s=0.65\) N and \(A=40\) mm. The shift is 5.0 mm and each sliding half-period is 0.314 s. Starting at +40 mm the successive turns are −30, +20, −10 and 0 mm. The mass sticks at the fourth turn after 1.257 s. A release from +43 mm instead reaches −33, +23, −13 and +3 mm. Since \(100(0.003)=0.30\) N lies below 0.65 N, the residual displacement persists under the assumptions.

Suppose a different test with \(k=200\) N/m shows successive positive peaks of 35, 27 and 19 mm. The 8 mm decrement gives

\[F_k=k(0.008)/4=0.40\,\mathrm{N}.\]

A viscous fit would infer logarithmic decrements 0.260 and 0.351 from adjacent pairs. Applying a small constant external force after the final stop can help identify static breakaway independently of the moving-friction estimate.

Compare releases at 40 and 43 mm. Predict the final resting position from the sequence of turning points. Then reduce the release below the static threshold; explain why an initial energy above zero need not imply any motion.

Tutorial · predict the last turning point even without crossing zero

Use the original mass, stiffness and friction values but release at +8 mm. Does it start, cross zero, and then reverse velocity? Give the final displacement and dissipated energy.

Follow the event conditions

The initial spring force 0.80 N exceeds 0.65 N so it moves left. The next turn is

\[2a-A=10-8=+2\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

It has not crossed zero. At that turn spring force is 0.20 N so it sticks. Friction work is \(0.50(0.008-0.002)=0.0030\) J, equal to \(\tfrac12(100)(0.008^2-0.002^2)\). Blindly subtracting a positive amplitude decrement would have assigned an impossible negative magnitude.
