From first principles

Distinguish mount travel from the absolute motion of the mass

A moving foundation imposes a position rather than a force directly on a machine mass. Let \(y(t)\) denote base displacement, \(x(t)\) absolute mass displacement, and take positive directions alike. A spring of stiffness \(k\) and damper of coefficient \(c\) act through relative deformation and relative velocity. Newton's second law therefore gives

\[m\ddot x=-k(x-y)-c(\dot x-\dot y).\]

For steady sinusoidal base motion at frequency \(\omega\), replace each derivative by \(i\omega\). Group absolute displacement terms on the left and prescribed motion on the right to obtain the transfer between their complex amplitudes.

\[\frac XY=\frac{k+ic\omega}{k-m\omega^2+ic\omega},\]
\[\frac{X-Y}{Y}=\frac{m\omega^2}{k-m\omega^2+ic\omega}.\]

At low frequency the mass follows the base and mount travel approaches zero. At sufficiently high frequency absolute motion decreases, while the mount must accommodate nearly the full base displacement. A clearance sensor spanning the mount and an inertial sensor attached to the mass therefore respond to different quantities during the same isolation experiment.

Derivation and applications

Choose damping by comparing two isolation frequencies

Damping can limit a resonant motion while increasing high-frequency vibration transmitted through a mount. Consider a machine mass \(m\) on a spring \(k\) and damper \(c\) in parallel, driven by a harmonic force on the mass with rigid support. Take \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\),

\[\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n),\]
\[r=\omega/\omega_n.\]

The complex force transmitted into the support equals \((k+ic\omega)X\), where \(X\) is the steady displacement. Dividing by the applied force gives

\[T_F^2=\left|\frac{F_{\rm base}}{F_{\rm drive}}\right|^2=\frac{1+4\zeta^2r^2}{(1-r^2)^2+4\zeta^2r^2}.\]

At a fixed nonzero frequency define \(h=4\zeta^2r^2\). Differentiating in \(h\) leaves a numerator independent of damping. Its sign determines whether the transmitted fraction increases or decreases as damping is added.

\[\frac{\partial T_F^2}{\partial h}=\frac{(1-r^2)^2-1}{[(1-r^2)^2+h]^2}=\frac{r^2(r^2-2)}{[(1-r^2)^2+h]^2}.\]

For \(r<\sqrt2\), added damping lowers transmissibility; above \(\sqrt2\) it raises it. At the boundary all damping choices give unity. A limit specified at resonance turns directly into a minimum damping ratio because the spring and inertial force cancel in the displacement denominator there.

\[T_F(1)=\frac{\sqrt{1+4\zeta^2}}{2\zeta},\]
\[\zeta_{\min}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{T_{\rm lim}^2-1}}\quad(T_{\rm lim}>1).\]

These results assume the same applied force amplitude across frequencies. A rotating imbalance has a force magnitude proportional to frequency squared, requiring that additional factor when predicting an absolute support load.

Worked example

Trade a resonant limit against running isolation

A mount must keep resonant support force below three times the drive amplitude. The minimum damping ratio is

\[1/(2\sqrt8)=0.1768.\]

At operating ratio \(r=3.00\) this choice gives \(h=1.125\) and \(T_F=\sqrt{2.125/65.125}=0.181\). A 50.0 N peak harmonic force then transmits 9.03 N at the operating frequency.

Second application

Determine whether more damping can meet a running target

Another design runs at \(r=2.00\). Its present \(\zeta=0.250\) gives \(h=1.00\) and \(T_F=\sqrt{2/10}=0.447\). To reach \(T_F=0.400\), solve \((1+h)/(9+h)=0.160\), giving \(h=0.524\) and \(\zeta=\sqrt h/(2r)=0.181\). The reduction of damping improves the running value but raises the predicted resonant ratio to about 2.94.

A moving foundation is a different input

Place a microscope on a spring-and-damper mount above a vibrating floor. An accelerometer on its body reports absolute acceleration, whereas a displacement gauge connected across the mount reports relative displacement. These can be very different. First write both coordinates in the same inertial frame. Let \(y(t)\) be the motion of the floor and \(x(t)\) the motion of the instrument. The spring force on the mass is \(-k(x-y)\) and the damper force is \(-c(\dot x-\dot y)\).

For mass \(m\), Newton’s law therefore becomes

\[m\ddot x+c(\dot x-\dot y)+k(x-y)=0.\]

A floor that moves sufficiently slowly carries the mass along with almost no relative motion. A floor moving very rapidly can move substantially beneath a nearly stationary mass. Whether the design is successful depends on which motion is constrained by the application.

Derive two transfer functions

For harmonic input \(y=\mathrm{Re}(Ye^{i\omega t})\), substitute \(x=\mathrm{Re}(Xe^{i\omega t})\). Collect terms in \(X\) and \(Y\). Writing \(r=\omega/\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2\sqrt{km})\) gives

\[\frac{X}{Y}=\frac{1+i2\zeta r}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r},\]
\[\frac{X-Y}{Y}=\frac{r^2}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r}.\]

Subtract the complex quantities before taking magnitudes. In general the magnitude of the relative displacement is not the difference of the absolute amplitudes, since the motions can have different phase angles. The acceleration ratio for harmonics equals \(|X/Y|\) because both displacements acquire the same factor \(-\omega^2\). That shortcut is inapplicable to arbitrary time records without decomposition into frequencies.

Compare the squares of numerator and denominator in the first ratio. Transmissibility falls below one when \((1-r^2)^2>1\), or \(r>\sqrt2\) for positive frequency. The crossing is independent of damping in this ideal model. Damping reduces the peak around resonance but provides a force path at high frequency, increasing the transmitted motion there compared with a lighter damper.

Choose an isolation range and a clearance

Take \(m=80\) kg and desired undamped frequency \(f_n=4.0\) Hz. Then \(k=m(2\pi f_n)^2=50.5\) kN/m. With \(\zeta=0.15\), the required viscous coefficient is \(c=2m(2\pi f_n)\zeta=603\) N·s/m. Effective isolation begins above \(4\sqrt2=5.66\) Hz. A floor excursion of 0.30 mm amplitude at 12 Hz gives \(r=3\) and \(2\zeta r=0.90\). The absolute ratio is \(\sqrt{1.81/64.81}=0.167\), hence instrument motion is 0.050 mm. The relative amplitude is

\[9(0.30)/\sqrt{64.81}=0.335\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The smaller number is appropriate for motion performance and the larger for mount clearance.

At 4 Hz the same base displacement gives relative amplitude \(0.30/(2\zeta)=1.00\) mm. Before checking that a stop is far enough away, add the static spring compression \(mg/k=15.5\) mm if the mount is vertical and its available stroke is specified from the unloaded position.

Ask what a sensor can distinguish

Measure floor and instrument motion simultaneously with a shared clock. An isolated mass at high frequency can seem quiet despite substantial relative movement. Conversely, close to resonance both acceleration and support force may be amplified. For harmonic motion the force on the base is the spring-plus-damper force. Its magnitude equals \(m\omega^2|X|\) when no other dynamic forces act on the mass. Comparing it with the measured acceleration is an independent check on force calibration and on the assumption of one moving coordinate.

Calculate what an accelerometer would report

A sensor bolted to the instrument measures absolute acceleration. With harmonic motion its amplitude is \(\omega^2|X|\), even when the clearance requirement is governed by \(|X-Y|\). For the 12 Hz condition, the 0.050 mm instrument displacement gives

\[(2\pi12)^2(0.000050)=0.284\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.\]

The floor acceleration is 1.71 m/s², so the acceleration transmissibility is the same 0.167 as the absolute displacement ratio at this frequency. Inferring spring force from the instrument’s absolute displacement would instead be wrong: the extension of the spring is the relative motion. The damper force also depends on relative velocity, and the two transmitted force components differ in phase.

Derive the response of a mounted accelerometer

An accelerometer is itself a base-excited oscillator. Let its casing follow the machine displacement \(y(t)\) while an internal proof mass moves at \(x(t)\). A spring and viscous element connect the two, and a transducer detects relative motion \(z=x-y\). Because damping and stiffness act on relative movement, Newton’s law gives

\[m\ddot z+c\dot z+kz=-m\ddot y.\]

The instrument manufacturer may scale the output as estimated acceleration \(\hat a=-\omega_n^2z\). This scale is exact at zero frequency in the linear model, but both gain and phase change as frequency approaches the sensor resonance.

For harmonic casing acceleration with phasor \(A\), the transfer from actual to indicated acceleration is

\[\frac{\hat A}{A}=\frac{1}{1-r^2+i2\zeta r},\]
\[r=\omega/\omega_n .\]

At small \(r\), the proof mass is almost in force equilibrium with the spring and the output tracks acceleration. At large \(r\), the proof mass is nearly inertially stationary and the relative displacement approximates negative casing displacement. This is the regime exploited by certain displacement-based seismic instruments. The very same equation permits two measurement principles, but their useful frequency ranges differ.

For \(\zeta=1/\sqrt2\) the squared magnitude denominator simplifies to \(1+r^4\). The leading magnitude error then appears at fourth order, although the phase still changes at first order. A sensor can consequently have an excellent amplitude calibration and a material timing error in the same frequency range.

Select a transducer for a base-isolation measurement

A vibration test uses an accelerometer with undamped frequency 100 Hz and \(\zeta=1/\sqrt2\). At a 30 Hz machine component, \(r=0.30\). The indicated magnitude is \(1/\sqrt{1+0.3^4}=0.9960\) times the true acceleration, whereas the lag is

\[\arctan[\sqrt2(0.30)/(1-0.30^2)]=25.0^\circ.\]

An amplitude requirement of 1% is met, but a phase comparison between this sensor and an independently calibrated force transducer would carry a large offset. For an actual acceleration amplitude of \(4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\), proof-mass travel is \(0.996(4)/(2\pi100)^2=10.1\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), which also has to fit the transducer’s linear travel range.

For the same damping, an amplitude error below 1% requires \(1/\sqrt{1+r^4}\ge0.99\), or \(r\le0.378\). A record extending to 50 Hz would thus require a resonance of at least about 132 Hz by magnitude alone. A 5° phase specification is much stricter: solving \(\sqrt2r/(1-r^2)=\tan5^\circ\) gives \(r\simeq0.0616\), or resonance above about 812 Hz for the 50 Hz component. If two identical, well-matched sensors measure base and payload at the same frequency, much of their common transfer cancels in a ratio. It does not cancel in a phase comparison to another instrument with a different dynamics.

An instrument range may be specified in acceleration rather than proof-mass stroke. Near resonance the displacement per unit base acceleration increases according to the same frequency response. A sensor that remains linear at a given low-frequency acceleration can therefore approach its internal stop at a lower applied acceleration near resonance. Mechanical range and dynamic calibration must be checked together.
