How a longitudinal pulse reflects from a change in rod section

From the cut force to a travelling disturbance

Measure the small axial displacement \(u(x,t)\) from a stress-free reference. In a slender homogeneous rod of area A, tensile force is \(N=EA u_x\). A slice of length dx and mass \(\rho A\,dx\) accelerates because its right and left forces differ by \(N_x dx\). For constant properties, cancelling A gives

\[\rho A u_{tt}=(EAu_x)_x,\]
\[u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},\]
\[c=\sqrt{E/\rho}.\]

Either \(u=f(x-ct)\) or \(u=g(x+ct)\) satisfies this equation by differentiation. Curves of constant phase move right or left with speed c. Long-wavelength assumptions suppress lateral inertia and radial modes. Rod area cancels from wave speed but remains in the force carried at given particle velocity. For a right-moving pulse \(v=u_t=-cu_x\), so tensile force \(N=-Zv\), with mechanical impedance \(Z=\rho Ac=EA/c\). The sign reverses for a left-moving wave.

Match velocity and force at a bonded junction

Let amplitudes I, R and T denote incident, reflected and transmitted particle velocity at the junction. A massless, perfectly bonded interface requires equal velocities and transmitted force. Apply the preceding sign convention separately to each travelling direction. The two equations determine two unknowns.

\[I+R=T,\]
\[-Z_1 I+Z_1 R=-Z_2 T,\]
\[{R\over I}={Z_1-Z_2\over Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[{T\over I}={2Z_1\over Z_1+Z_2}.\]

The instantaneous power transported to the right is \(-Nv=Zv^2\) for a single right-moving wave. For the same pulse shape the reflected energy fraction is \((R/I)^2\) and the transmitted fraction is \((Z_2/Z_1)(T/I)^2\). They sum to one. A transmitted velocity amplitude exceeding the incident amplitude can thus be compatible with conservation of energy.

A stepped rod and a pulse-echo observation

Join two aluminium rods of areas 80 and 160 mm², \(E=70\) GPa and \(\rho=2700\) kg/m³. Both have \(c=5092\) m/s, but the second impedance is twice the first. An incident velocity of 0.30 m/s produces a reflected −0.10 m/s and a transmitted 0.20 m/s. The reflected pulse carries one ninth of the energy. Reversing the experiment makes the reflection positive and the transmitted velocity 0.40 m/s for the same incident amplitude. The two energy fractions are unchanged on reversal.

A receiver at the tap detects a reflected pulse 0.40 ms after launch, before any far-end reflection contaminates it. The return path covers the distance twice, so the inferred junction is \(c(0.00040)/2=1.02\) m away. Measure pulse velocity rather than strain when comparing the signs to the plot. Incident tensile force has sign opposite incident velocity with the convention used here.

Keep the incoming pulse fixed and change the area ratio from 2 to 0.5. Determine which velocity component reverses sign and compare reflected energy fractions. Explain the difference by writing power balance for the two experiments.

Tutorial · infer a second cross-section

On a uniform material an isolated junction returns 25% of the incident velocity amplitude with the same sign. Infer the area ratio and transmitted energy fraction. How could an energy-only measurement leave the geometry ambiguous?

Show the inverse relation

Let \(z=Z_2/Z_1=A_2/A_1\). Then \(0.25=(1-z)/(1+z)\), so \(z=0.60\). The transmitted velocity is \(1.25 I\). Its energy fraction is \(0.60(1.25)^2=0.9375\). Measuring only the reflected energy gives 0.0625 and loses the sign of the amplitude. A thickening by a factor \(1/0.60\) would give the same energy reflection and the opposite sign in the velocity trace.
