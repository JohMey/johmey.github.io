From first principles

Eliminate the second actuator to find a constrained gain

With several actuators and outputs, a gain depends on what the other controller holds fixed. Consider small steady deviations described by a square two-by-two gain matrix \(G\). First hold \(u_2\) fixed at zero. The direct response of \(y_1\) to \(u_1\) has slope \(g_{11}\). Now assume an ideal second controller adjusts \(u_2\) slowly enough to maintain \(y_2=0\) in steady state.

\[y_1=g_{11}u_1+g_{12}u_2,\]
\[0=y_2=g_{21}u_1+g_{22}u_2 .\]

For \(g_{22}\neq0\), the second actuator must move by \(-g_{21}u_1/g_{22}\). Substitution gives the constrained gain seen by the first loop and the ratio of the direct to constrained gains.

\[\left.\frac{y_1}{u_1}\right|_{y_2=0}=\frac{\det G}{g_{22}},\]
\[\lambda_{11}=\frac{g_{11}g_{22}}{\det G}.\]

The ratio requires a nonsingular matrix. A value near unity means closing the second loop changes this steady gain little. A negative value signals a reversal of the gain sign under the constraint. Dynamic phase and actuator limits still affect whether the pairing is usable.

Derivation and applications

Determine how actuator limits reshape the reachable demands

A two-actuator mechanism may have abundant force in either output separately but be unable to deliver the two requested forces simultaneously. Work with a static local model and identical actuator units. Choose outputs as the sum \(y_1\) and difference \(y_2\) of two actuator forces \(u_1,u_2\). Assume each is bidirectional and bounded by magnitude \(U\), and the static gain holds over the region of interest. The forward and inverse maps are

\[y_1=u_1+u_2,\]
\[y_2=u_1-u_2,\]
\[u_1=\frac{y_1+y_2}{2},\]
\[u_2=\frac{y_1-y_2}{2}.\]

Substitution of the inverse map into the two component limits gives a pair of strips in output space. Their intersection is the reachable region. The maximum of the absolute sum and absolute difference of two real numbers is their absolute-value sum, which simplifies the description to a diamond.

\[|y_1+y_2|\leq 2U,\qquad |y_1-y_2|\leq 2U\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |y_1|+|y_2|\leq2U .\]

A supervisor can retain the relative amounts of the two requested outputs by scaling a nonzero desired vector \(d\) by one factor. The largest such factor up to unity follows by placing the scaled request on the reachable boundary whenever the original request exceeds capacity.

\[y=\alpha d,\]
\[\alpha=\min\!\left(1,\frac{2U}{|d_1|+|d_2|}\right).\]

This allocation preserves the requested direction. If one output has priority, it may be preferable to maintain that output and use only the residual capacity for the other. The physical feasible set is the same for both decisions.

Worked example

Preserve the direction of a combined request

Two linear actuators each permit 50.0 N. A requested combination \((80,60)\) N would need \((70,10)\) N. Scaling by \(100/140=0.7143\) yields outputs \((57.1,42.9)\) N and actuator loads \((50.0,7.14)\) N. Checking the inverse transformation shows exactly which actuator has reached its limit while the other retains substantial headroom.

Second application

Preserve a preload while alternating the differential force

A separate rig with \(U=30.0\) N requires total preload \(y_1=44.0\) N throughout a test. The reachable inequality leaves \(|y_2|\leq60.0-44.0=16.0\) N. At the positive extreme the actuator forces are \((30.0,14.0)\) N; at the negative extreme they exchange roles. A demanded 20.0 N alternating differential amplitude would require reducing the fixed preload to at most 40.0 N.

A second loop changes the experiment

Imagine two thermal zones with separate heaters and two temperature sensors. At steady state let \(y=G u\), with \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) K per unit power. Increasing heater 1 with heater 2 fixed raises both temperatures. Increasing heater 1 while actively holding temperature 2 fixed is a different experiment: heater 2 must back off. The effective gain in the first channel changes. This difference matters when a controller is tuned with its neighbour disconnected and then commissioned with both loops active.

For a general nonsingular two-by-two matrix hold \(y_2=0\). Solving \(g_{21}u_1+g_{22}u_2=0\) gives

\[y_1/u_1=g_{11}-g_{12}g_{21}/g_{22}.\]

The ratio of the fixed-input gain to this fixed-output gain is \(g_{11}g_{22}/\det G\). More generally the relative gain array has entries \(\Lambda_{ij}=g_{ij}(G^{-1})_{ji}\). Its rows and columns each sum to one. Large magnitudes suggest cancellation. A negative relative gain indicates that the paired steady response changes sign when the other output is perfectly held, an important warning for independent-loop tuning.

Compare candidate pairings

For the two-zone matrix above the diagonal relative gains are \(4/3\) and the off-diagonal gains are \(-1/3\). The direct pairing has fixed-input gain 2 and fixed-other-output gain 1.5. The cross-pairing changes sign. These steady results do not include transport delays or transient coupling. If the direct heater path is very slow but the cross path changes quickly, a frequency-dependent model is needed around the intended control bandwidth.

Diagonalise a symmetric plant by physical combinations

Commanding both heaters together excites the common direction \((1,1)\) with gain 3; commanding them oppositely excites \((1,-1)\) with gain 1. For the desired temperature increment \((1,-1)\), exactly equal and opposite input increments \((1,-1)\) are required. Negative input here is a reduction from a positive bias; a heater already at zero cannot supply it. Inspecting a transformed system without retaining the original actuator limits can make an impossible target seem easy.

Now bring the gains closer together:

\[G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.98\\0.98&1\end{smallmatrix}\right).\]

The common gain is 1.98, while the differential gain is just 0.02. A unit differential target requires differential input of magnitude 50 in each actuator. Static inversion \(u=G^{-1}v\) algebraically decouples the two commands but amplifies measurement, calibration and modelling errors along that weak direction. If both temperature sensors move together during every available test, the weak direction is also particularly hard to identify.

Choose a practical validation test

First perturb each actuator separately about the operating bias and measure both outputs. Repeat at different loads and frequencies. Next excite the predicted weakest input combination within safe operating limits and check whether the differential output emerges above the noise. A design can prioritise the common temperature and relax allowable imbalance when the plant is inherently hard to split. A constrained controller can express this trade directly by penalising both output errors while respecting individual actuator bounds.

Find difficult demand directions with a singular-value decomposition

Relative gains help pair individual inputs and outputs, but some machines are controlled through combinations of actuators. A steady mapping \(y=Gu\) with adequate static rank can still require a large actuator change in a particular direction. Scale inputs by allowed physical excursions and outputs by meaningful error or command scales. After scaling, the Euclidean norm is interpretable and singular values quantify directional gains. Without scaling, changing position units from metres to millimetres could dominate the result.

For a real square nonsingular \(G\), the symmetric positive definite matrix \(G^TG\) has orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_j\) and positive eigenvalues \(\sigma_j^2\). A unit actuator pattern \(v_j\) yields an output of length \(\sigma_j\) in direction \(u_j=Gv_j/\sigma_j\). Writing \(G=U\Sigma V^T\) then gives a transparent inverse. A desired output \(y_r\) requires actuator components \((u_j^Ty_r)/\sigma_j\) along \(v_j\). The smallest singular value therefore controls the largest gain of the inverse, and the ratio \(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) measures conditioning.

This geometry describes steady input effort, not necessarily dynamic stability. Two channels with different lags can have different difficult directions at higher frequencies. The singular values of the complex frequency-response matrix can reveal that change after consistent scaling at every frequency.

Allocate two actuators with a tight current budget

Consider a scaled steady plant \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.9\\0.9&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Its symmetry gives unit common and differential directions \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\), with singular values 1.9 and 0.1. A common output target \((0.2,0.2)\) requires only \(u=(0.1053,0.1053)\). A differential target \((0.2,-0.2)\) needs \(u=(2,-2)\), a factor nineteen more effort for the same output norm. If each drive is limited to magnitude one, the first target is feasible and the second is not.

One possible allocation trades exact tracking against effort by minimising \(\|Gu-y_r\|^2+\lambda\|u\|^2\). The stationary normal equations are

\[(G^TG+\lambda I)u=G^Ty_r;\]

each inverse singular value is replaced by \(\sigma_j/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)\). For \(\lambda=0.01\) and the differential target, that factor is

\[0.1/(0.01+0.01)=5.\]

The resulting input is \((1,-1)\) and output \((0.1,-0.1)\). The allocation respects these particular bounds while openly accepting half the requested differential change. A penalty does not enforce an input limit for every possible target. If exact limit enforcement is required, solve the bounded allocation problem or constrain the admissible command directions explicitly.

A simple perturbation test makes the weak direction visible in hardware. Command \(u=(0.1,0.1)\) in scaled units and predict \(y=(0.19,0.19)\). Reversing only the second command gives \(u=(0.1,-0.1)\) and outputs only \((0.01,-0.01)\). If each sensor has an uncertainty of ±0.01 output units, the first test has a comfortable signal level but the second barely resolves its predicted value. A poorly estimated differential gain may therefore vary considerably between identification records even though the common gain is repeatable. Increasing the differential test amplitude within operating bounds or averaging repeated experiments improves that estimate. A static inverse based on a single small pulse at each actuator may otherwise amplify calibration error precisely in the least observable output combination.
