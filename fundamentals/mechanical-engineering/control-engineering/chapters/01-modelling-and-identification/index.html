Choose variables that describe stored energy

A heater warms a well-stirred tank. A sensor measures only the liquid temperature, and a controller will eventually change the heating power. Before designing the controller, ask what information fixes the future of the plant when the power is given. If the tank, its contents and the thermometer can be represented by one uniform temperature, a single initial temperature suffices. A slow thermometer or a heavy tank wall may require its own initial condition. This is a physical way of deciding how many states to put in a model.

Let \(C\) in J/K be the combined heat capacity, \(H\) in W/K the thermal conductance to the room, \(T_a\) the ambient temperature and \(u\) the delivered heater power. Over an interval \(dt\) the heater adds \(u\,dt\), the room receives \(H(T-T_a)\,dt\), and storage increases by \(C\,dT\). Taking the limit of a short interval gives

\[C\dot T=u-H(T-T_a).\]

The signs can be checked without solving anything. Set \(u=0\) while \(T>T_a\). Stored energy must decrease. At equilibrium \(\dot T=0\), so a constant 20 W input and \(H=5\) W/K sustain a 4 K excess over the surroundings. This check will later reveal a faulty actuator calibration even if the transient looks smooth.

Separate gain, time scale and initial condition

Choose an operating point \((u_0,T_0)\) in equilibrium, keep the ambient temperature fixed and write \(v=u-u_0\), \(y=T-T_0\). Subtracting the equilibrium balance cancels the constant terms. Dividing by \(H\) gives \(\tau\dot y+y=Kv\), where \(K=1/H\) is the steady gain and \(\tau=C/H\) is a time. An input step of amplitude \(v_s\) moves the equilibrium to \(Kv_s\). Subtract this new equilibrium to leave a homogeneous decay equation. Integrating from zero time yields

\[y(t)=Kv_s+\big[y(0)-Kv_s\big]e^{-t/\tau}.\]

After one time constant, 36.8% of the initial distance to the new value remains. Reaching a specified fraction of the final temperature and reaching a tolerance relative to the final change are equivalent only if the output is initially zero in the chosen coordinates. Working in temperature deviations makes the convention explicit.

Recover a model from a step experiment

Start from equilibrium and increase the power by 30 W. The final rise is 6 K. At 20 s the rise is 2.36 K. The gain is \(6/30=0.20\) K/W, so \(H=5.0\) W/K. The remaining fraction is \((6-2.36)/6=0.607\). Taking logarithms gives \(\tau=-20/\ln(0.607)\simeq40\) s, hence \(C\simeq200\) J/K. At 60 s the predicted rise is \(6(1-e^{-1.5})=4.66\) K. A measurement at 60 s tests a prediction that was not used in the fit.

The same experiment can expose a missing state. Plot the logarithm of the remaining fraction against time. A single time constant gives a straight line with slope \(-1/\tau\). If the early trace has a shoulder but the late trace is straight, the sensor or a second thermal mass might be responsible. In the last few samples, the temperature difference in the logarithm may be comparable to measurement noise. Use those samples to estimate the final temperature rather than relying on their individual log slopes.

Transfer the argument to a rotating shaft

With shaft inertia \(J\), viscous drag \(b\) and driving torque \(u\), conservation of angular momentum gives \(J\dot\omega=u-b\omega\). A suspended flywheel with \(J=0.12\) kg·m² and \(b=0.030\) N·m·s/rad has a 4.0 s time constant. A 0.30 N·m step from rest eventually gives 10 rad/s. The equilibrium gain and transient have the same mathematical form as the thermal problem. Their units and the physical meaning of a disturbance differ. A load torque enters the shaft equation directly. A change in ambient temperature enters the tank balance through \(H\). This distinction matters once the loop must reject disturbances.

Linearise a gravity-drained tank around an operating level

A model identified with one small power step can be accurate locally and fail after the operating point shifts. A tank with a gravity outlet illustrates this without invoking an unknown internal mechanism. Let its horizontal cross-sectional area \(A\) be constant and inlet flow \(q\) be directly controlled. Approximate outlet flow by \(c\sqrt h\), where \(h\) is liquid level above the outlet datum and \(c\) includes effective opening area and gravity. With incompressible liquid, mass conservation reduces to \(A\dot h=q-c\sqrt h\). Both sides of this equation have units of volume per time.

At an equilibrium \(h_0>0\) the inlet is \(q_0=c\sqrt{h_0}\). Set \(h=h_0+\eta\) and \(q=q_0+u\), retaining only terms linear in the deviations. Since the tangent of \(c\sqrt h\) at \(h_0\) is \(c/(2\sqrt{h_0})\), the constant terms cancel and the incremental model is

\[A\dot\eta+k_h\eta=u,\qquad k_h=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{h_0}},\qquad K=1/k_h,\qquad \tau=A/k_h .\]

The gain \(K\) converts inlet-flow change into equilibrium level change, while the time constant contains the additional storage area. Increasing level makes the outlet characteristic flatter, increasing both gain and time constant. At an empty tank the square-root derivative is singular; the small-signal model around a positive level cannot be continued through that point.

Compare a small step with a finite change

Let \(A=0.20\,\mathrm{m^2}\), \(h_0=0.25\,\mathrm m\), and \(q_0=0.0010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\). Then \(c=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^{5/2}/s}\), \(k_h=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(K=500\,\mathrm{s/m^2}\) and \(\tau=100\,\mathrm s\). Increasing inlet flow by \(0.00010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) predicts a 0.050 m final rise in the linear model. The exact steady level is \((0.00110/0.0020)^2=0.3025\,\mathrm m\), a rise of 0.0525 m. Initially both models predict \(\dot h=0.00010/0.20=0.00050\,\mathrm{m/s}\), since the level has not yet changed. Immediate slope agreement therefore does not validate the predicted final gain.

Repeat the thought experiment for a 50% inlet increase. The linear result is 0.50 m absolute level, whereas the nonlinear equilibrium is 0.5625 m. The difference now matters for a level limit. An informative commissioning test uses small upward and downward steps of equal amplitude at more than one operating point. The tangent model predicts symmetric final increments; the measured curvature contributes a positive quadratic term to both absolute levels. Comparing that asymmetry with sensor uncertainty can distinguish a genuine level-dependent gain from a drifting measurement zero.

One can reconstruct the physical parameters independently from two parts of a measured step record. If a flow step of \(0.00010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) produces an initial level slope of \(0.00050\,\mathrm{m/s}\), the immediate mass balance gives \(A=\Delta q/\dot h(0)=0.20\,\mathrm{m^2}\), without knowing the outlet law. A sufficiently small steady rise of 0.050 m would separately give \(k_h=\Delta q/\Delta h=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). Their quotient predicts the 100 s time scale. A sensor mounted behind a narrow side passage could delay the observed initial rise, making this area estimate too large without changing the final level. Comparing a direct sight-glass record with the remote sensor helps locate that additional lag before adding another state to the tank model.

Distinguish delay from a slow first-order response

Three devices have the same DC gain and the same area under their step-error curve, two seconds, but represent different mechanisms. A transport delay contributes a dead interval; two identical first-order states give the factor \((1+t)e^{-t}\) in the step error.
