Chapter 1 · From a balance law to a measured plant

Predict a transient from conservation of energy, then use measurements to decide whether a first-order model is credible.

From first principles

Turn a thermal energy balance into a step response

A well-stirred body of heat capacity \(C\) stores the energy increment \(C\,dT\) for a small temperature increase \(dT\). Assume the ambient temperature \(T_a\) is constant, the heater supplies power \(u\), and heat loss is proportional to temperature excess with coefficient \(H>0\). Conservation of energy then gives a differential equation, with every term measured in watts.

\[C\dot T=u-H(T-T_a).\]

Hold the power at a constant value after a step. The new equilibrium requires zero stored-energy change, hence \(T_\infty=T_a+u/H\). Subtracting that constant solution leaves an equation proportional to the deviation itself, which integrates exponentially.

\[\frac{d(T-T_\infty)}{dt}=-\frac HC(T-T_\infty),\]
\[T(t)=T_\infty+[T(0)-T_\infty]e^{-t/\tau},\]
\[\tau=C/H .\]

The steady temperature rise per watt identifies \(1/H\). The time required for the residual deviation to fall to \(1/e\) identifies \(\tau\). Together these observations determine the stored heat capacity as \(C=H\tau\) under the assumed single-temperature model.

Derivation and applications

Identify a first-order time constant without knowing the final level

A thermometer in a stirred bath often stops recording before the new equilibrium is reached. Three equally spaced readings can still determine that equilibrium if a single relaxation dominates. Assume a fixed step in heat input, constant heat capacity and a linear heat-loss coefficient over the observed temperature range. Take the first observation after the input has finished changing. If the sampling interval is \(h\), the unknown remaining difference from equilibrium is multiplied by the same factor \(a\) over every interval.

\[T_j=T_\infty+(T_0-T_\infty)a^j,\]
\[a=e^{-h/\tau}.\]

Subtract successive readings. The unknown equilibrium cancels and the ratio of consecutive increments measures \(a\). The inversion uses differences of readings, so temperature offsets cancel as well. Once \(a\) is known, rearrange the one-step update to recover equilibrium in the original temperature scale.

\[a=\frac{T_2-T_1}{T_1-T_0},\]
\[\tau=-\frac{h}{\ln a},\]
\[T_\infty=\frac{T_1-aT_0}{1-a}.\]

A fourth reading tests the model against data excluded from the estimate. It can be predicted directly from the last increment. The condition for a stable monotonic first-order response is that \(a\) lie strictly between zero and one. Ratios near unity make the extrapolated final level very sensitive to measurement error.

\[T_{3,\rm pred}=T_2+a(T_2-T_1).\]

Worked example

Recover thermal capacity from a short record

A heater adds 6.00 W to a bath initially at equilibrium. At 20.0 s intervals the thermometer reads 20.0, 24.0 and 26.0 °C. The increments give \(a=2/4=0.500\), so \(\tau=28.9\) s and \(T_\infty=28.0\) °C. The heat-loss coefficient is \(6.00/8.00=0.750\) W/K. Multiplying by the time constant gives a lumped heat capacity of 21.6 J/K. The next predicted reading is 27.0 °C.

Second application

Test whether one relaxation fits a cooling record

An independent 5.00 s interval record reads 90.0, 80.0, 74.0 and 68.0 °C. The first three values give \(a=0.600\) and \(\tau=9.79\) s. They predict a fourth value of \(74.0-0.600(6.00)=70.4\) °C. The residual is \(-2.4\) K. With sensor repeatability of 0.1 K this discrepancy calls for checking the assumption of constant ambient temperature or a single thermal state.

Choose variables that describe stored energy

A heater warms a well-stirred tank. A sensor measures only the liquid temperature, and a controller will eventually change the heating power. Before designing the controller, ask what information fixes the future of the plant when the power is given. If the tank, its contents and the thermometer can be represented by one uniform temperature, a single initial temperature suffices. A slow thermometer or a heavy tank wall may require its own initial condition. This is a physical way of deciding how many states to put in a model.

Let \(C\) in J/K be the combined heat capacity, \(H\) in W/K the thermal conductance to the room, \(T_a\) the ambient temperature and \(u\) the delivered heater power. Over an interval \(dt\) the heater adds \(u\,dt\), the room receives \(H(T-T_a)\,dt\), and storage increases by \(C\,dT\). Taking the limit of a short interval gives

\[C\dot T=u-H(T-T_a).\]

The signs can be checked without solving anything. Set \(u=0\) while \(T>T_a\). Stored energy must decrease. At equilibrium \(\dot T=0\), so a constant 20 W input and \(H=5\) W/K sustain a 4 K excess over the surroundings. This check will later reveal a faulty actuator calibration even if the transient looks smooth.

Separate gain, time scale and initial condition

Choose an operating point \((u_0,T_0)\) in equilibrium, keep the ambient temperature fixed and write \(v=u-u_0\), \(y=T-T_0\). Subtracting the equilibrium balance cancels the constant terms. Dividing by \(H\) gives \(\tau\dot y+y=Kv\), where \(K=1/H\) is the steady gain and \(\tau=C/H\) is a time. An input step of amplitude \(v_s\) moves the equilibrium to \(Kv_s\). Subtract this new equilibrium to leave a homogeneous decay equation. Integrating from zero time yields

\[y(t)=Kv_s+\big[y(0)-Kv_s\big]e^{-t/\tau}.\]

After one time constant, 36.8% of the initial distance to the new value remains. Reaching a specified fraction of the final temperature and reaching a tolerance relative to the final change are equivalent only if the output is initially zero in the chosen coordinates. Working in temperature deviations makes the convention explicit.

Recover a model from a step experiment

Start from equilibrium and increase the power by 30 W. The final rise is 6 K. At 20 s the rise is 2.36 K. The gain is \(6/30=0.20\) K/W, so \(H=5.0\) W/K. The remaining fraction is \((6-2.36)/6=0.607\). Taking logarithms gives \(\tau=-20/\ln(0.607)\simeq40\) s, hence \(C\simeq200\) J/K. At 60 s the predicted rise is \(6(1-e^{-1.5})=4.66\) K. A measurement at 60 s tests a prediction that was not used in the fit.

The same experiment can expose a missing state. Plot the logarithm of the remaining fraction against time. A single time constant gives a straight line with slope \(-1/\tau\). If the early trace has a shoulder but the late trace is straight, the sensor or a second thermal mass might be responsible. In the last few samples, the temperature difference in the logarithm may be comparable to measurement noise. Use those samples to estimate the final temperature rather than relying on their individual log slopes.

Transfer the argument to a rotating shaft

With shaft inertia \(J\), viscous drag \(b\) and driving torque \(u\), conservation of angular momentum gives \(J\dot\omega=u-b\omega\). A suspended flywheel with \(J=0.12\) kg·m² and \(b=0.030\) N·m·s/rad has a 4.0 s time constant. A 0.30 N·m step from rest eventually gives 10 rad/s. The equilibrium gain and transient have the same mathematical form as the thermal problem. Their units and the physical meaning of a disturbance differ. A load torque enters the shaft equation directly. A change in ambient temperature enters the tank balance through \(H\). This distinction matters once the loop must reject disturbances.

Linearise a gravity-drained tank around an operating level

A model identified with one small power step can be accurate locally and fail after the operating point shifts. A tank with a gravity outlet illustrates this without invoking an unknown internal mechanism. Let its horizontal cross-sectional area \(A\) be constant and inlet flow \(q\) be directly controlled. Approximate outlet flow by \(c\sqrt h\), where \(h\) is liquid level above the outlet datum and \(c\) includes effective opening area and gravity. With incompressible liquid, mass conservation reduces to \(A\dot h=q-c\sqrt h\). Both sides of this equation have units of volume per time.

At an equilibrium \(h_0>0\) the inlet is \(q_0=c\sqrt{h_0}\). Set \(h=h_0+\eta\) and \(q=q_0+u\), retaining only terms linear in the deviations. Since the tangent of \(c\sqrt h\) at \(h_0\) is \(c/(2\sqrt{h_0})\), the constant terms cancel and the incremental model is

\[A\dot\eta+k_h\eta=u,\qquad k_h=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{h_0}},\qquad K=1/k_h,\qquad \tau=A/k_h .\]

The gain \(K\) converts inlet-flow change into equilibrium level change, while the time constant contains the additional storage area. Increasing level makes the outlet characteristic flatter, increasing both gain and time constant. At an empty tank the square-root derivative is singular; the small-signal model around a positive level cannot be continued through that point.

Compare a small step with a finite change

Let \(A=0.20\,\mathrm{m^2}\), \(h_0=0.25\,\mathrm m\), and \(q_0=0.0010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\). Then \(c=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^{5/2}/s}\), \(k_h=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(K=500\,\mathrm{s/m^2}\) and \(\tau=100\,\mathrm s\). Increasing inlet flow by \(0.00010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) predicts a 0.050 m final rise in the linear model. The exact steady level is \((0.00110/0.0020)^2=0.3025\,\mathrm m\), a rise of 0.0525 m. Initially both models predict \(\dot h=0.00010/0.20=0.00050\,\mathrm{m/s}\), since the level has not yet changed. Immediate slope agreement therefore does not validate the predicted final gain.

Repeat the thought experiment for a 50% inlet increase. The linear result is 0.50 m absolute level, whereas the nonlinear equilibrium is 0.5625 m. The difference now matters for a level limit. An informative commissioning test uses small upward and downward steps of equal amplitude at more than one operating point. The tangent model predicts symmetric final increments; the measured curvature contributes a positive quadratic term to both absolute levels. Comparing that asymmetry with sensor uncertainty can distinguish a genuine level-dependent gain from a drifting measurement zero.

One can reconstruct the physical parameters independently from two parts of a measured step record. If a flow step of \(0.00010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) produces an initial level slope of \(0.00050\,\mathrm{m/s}\), the immediate mass balance gives \(A=\Delta q/\dot h(0)=0.20\,\mathrm{m^2}\), without knowing the outlet law. A sufficiently small steady rise of 0.050 m would separately give \(k_h=\Delta q/\Delta h=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). Their quotient predicts the 100 s time scale. A sensor mounted behind a narrow side passage could delay the observed initial rise, making this area estimate too large without changing the final level. Comparing a direct sight-glass record with the remote sensor helps locate that additional lag before adding another state to the tank model.

Chapter 2 · Transfer functions and connections between systems

Build a model of a sensor-actuator chain from individual balances and determine which responses a measurement can reveal.

From first principles

Eliminate the feedback signal to find a closed-loop transfer

A linear transfer function relates an input perturbation to an output perturbation with zero initial deviations. For example, a first-order balance \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\) transforms to \((\tau s+1)Y=KU\) because the time derivative transforms to \(sY\) under that initial condition. Thus the plant transfer \(P\) is

\[P(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}=\frac K{1+\tau s}.\]

Add a controller \(C\) acting on negative feedback from a sensor with transfer \(H\). The error compares reference \(R\) with measured output \(HY\). Write each intermediate relation before eliminating it; feedback makes the command depend on the very output being solved for.

\[E=R-HY,\]
\[U=CE,\]
\[Y=PC(R-HY).\]

Collect the terms containing \(Y\) and divide by the reference. The common denominator is the characteristic factor of the connected loop.

\[\frac YR=\frac{PC}{1+PCH}.\]

This signal algebra assumes the subsystems' transfers remain valid after connection. In an actual mechanical or hydraulic interconnection, loading may change the plant equations themselves and must be included before forming the blocks.

Derivation and applications

Eliminate the states of a motor with electrical back reaction

A voltage-controlled motor resembles a circuit followed by a mechanical stage until the rotor is allowed to generate a back voltage. Consider a rigid rotor with inertia \(J\), viscous drag \(b\), winding resistance \(R\) and inductance \(L\). With constant torque and back-emf coefficients \(k_t,k_e\), use positive armature current for positive torque. Ignore load torque for the voltage-to-speed transfer and start with zero perturbations about a steady operating point. The transformed balances are

\[(Ls+R)I=V-k_e\Omega,\]
\[(Js+b)\Omega=k_t I .\]

Multiply the second balance by the electrical impedance and substitute the first. The back voltage is proportional to the output and opposes the applied voltage. It therefore contributes a positive constant to the denominator.

\[\frac{\Omega(s)}{V(s)}=\frac{k_t}{LJs^2+(Lb+RJ)s+Rb+k_tk_e}.\]

If the electrical time \(L/R\) is short relative to the mechanical transient of interest, current approximately follows its instantaneous resistive balance. The resulting speed gain and time constant both reflect extra damping supplied by current flowing in response to a speed change.

\[K_\omega=\frac{k_t}{Rb+k_tk_e},\]
\[\tau_\omega=\frac{J}{b+k_tk_e/R}.\]

The reduced model has one state fewer and retains the exact DC speed gain. It predicts an instantaneous current change at a voltage step, so estimates of the initial current derivative still require the full electrical state.

Worked example

Predict the unloaded speed and rise time

Take \(R=2.00\) Ω, \(L=10.0\) mH, \(J=0.0100\) kg m², \(b=0.0200\) N m s/rad and \(k_t=k_e=0.200\) in consistent SI units. The feedback adds \(0.200^2/2=0.0200\) N m s/rad. The mechanical time becomes 0.250 s against an electrical time of 0.00500 s. The gain is 2.50 rad/s/V, so a 4.00 V step produces a final 10.0 rad/s.

Second application

Infer viscous drag from a DC test

A second motor has \(R=4.00\) Ω and \(k_t=k_e=0.100\) in SI units. A steady 6.00 V produces 30.0 rad/s with no external load. Back voltage consumes 3.00 V, leaving 0.750 A of current and 0.0750 N m torque. Balancing viscous drag gives \(b=0.0750/30.0=0.00250\) N m s/rad. The exact transfer expression then gives \(K_\omega=5.00\) rad/s/V, consistent with the measured voltage and speed.

OneSimulation laboratory · Predict the settling time and offset of a feedback loop

Eliminate a state in the transform domain

Chapter 1 gave a differential equation and an initial condition for a single energy store. Cascading several components leads to simultaneous equations. The Laplace transform changes the time derivative into \(sY(s)-y(0)\). Applied to \(\tau\dot y+y=Kv\), this gives \((\tau s+1)Y=KV+\tau y(0)\). With zero initial deviation the ratio \(G(s)=Y/V=K/(\tau s+1)\) describes the forced response. For nonzero initial state the extra term predicts free decay. This is why an experimental transfer function should identify a particular operating point and initialisation procedure.

The pole at \(s=-1/\tau\) locates the decaying exponential. The DC gain is \(G(0)=K\). The high-frequency magnitude of this model tends to zero because the energy store cannot change its state instantaneously in response to bounded power. A transfer function can thus be assessed from dimensions, a static balance and a short-time thought experiment before any curve fitting.

Connect the sensor and plant

A sensor may have its own lag. Let the actual plant output be \(y\) and the indicated value \(z\). With plant \(G_p=1/(4s+1)\) and sensor \(G_s=1/(s+1)\), the individual relations \(Y=G_p U\) and \(Z=G_s Y\) give \(Z/U=1/[(4s+1)(s+1)]\). For a unit step divide by \(s\); partial fractions or substitution in the differential equation gives

\[z(t)=1-\frac43e^{-t/4}+\frac13e^{-t}.\]

Check \(z(0)=0\), \(z(\infty)=1\) and \(\dot z(0)=0\). The actual plant slope begins at \(1/4\) per second, but the indicated output starts with a horizontal tangent. At 4 s the plant has reached 0.632 and the indicator only 0.516. A designer who assigns the entire delay to the plant may demand an unnecessarily powerful actuator. A designer who ignores the sensor will underestimate the phase lag in feedback.

Close a loop algebraically

Let \(C(s)\) be a controller and use the indicated output in negative feedback, so \(U=C(R-G_sY)\). Substitute this in \(Y=G_pU\) and collect all terms multiplying \(Y\). The result is \(Y/R=G_pC/(1+G_pCG_s)\). The same denominator occurs in other responses, but the numerator depends on where the signal enters. A disturbance \(D\) added at the plant input gives \(Y/D=G_p/(1+G_pCG_s)\) with zero reference. Measurement noise added to the feedback signal gives \(Y/N=-G_pC/(1+G_pCG_s)\). Forcing every disturbance to enter at the reference would predict the wrong amplitudes.

Recognise an invisible mode

A rational expression may simplify by cancellation. Consider a cascade of an integrator-like unstable stage \(1/(s-1)\) and a downstream stage \((s-1)/(s+2)\). The algebraic transfer from external input to final output is \(1/(s+2)\) at zero initial conditions. The state between stages can still grow as \(e^t\). A small mismatch in either factor also leaves an unstable component in the observed output. Physically realising two factors gives internal variables that still need checking even when their product has a well-behaved step response.

For a stable example, the measured lag above can be estimated with a first-order fit from late observations alone. The resulting single pole describes the slow tail but loses the zero initial slope. Choosing the level of model detail therefore requires stating the quantity of interest: a long-time energy estimate and a rapid feedback correction need different bandwidths. The next chapter uses the true characteristic polynomial to determine stability before assessing accuracy of tracking.

Derive two interacting liquid levels before drawing blocks

It is tempting to represent every pair of connected tanks as a cascade of two independent first-order blocks. If the pipe between the tanks is submerged at both ends, the downstream level applies a back pressure to the upstream tank. Consider small deviations \(h_1,h_2\) from equilibrium, with positive forward intertank flow \(q_{12}=(h_1-h_2)/R_1\) and outlet flow \(q_o=h_2/R_2\). The incremental hydraulic resistances measure level difference per flow and have units s/m². They can represent local tangent slopes of nonlinear valve characteristics. Take constant tank areas \(A_1,A_2\) and a perturbation \(u\) of inlet flow.

The two balances are \(A_1\dot h_1=u-q_{12}\) and \(A_2\dot h_2=q_{12}-q_o\). For zero initial deviations, write \(H_1=(1+R_1/R_2+A_2R_1s)H_2\) from the second balance and substitute it into the first. Collecting equal powers of \(s\) leads to

\[\frac{H_2}{U}=\frac{R_2}{A_1A_2R_1R_2s^2+[A_1(R_1+R_2)+A_2R_2]s+1}.\]

The static gain equals \(R_2\), since at equilibrium all three volume rates are equal and \(h_2=R_2u\). The coefficient of \(s\) contains an interaction term absent if tank 1 drains freely to atmosphere. It describes the return action of the downstream level on intertank flow. An individual transfer block measured with downstream level fixed does not capture this additional path unless it is reconnected explicitly.

Distinguish a loaded connection from a cascade in a step test

Use \(A_1=A_2=0.10\,\mathrm{m^2}\) and \(R_1=R_2=100\,\mathrm{s/m^2}\). Each isolated area–resistance product is 10 s. The interacting transfer function is \(100/(100s^2+30s+1)\). Its decay rates are \((-30\pm\sqrt{900-400})/200=-0.0382\) and \(-0.2618\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), giving time constants 26.18 and 3.82 s. Two independent identical drains would instead give \(100/(10s+1)^2\), with repeated time constant 10 s. Both have the same final gain and initial zero slope at the downstream tank.

For a sustained flow increase of \(0.00050\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\), both models predict a 0.050 m final rise. Using the two interacting time constants, the normalised step value at 20 s is \(1-[26.18e^{-20/26.18}-3.82e^{-20/3.82}]/(26.18-3.82)\simeq0.455\), giving 22.8 mm. The independent cascade predicts \(1-(1+2)e^{-2}=0.594\), or 29.7 mm. Recording intermediate levels therefore distinguishes two plant structures that an equilibrium calibration cannot separate. As \(R_1\) approaches zero the two levels equalise rapidly; the limiting first-order time constant becomes \((A_1+A_2)R_2\), consistent with a single tank of combined storage area.

There is also a check that uses both level sensors without differentiating noisy data. Add the original tank balances and integrate over the first 20 s: \(A_1[h_1(20)-h_1(0)]+A_2[h_2(20)-h_2(0)]=\int_0^{20}(u-q_o)\,dt\). The intertank flow disappears identically. In this experiment the added inlet volume is 10.0 litres, so added liquid stored plus additional outlet delivery must equal that quantity. If the two measured storage changes already exceed 10 litres, no passive choice of intertank resistance can explain the data. Conversely, outlet and storage agreement validates the external balance but leaves the internal connection undetermined. The intermediate time reading and knowledge of whether the interconnecting pipe is submerged provide the extra evidence needed for choosing the internal transfer model.

Problem sheet 1 · Modelling a plant and interpreting its signals

Use balances, local linearisation and block diagrams to decide which parameters a commissioning test actually measures.

1. A step on a heated plate

A plate initially in equilibrium at 22 °C receives a further 40 W. Its final temperature is 32 °C and it reaches 28.32 °C after 50 s. (a) Estimate the incremental static gain and time constant. (b) Recover heat capacity and heat-loss conductance in a one-capacity model. (c) Describe a second experiment that could expose a temperature-dependent loss.

Recover the physical coefficients

Write \(C\dot\theta+G\theta=\Delta P\) about the initial equilibrium. The 10 K final increment gives \(K=0.25\) K/W and \(G=4\) W/K. The recorded increment is 63.2% of the final value, giving \(\tau=50\) s and \(C=\tau G=200\) J/K. Repeat a small equal step about a higher initial temperature with the same airflow. A change in incremental gain indicates a changed local loss slope; a second slow exponential could instead indicate another stored-energy state. One measurement at 50 s cannot distinguish these hypotheses.

2. A tank drained by gravity

An open tank of cross-section \(A=0.50\) m² discharges \(q_\mathrm{out}=c\sqrt H\) with \(c=0.020\) \(\mathrm{m}^{5/2}/\mathrm{s}\) and level \(H\) in metres. (a) Find the inflow at a 1 m operating level. (b) Derive the incremental transfer function and predict the rise for a sustained additional 0.002 m³/s. (c) Check the new equilibrium exactly and identify the sign of the approximation error.

Linearise the outflow at the operating level

The balance is \(A\dot H=q-c\sqrt H\). At \(H_0=1\) m, \(q_0=0.020\) m³/s. The slope of the outflow is 0.010 m²/s, so \(\delta H/\delta q=1/(0.50s+0.010)\) in the Laplace domain. Its time constant is 50 s and its final incremental prediction is 0.20 m. The exact new level is \((0.022/0.020)^2=1.21\) m. The local model underestimates the rise by 0.01 m because the outflow curve becomes flatter as the level rises.

3. A slow sensor on a fast drive

The transfer functions from torque to speed and from speed to the recorded signal are \(P=2/(1+0.5s)\) and \(H=1/(1+2s)\). (a) Determine the transfer from torque to the recording and its DC gain. (b) For a unit step derive the recording in time. (c) Would the dominant time visible in this record be a good plant-only identification?

Distinguish dynamics by tracing the signal

The recording has transfer \(PH=2/[(1+0.5s)(1+2s)]\) and DC gain 2. Partial fractions for a unit step give \(y_m=2[1-\tfrac43 e^{-t/2}+\tfrac13e^{-2t}]\). Its value and slope are zero at switch-on, whereas physical speed has initial slope 4 in the chosen units. The late transient is dominated by the sensor's 2 s time constant. An independently calibrated sensor model or a faster sensor separates its effect from the 0.5 s plant response.

Chapter 3 · Closed-loop poles, damping and performance

Translate a time-domain requirement into acceptable poles and find when additional gain destabilises a higher-order plant.

From first principles

Connect characteristic roots to decay and oscillation

Near an equilibrium, many closed-loop positioning stages reduce to a constant-coefficient second-order equation. Consider the free response after the reference has become constant. Let \(x\) be deviation from its final value, \(\omega_n>0\) the natural frequency and \(\zeta\) the dimensionless damping ratio. Try an exponential response because time derivatives of an exponential reproduce the same waveform.

\[\ddot x+2\zeta\omega_n\dot x+\omega_n^2x=0,\]
\[x=e^{st}\ \Longrightarrow\ s^2+2\zeta\omega_n s+\omega_n^2=0 .\]

For \(0<\zeta<1\) solving the quadratic produces conjugate roots. Their real part sets the exponential decay rate and their imaginary part sets the observed angular oscillation frequency.

\[s_{1,2}=-\zeta\omega_n\pm i\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},\]
\[x=e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}(A\cos\omega_dt+B\sin\omega_dt).\]

Here \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) and the initial displacement and velocity set \(A,B\). A measured period gives \(\omega_d\) while the decaying peak envelope gives \(\zeta\omega_n\). Stable poles describe decay of free modes. The numerator of a forced response also influences how prominently each mode appears.

Derivation and applications

Separate a cubic stability boundary from oscillation frequency

Feedback gain can move an oscillatory pair across the imaginary axis even with every characteristic coefficient positive. Take a continuous linear unity-feedback loop whose open-loop transfer is \(K/[s(s+a)(s+b)]\) with positive \(a,b,K\). The integral mode can represent a position obtained from velocity; the other poles can represent actuator and measurement dynamics. Add unity to the loop transfer and clear its denominator to obtain the closed-loop polynomial.

\[p(s)=s^3+(a+b)s^2+ab\,s+K .\]

A marginal oscillation has \(s=i\omega\) with real nonzero \(\omega\). Separate the real and imaginary parts. The imaginary equation sets the frequency without requiring the gain, while the real equation selects the gain that supports it.

\[K-(a+b)\omega^2=0,\]
\[\omega(ab-\omega^2)=0,\]
\[K_{\rm crit}=ab(a+b).\]

At this gain the cubic factors exactly into a real stable pole and a pair of purely imaginary roots. The cubic Routh first column determines which side of the boundary is stable. Its first two entries are positive, making the sign of the third entry decisive for positive gain.

\[p_{\rm crit}(s)=(s+a+b)(s^2+ab),\]
\[\text{first column: }1,\ a+b,\ ab-\frac{K}{a+b},\ K .\]

Below the threshold all entries have the same sign. Above it there are two sign changes and therefore two right-half-plane roots. The model treats actuator authority as unlimited and omits nonlinear friction; a finite observed oscillation amplitude requires additional physics beyond the pole crossing.

Worked example

Predict the onset period of a positioning loop

Set \(a=2.00\) s⁻¹ and \(b=8.00\) s⁻¹. Marginal gain is \(K_{\rm crit}=160\) s⁻³. The oscillation has frequency \(4.00\) rad/s, hence period \(2\pi/4.00=1.57\) s. At \(K=120\) s⁻³ the third entry is \(16-12=4.00\), giving stability. At \(K=180\) s⁻³ it is \(-2.00\) and the pair grows.

Second application

Recover the two lag rates from a marginal test

Another loop reaches marginality at \(K=60.0\) s⁻³ with a measured oscillation of \(\sqrt{10}\) rad/s. The rates have product \(ab=10.0\) s⁻² and sum \(K/(ab)=6.00\) s⁻¹. These observations cannot describe two positive real lag rates because the maximum product at fixed sum is \(6^2/4=9.00\) s⁻². The failed consistency check flags model structure or calibration before attributing a unique time constant to either stage.

Relate polynomial coefficients to observed motion

For a normalised second-order system, the free response satisfies \(\ddot y+2\zeta\omega_n\dot y+\omega_n^2y=0\). Substitution of \(e^{st}\) gives \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). The real part sets the decay rate. For \(0<\zeta<1\), the imaginary part \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) sets the time between successive extrema. Doubling \(\omega_n\) at fixed \(\zeta\) halves all characteristic times but leaves the fraction of overshoot unchanged.

Assume a unit DC gain, a constant numerator and zero initial displacement and velocity. The step response is \(y=1-e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}[\cos(\omega_dt)+(\zeta\omega_n/\omega_d)\sin(\omega_dt)]\). Differentiate the bracket together with its envelope. The cosine terms cancel and the remaining derivative is proportional to \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_dt)\). The first peak occurs at \(t_p=\pi/\omega_d\). Substituting that time gives the fractional overshoot

\[M_p=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right).\]

Design for a specified peak

Suppose a positioning axis may overshoot a 10 mm command by at most 1 mm. Solving the previous expression for the limiting damping ratio gives \(\zeta=-\ln M_p/\sqrt{\pi^2+(\ln M_p)^2}=0.591\). At \(\omega_n=5\) rad/s the corresponding poles are approximately \(-2.96\pm4.03i\) per second and the first peak occurs at 0.779 s. A nominal oscillatory second-order system with this damping just meets the 1 mm bound. A zero near the dominant poles, a saturated drive or another slow pole changes the transient, so the result should be checked against the full model after the poles are chosen.

A settling specification differs from a peak specification. The absolute normalised error stays below the envelope \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). To guarantee a 2% settling band using this bound, solve \(t\ge-\ln[0.02\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}]/(\zeta\omega_n)\). This conservative time for the example is about 1.40 s. The commonly used \(4/(\zeta\omega_n)\) is a convenient estimate. The envelope bound spells out both the tolerance and its assumptions.

Follow a gain change on a third-order plant

A plant with two lags and an integrator has open-loop transfer \(K/[s(s+1)(s+2)]\) in units normalised to a common time base. Close unity negative feedback. The characteristic polynomial is \(s^3+3s^2+2s+K\). Its coefficients are all positive for \(K>0\), which rules out positive real roots but allows an unstable complex pair. Form the first column of the Routh array: \(1,\ 3,\ (6-K)/3,\ K\). All entries must have the same sign for strict stability. Thus \(0<K<6\).

At \(K=6\) the polynomial factors as \((s+3)(s^2+2)\). The step excites an undamped pair at angular frequency \(\sqrt2\), alongside a decaying component. At \(K=7\) the first-column signs change twice, so two roots have crossed into the right half-plane. The input-output gain alone did not reveal this upper bound. Additional lags allow feedback to arrive out of phase with motion. Chapter 4 reaches the same type of conclusion by examining sine-wave responses, which can often be measured even when coefficients of a polynomial are poorly known.

Find the effect of a finite closed-loop zero

Pole locations specify the available exponential terms of a causal linear response. Their coefficients depend on the numerator and initial condition. To isolate this distinction, choose two positive times \(\tau_1\ne\tau_2\) and a transfer function \(G(s)=(1+T_zs)/[(1+\tau_1s)(1+\tau_2s)]\). It has unit static gain for every \(T_z\), so changing the numerator neither moves the poles nor changes the final unit-step response. For zero initial state and a unit step \(Y(s)=G(s)/s\). Write \(Y=1/s+A/(s+1/\tau_1)+B/(s+1/\tau_2)\).

The initial value \(y(0^+)=0\) gives \(A+B=-1\). The initial-slope theorem gives \(\dot y(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}sG(s)=T_z/(\tau_1\tau_2)\); equivalently \(-A/\tau_1-B/\tau_2=T_z/(\tau_1\tau_2)\). Solving and transforming back gives

\[y(t)=1-\frac{\tau_1-T_z}{\tau_1-\tau_2}e^{-t/\tau_1}+\frac{\tau_2-T_z}{\tau_1-\tau_2}e^{-t/\tau_2}.\]

For \(T_z=\tau_1\) one mode has zero coefficient in this particular input–output map. A physical internal state with that mode can still respond to a nonzero initial condition or another input. With \(T_z<0\) the zero lies in the right half-plane and the initial output slope opposes the eventual response. That inverse response can be a serious limitation when interpreting an early excursion as a sign error in the actuator.

Time the undershoot of two competing actuator paths

Consider an actuator that changes a measured quantity through a slow positive effect and an immediate faster opposing effect. Let \(\tau_1=4\) s, \(\tau_2=1\) s and \(T_z=-2\) s. The poles are both stable, and the output approaches one under a unit command. Yet the response is \(y=1-2e^{-t/4}+e^{-t}\), with initial slope \(-0.50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Set \(\dot y=0.5e^{-t/4}-e^{-t}=0\). It follows that \(e^{-3t/4}=0.5\), so the minimum occurs after \(t=4\ln2/3=0.924\,\mathrm s\). Its value is \(1-2(0.794)+0.397=-0.191\).

For a 10 mm final position change this model briefly produces about 1.91 mm motion in the wrong direction. A physical travel limit 1.0 mm behind the starting point would therefore be violated even though the real parts of both poles meet a settling-time target. Compare instead \(T_z=0\) with identical poles and DC gain: the initial slope vanishes and the response rises monotonically. Before changing controller gain to correct an observed undershoot, measure which pathway acts first. Inverting the right-half-plane zero with a causal stable feedforward filter is impossible, since exact inversion would introduce an unstable pole.

For this response the first crossing back through the initial position is later than the minimum. Let \(u=e^{-t/4}\); solving \(1-2u+u^4=0\) gives the trivial root \(u=1\) at release and a further root near 0.544. Thus the measured position remains on the wrong side for about \(-4\ln(0.544)=2.44\) s. A simple diagnostic that samples only once after two seconds would label the overall gain as negative. At one minute the same test reads nearly the full positive change. Recording the full waveform distinguishes an initial inverse response from an overall sensor polarity error. A disturbance entering the two paths at a different location can exhibit yet another zero, which is why a zero should always be quoted for a specified input and output.

Assign the poles of a spring-mounted positioning stage with PID

A positioning loop can measure displacement, accumulate position error and damp measured motion. Let a stage of mass \(m\), viscous damping \(c\) and return spring \(k\) obey \(m\ddot y+c\dot y+ky=u+d\), with actuator force \(u\) and disturbance force \(d\). The controller uses \(e=r-y\), \(\dot z=e\), and \(u=K_pe+K_iz-K_d\dot y\). Differentiating the measured position supplies damping without turning a step in commanded position into an impulsive force command.

To find the natural motion, set \(r=d=0\) and substitute \(u=-K_py+K_iz-K_d\dot y\). Move the feedback terms to the left and differentiate once to eliminate \(z\), using \(\dot z=-y\). The resulting characteristic polynomial is

\[m s^3+(c+K_d)s^2+(k+K_p)s+K_i.\]

The derivative gain adds viscous damping, the proportional gain adds stiffness, and closing an integral loop adds a state. At any stable equilibrium with a constant reference, \(\dot z=0\) requires \(y=r\). The integrator can then supply whatever constant force the spring and disturbance require, within actuator limits. The transient still depends on how the reference enters the controller.

Take \(m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(c=3.0\,\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}\), \(k=5.0\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). Suppose the desired poles are \(-2,-3,-4\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Multiplying the three factors gives the desired polynomial \(2(s+2)(s+3)(s+4)=2s^3+18s^2+52s+48\). Comparing coefficients produces \(K_d=15\,\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}\), \(K_p=47\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), \(K_i=48\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}s^{-1}}\). For a 20 mm command from rest the initial proportional force is \(0.940\,\mathrm N\). At the final resting position with \(d=0\), the spring needs \(0.100\,\mathrm N\). \(K_i z\) supplies it and the proportional contribution is zero. A filtered derivative introduces an additional pole, so this ideal coefficient match must be checked again against the implemented controller bandwidth.

Chapter 4 · Frequency response, disturbance rejection and robustness

A loop that tracks slowly changing commands may amplify a disturbance at another frequency. Work from the sinusoidal response to a defensible choice of bandwidth.

From first principles

Read sinusoidal tracking and disturbance rejection from one loop

For a stable linear time-invariant plant driven at angular frequency \(\omega\), time derivatives of \(e^{i\omega t}\) multiply it by \(i\omega\). After transients decay, the ratio of complex output and input amplitudes is therefore the transfer function evaluated at \(s=i\omega\). Its magnitude measures the amplitude ratio; its argument measures phase shift. With an ideal negative-feedback sensor define loop transfer \(L=PC\) for plant \(P\) and controller \(C\).

\[U=C(R-Y),\]
\[Y=PU+D=L(R-Y)+D .\]

Here \(D\) is a disturbance added to the physical output. Rearrangement separates the contributions of the command and disturbance at the same frequency.

\[Y=\frac{L}{1+L}R+\frac{1}{1+L}D,\]
\[T=\frac{L}{1+L},\]
\[S=\frac{1}{1+L}.\]

Adding the two ratios gives \(S+T=1\). Large loop magnitude generally permits tracking and disturbance reduction at low frequency. When the loop phasor approaches \(-1\), the common denominator becomes small; phase information is therefore essential when interpreting a measured gain curve.

Derivation and applications

Infer damping and bandwidth from a resonant frequency sweep

A measured sinusoidal peak need not occur at the undamped natural frequency. Consider a stable second-order plant with positive static gain \(K\), no finite zero and transfer \(G(s)=K\omega_n^2/(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)\). A slow swept-sine test measures the steady response after transients have decayed. With the frequency ratio \(r=\omega/\omega_n\), the squared magnitude has a denominator that is a quadratic in \(r^2\).

\[\frac{K^2}{|G(i\omega)|^2}=(1-r^2)^2+4\zeta^2r^2=1+(4\zeta^2-2)r^2+r^4 .\]

Find the minimum of the denominator at a positive frequency by differentiating with respect to \(r^2\). A positive root requires \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2\). Substitution at this root gives the peak gain relative to the measured static gain.

\[r_p^2=1-2\zeta^2,\]
\[M_p=\frac{|G(i\omega_p)|}{K}=\frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} .\]

A different frequency occurs where the gain has fallen to \(K/\sqrt2\). Set the squared denominator to two and select the positive root in \(r^2\). This bandwidth is referenced to the DC gain, even when the system has a large resonant peak.

\[r_{\rm bw}^2=1-2\zeta^2+\sqrt{(1-2\zeta^2)^2+1}.\]

The location of the measured peak approaches \(\omega_n\) only for small damping. As damping approaches the threshold from below, the peak moves to zero frequency and becomes difficult to distinguish from the static response.

Worked example

Interpret a peak in a stage measurement

A stage has \(\zeta=0.300\) and \(\omega_n=20.0\) rad/s. The predicted peak occurs at \(20\sqrt{0.820}=18.1\) rad/s. Its amplitude is \(1/[0.600\sqrt{0.910}]=1.75\) times the static response. The DC-referenced bandwidth is \(20\sqrt{0.820+\sqrt{1.6724}}=29.1\) rad/s. A test recording only the peak frequency would therefore underestimate the bandwidth.

Second application

Estimate damping without measuring phase

A second stage shows a peak twice its static gain at 12.0 Hz. Solving \(4\zeta^2(1-\zeta^2)=1/4\) on the resonant branch gives \(\zeta^2=(1-\sqrt{3/4})/2\) and \(\zeta=0.259\). Its natural frequency is \(12.0/\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}=12.9\) Hz. The phase should be \(-90^\circ\) there, offering a separate frequency-response check on the no-zero model.

Read a complex gain as an experiment

Drive a stable linear plant with a small sinusoid about its operating point. Once the transient dies away, the output is a sinusoid at the same angular frequency. Inserting \(u=Ue^{i\omega t}\) and \(y=Ye^{i\omega t}\) in the differential equation gives \(Y=P(i\omega)U\). The modulus gives the amplitude ratio and the argument gives the time shift as a fraction of a cycle. The units on a Bode plot matter: for a dimensional plant gain its magnitude in decibels presupposes a reference gain. The product around a feedback loop is dimensionless.

For \(P=K/(1+s\tau)\), the modulus falls to \(K/\sqrt{2}\) at \(\omega=1/\tau\), where its phase is \(-45^\circ\). Above this break, doubling frequency roughly halves the output amplitude. Adding a transport delay multiplies the frequency response by \(e^{-i\omega T}\); the magnitude is unchanged while phase falls by \(\omega T\) radians. A magnitude measurement alone cannot detect this mechanism.

Derive the sensitivity functions

Let \(u=C(r-y-n)\) for command \(r\) and sensor noise \(n\), with disturbance \(d\) added directly to the physical plant output \(y=Pu+d\). Substitution gives

\[S=(1+PC)^{-1},\quad T=PC(1+PC)^{-1},\qquad y=Tr+Sd-Tn .\]

The identity \(S+T=1\) holds for complex numbers at each frequency. A slow output disturbance is strongly rejected when the loop gain is large and far from \(-1\). Sensor noise at the same frequency is then nearly reproduced by the physical output with opposite sign. At high frequencies a deliberately small loop gain protects the output against measurement noise. If the disturbance enters at the actuator instead, its output transfer function is \(PS\). Mark its point of entry before reading any plot.

How much delay can a loop tolerate?

For a unity feedback loop take \(L(s)=4/[s(1+s)]\), with time in seconds. A unity-gain crossover satisfies \(\omega^2(1+\omega^2)=16\). The positive root gives \(\omega_c=\sqrt{(\sqrt{65}-1)/2}=1.88\) rad/s. At that frequency the phase is \(-90^\circ-\tan^{-1}1.88=-152.0^\circ\). A further \(28.0^\circ\) of lag would bring the Nyquist curve through \(-1\), where the closed-loop denominator vanishes. For pure delay alone the first corresponding delay is about \(0.489/1.88=0.260\) s. A 0.10 s delay consumes about \(10.8^\circ\) at crossover.

This estimate uses this plant's unique gain crossover and its initially stable closed loop. Multiple crossovers, unstable plant poles and poorly measured resonances require inspection of the entire Nyquist curve. Even before a pole crosses into instability, the distance to \(-1\) controls amplification: \(|S|=1/|1+L|\). For example \(L=-0.9+0.1i\) gives \(|S|=7.07\). A loop with low steady error may therefore magnify a narrow band of disturbance by a factor of seven.

Translate specifications into a measurement

Suppose a speed drive must reject load variations below 0.5 rad/s while its encoder contains noise above 20 rad/s. First locate the disturbance and noise on the block diagram. Specify maximum allowed output amplitude per unit amplitude at each point. Shape the gain, then inspect the two resulting transfer functions across the entire frequency range, including the transition between bands. Raising proportional gain alone shifts the crossover into regions of additional lag. A measured resonance there can set the achievable speed even when the motor model at low frequency is excellent.

Derive the phase contributed by a lead compensator

Suppose a plant has enough low-frequency authority to reject a slowly varying disturbance, but its phase at the desired gain crossover leaves too little damping margin. A real lead compensator \(C(s)=K(1+s/\omega_z)/(1+s/\omega_p)\) with \(0<\omega_z<\omega_p\) contributes phase \(\phi=\arctan(\omega/\omega_z)-\arctan(\omega/\omega_p)\). It also raises gain towards high frequencies. A useful design therefore fixes the desired crossover, calculates the new phase there and then accounts for the gain change rather than assuming crossover stays where it was.

Differentiate phase with respect to \(\ln\omega\). Its stationary point satisfies \((\omega/\omega_z)/[1+(\omega/\omega_z)^2]=(\omega/\omega_p)/[1+(\omega/\omega_p)^2]\), giving \(\omega_m=\sqrt{\omega_z\omega_p}\). Let \(\alpha=\omega_z/\omega_p<1\). At this frequency the magnitude ratio excluding \(K\) is \(1/\sqrt\alpha\) and \(\sin\phi_{\max}=(1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)\). One can consequently choose a target phase, infer \(\alpha\), centre the lead at crossover and determine \(K\) from \(|CP|=1\).

The increased gain at high frequency can pass measurement noise into the actuator. Check the control-signal sensitivity as well as the output’s disturbance attenuation, particularly when the plant attenuates high frequencies and the sensor has its own noise floor.

Design a compensator from a measured frequency point

Take the normalised positioning plant \(P(s)=1/[s(1+0.2s)]\) and target crossover 5 rad/s. Its magnitude there is \(1/[5\sqrt2]=0.1414\); phase is \(-90^\circ-\arctan1=-135^\circ\). A constant gain chosen for that crossover would leave 45° phase margin. Allow an extra 15° of phase for a target 60° nominal margin before measurement delay. Choosing 15° peak lead gives \(\alpha=(1-\sin15^\circ)/(1+\sin15^\circ)=0.589\). The zero and pole are placed at \(5\sqrt\alpha=3.84\) and \(5/\sqrt\alpha=6.52\) rad/s.

The lead factor’s magnitude at crossover is \(1/\sqrt{0.589}=1.303\), so \(K=1/[0.1414(1.303)]=5.43\). The loop crosses with phase −120°, leaving the intended nominal margin. A previously omitted 20 ms pure delay adds \(-5(0.020)=-0.100\) rad, or −5.73°, at this point without changing magnitude. The resulting margin is approximately 54.3°. The compensator gain tends to \(K/\alpha=9.22\) at high frequency, compared with the proportional-only crossover gain 7.07. A noisy encoder can therefore change the actuator demand when this seemingly modest phase correction is installed. Verify the measured return ratio near crossover and observe the actuator spectrum above it after implementation.

Estimate the timing allowance explicitly by converting the margin reserve back into seconds. If the specification requires at least 45°, a nominal 60° design has 15° available for a pure delay at its unchanged crossover. The maximum delay under this model is \(15\pi/[180(5)]=0.0524\,\mathrm s\), leaving about 32 ms beyond the omitted 20 ms. This is a local phase-margin budget and assumes a single relevant gain crossover and a stable Nyquist configuration. An unmodelled resonance can create another crossover with a smaller margin. The frequency measured by a signal generator may be labelled in hertz; the relevant crossover here is \(5/(2\pi)=0.796\) Hz. Mistakenly using five hertz in the delay calculation would consume more than six times the actual phase and lead to an unnecessarily severe timing constraint.

Use a winding count when the uncontrolled plant is unstable

A gain or phase margin read at a single crossover can hide unstable modes if the open loop already has right-half-plane poles. For negative unity feedback the closed-loop characteristic condition is \(F(s)=1+L(s)=0\). Trace the boundary of the right half-plane up the imaginary axis from \(-i\infty\) to \(+i\infty\) and return on a large semicircle to the right. This contour runs clockwise. Assume a strictly proper rational loop with no poles or characteristic roots on the contour, and no hidden unstable pole cancellations.

A factor \(s-z\) with \(z\) inside contributes one clockwise turn of \(F\). An enclosed denominator factor contributes a turn in the opposite direction. If \(Z\) is the number of right-half-plane zeros of \(F\) and \(P\) its number of right-half-plane poles, the net counterclockwise winding of \(F\) about zero is \(P-Z\). Equivalently, count the turns \(N\) of \(L\) about \(-1\) with counterclockwise positive. Then \(Z=P-N\). Closed-loop stability requires \(N=P\) under this stated contour and sign convention.

Consider a linearized temperature imbalance with loop transfer \(L(s)=K/(s-a)\), where \(a=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(K>0\) has the same unit. The unregulated imbalance grows exponentially. Along \(s=i\omega\), the real and imaginary parts of \(L\) are \(-Ka/(a^2+\omega^2)\) and \(-K\omega/(a^2+\omega^2)\). Completing the square shows a circle centred at \(-K/(2a)\) on the real axis with radius \(K/(2a)\). Increasing frequency traces its upper half first and then the lower half, one counterclockwise turn. At \(K=3\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) the circle spans \([-1.5,0]\) and encloses \(-1\). Hence \(P=1\), \(N=1\), \(Z=0\). At \(K=1.5\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) the circle spans only \([-0.75,0]\), giving \(Z=1\). The explicit characteristic root \(s=a-K\) confirms both conclusions. At \(K=a\) the locus passes through \(-1\) at zero frequency and the closed loop has a pole at the origin.

Chapter 5 · States, reachability and estimation

Two mechanisms can produce the same output at one instant and different outputs an instant later. Determine what the actuator can change and what a sensor history can reveal.

From first principles

Build a position and velocity state model from force balance

For a moving mass \(m\) acted on by commanded force \(u\), a spring \(k\) and viscous drag \(b\), Newton's second law relates acceleration to present displacement and velocity. Assume constant coefficients, a rigid base and small linear motions. Defining \(x_1\) as position and \(x_2\) as velocity turns the second-order relation into two first-order state equations.

\[\dot x_1=x_2,\]
\[m\dot x_2=u-kx_1-bx_2 .\]

State feedback chooses force from the measured or estimated state. For \(u=-k_px_1-k_vx_2+nr\), the command \(r\) also supplies a reference input. Collecting terms yields the closed-loop position equation.

\[m\ddot x_1+(b+k_v)\dot x_1+(k+k_p)x_1=nr .\]

Comparing its characteristic polynomial with a desired \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2\) determines both feedback coefficients directly.

\[k_p=m\omega_n^2-k,\]
\[k_v=2m\zeta\omega_n-b.\]

With \(n=k+k_p\), a constant position reference is reproduced at equilibrium in the nominal unloaded model. Position measurements alone cannot specify the instantaneous force law until velocity is also measured or reconstructed from the sensor history.

Derivation and applications

Separate observer error from the state-feedback poles

When only position is measured, feedback must operate on estimated position and velocity. Consider a continuous linear plant \(\dot x=Ax+Bu\), \(y=Cx\), with constant matrices and a known applied input. Use a full-order observer with correction proportional to the difference between measured and predicted output. Subtract its state equation from the plant with estimation error \(e=x-\hat x\).

\[\dot{\hat x}=A\hat x+Bu+L(y-C\hat x),\]
\[\dot e=(A-LC)e .\]

The known input cancels exactly. Now apply \(u=-K\hat x\) and use \(\hat x=x-e\). The physical plant sees a perturbing term proportional to the residual estimation error. Writing the pair of equations together exposes an upper triangular block structure.

\[\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}x\\e\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A-BK&BK\\0&A-LC\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\e\end{pmatrix}.\]

Taking the determinant of the characteristic matrix gives the product of the two diagonal determinants. For a controllable and observable nominal model the feedback poles and observer poles can therefore be chosen separately. Faster estimation may amplify sensor noise, which this deterministic pole calculation does not quantify.

\[p(s)=\det(sI-A+BK)\det(sI-A+LC).\]

Worked example

Place poles for a position stage

Use the unit-mass double integrator \(\dot x_1=x_2\), \(\dot x_2=u\), \(y=x_1\). The feedback polynomial is \(s^2+k_2s+k_1\) and the observer polynomial is \(s^2+l_1s+l_2\). For feedback poles \(-2,-3\) s⁻¹ select \(K=(6,5)\). For observer poles \(-8,-9\) s⁻¹ select \(L=(17,72)^\mathsf T\) in consistent units. The complete system has all four requested poles. The observer correction on the velocity state is large because it must reconstruct velocity from a position discrepancy.

Second application

Predict the effect of an unknown constant acceleration

For a separate double integrator take \(L=(10,24)^\mathsf T\) and an unmodelled constant acceleration \(d=0.120\) m/s². At steady state \(0=e_2-10e_1\) and \(0=-24e_1+d\), giving position error 5.00 mm and velocity error 0.0500 m/s. Stable observer poles remove initial-state errors, while this persistent disturbance supplies a continuing input to the error dynamics. A sensor recording the innovation should see the 5.00 mm bias.

From balances to independent states

A cart of mass \(m\) moves against a spring \(k\) and damper \(b\). Choose \(x=(q,v)^\mathsf{T}\), displacement and velocity. Newton's law gives \(\dot q=v,\ m\dot v=-kq-bv+u\). A state contains the initial information needed to propagate the motion with specified input. Measuring displacement at one instant supplies only half of that information. The state choice is not unique: replacing velocity by momentum changes the matrix entries, yet the predicted displacement history is the same.

\[\dot x=Ax+Bu,\quad y=Cx,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-b/m\end{pmatrix},\ B=\binom{0}{1/m},\ C=(1\;\;0).\]

With zero input the transition \(e^{At}\) propagates initial conditions. Integrating the effect of forces applied at each earlier time gives \(x(t)=e^{At}x(0)+\int_0^t e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds\). This decomposition explains why both the autonomous motion and the input direction enter control design.

Test an actuator and a sensor

Over a short time, the vectors \(e^{At}B=B+tAB+\cdots\) show the directions that a force can excite. In two dimensions \(B,AB\) suffice by the characteristic equation of \(A\). For the cart their columns have determinant \(-1/m^2\); a finite mass allows the actuator to change both position and velocity over time. This rank test assumes arbitrary inputs and sufficient time. Getting to a distant position in a very short time can still exceed an actuator limit.

With a known input, output and its first derivative contain \(Cx\) and \(CAx\). Here they are position and velocity, so the observability matrix has full rank. As a counterexample, consider two uncoupled tanks with equal decay rates, driven equally, and one sensor measuring the sum of levels. The difference evolves autonomously and never enters the sum. Recording a longer noiseless trace with that sensor cannot recover the initial difference. Moving a sensor or adding a distinct excitation has to break the symmetry responsible for the missing information.

Place poles and restore the reference scale

Take \(m=1,\ b=1,\ k=2\) in compatible SI units. Apply \(u=-f_q q-f_v v+n r\). The homogeneous characteristic polynomial is \(s^2+(1+f_v)s+(2+f_q)\). Desired poles at \(-2,-3\) give \(f_v=4,\ f_q=4\) by coefficient matching. At constant reference the velocity vanishes and \((2+f_q)q=nr\), so \(n=6\) gives \(q=r\) in the nominal model. A constant unmodelled force shifts this equilibrium. State feedback with a static reference multiplier alone supplies no mechanism to learn that force.

Reconstruct velocity without differentiating noisy measurements

An observer runs the model and uses the disagreement with the measured output to correct its state. Write \(\dot{\hat x}=A\hat x+Bu+H(y-C\hat x)\). In the ideal deterministic model the estimation error obeys \(\dot e=(A-HC)e\). Here its polynomial is \(s^2+(1+h_1)s+(h_1+2+h_2)\). Desired observer poles \(-4,-5\) give \(h_1=8,\ h_2=10\). Thus an initial state error decays even though the sensor supplies only displacement.

With the control law evaluated at \(\hat x\), the combined state and estimation-error dynamics are block triangular. The two sets of assigned poles therefore survive in the ideal linear model. Faster observer poles also give sensor noise larger correction gains. Compare simulated or measured velocity estimates during a quiet hold and during a sharp manoeuvre before accepting the faster design. A missing mode, a noisy estimate and the inability to measure an initial state exactly have distinct engineering implications.

For additional examples of state estimation and output feedback, see Åström and Murray’s Feedback Systems.

Add a state that estimates a constant load through tracking error

A feedforward reference gain can remove steady offset for an exact plant model. When an unknown constant load enters at an actuator, the equilibrium input changes. Integrating the tracking error gives the controller a memory with which to supply the missing steady effort. For a state model \(\dot x=Ax+Bu+Ed,\ y=Cx\), augment the state by \(z\) with \(\dot z=r-Cx\). If the augmented closed loop is stable and reaches equilibrium, then \(\dot z=0\) enforces \(y=r\). This argument assumes constant feasible \(r,d\), no actuator saturation and an unbiased measurement.

Take a velocity servo \(\dot v=-av+bu+d\) with \(u=-k_vv+k_i z\); use \(v\) in m/s, \(u\) in volts and \(z\) in metres. The augmented characteristic polynomial follows by differentiating the velocity equation for a constant disturbance, or directly from its two-by-two state matrix:

\[\dot v=-(a+bk_v)v+bk_i z+d,\quad \dot z=r-v,\qquad p(s)=s^2+(a+bk_v)s+bk_i .\]

The augmented controllability test is important in more general plants: a servo integrator at zero frequency cannot arbitrarily impose an output that the input cannot sustain. Even here \(b=0\) would make the augmented system uncontrollable. Physical travel or voltage limits can also restrict the equilibrium although the ideal linear test passes.

Place the integral servo poles and check the required voltage

Assume \(a=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(b=3\,\mathrm{(m/s^2)/V}\) and target poles at −3 and −4 s⁻¹. Matching \(p(s)=s^2+7s+12\) gives \(k_v=(7-2)/3=1.667\,\mathrm{V\,s/m}\) and \(k_i=12/3=4.0\,\mathrm{V/m}\). At command \(r=1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and constant opposing disturbance \(d=-0.60\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\), equilibrium requires \(u=(ar-d)/b=0.867\,\mathrm V\). The integrator settles at \(z=(u+k_vr)/k_i=0.633\,\mathrm m\). The unit assigned to this internal state is integrated velocity error, not physical servo displacement.

For a pure disturbance step with reference held fixed, eliminating \(z\) gives \(V(s)/D(s)=s/[(s+3)(s+4)]\) in deviation variables. A −0.60 m/s² step therefore produces \(\Delta v(t)=-0.60(e^{-3t}-e^{-4t})\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Its minimum occurs at \(t=\ln(4/3)=0.288\,\mathrm s\), with a speed error of about −0.063 m/s before recovery. An actuator limited to ±0.75 V could not maintain the stated final speed even though the assigned poles are stable. Integral feedback enforces the available equilibrium, and a command governor or anti-windup logic becomes necessary when the physical equilibrium lies outside the reachable input range.

The disturbance response also gives a way to separate a constant accelerative load from a sensor bias. Once a real opposing load has been rejected, an independent speed measurement still reads the requested 1.0 m/s while the final actuator voltage has risen by \(-d/b=0.20\) V above its no-load value. A constant positive sensor bias of 0.05 m/s instead drives the measured velocity to the set point with a true speed of 0.95 m/s. If both effects are allowed but only the controller’s own sensor is logged, a final reading exactly at set point does not identify the source of error. An independent tachometer or a measured load torque supplies the missing observation. Integral convergence cannot by itself validate sensor calibration.

Chapter 6 · Sampling, saturation and commissioning

Carry a continuous-time design onto a sampled actuator, predict where its operating assumptions fail, and distinguish an observed final value from an equilibrium.

From first principles

Integrate a held command across one sample

A sampled actuator holds its command fixed until the next update. Consider a stable first-order plant \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\) with constant coefficients and sample interval \(h\). Let \(y_k\) be the value at the start of interval \(k\) and \(u_k\) the command held throughout it. During that interval the equilibrium for the applied command is the constant \(Ku_k\). Subtract it and integrate the homogeneous decay over exactly one interval.

\[\frac d{dt}(y-Ku_k)=-\frac{y-Ku_k}{\tau},\]
\[y_{k+1}-Ku_k=e^{-h/\tau}(y_k-Ku_k).\]

Set \(a=e^{-h/\tau}\). The resulting discrete plant is an exact sample-to-sample model under a zero-order hold. For proportional feedback with unit reference scale, substitute \(u_k=g(r_k-y_k)\) to see where the sampled free response pole lies.

\[y_{k+1}=a y_k+K(1-a)u_k,\]
\[z_{\rm cl}=a-K(1-a)g .\]

A sample-to-sample deviation is multiplied repeatedly by \(z_{\rm cl}\), so decay requires \(|z_{\rm cl}|<1\). This condition depends on the time at which the command is applied. A full sample of computational delay changes the state update and its characteristic equation.

Derivation and applications

Quantify the phase and attenuation introduced by a hold

A digital controller sends a piecewise-constant command to a continuous actuator. Even ideal sampling and instantaneous arithmetic do not make the resulting fundamental sinusoid identical to the requested one. Consider samples of a complex sinusoid at period \(h\). A zero-order hold outputs each sample unchanged over the following interval. For times \(t=kh+s\) within interval \(k\), the ratio of held signal to the desired continuous sinusoid is periodic in the within-sample time \(s\).

\[u_h(kh+s)=e^{i\omega kh},\]
\[\frac{u_h(kh+s)}{e^{i\omega(kh+s)}}=e^{-i\omega s},\]
\[0\leq s<h .\]

The zero-frequency Fourier coefficient of this periodic ratio gives the component at the original sinusoidal frequency. The remaining coefficients produce spectral images spaced by the sample frequency. Integrate the ratio over one hold interval and factor out the midpoint phase.

\[H_0(i\omega)=\frac1h\int_0^h e^{-i\omega s}ds=e^{-i\omega h/2}\frac{\sin(\omega h/2)}{\omega h/2}.\]

Below Nyquist the sine factor is positive. The fundamental therefore has phase equivalent to a delay of half a sample and a frequency-dependent gain. For small \(\omega h\), the sine expansion makes the leading attenuation quadratic while phase lag is linear.

\[\arg H_0=-\frac{\omega h}{2},\]
\[|H_0|=1-\frac{(\omega h)^2}{24}+O((\omega h)^4).\]

This calculation concerns the fundamental component of a held command. Additional computation latency and sensor filtering accumulate their own phase. Frequencies approaching Nyquist also require examining the sampled closed-loop dynamics directly.

Worked example

Check a servo's phase allowance

At 50.0 Hz crossover and 1.00 kHz sampling, \(\omega h/2=\pi(50/1000)=0.1571\). The hold contributes \(-9.00^\circ\) and its fundamental magnitude is 0.9959. A further full sample of computation delay contributes \(-18.0^\circ\), giving \(-27.0^\circ\) combined at that frequency. The small gain change would conceal this phase cost in a magnitude-only measurement.

Second application

At a proposed crossover of 80.0 Hz, allocate at most 6.00° lag to the hold. Since the magnitude of the phase in degrees is \(180fh\), the interval must satisfy \(h\leq6/(180\times80)=0.417\) ms, or a sample rate of at least 2.40 kHz. At this limit the fundamental magnitude is \(\sin(6^\circ)/(6\pi/180)=0.9982\). The resulting condition addresses the hold alone.

Derive the held-input model

A processor reads a sensor every \(h\) seconds and holds its most recent command until the next sample. Solve \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku_k\) exactly from \(kh\) to \((k+1)h\) with this constant input. The result is \(y_{k+1}=a y_k+K(1-a)u_k\), where \(a=e^{-h/\tau}\). This recursion is exact at the sampling instants for the assumed hold. Taking a large Euler step through the continuous closed-loop equation describes a different experiment. A real controller has only the sampled information available when it computes \(u_k\).

With proportional control \(u_k=K_p(r_k-y_k)\) the discrete pole is \(z=a-KK_p(1-a)\). Stability requires \(|z|\lt1\). A negative pole of modulus less than one alternates sign each update while decaying. For \(K=1,\ \tau=1\) s and \(K_p=3\), \(h=0.2\) s gives \(z=0.275\); \(h=0.8\) s gives \(z=-1.203\). The same nominal continuous design gives radically different sampled records. For positive gain the upper gain limit is \((1+a)/[K(1-a)]\). Additional computation and transport delays lower the available margin further.

Why a saturated integrator remembers the past

Let \(v_k=K_p e_k+I_k\) be the requested command, and \(u_k\) its value clipped to the actuator range. An unconstrained integrator adds \(hK_i e_k\) to \(I_k\) at every sample. While an unreachable command holds a heater at its upper limit, positive error keeps accumulating; when the command becomes reachable again the stored integral may keep the heater fully on. This delayed recovery is called windup. Logging only actual heater power hides the growth of the internal demand. Log both signals and the integral state.

A back-calculation update feeds the clipping discrepancy back to the integrator:

\[I_{k+1}=I_k+h[K_i e_k+(u_k-v_k)/T_t].\]

When the request exceeds the upper stop, the second term is negative. At fixed error and fixed clipping level, this update attracts the requested command toward a value consistent with saturation, provided its discrete tracking dynamics are stable. For example, with \(h=0.1\) s, \(K_i=1\), \(e=2\), \(u=5\), \(v=8\), and \(T_t=0.5\) s, the integral increment is \(-0.4\), compared with \(+0.2\) without correction. Tuning \(T_t\) must account for the sample interval and actuator dynamics. Anti-windup cannot make an infeasible steady torque available.

Derive an exact sampled stability boundary with a delayed command

A digital controller may acquire a sample at the start of an interval but only deliver its computed command at the following update. The one-sample computation delay then introduces an additional dynamic state. Consider a first-order plant \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\) with an ideal zero-order hold and sampling period \(T\). Its exact sample-to-sample relation is \(y_{k+1}=a y_k+b u_k\), with \(a=e^{-T/\tau}\) and \(b=K(1-a)\). Assume proportional negative feedback about a zero reference and express gains with dimensions chosen so that \(bg\) is dimensionless.

With negligible computation delay, \(u_k=-gy_k\) yields one pole \(a-bg\), so stability requires \(|a-bg|<1\). With a one-sample delay the applied command is \(u_k=-gy_{k-1}\), giving \(y_{k+1}=a y_k-bg y_{k-1}\). Substitution of a trial sequence \(z^k\) gives the characteristic polynomial \(z^2-az+bg\). For a real monic quadratic \(z^2+c_1z+c_0\), the unit-disc conditions are \(1+c_1+c_0>0,\ 1-c_1+c_0>0,\ 1-c_0>0\). For \(0<a<1,\ b>0,\ g>0\) only the last condition limits increasing gain: \(bg<1\).

Once the delayed poles form a complex pair, their product equals their squared modulus. That makes the bound physically transparent: as \(bg\) reaches one the oscillation no longer decays between samples. This mechanism is invisible if a continuously implemented controller is analysed and merely evaluated at plotting intervals.

Compare two software schedules at the same sampling rate

Take \(K=1\), \(\tau=0.50\,\mathrm s\), and \(T=0.10\,\mathrm s\). The exact coefficients are \(a=0.8187,\ b=0.1813\). Without delay the positive gain limit is \((1+a)/b=10.03\). With a delayed command it is \(1/b=5.52\). A gain of 7.0 gives a stable immediate pole \(0.8187-7(0.1813)=-0.450\). The negative sign predicts an alternating error whose sample values decrease in magnitude.

Apply the same gain through the delayed schedule. The characteristic polynomial becomes \(z^2-0.8187z+1.2689\). Its roots are complex with modulus \(\sqrt{1.2689}=1.126\), implying about 12.6% growth per sample in the envelope. An input limit may eventually bound the motion or produce a limit cycle, but it does not restore local linear stability around the unsaturated equilibrium. A controller test that records sensor samples and commanded values with separate timestamps can distinguish this delay from a sign error or an incorrectly discretised plant. Reduce the delayed gain to 4.0 and the predicted modulus becomes 0.852; the same clock can operate stably with a different response speed.

To recognise the delayed oscillation in a recorded trace, compute its angle as well as its modulus. At gain seven each real part is \(a/2=0.4094\). Dividing by the modulus 1.126 gives \(\cos\theta=0.364\), so \(\theta=1.20\,\mathrm{rad/sample}\). The apparent oscillation period is about \(2\pi(0.10)/1.20=0.52\) s. By comparison the immediate negative real pole produces a two-sample alternating pattern with period 0.20 s. A controller log showing roughly five samples per growing cycle accords with the delayed implementation, whereas alternating every sample points towards the immediate model operating too aggressively. This interpretation uses the synchronised physical actuator output; a software variable computed today for application tomorrow may have a different timestamp from the hardware.

Why a fast vibration can appear as a slow feedback error

An analogue position sensor may detect a genuine low-frequency displacement and a much faster frame vibration. Sampling can make the two contributions indistinguishable. For a sampling interval \(T_s=1/f_s\), evaluate a complex sinusoid at sample \(n\):

\[e^{i2\pi(f+kf_s)nT_s}=e^{i2\pi fnT_s}e^{i2\pi kn}=e^{i2\pi fnT_s},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.\]

A real cosine is also unchanged by reversal of frequency, so its apparent frequency can be folded into \(0\le f_a\le f_s/2\). At \(f_s=100\,\mathrm{Hz}\), a 92 Hz cosine gives precisely the samples of an 8 Hz cosine with reversed phase angle. If both are present, their sampled amplitudes combine according to phase. Removing 8 Hz in software would remove the true 8 Hz motion as well. Filter the continuous sensor signal before sampling if the higher component must be attenuated independently.

Suppose an encoder sees an unwanted sinusoidal displacement of amplitude \(0.50\,\mathrm{mm}\) at 92 Hz, while feedback must track motion through 8 Hz. A fourth-order analogue Butterworth low-pass filter with cutoff 20 Hz has magnitude \(|H(f)|=[1+(f/20\,\mathrm{Hz})^8]^{-1/2}\). At 92 Hz the ratio is 0.00223, or approximately \(-53.0\,\mathrm{dB}\): the folded displacement amplitude is only \(1.12\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). At 8 Hz the ratio is 0.99967, so a \(0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) desired component retains about \(99.97\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). These are separate amplitude tests on the analogue filter. If both vibrations persist, the sampled 8 Hz component can vary with their relative phase.

A passband magnitude close to one does not bound the filter's phase lag. A controller designed with substantial gain near 8 Hz must also include the filter's complex frequency response in its phase-margin calculation. Aliasing, sample-and-hold delay and computational delay are three different contributions to the observed loop dynamics.

Chapter 7 · Following poles as a controller gain changes

Use the geometry of the characteristic equation to discover which transient specifications one adjustable gain can meet.

From first principles

Derive the angle and gain conditions for a root locus

For a linear negative-feedback system with open-loop transfer \(K G(s)\), the free closed-loop motion has a nonzero amplitude only when the characteristic factor vanishes. Treat \(K\) as a real nonnegative adjustable parameter. A candidate pole \(s\) is on the root locus precisely when the complex number \(K G(s)\) equals the real number \(-1\).

\[1+KG(s)=0,\]
\[|KG(s)|=1,\]
\[\arg G(s)=(2j+1)\pi .\]

Factor the fixed transfer into real positive leading gain \(g\), zeros \(z_i\) and poles \(p_j\). Multiplication adds complex angles and division subtracts them. The resulting geometric test and gain value are

\[\sum_i\arg(s-z_i)-\sum_j\arg(s-p_j)=(2\ell+1)\pi,\]
\[K=\frac{\prod_j|s-p_j|}{g\prod_i|s-z_i|}.\]

A point can have an attractive decay rate or oscillation frequency yet fail the angle condition for every positive gain. Once that condition holds, the distance products give the unique gain at an ordinary, finite point of the locus. At zero gain the branches start at the open-loop poles by continuity.

Derivation and applications

Predict where a root-locus branch approaches its asymptote

A pole sketch can show the direction in which a gain change sends fast modes. Consider real-coefficient negative feedback with characteristic equation \(D(s)+KN(s)=0\). Take monic polynomials with \(n\) open-loop poles \(p_j\), \(m\) finite zeros \(z_j\), \(n>m\), and positive adjustable gain \(K\). Include repeated roots with their multiplicities and absorb any constant numerator factor into the gain. For large \(|s|\), expand the product of root factors and divide the leading terms.

\[\frac{D(s)}{N(s)}=s^{n-m}\left[1-\frac{\sum_jp_j-\sum_jz_j}{s}+O(s^{-2})\right].\]

Write \(q=n-m\). A shifted power \((s-\sigma)^q\) has the same first two large-\(s\) coefficients if \(q\sigma\) equals the difference of root sums. The remaining characteristic equation for distant branches then reduces to a power equal to negative real gain.

\[\sigma=\frac{\sum_jp_j-\sum_jz_j}{q},\]
\[(s-\sigma)^q\simeq -K .\]

Taking the \(q\) roots gives the asymptote directions. Complex poles and zeros come in conjugate pairs, so the centroid \(\sigma\) lies on the real axis. These are asymptotic guides; a branch at finite gain generally curves towards its line.

\[s\simeq \sigma+K^{1/q}\exp\!\left[i\frac{(2j+1)\pi}{q}\right],\]
\[j=0,\ldots,q-1 .\]

Worked example

Identify a high-gain difficulty in a three-lag loop

For poles at \(-1,-2,-6\) s⁻¹ and no finite zeros, \(q=3\) and \(\sigma=-3\) s⁻¹. The three angles are 60°, 180° and 300°. Two branches ultimately enter the right half-plane. As a check the exact polynomial is \(s^3+9s^2+20s+12+K\); cubic stability demands \(9(20)>12+K\), or \(K<168\). The sketch predicted a finite gain limit and the polynomial supplies its exact value.

Second application

Recover a missing zero from measured asymptotes

A separate system has poles at \(0,-4,-8\) s⁻¹ and one real zero. Its two high-gain branches approach vertical lines with real coordinate \(-4.50\) s⁻¹. The centroid equation gives \(2(-4.50)=-12-z\), hence \(z=-3.00\) s⁻¹. The remaining finite branch tends to that zero as gain grows. Vertical asymptotes alone identify the pole excess; their real intercept supplies the missing location.

A family of closed-loop systems

The earlier pole calculations chose a gain and found the poles. Reverse that question: if an engineer turns one gain dial continuously, which parts of the complex plane can the poles ever visit? For unity negative feedback write the open-loop transfer as \(K N(s)/D(s)\), with \(N,D\) coprime and \(K\ge0\). The closed-loop characteristic equation is \(D(s)+K N(s)=0\). At a trial complex point away from an open-loop zero, the required gain is \(-D(s)/N(s)\). The point is attainable only if this number is real and nonnegative. Its magnitude gives the gain; its phase supplies a separate feasibility test.

At \(K=0\) branches begin at open-loop poles. At very large gain, finite roots must approach zeros of \(N\). If the denominator has greater degree, the remaining roots go to infinity. Since a real-coefficient polynomial has conjugate roots, the locus is symmetric about the real axis. These observations put strong constraints on a sketch even before computing roots.

Derive a breakaway and read the transient

Take a positioning plant \(P=1/[s(s+4)]\) with proportional gain \(K\). The closed-loop poles are \(s=-2\pm\sqrt{4-K}\). They start at 0 and −4, approach each other along the real axis and meet at −2 when \(K=4\). Equivalently the real-axis gain \(K(s)=-s(s+4)\) has a stationary point at −2. Above the breakaway the complex pair moves vertically, so raising gain increases oscillation frequency while leaving the exponential envelope \(e^{-2t}\) unchanged.

The constant term gives \(\omega_n=\sqrt K\) and the linear coefficient gives \(\zeta=2/\sqrt K\). A specification of decay rate 3 s⁻¹ and damped frequency 4 rad/s would require poles \(-3\pm4i\). Since every complex pole here has real part −2, no choice of proportional gain meets both requirements. Failure to find such a gain numerically is a statement about controller structure.

Add a second degree of freedom

Feed back measured velocity as well as position: \(u=K_p(r-y)-K_v\dot y\). The closed-loop denominator becomes \(s^2+(4+K_v)s+K_p\); the reference numerator is just \(K_p\). For desired poles \(-3\pm4i\) the polynomial is \(s^2+6s+25\), hence \(K_v=2,\ K_p=25\). The derivative acts only on measured output, so a discontinuous reference does not directly request an impulse. Differentiating a noisy measurement can nevertheless demand excessive actuator effort. A separate velocity sensor or a filtered estimate changes the high-frequency dynamics and must be included in the design check.

Include the numerator when interpreting a pole plot

Moving a controller zero also reshapes the locus because it changes the angle of \(N/D\). A zero in the reference-to-output map changes step-response residues even if the poles coincide with a previous design. In particular a positive real zero imposes a severe tracking constraint. For the stable unit-DC transfer \(G=(1-s/z)/(1+s\tau)^2\) with \(z>0\), the unit-step initial slope is \(\lim_{s\to\infty}sG(s)=-1/(z\tau^2)\). Yet the final output is positive. The initial motion in the wrong direction follows directly from the zero. Increasing gain to make the initial transient faster cannot remove that geometry.

Place a controller zero from a specified root

A proportional controller moves poles along the root locus of its plant. A chosen design point off that locus can sometimes be made reachable with a controller zero. For a unity-feedback plant \(P(s)=1/[s(s+2)]\), take an ideal proportional–derivative compensator \(C(s)=K(s+z)\) with real \(K,z>0\). The locus condition \(1+C(s)P(s)=0\) becomes \(K=-s(s+2)/(s+z)\). For a desired nonreal pole \(s_d\), this ratio must be real and positive. This single complex constraint is equivalent to one phase and one magnitude condition.

Choose \(s_d=-3+4i\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so the target characteristic polynomial is \(s^2+6s+25\). Coefficient matching is particularly transparent for this second-order plant: the characteristic polynomial is \(s^2+(2+K)s+Kz\). Equating coefficients gives \(K=4\) and \(z=6.25\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Geometrically the zero at −6.25 supplies precisely the missing phase at the specified point. Substituting \(s_d\) in \(-s(s+2)/(s+6.25)\) recovers positive real gain 4, an independent check that this is the positive-gain branch.

The target pair has \(\omega_n=5\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(\zeta=0.60\). The ideal controller contributes derivative gain 4 and proportional gain 25 in consistent normalised units. The closed-loop reference numerator contains the zero, so predicting exact overshoot requires the complete response even though the poles now match the design point.

Determine the consequence of giving differentiation a finite bandwidth

The ideal derivative gain grows without bound at high frequency. A real implementation can use \(C_f=25+4s/(1+s/20)\), placing a filter pole at −20 s⁻¹ on the derivative path. Combining terms gives \(C_f=(25+5.25s)/(1+s/20)\), which shifts the controller zero to \(-25/5.25=-4.76\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The characteristic equation of the actual loop is

\[s(s+2)(1+s/20)+25+5.25s=0,\qquad s^3+22s^2+145s+500=0 .\]

The target point no longer satisfies it: inserting \(-3+4i\) gives \(28+96i\), a nonzero residual in the monic characteristic polynomial. The implementation remains stable by the cubic Routh test because all coefficients are positive and \(22(145)>500\), but its exact transient differs. The high-frequency controller gain approaches \(25+4(20)=105\), now finite and directly relevant when estimating actuator activity from encoder noise. Placing the filter much farther left reduces the difference near the designed poles while increasing this asymptotic noise gain. A design specification with both pole placement and an allowable actuator-noise level must therefore include the filter during synthesis rather than adding it only in software.

A reference ramp distinguishes this controller from a high static-gain proportional loop that happens to yield similar dominant poles. With unity feedback and plant integrator, the ramp error to \(r(t)=vt\) tends to \(v/K_v\) provided the closed loop is stable, where \(K_v=\lim_{s\to0}s C(s)P(s)\). Both ideal and filtered designs have \(C(0)=25\), giving \(K_v=25/2=12.5\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). A 5 mm/s position ramp therefore leaves a 0.40 mm asymptotic tracking error. Moving the derivative filter changes fast behaviour while leaving this low-frequency comparison unchanged. If a much smaller ramp error is required, speed feedforward or additional low-frequency loop gain must be assessed separately; the selected complex poles by themselves do not establish that tracking specification.

Chapter 8 · Uncertainty envelopes and robust stability

Turn a bounded discrepancy between plant and model into a frequency-by-frequency test of the feedback design.

From first principles

Derive a feedback bound from a fractional plant error

A frequency-dependent model error matters through the feedback path in which it enters. Suppose an actual plant can be represented as \(P=P_0(1+W\Delta)\), with nominal plant \(P_0\), a known stable weight \(W\) and stable uncertainty satisfying \(|\Delta(i\omega)|\leq1\). Use a fixed controller \(C\) and assume the nominal interconnection is internally stable. Define \(L_0=CP_0\) and expand the perturbed characteristic factor.

\[1+CP=1+L_0+L_0W\Delta=(1+L_0)(1+T_0 W\Delta),\]
\[T_0=\frac{L_0}{1+L_0}.\]

The second factor describes how the unknown part feeds back around the nominal closed loop. If the magnitude of its variable term stays strictly below one at every frequency, it cannot reach the critical value \(-1\). The small-gain condition for stable interconnected factors is

\[\sup_\omega |T_0(i\omega)W(i\omega)|<1 .\]

This gives a sufficient certificate for every uncertainty in the specified class. The bound permits less modelling error where the complementary sensitivity is large. A failed inequality identifies a missing certificate; determining actual instability requires the particular plant error or a tighter description of it.

Derivation and applications

Represent a transport-delay error as multiplicative uncertainty

A frequency sweep of a conveyor or thermal transport path may fit the gain well while leaving an uncertain delay. Suppose the actual stable plant is \(P(s)=P_0(s)e^{-s\Delta t}\), with a nominal model containing the accurately known part of the delay. Let the extra delay lie between zero and \(d\). At sinusoidal frequency \(\omega\), define the fractional model error as \(E=P/P_0-1\). Factoring the difference of the two unit phasors gives

\[E(i\omega)=e^{-i\omega\Delta t}-1=-2i\,e^{-i\omega\Delta t/2}\sin(\omega\Delta t/2).\]

This is a chord on the unit circle. Its magnitude grows with delay until the phasors point in opposite directions. Maximising over the stated delay interval therefore gives a monotone envelope which stays at two after the interval first contains a half-cycle delay.

\[W_d(\omega)=\max_{0\leq\Delta t\leq d}|E|=\begin{cases}2\sin(\omega d/2),&\omega d\leq\pi,\\2,&\omega d\geq\pi.\end{cases}\]

For nominal loop \(L_0=C P_0\) the perturbation multiplies the complementary sensitivity \(T_0=L_0/(1+L_0)\) in the characteristic equation. If the nominal closed loop is internally stable and the bounded error is stable, keeping the product below unity at all frequencies gives a sufficient robust-stability test.

\[1+CP=(1+L_0)(1+T_0E),\]
\[|T_0(i\omega)|W_d(\omega)<1 .\]

At low frequencies the envelope approaches \(\omega d\). This dependence on frequency explains how an almost exact steady gain can coexist with a substantial feedback discrepancy near crossover.

Worked example

Audit a transport path at one frequency

A thermal experiment bounds extra delay by 20.0 ms. At \(\omega=30.0\) rad/s the worst relative error is \(2\sin(0.300)=0.591\). If \(|T_0|=1.40\), the pointwise product is 0.827, within the bound at that frequency. Repeating at every relevant frequency is necessary for the full small-gain conclusion; this single value establishes only the local allowance.

Second application

Derive a timing requirement from a measured peak

At 40.0 rad/s another loop has \(|T_0|=2.00\). The allowable envelope must be below 0.500. On the rising branch the maximum extra delay is therefore \(d<2\arcsin(0.250)/40.0=12.6\) ms. A measured 15.0 ms spread would give \(4\sin(0.300)=1.18\) for the product, so this sufficient certificate would no longer settle stability there.

What do several plant tests tell us?

The gain of a drive changes as it warms up, and a flexible mounting introduces poorly measured phase lag at high frequency. Let \(P_0\) be a nominal model. One useful description is multiplicative uncertainty, \(P=P_0(1+W\Delta)\), with stable dimensionless \(\Delta\) of gain at most one and a specified frequency weight \(W\). The magnitude of \(W\) bounds relative error. Estimate it from \(|P/P_0-1|\) across an ensemble of measurements, retaining phase in the complex ratio. A 20% amplitude difference and a 20° phase difference generally produce a larger relative error than either alone.

Where \(P_0\) is close to zero this relative description can be poorly conditioned. An additive uncertainty \(P=P_0+W_a\Delta\) can describe an absolute error floor there. A bound fitted only at sampled frequencies requires a reason to hold between samples, especially near flexible resonances. A robustness calculation is conditional on this modelling work.

Factor the perturbed characteristic equation

For the nominal controller \(C\), set \(L_0=P_0C\) and \(T_0=L_0/(1+L_0)\). Under multiplicative perturbation,

\[1+PC=(1+L_0)(1+W T_0\Delta).\]

Assume internal stability of the nominal interconnection and stable, proper uncertainty factors with no hidden unstable cancellation. If \(\sup_\omega|W(i\omega)T_0(i\omega)|<1\), the perturbation loop has gain less than one and cannot destabilise the feedback. One way to see the mechanism is the convergent inverse series \(1-G+G^2-\cdots\) for \(G=WT_0\Delta\) in the stable-system norm. The pointwise bound protects against all allowed dynamic phase choices. Failure of a sufficient test alone does not prove the particular measured plant unstable.

Work a frequency-domain budget

At one frequency a nominal loop has \(L_0=-0.8+0.4i\). Then \(|1+L_0|=\sqrt{0.2}\), \(|L_0|=\sqrt{0.8}\), and \(|T_0|=2\). Relative uncertainty below 50% at that frequency keeps \(|W T_0|<1\) there. A measured 60% bound crosses this threshold. Geometrically the perturbed loop samples a disk centred at \(L_0\) of radius \(|L_0 W|\). At 50% its rim touches \(-1\). Other frequencies still require examination, and a physically narrower family such as a single real gain may occupy far less than the full disk.

Add a performance requirement

A stable loop can still amplify an output disturbance unacceptably. For the uncertain plant the sensitivity is \(S=S_0/(1+WT_0\Delta)\). The triangle inequality gives \(|S|\le |S_0|/(1-|WT_0|)\) where the denominator is positive. If \(|S_0|=0.10\) and \(|WT_0|\le0.25\), disturbances can be amplified by at most 0.133 in this bound. Very near the robustness limit that bound becomes large. Shaping the nominal loop for a small uncertainty product near troublesome resonances can therefore protect both stability and useful performance. Record the sensor configuration, loading and frequency band over which the underlying plant measurements were made.

Distinguish absolute from relative uncertainty

Several tested plants may differ by an approximately fixed absolute transfer at high frequency although their nominal output gain is nearly zero there. Dividing each difference by a near-zero nominal transfer gives an enormous relative error and may make a multiplicative envelope awkward. An additive description writes \(P_a=P_0+W_a\Delta\), where \(|\Delta(i\omega)|\le1\), the stable filter \(W_a\) has the same units as the plant and its magnitude bounds absolute discrepancies. The controller \(C\) and nominal plant first form an internally stable feedback system. Assume the uncertainty interconnection is well posed and the normalised uncertainty is stable.

Factor the uncertain return difference using the nominal sensitivity \(S_0=1/(1+C P_0)\):

\[1+CP_a=(1+CP_0)[1+W_a C S_0\Delta].\]

For unstructured complex uncertainty the small-gain bound \(\|W_a C S_0\|_\infty<1\) is sufficient for robust stability. The relevant nominal transfer is from measurement error to control effort, \(CS_0\), rather than complementary sensitivity alone. At high frequency \(S_0\) often approaches one, so a derivative action that increases high-frequency controller gain can stress an additive uncertainty bound even where nominal loop gain has already fallen.

The inequality uses the worst permitted complex phase at each frequency. A point violating the envelope test indicates loss of this guarantee. It does not by itself establish that a particular measured plant is unstable. Conversely a few passing frequency samples do not certify the continuous-frequency supremum.

Budget uncertainty for a lightly coupled actuator resonance

Suppose a calibration expresses position in millimetres and actuator input in volts. At 40 rad/s the nominal gain is only \(0.020\,\mathrm{mm/V}\), while an unmodelled flexible path can add up to \(0.030\,\mathrm{mm/V}\) in any phase. A measured nominal loop has \(|CS_0|=18\,\mathrm{V/mm}\) there, giving an uncertainty product \(0.030(18)=0.54\). The corresponding allowable absolute uncertainty before the unit threshold is \(1/18=0.0556\,\mathrm{mm/V}\). Stating only the 150% relative plant discrepancy would conceal the controller-dependent margin at that frequency.

At 70 rad/s suppose the absolute uncertainty bound increases to \(0.045\,\mathrm{mm/V}\) and \(|CS_0|=25\,\mathrm{V/mm}\). Their product is 1.125. To reserve a factor of 1.25 on the uncertainty envelope, impose a target product of at most 0.80. Then \(|CS_0|\) must be at most \(0.80/0.045=17.8\,\mathrm{V/mm}\) at that frequency. A revised compensator with measured \(|CS_0|=16\) gives 0.72 at the suspect point. Check the complete response around any new filter resonance and recompute nominal tracking. Reducing this single frequency peak cannot establish whether the remainder of the frequency band also satisfies the reserve.

Suppose the absolute uncertainty envelope comes from three nominally identical machines. At each frequency calculate the complex difference of each measured response from the nominal complex response before taking magnitudes. At 70 rad/s, two transfer measurements of equal magnitude but opposite phase can differ by twice that magnitude. Subtracting magnitudes alone would assign zero discrepancy. Also retain independent test uncertainty such as force calibration and motion-sensor noise, which may be especially important where nominal motion is small. The largest observed discrepancy only covers those three machines and operating conditions. A justified production bound requires considering the frequency regions between measurement points and whether later mounting or payload changes can introduce another flexible path.

Test a frequency-dependent uncertainty budget

Use the same nominal loop and model multiplicative uncertainty by the weight \(W(s)=(0.1+a s)/(1+s)\) times a stable unit-gain perturbation. Inspect the product \(|WT|\) rather than the two factors separately. A uniform bound below one supplies a sufficient robust-stability test.

Chapter 9 · Optimal state feedback and the Riccati equation

Choose how heavily to penalise tracking error and actuator effort, then derive the feedback gain that minimises that stated cost.

From first principles

Derive a feedback gain by minimising future cost

A control objective must say what counts as expensive. Consider a known continuous linear system \(\dot x=Ax+Bu\) with state penalty \(x^\mathsf TQx\) and effort penalty \(u^\mathsf TRu\), where \(Q\) is positive semidefinite and \(R\) positive definite. Assume the usual stabilisability and detectability conditions for the infinite-horizon problem. If \(V(x)\) is the least remaining accumulated cost, minimising over one short interval and then using the optimal continuation gives

\[0=\min_u\{x^\mathsf TQx+u^\mathsf TRu+\nabla V^\mathsf T(Ax+Bu)\}.\]

Take the quadratic value form \(V=x^\mathsf TPx\) with symmetric \(P\). Differentiate the expression inside braces with respect to \(u\). The positive effort penalty makes its stationary value a minimum.

\[2Ru+2B^\mathsf TPx=0,\]
\[u_*=-R^{-1}B^\mathsf TPx .\]

Substitute this control back into the optimality condition and require the quadratic state coefficient to vanish for every state.

\[A^\mathsf TP+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\mathsf TP+Q=0.\]

The stabilising solution gives the feedback gain and the predicted least cost from the initial state. Hard limits on actuator effort require a further model constraint.

Derivation and applications

Relate an optimal feedback value function to energy spent

The Riccati solution predicts more than a feedback gain. Consider a scalar integrator \(\dot x=bu\) with \(b>0\) and a cost accumulated over an unlimited horizon. Use positive weights \(q,r\) and charge both state deviation and control amplitude squared. Assume the actuator has no amplitude or rate limit and the chosen stabilising control returns the state to zero. A quadratic candidate value \(V=Px^2\) changes at rate \(2Pbxu\). Add this derivative to the running cost and complete the square in the control.

\[qx^2+ru^2+\dot V=r\left(u+\frac{bP}{r}x\right)^2+\left(q-\frac{b^2P^2}{r}\right)x^2 .\]

Choose \(P\) so that the residual state term vanishes. The positive solution makes the candidate value nonnegative and gives a stabilising feedback. Integrating in time then writes the entire cost as its initial value plus a nonnegative penalty for departures from that control.

\[P=\frac{\sqrt{qr}}{b},\]
\[J=Px_0^2+\int_0^\infty r\left(u+\frac{bP}{r}x\right)^2dt .\]

Equality holds when \(u=-Kx\) with \(K=\sqrt{q/r}\). Substitution into the state balance gives an exponential return with time constant \(1/(bK)\). The optimum assigns equal instantaneous penalties to state and effort in this specific integrator model.

\[x=x_0e^{-bKt},\]
\[J_x=J_u=\frac{q x_0^2}{2bK},\]
\[J_{\min}=Px_0^2 .\]

Worked example

Price a change in the initial deviation

A normalised stage has \(b=2.00\), \(q=9.00\) and \(r=4.00\). Its optimal \(K=1.50\) gives a decay rate of 3.00 s⁻¹. For \(x_0=0.400\), the minimum cost is \((\sqrt{36}/2)(0.400)^2=0.480\) weighted units. Half comes from control effort. Doubling the starting deviation quadruples total cost to 1.92 and doubles the magnitude of the initial command.

Second application

Test a proposed control weight against an actuator limit

A second integrator has \(b=1.00\), \(q=4.00\), initial deviation 0.300 and an available command magnitude of 0.200. Since peak command is \(K|x_0|\), the unconstrained solution fits this experiment only when \(K\leq2/3\), requiring \(r\geq9.00\). At the boundary the time constant is 1.50 s and total optimal cost is \(\sqrt{4\times9}(0.300)^2=0.540\). A larger starting deviation would need its own constraint check.

Make the design objective explicit

Pole placement prescribes decay rates but says nothing about how much actuator energy a manoeuvre consumes. For a continuous linear system \(\dot x=Ax+Bu\) consider the regulator objective \(J=\int_0^\infty(x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru)\,dt\). Assume \(Q\) positive semidefinite and \(R\) positive definite. A large displacement penalty or a small control penalty generally encourages stronger corrective action. The ratio matters: multiplying both matrices by the same positive scalar leaves the best control unchanged. Scale states and inputs before comparing entries; a displacement in millimetres and a velocity in metres per second cannot share an arbitrary numerical weight without a unit convention.

Derive the continuous algebraic Riccati equation

For an infinite-horizon stationary problem propose a quadratic remaining cost \(V(x)=x^\mathsf{T}Px\). During a short interval \(dt\), optimality requires the minimum of instantaneous cost plus the change in remaining cost to vanish. In the limit this gives \(0=\min_u[x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru+2x^\mathsf{T}P(Ax+Bu)]\). Completing the square in \(u\) yields

\[u^*=-R^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}Px,\qquad A^\mathsf{T}P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P+Q=0.\]

The desired solution is the stabilising symmetric solution. Under standard stabilisability and detectability conditions it exists and yields finite nonnegative cost. Other algebraic roots need not make sense as stored future cost. Once a candidate is found, check the eigenvalues of \(A-BR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P\) and the residual of the matrix equation.

Solve the scalar unstable case

Let \(\dot x=x+u\) with \(Q=R=1\). The scalar equation is \(2P-P^2+1=0\), giving \(P=1\pm\sqrt2\). Only \(1+\sqrt2\) is positive and makes the closed-loop pole \(1-P=-\sqrt2\). From initial state \(x_0=2\), the theoretical minimum cost is \(4(1+\sqrt2)\simeq9.657\). Verify independently: insert \(x=2e^{-\sqrt2t}, u=-Px\) into the cost integral to obtain \(4(1+P^2)/(2\sqrt2)\), the same value. By contrast a stabilising gain of 2 gives state pole −1 and cost 10. Stabilising the state is necessary but still leaves a meaningful optimisation problem.

Distinguish control penalties from hard actuator limits

The optimal initial input for the scalar example has magnitude \(2P=4.83\). If the actuator is limited to 3, clipping the command invalidates the quadratic unconstrained solution during the clipped interval. Increasing \(R\) makes large inputs more expensive but generally supplies no absolute guarantee for arbitrary initial conditions. A concrete certificate is possible inside a stated region. In one dimension \(|u|\le u_{\max}\) is guaranteed by \(|x|\le u_{\max}/P\) under this gain, and the resulting stable exponential stays in that interval. Later we will impose hard constraints directly in a finite-horizon design.

The MIT notes on linear quadratic regulators extend this derivation to time-varying and finite-horizon problems.

Solve the Riccati equation for a position-and-velocity plant

A carriage moving on nearly frictionless guides is locally a double integrator. Take its position error \(x_1\) and velocity \(x_2\) as states and acceleration command \(u\) as input. After selecting consistent physical scales, use \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(B=(0,1)^T\) and diagonal cost \(Q=\operatorname{diag}(q_x,q_v)\), \(R=r\). Assume \(q_x>0,\ q_v\ge0,\ r>0\). Both states are reachable because acceleration changes velocity immediately and position through integration. Choosing a positive position penalty also makes a stationary position error visible to the cost.

Set \(P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p_{11}&p_{12}\\p_{12}&p_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) in \(A^TP+PA-PBr^{-1}B^TP+Q=0\). Matching the upper-left, lower-right and off-diagonal entries gives three scalar equations:

\[p_{12}^2/r=q_x,\qquad p_{22}^2/r=2p_{12}+q_v,\qquad p_{11}=p_{12}p_{22}/r .\]

Choose the positive roots giving stabilising feedback. The gains in \(u=-k_x x_1-k_v x_2\) become \(k_x=\sqrt{q_x/r}\), \(k_v=\sqrt{2\sqrt{q_x/r}+q_v/r}\). Substitution into the state equations gives \(s^2+k_vs+k_x=0\). The effort penalty and position penalty enter the squared natural frequency through a square root; the natural frequency itself varies as a fourth root. This explains why a moderate change of settling speed may require a surprisingly large change in a numerical cost weight.

Set a settling speed and calculate its actuator cost

In chosen metre, second and acceleration units let \(q_x=16,\ q_v=0,\ r=1\), with weights understood to include the needed unit conversions. Then \(k_x=4\,\mathrm{s^{-2}},\ k_v=\sqrt8=2.828\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The poles are \(-1.414\pm1.414i\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), with natural frequency 2 rad/s and damping ratio \(1/\sqrt2\). From an initial positive position error of 0.20 m and zero speed, commanded acceleration is −0.80 m/s². The optimum quadratic cost is \(V=x^TPx\). Here \(p_{12}=4\), \(p_{22}=2.828\), \(p_{11}=11.314\), so the initial cost value is \(11.314(0.2)^2=0.453\) in the chosen cost units.

To double natural frequency while keeping \(q_v=0,\ r=1\), increase \(q_x\) by sixteen to 256. The new position gain is 16 s⁻², velocity gain is \(\sqrt{32}=5.657\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), and the same initial condition demands −3.2 m/s². An acceleration-limited drive rated for ±1.5 m/s² cannot execute this ideal motion from that initial error. For the larger gain, the zero-velocity initial error must be no more than \(1.5/16=0.0938\,\mathrm m\) for the first command to lie within its limit. That calculation addresses the initial state only; subsequent velocity feedback must also be checked. Comparing physical demand rather than weight values makes the practical tradeoff visible.

The chosen gains can be tested without explicitly solving the Riccati equation again. Along an optimal unconstrained trajectory \(V=x^TPx\) decreases according to \(\dot V=-(x^TQx+u^TRu)\). At the original initial state the instantaneous cost is \(16(0.20)^2+(-0.80)^2=1.28\) cost units per second. Directly differentiating \(V\) there gives \(2x^TP\dot x=2(0.20)(4)(-0.80)=-1.28\), since initial speed is zero and acceleration equals the command. The sign and magnitude agree. This check simultaneously tests the gain sign, state ordering and cost convention. Introducing an input clip breaks the square-completion identity for the proposed unconstrained controller, so the same matrix cannot simply be read as the realised accumulated cost of the clipped manoeuvre.

Chapter 10 · Estimating hidden states from noisy measurements

Propagate both the state estimate and its uncertainty, and derive how much of an unexpected sensor reading to trust.

From first principles

Choose a measurement gain by minimising estimation variance

Suppose a scalar prediction \(\hat x^-\) has zero-mean error of variance \(P^-\), while an independent unbiased sensor gives \(y=x+v\) with noise variance \(R\). Correct the prediction by a fraction \(K\) of its disagreement with the sensor. The posterior estimation error combines the original error and sensor noise with different weights.

\[\hat x^+=\hat x^-+K(y-\hat x^-),\]
\[x-\hat x^+=(1-K)(x-\hat x^-)-Kv .\]

Independence eliminates the cross term when the squared error is averaged. Differentiate the resulting variance with respect to the gain and solve for its minimum.

\[P^+=(1-K)^2P^-+K^2R,\]
\[\frac{dP^+}{dK}=-2(1-K)P^-+2KR=0 .\]

The optimum gain grows when prediction uncertainty rises relative to sensor uncertainty. Substituting it into the variance gives

\[K=\frac{P^-}{P^-+R},\]
\[P^+=\frac{P^-R}{P^-+R}.\]

The innovation \(y-\hat x^-\) has variance \(P^-+R\) under the same assumptions. Monitoring that disagreement in actual samples checks whether the stated uncertainty model is consistent with the observed data.

Derivation and applications

Find the steady uncertainty of a repeatedly sampled sensor

A sequence of scalar Kalman updates can settle to a fixed confidence even if the physical state continues wandering. Model the state as \(x_{k+1}=x_k+w_k\) with independent zero-mean increments of variance \(Q\). A sensor gives \(y_k=x_k+v_k\) with independent noise variance \(R>0\). Use the same sampling interval each time and assume known, constant variances. If \(P\) denotes the posterior variance immediately after one reading, propagation adds the process variance before the next reading.

\[P^-=P+Q,\]
\[K=\frac{P^-}{P^-+R},\]
\[P_{\rm next}=(1-K)P^-=\frac{R(P+Q)}{P+Q+R}.\]

At steady operation set \(P_{\rm next}=P\) and multiply by the denominator. The product \(RP\) cancels from both sides, leaving a quadratic. Variance must be nonnegative, so only one root is admissible for \(Q>0\).

\[P^2+QP-RQ=0,\]
\[P_\infty=\frac{\sqrt{Q^2+4QR}-Q}{2}.\]

Since the posterior update can also be written \(P_{\rm next}=RK\), its steady value immediately fixes the long-run gain. Conversely, measuring a gain and knowing sensor variance supplies an estimate of the assumed process increment variance.

\[K_\infty=P_\infty/R,\]
\[\frac QR=\frac{K_\infty^2}{1-K_\infty}.\]

For large \(Q/R\), the state changes substantially between readings and the gain approaches one. With vanishing process noise repeated independent readings continue reducing uncertainty; the positive fixed point then tends to zero.

Worked example

Predict a level sensor's residual spread

A drifting liquid level has \(Q=1.00\) mm² per sample and sensor variance \(R=4.00\) mm². The posterior variance tends to \((\sqrt{17}-1)/2=1.56\) mm², giving a posterior standard deviation of 1.25 mm. The steady gain is 0.390. A 3.00 mm innovation consequently moves the estimate 1.17 mm. The innovation variance includes both prediction and sensor uncertainty, giving \(1.56+1+4=6.56\) mm².

Second application

Infer the assumed drift from a filter log

Another estimator settles at gain 0.200 with independently measured \(R=9.00\) K². Then \(Q=9(0.200)^2/(1-0.200)=0.450\) K² per sample and posterior variance is \(RK=1.80\) K². If the physical drift is a random walk with variance proportional to elapsed time, doubling the sample interval would double \(Q\) to 0.900 K². The predicted new steady gain becomes \((\sqrt{0.900^2+4(0.900)(9)}-0.900)/18=0.270\).

A prediction and an observation carry different information

Consider a sampled model \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k+w_k\), \(y_k=Cx_k+v_k\). Model disturbances \(w_k\) describe unmeasured forcing or model error, while \(v_k\) describes measurement error. Suppose they are independent, zero mean and white, with known covariances \(W,V\), and independent of the initial estimation error. Start each update with a predicted mean \(\hat x^-\) and covariance \(P^-\). The innovation \(y-C\hat x^-\) is the part of the reading that the model did not predict. Its covariance is \(S=CP^-C^\mathsf{T}+V\). Units are those of the sensor squared; a large innovation in volts is meaningful only relative to this scale.

Minimise posterior variance

Update the estimate by \(\hat x^+=\hat x^-+K(y-C\hat x^-)\). In the scalar direct-measurement case the squared error has expected value \(P^+=(1-K)^2P^-+K^2V\). Differentiating with respect to \(K\) yields \(K=P^-/(P^-+V)\). A precise sensor raises the gain; a precise prior prediction lowers it. The same covariance minimisation gives the matrix update

\[K=P^-C^\mathsf{T}S^{-1},\quad P^+=(I-KC)P^-(I-KC)^\mathsf{T}+KVK^\mathsf{T}.\]

The displayed covariance formula retains positive-semidefinite structure in computation better than a raw subtraction of nearly equal matrices. For the next prediction use \(\hat x^-_{k+1}=A\hat x^+_k+Bu_k\) and \(P^-_{k+1}=AP^+_kA^\mathsf{T}+W\). The control input affects the mean; assumed known exactly, it adds no direct covariance term. With Gaussian assumptions the recursion gives the conditional mean and covariance, and with just second moments it gives the optimal linear estimate under the stated model.

Recover velocity from a position reading

Suppose the prior covariance for position and velocity is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1.5\\1.5&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) in compatible metre and second units. A position sensor has variance 1. Then \(S=5\), \(K=(0.8,0.3)^\mathsf{T}\). If measured position exceeds prediction by 2 m, the updated position increases by 1.6 m and velocity by 0.6 m/s. The sensor did not measure velocity directly: the nonzero cross-covariance makes a position surprise informative about velocity. The posterior covariance is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.8&0.3\\0.3&0.55\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A robust implementation would obtain the gain by solving with \(S\) instead of constructing a matrix inverse.

Test innovations during commissioning

Under a correct linear Gaussian model the standardised innovations should have mean zero, appropriate variance and little serial correlation. A persistent offset points toward a biased sensor or missing force. A succession of innovations of the same sign during accelerations suggests unmodelled dynamics or too little process uncertainty. Simply enlarging \(V\) to suppress the innovations in the correction makes the filter trust the model more; it does not remove a systematic error. Compare held-out records that include manoeuvres and quiet intervals. When observer and regulator are combined, estimation and control share the quality of the underlying dynamic model.

Fuse two measurements with a shared error source

Two temperature sensors mounted on one test head can share calibration drift or electromagnetic interference. Treating their errors as independent exaggerates the information gained by averaging. Suppose they measure the same scalar state at a single instant, \(y_1=x+v_1,\ y_2=x+v_2\), with zero-mean errors, variances \(r_1,r_2\) and covariance \(c\). Seek an unbiased linear estimate \(\hat x=w y_1+(1-w)y_2\), initially without a separate prior. Its error variance is \(J(w)=w^2r_1+(1-w)^2r_2+2w(1-w)c\). Known covariance makes this a scalar optimisation rather than a heuristic choice of weights.

Differentiating and setting \(J'(w)=0\) gives

\[w=\frac{r_2-c}{r_1+r_2-2c},\qquad R_{\rm fused}=\frac{r_1r_2-c^2}{r_1+r_2-2c}.\]

The denominator is exactly the variance of the sensor difference. If their errors are identical, this difference contains no information for estimating a weighting and the duplicate measurement cannot improve precision. Positive semidefiniteness of the covariance requires \(c^2\le r_1r_2\). Negative weights can appear when one error contains a predictable correlated component that can be subtracted. Such weights require a well-supported covariance model, especially when the denominator is small.

Update one state without counting a common disturbance twice

Take sensor standard deviations 2 K and 3 K and covariance \(c=3\,\mathrm K^2\), corresponding to correlation 0.50. The first sensor’s optimal weight is \((9-3)/(4+9-6)=6/7\), with \(1/7\) on the second. Readings of 305 and 308 K give a fused reading of 305.429 K and variance \((36-9)/7=3.857\,\mathrm K^2\). The independent-sensor formula would instead claim \(36/13=2.769\,\mathrm K^2\), materially overstating the improvement. Each sensor may appear repeatable by itself while both shift in the same direction between experiments.

Suppose a prior estimate from a thermal model is 302 K with variance 4 K², independent of both current measurement errors. One scalar Kalman update using the fused measurement has gain \(4/(4+3.857)=0.5091\). Its estimate is \(302+0.5091(305.429-302)=303.745\,\mathrm K\) and its posterior variance is \((1-0.5091)4=1.964\,\mathrm K^2\). The same answer follows from a two-row measurement matrix and the full covariance matrix with its off-diagonal terms. If the model has already incorporated sensor 1 at this same instant, reusing the fused reading as an independent update counts some evidence twice. Synchronisation and calibration history therefore determine which covariance belongs in the estimator, as well as the arithmetic of its gain.

Sensor disagreement provides a second diagnostic that does not require knowing the true temperature. Because the two sensors observe the same instantaneous state, their difference \(y_1-y_2\) should have variance \(r_1+r_2-2c=7\,\mathrm K^2\). The observed difference of −3 K corresponds to about \(-3/\sqrt7=-1.13\) standard deviations. A long sequence of such differences tests the assumed relative-error covariance without a calibrated thermal reference. It cannot identify a common offset, since that cancels from every difference. If both instruments warm their local mounting head or use the same drifting reference voltage they may agree closely while being wrong together. Periodic comparison against an independent standard is needed before interpreting small disagreement as strong evidence of absolute accuracy.

Chapter 11 · Interacting actuators, pairing and decoupling

Predict how closing one loop changes the apparent gain of another, and identify a physically expensive actuator combination.

From first principles

Eliminate the second actuator to find a constrained gain

With several actuators and outputs, a gain depends on what the other controller holds fixed. Consider small steady deviations described by a square two-by-two gain matrix \(G\). First hold \(u_2\) fixed at zero. The direct response of \(y_1\) to \(u_1\) has slope \(g_{11}\). Now assume an ideal second controller adjusts \(u_2\) slowly enough to maintain \(y_2=0\) in steady state.

\[y_1=g_{11}u_1+g_{12}u_2,\]
\[0=y_2=g_{21}u_1+g_{22}u_2 .\]

For \(g_{22}\neq0\), the second actuator must move by \(-g_{21}u_1/g_{22}\). Substitution gives the constrained gain seen by the first loop and the ratio of the direct to constrained gains.

\[\left.\frac{y_1}{u_1}\right|_{y_2=0}=\frac{\det G}{g_{22}},\]
\[\lambda_{11}=\frac{g_{11}g_{22}}{\det G}.\]

The ratio requires a nonsingular matrix. A value near unity means closing the second loop changes this steady gain little. A negative value signals a reversal of the gain sign under the constraint. Dynamic phase and actuator limits still affect whether the pairing is usable.

Derivation and applications

Determine how actuator limits reshape the reachable demands

A two-actuator mechanism may have abundant force in either output separately but be unable to deliver the two requested forces simultaneously. Work with a static local model and identical actuator units. Choose outputs as the sum \(y_1\) and difference \(y_2\) of two actuator forces \(u_1,u_2\). Assume each is bidirectional and bounded by magnitude \(U\), and the static gain holds over the region of interest. The forward and inverse maps are

\[y_1=u_1+u_2,\]
\[y_2=u_1-u_2,\]
\[u_1=\frac{y_1+y_2}{2},\]
\[u_2=\frac{y_1-y_2}{2}.\]

Substitution of the inverse map into the two component limits gives a pair of strips in output space. Their intersection is the reachable region. The maximum of the absolute sum and absolute difference of two real numbers is their absolute-value sum, which simplifies the description to a diamond.

\[|y_1+y_2|\leq 2U,\]
\[|y_1-y_2|\leq 2U\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |y_1|+|y_2|\leq2U .\]

A supervisor can retain the relative amounts of the two requested outputs by scaling a nonzero desired vector \(d\) by one factor. The largest such factor up to unity follows by placing the scaled request on the reachable boundary whenever the original request exceeds capacity.

\[y=\alpha d,\]
\[\alpha=\min\!\left(1,\frac{2U}{|d_1|+|d_2|}\right).\]

This allocation preserves the requested direction. If one output has priority, it may be preferable to maintain that output and use only the residual capacity for the other. The physical feasible set is the same for both decisions.

Worked example

Preserve the direction of a combined request

Two linear actuators each permit 50.0 N. A requested combination \((80,60)\) N would need \((70,10)\) N. Scaling by \(100/140=0.7143\) yields outputs \((57.1,42.9)\) N and actuator loads \((50.0,7.14)\) N. Checking the inverse transformation shows exactly which actuator has reached its limit while the other retains substantial headroom.

Second application

Preserve a preload while alternating the differential force

A separate rig with \(U=30.0\) N requires total preload \(y_1=44.0\) N throughout a test. The reachable inequality leaves \(|y_2|\leq60.0-44.0=16.0\) N. At the positive extreme the actuator forces are \((30.0,14.0)\) N; at the negative extreme they exchange roles. A demanded 20.0 N alternating differential amplitude would require reducing the fixed preload to at most 40.0 N.

A second loop changes the experiment

Imagine two thermal zones with separate heaters and two temperature sensors. At steady state let \(y=G u\), with \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) K per unit power. Increasing heater 1 with heater 2 fixed raises both temperatures. Increasing heater 1 while actively holding temperature 2 fixed is a different experiment: heater 2 must back off. The effective gain in the first channel changes. This difference matters when a controller is tuned with its neighbour disconnected and then commissioned with both loops active.

For a general nonsingular two-by-two matrix hold \(y_2=0\). Solving \(g_{21}u_1+g_{22}u_2=0\) gives \(y_1/u_1=g_{11}-g_{12}g_{21}/g_{22}\). The ratio of the fixed-input gain to this fixed-output gain is \(g_{11}g_{22}/\det G\). More generally the relative gain array has entries \(\Lambda_{ij}=g_{ij}(G^{-1})_{ji}\). Its rows and columns each sum to one. Large magnitudes suggest cancellation. A negative relative gain indicates that the paired steady response changes sign when the other output is perfectly held, an important warning for independent-loop tuning.

Compare candidate pairings

For the two-zone matrix above the diagonal relative gains are \(4/3\) and the off-diagonal gains are \(-1/3\). The direct pairing has fixed-input gain 2 and fixed-other-output gain 1.5. The cross-pairing changes sign. These steady results do not include transport delays or transient coupling. If the direct heater path is very slow but the cross path changes quickly, a frequency-dependent model is needed around the intended control bandwidth.

Diagonalise a symmetric plant by physical combinations

Commanding both heaters together excites the common direction \((1,1)\) with gain 3; commanding them oppositely excites \((1,-1)\) with gain 1. For the desired temperature increment \((1,-1)\), exactly equal and opposite input increments \((1,-1)\) are required. Negative input here is a reduction from a positive bias; a heater already at zero cannot supply it. Inspecting a transformed system without retaining the original actuator limits can make an impossible target seem easy.

Now bring the gains closer together: \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.98\\0.98&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). The common gain is 1.98, while the differential gain is just 0.02. A unit differential target requires differential input of magnitude 50 in each actuator. Static inversion \(u=G^{-1}v\) algebraically decouples the two commands but amplifies measurement, calibration and modelling errors along that weak direction. If both temperature sensors move together during every available test, the weak direction is also particularly hard to identify.

Choose a practical validation test

First perturb each actuator separately about the operating bias and measure both outputs. Repeat at different loads and frequencies. Next excite the predicted weakest input combination within safe operating limits and check whether the differential output emerges above the noise. A design can prioritise the common temperature and relax allowable imbalance when the plant is inherently hard to split. A constrained controller can express this trade directly by penalising both output errors while respecting individual actuator bounds.

Find difficult demand directions with a singular-value decomposition

Relative gains help pair individual inputs and outputs, but some machines are controlled through combinations of actuators. A steady mapping \(y=Gu\) with adequate static rank can still require a large actuator change in a particular direction. Scale inputs by allowed physical excursions and outputs by meaningful error or command scales. After scaling, the Euclidean norm is interpretable and singular values quantify directional gains. Without scaling, changing position units from metres to millimetres could dominate the result.

For a real square nonsingular \(G\), the symmetric positive definite matrix \(G^TG\) has orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_j\) and positive eigenvalues \(\sigma_j^2\). A unit actuator pattern \(v_j\) yields an output of length \(\sigma_j\) in direction \(u_j=Gv_j/\sigma_j\). Writing \(G=U\Sigma V^T\) then gives a transparent inverse. A desired output \(y_r\) requires actuator components \((u_j^Ty_r)/\sigma_j\) along \(v_j\). The smallest singular value therefore controls the largest gain of the inverse, and the ratio \(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) measures conditioning.

This geometry describes steady input effort, not necessarily dynamic stability. Two channels with different lags can have different difficult directions at higher frequencies. The singular values of the complex frequency-response matrix can reveal that change after consistent scaling at every frequency.

Allocate two actuators with a tight current budget

Consider a scaled steady plant \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.9\\0.9&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Its symmetry gives unit common and differential directions \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\), with singular values 1.9 and 0.1. A common output target \((0.2,0.2)\) requires only \(u=(0.1053,0.1053)\). A differential target \((0.2,-0.2)\) needs \(u=(2,-2)\), a factor nineteen more effort for the same output norm. If each drive is limited to magnitude one, the first target is feasible and the second is not.

One possible allocation trades exact tracking against effort by minimising \(\|Gu-y_r\|^2+\lambda\|u\|^2\). The stationary normal equations are \((G^TG+\lambda I)u=G^Ty_r\); each inverse singular value is replaced by \(\sigma_j/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)\). For \(\lambda=0.01\) and the differential target, that factor is \(0.1/(0.01+0.01)=5\). The resulting input is \((1,-1)\) and output \((0.1,-0.1)\). The allocation respects these particular bounds while openly accepting half the requested differential change. A penalty does not enforce an input limit for every possible target. If exact limit enforcement is required, solve the bounded allocation problem or constrain the admissible command directions explicitly.

A simple perturbation test makes the weak direction visible in hardware. Command \(u=(0.1,0.1)\) in scaled units and predict \(y=(0.19,0.19)\). Reversing only the second command gives \(u=(0.1,-0.1)\) and outputs only \((0.01,-0.01)\). If each sensor has an uncertainty of ±0.01 output units, the first test has a comfortable signal level but the second barely resolves its predicted value. A poorly estimated differential gain may therefore vary considerably between identification records even though the common gain is repeatable. Increasing the differential test amplitude within operating bounds or averaging repeated experiments improves that estimate. A static inverse based on a single small pulse at each actuator may otherwise amplify calibration error precisely in the least observable output combination.

Chapter 12 · Finite-horizon control with real actuator constraints

Build a small predictive controller from a sampled model and determine when the requested target is physically reachable.

From first principles

Predict a finite trajectory before choosing the first command

Consider a scalar sampled plant \(x_{k+1}=ax_k+bu_k\), with a measured initial state and known coefficients. A predictive controller uses the model repeatedly to compare candidate future command sequences. For two steps, substitute the first state update into the second; each input affects a different interval and the first input also affects the second predicted state through propagation.

\[x_1=ax_0+bu_0,\]
\[x_2=a^2x_0+abu_0+bu_1 .\]

Even a one-step horizon shows how a quadratic objective selects a command. Let \(r\) be the desired next state, \(q>0\) a tracking penalty and \(\rho>0\) an effort penalty. Differentiate the objective after substituting its predicted state.

\[J=q(ax_0+bu_0-r)^2+\rho u_0^2,\]
\[u_0^*=\frac{qb(r-ax_0)}{qb^2+\rho}.\]

For this single scalar step, an interval limit on \(u_0\) clips the unconstrained minimiser to the nearest feasible endpoint. Over longer horizons the state and input constraints couple the commands. Only the first chosen command is applied before the state is measured again and the prediction repeated.

Derivation and applications

Predict the stopping distance of a sampled axis

A predictive controller can satisfy every instantaneous position bound and still enter a state from which the next position constraint must be violated. To see the role of velocity, consider a frictionless axis moving towards a positive position limit. The command is constant over sample interval \(h\) and bounded in acceleration by \(a>0\). Suppose the current speed \(v\) is positive, the axis must stop without reversing, and a final fractional braking command is allowed. Constant acceleration integrates exactly over each step.

\[v_{k+1}=v_k+h u_k,\]
\[x_{k+1}=x_k+h v_k+\tfrac12 h^2u_k,\]
\[|u_k|\leq a .\]

Apply maximum braking for \(n=\lfloor v/(ah)\rfloor\) full samples. The remaining speed \(w=v-nah\) is in \([0,ah)\). During these \(n\) samples the speed decreases linearly, so the travelled distance is their duration times the average of initial and final speed.

\[d_n=nh\,\frac{v+w}{2}=\frac{v^2-w^2}{2a}.\]

If \(w>0\), select \(u=-w/h\) for the last interval. The last step travels \(hw/2\) and ends at rest exactly on a sampling instant. Combining the two contributions yields the minimum stopping distance with this no-reversal and held-command requirement.

\[d_{\rm stop}=\frac{v^2}{2a}+\frac{w(ah-w)}{2a}.\]

Maximum early braking minimises future speeds and hence the travelled distance. The extra term compared with continuous switching is nonnegative and vanishes for an integer number of full braking intervals. A position-only terminal constraint can overlook this extra travel.

Worked example

Check if a carriage can stop before its limit

Let \(v=0.550\) m/s, \(a=2.00\) m/s² and \(h=0.100\) s. Two full braking steps leave \(w=0.150\) m/s. They consume \(0.200(0.550+0.150)/2=0.0700\) m. The final step needs \(u=-1.50\) m/s² and travels 7.50 mm, giving 77.5 mm total. A remaining clearance of 76.0 mm exceeds the continuous stopping distance of 75.6 mm but is insufficient for the sampled axis.

Second application

Bound the error without knowing the initial speed

For \(a=1.20\) m/s² and \(h=0.200\) s the correction is largest at \(w=ah/2\). Its maximum is \(ah^2/8=6.00\) mm. At \(v=0.360\) m/s a full step leaves exactly 0.120 m/s, so the continuous estimate of 54.0 mm increases to 60.0 mm. Reducing the sample interval by half reduces this worst-case excess distance by a factor of four.

Predict a sequence before applying the first action

For \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\) with measured or estimated \(x_k\), successive substitutions give \(x_{k+2}=A^2x_k+ABu_k+Bu_{k+1}\). The initial state sets the free response; the planned input sequence contributes linearly. With quadratic tracking and effort penalties, linear dynamics and linear bounds on states or inputs, planning becomes a convex quadratic programme. At each update apply only the first planned command, acquire a new measurement and solve again. Receding the horizon provides feedback when disturbances make actual motion depart from the earlier prediction.

Solve a two-step constrained problem

Take the dimensionless integrator \(x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k\), \(x_k=2\), and target zero. Minimise \(x_{k+1}^2+x_{k+2}^2+u_k^2+u_{k+1}^2\). With \(u=u_k,v=u_{k+1}\), the objective is \((2+u)^2+(2+u+v)^2+u^2+v^2\). Its stationary equations are \(3u+v=-4,\ u+2v=-2\). The unconstrained plan \(u=-1.2,\ v=-0.4\) reaches states 0.8 and 0.4. If both input magnitudes are limited to one, the first command is infeasible. Fixing \(u=-1\) and reoptimising the second gives \(v=-0.5\), reaching 1 and 0.5. The derivative with respect to \(u\) points toward smaller \(u\) at this boundary, confirming the active limit.

After applying −1, suppose the new state is exactly 1. The next two-step unconstrained plan scales the original one by one half, giving first action −0.6. The earlier second action was −0.5. This change is consistent even without disturbances, since the new optimisation includes a further future step. A terminal cost approximating the remaining cost of control can reduce this artificial dependence on the planning horizon.

Feasible commands and feasible trajectories differ

A command bound always has feasible choices if zero lies within it. Adding a hard state constraint, such as a position stop, may make all predicted trajectories infeasible from the current state. Before asking for a time-optimal move, compute elementary reachability bounds. With \(|u|\le1\), a scalar integrator can move at most \(N\) units in \(N\) samples. A terminal constraint demanding zero from state 3 in two samples is impossible. Allowing slack in a comfort-related temperature band may be defensible; a physical actuator limit must still be enforced. Report which constraints were active and what performance was sacrificed.

Close the engineering loop

For a practical comparison, first establish a verified baseline with the OneSimulation proportional-loop record. Reconstruct its pole and finite-record bias from the model parameters. This record witnesses how an unconstrained linear loop behaves; it does not itself exercise a predictive optimiser. Now design a separate held-input or constrained test whose command reaches a known bound, record measured timestamps and actuator signals, and compare with a by-hand prediction of the first active limit. The useful performance claims differ: accurate steady tracking, stable sampled dynamics, adequate estimator uncertainty and feasibility under limits each require an observation capable of testing them.

Derive a terminal region that can be maintained indefinitely

A finite-horizon planner can reach a state at its last predicted sample that looks inexpensive but has no feasible continuation. A terminal set provides a constructive remedy. For \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\), choose local feedback \(u=Kx\). A set \(X_f\) is positively invariant under this feedback if every \(x\in X_f\) satisfies the state and input bounds and \((A+BK)x\in X_f\). Requiring the last predicted state to belong to this set gives the next optimisation a candidate, provided the model is exact and a feasible plan already exists.

To see this, suppose the accepted plan contains controls \(u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}\) and states \(x_0,\ldots,x_N\in X\), with \(x_N\in X_f\). Apply \(u_0\). At the next time, shift the remaining controls one step and append \(Kx_N\). Its last state is \((A+BK)x_N\), still in \(X_f\). Every intermediate state and command came from the old feasible plan. This argument establishes recursive feasibility for the ideal deterministic system; the cost can then be chosen to establish a separate performance or stability claim.

For an unstable scalar plant \(x^+=1.2x+u\), bounds \(|u|\le0.5,\ |x|\le2\), choose \(u=-0.7x\). The resulting factor is 0.5. Choosing \(|x|\le0.5/0.7=5/7\) protects the local input limit and the next-state magnitude contracts. This interval is one admissible terminal set; it need not be the largest invariant region obtainable with all possible controls.

Construct the two-step feasible region by working backwards

For the same plant, suppose a terminal interval is \([-b,b]\). A current state can reach it in one step if the reachable interval \([1.2x-0.5,1.2x+0.5]\) intersects \([-b,b]\). Solving the two endpoint inequalities gives \(|x|\le(b+0.5)/1.2\), additionally restricted by \(|x|\le2\). Starting from \(b_0=5/7=0.7143\), the one-step interval has half-width \(b_1=1.0119\), and the two-step interval has \(b_2=1.2599\). A current error \(x=1.20\) is therefore feasible for two steps but cannot reach the terminal set in one.

A feasible two-step plan applies \(u_0=u_1=-0.5\). It generates \(x_1=0.94,\ x_2=0.628\), inside the terminal interval. The appended local command is \(-0.7(0.628)=-0.440\), generating \(x_3=0.314\). From \(x=1.40\), however, even two maximal negative commands yield \(x_1=1.18,\ x_2=0.916>0.7143\). A solver reporting infeasibility there has identified a physical horizon limitation. Lengthening the horizon can enlarge the feasible region. External disturbances and estimator error can spoil the exact shifted-plan construction, motivating reserve inside the hard bounds or an explicitly disturbance-aware terminal set.

Observe the limiting sequence of backwards-reachable interval widths. Ignoring the physical state cap for a moment, the recursion has fixed point \(b=(b+0.5)/1.2=2.5\). At \(x=2.5\) the strongest negative input merely holds position, and beyond it even that input allows outward divergence. With the stated state limit the physically relevant bound is tighter at 2.0. From \(x=2.0\) the strongest input moves to 1.9, so that boundary can be brought inward over enough samples. This examination distinguishes insufficient horizon from insufficient actuator authority. A solver change cannot rescue a state above the unrestricted 2.5 threshold with the given input, whereas increasing horizon can rescue a feasible physical state that lies just beyond the current backwards-reachable interval.

Now suppose implementation records establish an additive one-step disturbance bound \(|w|\le0.05\) in \(x^+=1.2x+u+w\). At local feedback \(u=-0.7x\), guaranteeing that \([-b,b]\) maps to itself under every such disturbance requires \(0.5b+0.05\le b\). The terminal interval above satisfies this because \(b=0.7143>0.10\), and the feedback still respects the input limit. To guarantee that a chosen predecessor reaches this interval in one step, the nominal next state needs to lie within \([-b+0.05,b-0.05]\). The robust predecessor half-width is thus \((b-0.05+0.5)/1.2=0.970\), smaller than the nominal 1.012.

Applying the backward rule twice gives the robust two-step half-width \((0.970-0.05+0.5)/1.2=1.183\) when an updated action can be selected at the intermediate state. The previously feasible initial state 1.20 is outside that guarantee. Indeed maximal negative actuation with two consecutive positive disturbances yields \(x_1=0.99,\ x_2=0.738>0.7143\). A nominally feasible trajectory and an invariant terminal set alone therefore do not make the whole finite-horizon plan robust. The intermediate constraints and the information available when choosing the next input must also be included in the guarantee.

Worked solution

Integration during one hold gives yₖ₊₁ = ayₖ+(1−a)uₖ with a = exp(−h). Before clipping, uₖ = 3(1−yₖ), the pole is 4a−3, and the equilibrium is 0.75. The error 0.25 is independent of h for a stable loop. Requiring the pole to exceed −1 gives h less than ln 2 seconds. At h = 0.50 the pole is −0.5739; the other stability inequality holds for positive h.

The first three outputs are 0.75[1−(4a−3)ᵏ] for k = 1, 2 and 3, approximately 1.180, 0.503 and 0.892. The first unconstrained command is 3. After clipping the actuator stays at one until a sample reaches y ≥ 2/3, when the requested input no longer exceeds one.

While the limit is active the output ticks follow yₖ = 1−exp(−0.5k). At k = 2 the output is 0.632 and the controller still requests 1.104. At k = 3 the output is 0.777 and the request is 0.669. Applied study

Design feedback for a tracking requirement and examine how loop gain changes speed, offset and stability.

Turn a machine balance into a plant model

A speed controller drives a motor against viscous drag. The shaft has inertia \(J\), drag coefficient \(b\), angular speed \(\omega\) and commanded torque \(u\). With a constant opposing load torque \(d\), the balance is \(J\dot\omega=u-b\omega-d\). Without feedback a step in load changes the steady speed by \(-d/b\). An engineer who can measure speed can command more torque as soon as the speed falls.

Divide by \(b\). With output \(y=\omega\), time constant \(\tau=J/b\) and static gain \(K=1/b\), the unloaded plant becomes \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\). A heating element with finite heat capacity and heat loss has the same first-order form under constant material properties. Transfer functions or step-response measurements can therefore guide controller design before the full machine is available.

Choose the loop gain from a tracking requirement

A proportional controller applies \(u=K_p(r-y)\) for a reference \(r\). Substitution gives \(\tau\dot y+(1+KK_p)y=KK_pr\). If \(1+KK_p>0\), the closed loop settles with time constant \(\tau_{\rm cl}=\tau/(1+KK_p)\). Following a step from rest,

\[y(t)=\frac{KK_p}{1+KK_p}r\,(1-e^{-t/\tau_{\rm cl}}),\qquad e_\infty=\frac{r}{1+KK_p}.\]

The steady error supplies the command that balances the drag. For a normalised model with \(K=2\), \(\tau=20\) s, \(r=1\) and \(K_p=4\), the loop gain is 8. The output tends to \(8/9=0.8889\) with time constant 2.222 s. Keeping the steady error within 5% of the setpoint requires \(1/(1+2K_p)\le0.05\), hence \(K_p\ge9.5\). At the instant of a unit step this higher gain asks for 9.5 units of actuator effort. A drive limited to 5 units initially saturates, so the linear prediction for the rise time must be revised.

Reject a load disturbance with integral action

For the motor with a constant load, proportional control settles at \(y=(K_pr-d)/(b+K_p)\). Adding \(K_i\int (r-y)\,dt\) to the command lets the controller supply constant torque even at zero error. If the loop settles and the actuator has sufficient reserve, a bounded steady integrator state requires \(r-y=0\).

The extra state changes stability and damping. For the normalised plant and a PI controller, the closed-loop characteristic polynomial is \(\tau s^2+(1+KK_p)s+KK_i\). At \(K=2\), \(\tau=20\) s, \(K_p=4\) and \(K_i=1\) per second, its roots are \(-0.225\pm0.222i\) per second. Both decay, but a disturbance can now excite an oscillation. A gain that removes steady error can change the transient response substantially.
