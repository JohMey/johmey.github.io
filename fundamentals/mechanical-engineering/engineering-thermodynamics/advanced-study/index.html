Equations of state and response functions

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Use compressibility and thermal expansion to predict what a pressure gauge will record when a material is heated in a closed vessel.

From first principles

Derive ideal-gas pressure from impacts on a wall

Pressure is the normal force on a wall per unit area. Consider \(N\) classical molecules of mass \(m\) in a cubic box of side \(L\). Neglect intermolecular forces and molecular volume. For an elastic collision at a wall perpendicular to the \(x\) axis, a molecule transfers normal momentum \(2m|v_x|\). It visits the same wall once in a round trip of duration \(2L/|v_x|\). Its time-averaged force there is

\[F_{x,1}=\frac{2m|v_x|}{2L/|v_x|}=\frac{mv_x^2}{L}.\]

Sum all contributions and divide by the wall area \(L^2\). With \(V=L^3\), equilibrium isotropy gives \(\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle/3\). Classical thermal equilibrium assigns mean energy \(k_BT/2\) to each quadratic velocity component. These inputs give

\[p=\frac{Nm}{V}\langle v_x^2\rangle=\frac{Nm}{3V}\langle v^2\rangle,\]
\[pV=Nk_BT .\]

This equation of state connects three directly measurable quantities at fixed particle number. The kinetic calculation also identifies where a real gas can differ: intermolecular forces transfer momentum, and dense molecules occupy an appreciable fraction of the volume.

Derivation and applications

Connect a sound-speed measurement to two compressibilities

A slowly compressed sample has time to exchange heat with its surroundings. A small sound pulse usually compresses a fluid element faster than heat can diffuse across a wavelength. The elastic response relevant to sound is then nearly adiabatic. Consider a homogeneous single-phase fluid at equilibrium and a small-amplitude pulse. Assume negligible viscosity and thermal diffusion over one oscillation, so the specific entropy of a moving element remains constant.

Let \(v\) be volume per unit mass, \(c_p\) the specific heat at constant pressure, \(\alpha=v^{-1}(\partial v/\partial T)_p\), and \(\kappa_T=-v^{-1}(\partial v/\partial p)_T\). From the specific Gibbs differential \(dg=-s\,dT+v\,dp\), equality of mixed derivatives gives \((\partial s/\partial p)_T=-(\partial v/\partial T)_p\). The entropy differential determines a fluid element’s temperature change under isentropic compression,

\[ds=\frac{c_p}{T}dT-v\alpha\,dp=0,\]
\[\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_s=\frac{Tv\alpha}{c_p}.\]

Insert this derivative into the volume response \(dv/v=\alpha\,dT-\kappa_T\,dp\). The resulting isentropic compressibility is

\[\kappa_s=-\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}\right)_s=\kappa_T-\frac{Tv\alpha^2}{c_p}=\kappa_T\frac{c_v}{c_p}.\]

The last step uses the heat-capacity difference for a simple compressible material. For \(\rho=1/v\), a density perturbation gives \(p'=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\rho'\). Linear momentum balance and continuity then combine into a wave equation with speed \(c\),

\[\partial_t^2\rho'=c^2\nabla^2\rho',\]
\[c^2=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_s=\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_s}.\]

Adiabatic heating adds a pressure response on compression for an ordinary expanding fluid. The two compressibilities coincide where thermal expansion vanishes.

Worked example

Predict the speed in an ideal gas

For dry model air at \(p=1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\), take \(\rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(c_p/c_v=1.40\). Since \(\kappa_T=1/p\), sound propagates at \(\sqrt{1.40p/\rho}=342\,\mathrm{m/s}\). An isothermal model gives \(289\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Comparing the two speeds measures the square root of the heat-capacity ratio under these gas assumptions.

Second application

Infer a heat-capacity ratio for a liquid

An ultrasonic pulse travels at \(1480\,\mathrm{m/s}\) through a liquid of density \(998\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). It gives \(\kappa_s=1/(998\times1480^2)=4.57\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}\). A slow isothermal experiment gives \(4.65\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}\). Their ratio yields \(c_p/c_v=1.017\), which explains why the choice of compressibility produces a much smaller acoustic correction for this liquid.

Decide which variables specify a state

An equilibrium sample of one simple phase and fixed composition can be described by pressure, volume and temperature related by an equation of state. A piston experiment can prescribe pressure while changing volume; a sealed rigid vessel instead fixes volume. The distinction matters because the same temperature change can lead to expansion in one apparatus and a large pressure increase in the other. Assume quasistatic measurements so that a single pressure and temperature describe the sample.

An equation of state is a constitutive statement. The ideal-gas relation \(pV=nRT\) treats particles as dilute enough for their mutual interactions to have negligible effect on the measured pressure. An incompressible-liquid approximation often gives an adequate volume balance at a chosen pressure but can make a constrained heating problem ill posed if applied literally. Both small thermal expansion and small but finite compressibility may be needed to compute the pressure response.

Describe a neighbourhood of equilibrium

Treat volume as a differentiable function \(V(T,p)\) near a reference state. Its total differential contains the two independent slopes \((\partial V/\partial T)_p\) and \((\partial V/\partial p)_T\). Define the volume expansion coefficient \(\alpha=(1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_p\) and the isothermal compressibility \(\kappa_T=-(1/V)(\partial V/\partial p)_T\). For ordinary mechanically stable material the latter is positive. The local relation is

\[\frac{dV}{V}=\alpha\,dT-\kappa_T\,dp.\]

Both terms are dimensionless. Expansion has units of inverse kelvin, while compressibility has units of inverse pascal. For fixed volume the pressure slope is \((\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha/\kappa_T\). This relation is a consequence of using the same volume differential. It does not assume a particular molecular model and it remains informative for a liquid for which the ideal-gas equation would give a poor answer.

For an ideal gas at fixed amount, differentiating \(V=nRT/p\) gives \(\alpha=1/T\) and \(\kappa_T=1/p\). Their ratio is \(p/T\), as follows independently from \(p=nRT/V\). At 300 K and 100 kPa, warming a sealed ideal gas by 3.0 K raises the pressure by 1.0 kPa. At fixed pressure instead the relative volume increase is one percent.

Separate intensive, extensive and measured responses

Doubling the amount of material at the same temperature and pressure doubles volume but leaves the two normalised coefficients unchanged. Such intensive coefficients can be compared across samples. A vessel’s own volume, however, can respond to both pressure and temperature. If its expansion coefficients are appreciable, the measured pressure slope describes the coupled sample and vessel. One cannot simply substitute the unconstrained expansion of the sample into a rigid-vessel formula.

To see this, let the vessel’s fractional volume response be \(\alpha_v\,dT+C_v^{\rm vessel}dp\), where its mechanical compliance is positive for an internal pressure increase. Equal fractional changes of vessel and sample give \((\alpha-\alpha_v)dT=(\kappa_T+C_v^{\rm vessel})dp\). A compliant wall reduces the pressure rise, and a vessel that expands just as much as the sample can nearly cancel the temperature signal. Here the symbol for vessel compliance denotes a mechanical quantity and has different units from heat capacity.

Compare liquid and container data

Take a representative liquid with \(\alpha=2.5\times10^{-4}\) K⁻¹ and \(\kappa_T=5.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹ near its operating point. In an ideally rigid container, a 2.0 K increase gives \(\Delta p\simeq1.0\) MPa. The unconstrained volume change at fixed pressure would be only 0.050%. This combination explains how a small fractional expansion can produce a significant pressure response when the fluid is only weakly compressible.

If the container has \(\alpha_v=5.0\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and \(C_v^{\rm vessel}=3.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹, the same warming gives about 0.50 MPa. Inverting a measured slope without the vessel data could thus give a misleading estimate of compressibility. These are local linear predictions. Over a broad pressure or temperature range the coefficients must be evaluated along the path, and crossing a phase boundary can invalidate the single-phase differential altogether.

Convert an isobaric calorimeter reading into an isochoric one

A sample can absorb different amounts of heat per kelvin depending on which variable is held fixed. For a fixed amount of a simple, single-phase material in equilibrium, \(C_V=T(\partial S/\partial T)_V\) and \(C_p=T(\partial S/\partial T)_p\). Apply the chain rule to \(S(T,V)\) along the constant-pressure path. Subtracting the two derivatives leaves the effect of expansion. From \(dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV\) equality of mixed derivatives gives \((\partial S/\partial V)_T=(\partial p/\partial T)_V\). Therefore

\[C_p-C_V=T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p=\frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}.\]

Here \(\alpha\) is the volume expansion coefficient and \(\kappa_T\) the isothermal compressibility. The last equality uses \(dV/V=\alpha dT-\kappa_Tdp\). For a mechanically stable material \(\kappa_T>0\), so the difference is nonnegative even for a material that contracts when warmed. For one mole of ideal gas, inserting \(\alpha=1/T\), \(\kappa_T=1/p\) and \(pV=RT\) gives \(C_p-C_V=R\).

At 300 K a solid has molar volume \(1.20\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol, volumetric expansion coefficient \(6.00\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and bulk modulus \(B_T=1/\kappa_T=100\) GPa. The molar difference is \(TV_m B_T\alpha^2=1.30\) J/(mol K). A constant-pressure measurement of 25.0 J/(mol K) thus implies \(C_{V,m}=23.7\) J/(mol K). For a cubic solid the linear expansion coefficient is approximately one third the volume coefficient. Using it directly in this formula would underestimate the correction ninefold. This conversion matters when comparing a tabulated calorimetric result with a vibrational model calculated at fixed volume.

Predict the temperature change of a throttled gas

Some pressure changes take place in flowing rather than closed matter. Consider a well-insulated porous plug that sustains a pressure difference between two steady streams of the same single-phase gas. There is no shaft work and inlet and outlet speeds and heights have negligible differences in kinetic and gravitational energy. Flow work at each end is already included in the molar enthalpy \(h=u+pv\). The steady energy balance then gives \(h_{\rm out}=h_{\rm in}\). A pressure drop through the restriction can nevertheless change temperature if the gas is nonideal.

From \(dh=T\,ds+v\,dp\) and the Maxwell relation \((\partial s/\partial p)_T=-(\partial v/\partial T)_p\), write the enthalpy differential in terms of \(T,p\). The coefficient of \(dT\) at fixed pressure is the molar heat capacity \(c_p\). Setting \(dh=0\) gives the Joule–Thomson coefficient

\[\mu_{\rm JT}=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_h=\frac{T(\partial v/\partial T)_p-v}{c_p}=\frac{v(T\alpha-1)}{c_p}.\]

For an ideal gas \(\alpha=1/T\), so an isenthalpic throttle produces no temperature change. Isentropic expansion in a turbine is a different process. A throttle's pressure drop is irreversible; constant enthalpy follows here from the steady energy balance and does not imply constant entropy.

Estimate a small temperature drop from gas data

Near a chosen operating point let the low-pressure molar volume be \(v=RT/p+B(T)\), retaining a second virial coefficient \(B=b-a/(RT)\). Here \(a\) and \(b\) characterize an approximate real-gas equation of state. The ideal-volume term cancels in \(T(\partial v/\partial T)_p-v\). The remaining numerator is \(TB'-B=2a/(RT)-b\). At fixed local heat capacity, attractions and excluded volume therefore compete in determining the sign of the temperature response.

Take \(a=0.140\) Pa m⁶/mol², \(b=4.00\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol, \(T=300\) K and \(c_p=29.0\) J/(mol K). Then \(2a/(RT)=1.12\times10^{-4}\) m³/mol and \(\mu_{\rm JT}=2.49\times10^{-6}\) K/Pa, or 0.249 K/bar. A pressure change of \(-2.0\) bar predicts temperature change \(-0.50\) K if the properties remain close to these values. The leading model changes sign at \(T=2a/(Rb)=842\) K. This inversion estimate should not be extrapolated into a dense or multiphase region. A proper finite pressure drop integrates the measured local coefficient along an isenthalp. Recording both enthalpy balance and inlet-to-outlet entropy increase offers an independent test of whether unwanted heat transfer through the apparatus affected the result.

The entropy change of the throttled stream can be checked without a calorimeter. Along an isenthalp the Gibbs relation gives \(ds=-v\,dp/T\). Since molar volume and temperature are positive, any pressure decrease raises the molar entropy. Even when an ideal gas shows zero temperature change, its pressure decrease leaves a positive entropy difference. This reveals precisely why absence of observed cooling is insufficient evidence for thermodynamic reversibility. A pressure reading, inlet temperature and appropriate volume data together supply a sign test independently of the small temperature slope used to estimate the Joule–Thomson coefficient.

An independent heat-leak estimate is particularly important near inversion, where a small parasitic heat flow can dominate the intrinsic temperature change.

Heat, work and the limits of a cycle

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Track changes of internal energy around a gas cycle and derive the temperature bound on the efficiency of a reversible engine.

From first principles

Derive the reversible engine efficiency from two balances

A working substance in a cyclic engine returns to its initial state. Its internal energy is a state function, so the energy gained from a hot reservoir must equal the useful work plus the heat delivered to a cold reservoir. Let \(Q_h,Q_c\) be positive heat magnitudes and \(W\) positive work delivered by the engine. Energy conservation gives

\[W=Q_h-Q_c,\]
\[\eta=\frac{W}{Q_h}=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}.\]

To determine the smallest possible heat rejection, apply the second law. Reservoirs at constant absolute temperatures \(T_h>T_c\) lose entropy \(Q_h/T_h\) and gain entropy \(Q_c/T_c\) respectively. The engine itself returns to its original entropy. The total entropy production is therefore

\[S_{\rm gen}=\frac{Q_c}{T_c}-\frac{Q_h}{T_h}\ge0,\]
\[\eta\le1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.\]

A reversible cycle attains equality. Friction or heat transfer across finite temperature differences produces entropy and reduces efficiency at fixed reservoir temperatures. The ratio uses absolute temperatures because reservoir entropy changes are defined by reversible heat divided by thermodynamic temperature.

Derivation and applications

Derive the efficiency of an idealized four-stroke gas cycle

A compression ratio predicts an efficiency for a model cycle only after the heat-transfer paths are specified. Consider a fixed amount of ideal gas with constant molar heat capacities and \(\gamma=C_p/C_V>1\). In the Otto model the gas undergoes reversible adiabatic compression from state 1 to 2, heat addition at fixed volume from 2 to 3, reversible adiabatic expansion from 3 to 4, then heat rejection at fixed volume back to 1. Use positive magnitudes \(Q_h,Q_c\) for the absorbed and rejected heat.

For either adiabat the first law gives \(nC_VdT=-p\,dV=-nRT\,dV/V\). Separation and integration give \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}\). Introducing \(r=V_1/V_2=V_4/V_3>1\) sets both adiabatic temperature ratios,

\[\frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{T_3}{T_4}=r^{\gamma-1}.\]

The two fixed-volume steps do no boundary work. Their respective heats are internal energy changes, and cyclic energy conservation converts the heat ratio to efficiency,

\[Q_h=nC_V(T_3-T_2),\]
\[Q_c=nC_V(T_4-T_1),\]
\[\eta=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}.\]

Substitute \(T_3=r^{\gamma-1}T_4\) and \(T_2=r^{\gamma-1}T_1\). Both temperature differences contain the same factor \(T_4-T_1\), which cancels and leaves

\[\eta=1-r^{1-\gamma}.\]

The gas absorbs and rejects heat over intervals of temperature. Its efficiency therefore depends on the chosen paths even with reversible adiabatic strokes. At \(r=1\) the pressure-volume loop collapses and produces no net work. Increasing the compression ratio also raises the temperature reached before heat addition, which can invalidate a constant-\(\gamma\) model.

Worked example

Close a cycle with measured endpoint temperatures

For one mole with \(r=8.00\), \(\gamma=1.40\), \(T_1=300\,\mathrm K\) and \(T_3=1600\,\mathrm K\), the adiabatic factor is 2.297. Hence \(T_2=689\,\mathrm K\), \(T_4=696\,\mathrm K\), and \(C_V=R/0.40=20.8\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}\). The gas absorbs \(18.9\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and rejects \(8.24\,\mathrm{kJ}\), yielding \(10.7\,\mathrm{kJ}\) of work and efficiency \(0.565\).

Second application

Infer a compression ratio for a different working gas

A model monatomic gas has \(\gamma=5/3\). Achieving efficiency \(0.600\) requires \(r=(1-0.600)^{-1/(\gamma-1)}=3.95\). Starting at \(280\,\mathrm K\), its temperature at the end of compression is \(280/(1-0.600)=700\,\mathrm K\). A cycle whose hottest temperature were \(650\,\mathrm K\) could not realize the specified heat-addition stroke with this initial state.

Put signs on a physical boundary

Enclose a gas inside a movable cylinder. When the gas expands, it can raise an external weight. When heated at fixed volume, it can increase its temperature while doing no boundary work. To fix signs, take heat \(Q\) into the gas as positive and work \(W\) done by the gas as positive. The first law is \(\Delta U=Q-W\). Internal energy is a state property; the other two quantities describe transfer during a process. A cyclic device returns to its initial state, so its net work equals its net heat input.

For a quasistatic expansion, a piston moving distance \(dx\) under external pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\) receives work \(p_{\rm ext}A\,dx=p_{\rm ext}dV\). In mechanical equilibrium \(p_{\rm ext}\) approaches the gas pressure, giving the usual area under a pressure–volume curve. A suddenly expanding gas is not generally uniform or in mechanical equilibrium. Extending the equilibrium pressure formula to an unconstrained free expansion would predict work when no piston or other boundary force actually receives it.

Follow an ideal gas along two paths

For a calorically ideal gas, \(dU=nC_{V,m}dT\) with constant molar heat capacity. Reversible isothermal expansion from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) therefore gives zero internal energy change and \(Q=W=nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\). Revise the process so that the gas is thermally insulated but still expands reversibly. Then \(nC_{V,m}dT=-p\,dV=-nRT\,dV/V\). Integrating and introducing \(\gamma=C_{p,m}/C_{V,m}=1+R/C_{V,m}\) gives \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant}\). Temperature now falls because the gas supplies work from its energy store.

The two paths can pass through a common starting state but they generally end at different pressures and temperatures for a given final volume. If one wants to compare work between identical endpoints one must specify a connecting step as well. This prevents a common misreading of a pressure–volume plot: an isotherm and an adiabat through the same point have different slopes and cannot serve as alternate paths to the same later point without additional processes.

Construct a reversible two-reservoir cycle

Let the gas expand isothermally at \(T_h\), then expand adiabatically down to \(T_c\), compress isothermally there and return adiabatically to the original state. If the first isotherm changes volume by a factor \(r\), the two adiabatic relations force the cold compression to change volume by the reciprocal factor. The hot heat input is \(Q_h=nRT_h\ln r\). The magnitude of heat expelled during cold compression is \(Q_c=nRT_c\ln r\). Thus

\[W=Q_h-Q_c,\qquad \eta=\frac W{Q_h}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.\]

The working gas provides one explicit construction. More generally a reversible cycle exchanges entropy \(Q_h/T_h\) with the hot reservoir and returns exactly that entropy to the cold one. For an irreversible cyclic engine between the same two reservoirs, the reservoir entropy change \(Q_c/T_c-Q_h/T_h\) is nonnegative. This gives the same expression as an upper bound on efficiency. The temperatures must be absolute and must refer to the reservoirs actually exchanging heat.

Test a proposed engine

An engine receives 1.50 kJ per cycle from a 450 K reservoir and rejects heat to a 300 K sink. Reversibility permits at most 0.500 kJ of useful work. A measured cycle delivering 0.360 kJ must reject 1.140 kJ by the first law. The working fluid’s entropy returns to its original value, while the two reservoirs together gain \(1140/300-1500/450=0.467\) J/K per cycle.

The energy and entropy balances answer different design questions. Energy accounting gives the amount of rejected heat. Entropy generation explains why some of the available work has been lost relative to the reversible limit. Raising the running frequency can increase power even at lower efficiency, since power depends on work per unit time. The reversible limit describes a thermodynamic bound, and achieving a useful power output additionally depends on finite rates of heat transfer and mechanical motion.

Extract work from two finite heat reservoirs

The usual Carnot limit assumes two fixed reservoir temperatures. Two finite bodies instead change temperature as a reversible engine transfers energy between them. Consider insulated hot and cold blocks with constant heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\). A cyclic engine operating between them can deliver work to an external mechanical store. The block temperatures vary slowly relative to internal equilibration, and the reversible engine draws and rejects each small amount of heat at its corresponding instantaneous block temperature.

If an infinitesimal amount of entropy passes through the engine reversibly, the hot block loses exactly the amount gained by the cold block. Integrating until both reach a common final temperature \(T_f\) yields

\[C_h\ln(T_f/T_{h,0})+C_c\ln(T_f/T_{c,0})=0,\qquad T_f=T_{h,0}^{C_h/(C_h+C_c)}T_{c,0}^{C_c/(C_h+C_c)}.\]

The logarithms contain temperature ratios, so the product expression is dimensionally a temperature. Once temperatures coincide a cyclic engine has no thermal difference left from which to extract work. Conservation of energy gives \(W_{\max}=C_h(T_{h,0}-T_f)+C_c(T_{c,0}-T_f)\). Any irreversible entropy production raises the attainable common temperature relative to the reversible result at the same initial conditions, leaving more energy in the blocks and less exported as work.

Compare a reversible engine with passive contact

Let \(C_h=C_c=1.00\) kJ/K, \(T_{h,0}=400\) K and \(T_{c,0}=300\) K. The reversible common temperature is the geometric mean \(\sqrt{400\times300}=346.41\) K. The engine receives 53.59 kJ from the hot block and transfers 46.41 kJ to the cold block; their difference is 7.18 kJ of work. The overall work fraction of extracted hot-side heat is 0.134. At the beginning the instantaneous reversible efficiency is \(1-300/400=0.250\), falling to zero at the end. Applying that initial efficiency throughout would overestimate the work.

Now put the same blocks directly in thermal contact without an engine. Energy conservation gives the arithmetic mean of 350 K and zero work. The positive entropy produced is \(1000\ln[350^2/(400\times300)]=20.6\) J/K. The final temperature exceeds the reversible result by 3.59 K for each block, precisely accounting for 7.18 kJ retained as thermal energy. In the limit of a very small initial temperature difference the geometric and arithmetic means coincide to first order. Recoverable work thus starts at second order in the initial difference even though the heat passed between the blocks is first order, explaining why small temperature contrasts are poor resources for mechanical work.

The arithmetic and geometric means also reveal how sensitive the possible work is to initial temperature calibration. For equal heat capacities, write initial temperatures as \(T_m+\delta\) and \(T_m-\delta\). The reversible common value is \(\sqrt{T_m^2-\delta^2}\simeq T_m-\delta^2/(2T_m)\). Multiplying the difference from the arithmetic mean by the combined heat capacity gives \(W_{\max}\simeq C\delta^2/T_m\). Thus a small absolute error in a temperature difference can have a doubled relative effect on the predicted maximum work. Measuring both blocks with matched instruments matters more here than obtaining many digits for the mean temperature alone.

Thermodynamic potentials and phase boundaries

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Select the appropriate potential for a controlled experiment and infer the slope of a coexistence line from latent heat and volume change.

From first principles

Derive a coexistence slope from equality of chemical potentials

At fixed temperature and pressure a closed single-component system lowers its Gibbs energy when matter moves into a phase with lower molar chemical potential. Transfer an infinitesimal amount \(dn\) from phase 1 to phase 2. Along the coexistence line, either direction is possible in equilibrium, so

\[dG=(\mu_2-\mu_1)\,dn=0,\]
\[\mu_1(T,p)=\mu_2(T,p).\]

Moving along the line must preserve that equality. The Gibbs differential for one mole gives \(d\mu=-s_m\,dT+v_m\,dp\), with molar entropy \(s_m\) and volume \(v_m\). Equating the differentials of the two phases leaves

\[(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})\,dp=(s_{m,2}-s_{m,1})\,dT .\]

The latent heat per mole for reversible conversion \(1\to2\) is \(L_m=T(s_{m,2}-s_{m,1})\). Therefore the slope is \(dp/dT=L_m/[T(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})]\) for a nonzero volume jump. Its sign depends on both latent heat and volume change. This relation requires measured or modeled properties of the two phases at the same coexistence point.

Derivation and applications

Calculate how a curved interface changes the equilibrium vapour pressure

A small liquid droplet in its own vapour reaches equilibrium at a different vapour pressure from a flat liquid surface. Consider a spherical droplet of radius \(r\) at uniform temperature \(T\), with surface tension \(\gamma\) independent of size. Let \(p_v\) and \(p_l\) be the exterior vapour and interior liquid pressures. A virtual change of radius changes area and volume by \(dA=8\pi r\,dr\) and \(dV=4\pi r^2dr\). Balancing the pressure work against the surface free energy gives

\[(p_l-p_v)dV=\gamma\,dA,\]
\[p_l=p_v+\frac{2\gamma}{r}.\]

Use a flat interface at the same temperature as a chemical-potential reference. Both phases there have pressure \(p_\infty\). The difference of their chemical potentials is zero for the flat reference and for the equilibrium droplet. Integrating \(d\mu=v_m\,dp\) along an isotherm for each phase then gives, for ideal vapour and nearly incompressible liquid of molar volume \(v_l\),

\[RT\ln(p_v/p_\infty)=v_l(p_l-p_\infty)=v_l(p_v-p_\infty)+\frac{2\gamma v_l}{r}.\]

For a dilute vapour the molar volume greatly exceeds the liquid value. When \(p_v-p_\infty\) is also small compared with the curvature-induced pressure jump, its contribution on the right is negligible. The Kelvin relation follows,

\[\ln(p_v/p_\infty)\simeq\frac{2\gamma v_l}{rRT}.\]

A convex liquid surface raises the required equilibrium vapour pressure. The result tends continuously to flat coexistence at large radius. At molecular radii the continuum description, including the assumption of a bulk surface tension, requires independent justification.

Worked example

Estimate the supersaturation over a droplet

For water-like material at \(298\,\mathrm K\) take \(\gamma=0.0720\,\mathrm{N/m}\), \(v_l=18.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3/mol}\), and \(r=20.0\,\mathrm{nm}\). The exponent is \(0.0523\), giving \(p_v/p_\infty=1.054\). The mechanical pressure jump is \(7.20\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Even with \(p_\infty=3.17\,\mathrm{kPa}\), the exterior pressure increase is only about \(170\,\mathrm{Pa}\), verifying the term dropped in obtaining the Kelvin relation.

Second application

Infer the radius that can coexist with a given vapour

A different liquid has \(\gamma=0.0250\,\mathrm{N/m}\) and \(v_l=100\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3/mol}\) at \(300\,\mathrm K\). A chamber stabilized at \(p_v/p_\infty=1.020\) matches radius \(r=2\gamma v_l/[RT\ln(1.020)]=101\,\mathrm{nm}\). A smaller droplet has a higher required vapour pressure and tends to evaporate in that chamber. A larger one tends to grow, so matching the pressure selects an unstable balance if the vapour pressure is held fixed.

Change which variables are controlled

Consider a closed simple compressible system that can exchange heat with a reservoir and work by moving a boundary. In equilibrium its fundamental energy differential for fixed amount is \(dU=T\,dS-p\,dV\). This expression makes entropy and volume natural independent variables, yet laboratory experiments more frequently control temperature and pressure. A change of thermodynamic potential exchanges one independent variable for its conjugate without losing any equilibrium information.

For the Helmholtz free energy \(F=U-TS\), differentiation gives \(dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV\). Its slope with respect to volume at fixed temperature is minus the pressure. For the Gibbs free energy \(G=U+pV-TS\), the differential is \(dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp\). At fixed temperature and pressure a spontaneous change in a closed sample with only pressure–volume work tends to lower its total \(G\). The external reservoir can exchange heat during that change, so a fall in sample energy by itself would not be the correct selection criterion.

Extract a measurable cross derivative

The entropy of a gas is harder to measure directly than its thermal expansion. Since \(G(T,p)\) is a state function with commuting mixed derivatives inside a smooth single phase, \(-(\partial S/\partial p)_T=(\partial V/\partial T)_p\). This is one Maxwell relation. It converts an entropy response to a derivative accessible from volume measurements. The equality applies to the same material and the same choice of fixed variables on both sides.

For one mole of ideal gas \((\partial V/\partial T)_p=R/p\). Compressing isothermally therefore changes its entropy by \(\Delta S=-R\ln(p_2/p_1)\). Doubling pressure gives \(-5.76\) J/(mol·K), in agreement with an independent reversible-heat calculation. The Maxwell relation obtains the state difference without requiring the actual compression process to be reversible. Only an equilibrium path used to evaluate it has to connect well-defined states.

Derive a coexistence-line slope

For one pure substance the molar Gibbs energy is the chemical potential. At two-phase equilibrium both phases must have the same chemical potential; otherwise transferring matter to the phase of lower value decreases total \(G\). Label the phases 1 and 2. Along their coexistence curve a small change in \(T\) and \(p\) changes their chemical potentials equally. Substituting \(d\mu=-s_m dT+v_m dp\) for each yields

\[\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{s_{m,2}-s_{m,1}}{v_{m,2}-v_{m,1}}=\frac{L_m}{T(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})}.\]

Here \(L_m\) is the molar latent heat for the transition from phase 1 to 2. The slope can be negative for melting when the liquid has smaller molar volume than the solid. For vaporisation the volume difference is usually positive and large at modest pressure. A pressure reading and a thermometer together can therefore constrain a latent heat through measurements of the slope and molar volumes.

Estimate a boiling temperature near atmospheric pressure

For vaporising water near 373 K use \(L_m=40.7\) kJ/mol, a vapour volume of 0.0306 m³/mol and a much smaller liquid volume. The coexistence slope is approximately \(40700/[373(0.0306)]=3.57\) kPa/K. An increase of pressure by 10 kPa therefore raises the boiling temperature by roughly 2.8 K over this limited interval. The dimensional check is J/(m³·K), equivalent to Pa/K.

Over a larger interval, approximate the vapour as ideal, neglect the liquid volume and treat \(L_m\) as constant. The slope becomes \(d\ln p/dT=L_m/(RT^2)\), giving \(\ln(p_2/p_1)=-(L_m/R)(1/T_2-1/T_1)\). This form clarifies why a straight line may appear on a logarithmic-pressure versus reciprocal-temperature plot. Curvature in measured data can carry information about temperature-dependent latent heat and departures from ideal-gas behaviour. Close to a critical point the assumptions behind the simple integrated expression cease to be reliable.

For a substance whose solid is less dense than its liquid, melting can have a negative coexistence slope even though the latent heat of melting is positive. In the Clapeyron relation the sign then comes from the volume difference. Increasing pressure at fixed temperature favours the phase taking less volume. This sign check connects the derivative calculation to an independently measurable density contrast.

Choose the equilibrium pressure inside a two-phase region

A nonmonotone model isotherm can predict several volumes at one pressure below the critical temperature. The middle segment with \((\partial p/\partial v)_T>0\) is mechanically unstable. Removing that segment alone does not identify the two coexisting phases. At the coexistence pressure \(p_*\) both endpoints have equal molar chemical potential. Write molar Gibbs energy as \(\mu=f+p_*v\), where \(f\) is molar Helmholtz energy on this isotherm. Since \(df=-p(v)\,dv\) at fixed temperature, equality of endpoint values implies

\[0=\mu_g-\mu_l=-\int_{v_l}^{v_g}p(v)\,dv+p_*(v_g-v_l).\]

Geometrically, the areas above and below the horizontal line \(p_*\) cancel. The same condition makes that line's negative slope the common tangent of \(f(v)\) at \(v_l\) and \(v_g\). Between these endpoint volumes the equilibrium material changes the relative amounts of liquid and gas at fixed pressure.

As a local model take \(p=p_0+A(x-x^3)\) with \(x=(v-v_0)/b\), \(A>0\), \(v_0=2.00\times10^{-4}\) and \(b=3.00\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol. At \(p=p_0\), the outer roots \(x=\pm1\) have negative slopes, and the excess pressure is odd in \(x\), verifying equal areas exactly. The stable molar volumes are \(1.70\times10^{-4}\) and \(2.30\times10^{-4}\) m³/mol. At average volume \(2.12\times10^{-4}\) m³/mol, conservation of volume gives a gas mole fraction \((2.12-1.70)/(2.30-1.70)=0.700\). The spinodals, where the original slope vanishes, instead occur at \(x=\pm1/\sqrt3\). They delimit local stability. They are not the coexistence volumes. This cubic is a local representation of the loop and should not be extrapolated to very large or negative volumes.

Why a phase can persist beyond the coexistence line

Equality of bulk chemical potentials determines coexistence, yet a sample can remain liquid below its equilibrium freezing temperature. Forming a small solid nucleus introduces an interface whose free-energy cost can initially outweigh the bulk driving force. Consider a spherical nucleus of radius \(r\) in a pure, uniformly undercooled liquid. Assume an isotropic solid–liquid interfacial energy \(\gamma>0\) and a positive bulk free-energy advantage per volume \(\Delta g_v\) for the solid. Ignore elastic accommodation, walls, impurities and curvature dependence of \(\gamma\).

The gain in stable bulk material is proportional to the volume while the cost is proportional to area. Relative to uniform liquid the work is

\[\Delta G(r)=4\pi r^2\gamma-\frac{4\pi}3r^3\Delta g_v .\]

Differentiating gives the nonzero stationary size \(r_*=2\gamma/\Delta g_v\). The second derivative there is negative, so the critical nucleus lies at a barrier rather than at a stable droplet size. Smaller nuclei reduce their free energy by shrinking, and larger ones reduce it by growing. Substituting the radius into the original expression gives \(\Delta G_*=16\pi\gamma^3/(3\Delta g_v^2)\). Near coexistence, the difference of the two bulk free energies changes as the entropy-of-fusion difference times undercooling. With latent heat per unit volume \(L_v\), use \(\Delta g_v\simeq L_v(T_m-T)/T_m\) for a small fractional undercooling.

Determine how an undercooling changes the barrier

For an illustrative melt take \(T_m=400\) K, \(T=380\) K, \(L_v=3.00\times10^8\) J/m³ and \(\gamma=0.0300\) J/m². The bulk drive is \(1.50\times10^7\) J/m³. Critical radius is \(4.00\) nm and the barrier is approximately \(2.01\times10^{-18}\) J. Since \(k_BT=5.25\times10^{-21}\) J at 380 K, this is roughly 383 thermal energy units, a formidable homogeneous nucleation barrier. This comparison alone is insufficient for an exact waiting time, which also requires a kinetic prefactor and the sample volume.

Doubling undercooling to 40 K doubles the approximate bulk drive, halves the critical radius to 2.00 nm and reduces the energy barrier by four. The nucleation probability per attempt can thus vary by many orders of magnitude while the equilibrium transition temperature remains fixed. As undercooling tends to zero, both critical radius and barrier diverge in this ideal capillarity model. A container wall or an impurity can reduce the interface area needed and initiate crystallization much earlier. This distinction between the coexistence curve and observed onset explains why reducing the cooling rate or changing surface preparation can change a freezing record.

The spherical calculation exposes a distinction between a critical nucleus and a nucleus for which the total formation work has already become negative. Set the formation work itself, rather than its derivative, to zero. Apart from zero radius this gives \(r=3\gamma/\Delta g_v\), larger than the critical radius by a factor of one and a half. A cluster just above the critical radius can still have positive total formation work relative to the uniform liquid, yet growth reduces its free energy. Using the zero-work radius as a kinetic threshold would therefore overestimate the size needed for downhill growth.

Estimate the boiling temperature under reduced pressure

Anchor a liquid’s saturation curve at 373.15 K and 101.325 kPa. For a dilute ideal vapour, negligible liquid volume and approximately constant molar heat of vaporisation, integrate \(d\ln p/dT=L/(RT^2)\). The three curves test sensitivity to the assumed latent heat.

Indistinguishable particles and quantum gases

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Use single-state occupancy to derive Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions and estimate when classical gas statistics cease to apply.

From first principles

Derive the mean population of one quantum state

Consider a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\) exchanging particles and energy with a large reservoir at temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\). In a noninteracting gas, occupation \(n\) of this state changes \(E-\mu N\) by \(n(\epsilon-\mu)\). Its statistical weight is \(q^n\), where \(q=e^{-(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}\). For one specified state including its spin label, fermions allow occupations 0 and 1. Bosons allow every nonnegative integer,

\[Z_F=1+q,\]
\[Z_B=1+q+q^2+\cdots=\frac{1}{1-q}.\]

The Bose sum requires \(q<1\). Differentiating a weight with respect to \(q\) brings down its occupancy, so the normalized average is \(q\,\partial_q\ln Z\). Applying this to the two sums gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1},\]
\[\bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

For \(q\ll1\), both means approach \(q\); visits by additional particles to the same state are then rare. The difference between the distributions follows entirely from which occupation numbers the quantum statistics allow.

Derivation and applications

Derive the first exchange correction to ideal-gas pressure

Before quantum degeneracy becomes strong, exchange statistics already affect the relation between pressure and density. Consider noninteracting particles of mass \(m\) in a large three-dimensional box. All \(g\) internal states have the same energy and chemical potential. Introduce fugacity \(z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\), and a sign \(s=+1\) for bosons or \(-1\) for fermions. At small fugacity the occupation of a one-particle energy \(\epsilon\) is a geometric expansion,

\[\bar n(\epsilon)=z e^{-\beta\epsilon}+s z^2e^{-2\beta\epsilon}+O(z^3),\]
\[\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}.\]

Integrating the first term over momentum gives \(g/\lambda_T^3\) times \(z\). In the second term, doubling the inverse temperature narrows each Cartesian Gaussian by \(\sqrt2\). The density integral therefore gains a factor \(2^{-3/2}\). At fixed temperature the Gibbs–Duhem relation \(dp=n\,d\mu\) gives \(d[p/(k_BT)]=n\,dz/z\). Integration from the empty-gas limit gives the pressure series,

\[\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{3/2}}+O(z^3),\]
\[\frac{p\lambda_T^3}{g k_BT}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(z^3).\]

Experiments often fix density. Put \(y=n\lambda_T^3/g\) and invert the density series once to get \(z=y-s y^2/2^{3/2}+O(y^3)\). Substituting into the pressure eliminates the unmeasured chemical potential,

\[\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1-\frac{s}{2^{5/2}}\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}+O(y^2).\]

At fixed density exchange raises the pressure of fermions and lowers that of bosons. The magnitude grows on cooling because the thermal wavelength increases. Real interactions can also alter the pressure, and the truncated expansion requires small \(y\).

Worked example

Estimate a pressure excess for a dilute Fermi gas

A spin-balanced ideal Fermi gas has \(g=2\), \(n=2.00\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(\lambda_T=400\,\mathrm{nm}\). It has \(y=0.0640\) and \(p/(nk_BT)\simeq1.0113\). A measured classical pressure scale \(nk_BT=20.0\,\mathrm{pPa}\) would therefore acquire an exchange correction of about \(0.226\,\mathrm{pPa}\).

Second application

Recover density from a bosonic pressure deficit

A single-state Bose gas at known \(\lambda_T=500\,\mathrm{nm}\) gives \(p/(nk_BT)=0.9950\). To leading order the deficit fixes \(y=0.0050\times2^{5/2}=0.0283\). The inferred density is \(y/\lambda_T^3=2.26\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). At unchanged temperature halving this density halves the fractional pressure deficit, whereas the absolute pressure correction falls by a factor of four.

Decide which exchanges leave the state unchanged

An ideal classical gas can often be treated with Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. Cooling the gas or increasing its density eventually makes wave packets overlap appreciably. For indistinguishable quantum particles, merely assigning names to exchanged particles must not create extra states. In an ideal gas one can instead specify the occupation of each single-particle state. Identical fermions occupy a given quantum state at most once, including its spin label. Identical bosons can accumulate in the same state.

Use a reservoir that exchanges both energy and particles with the system. Its temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\) are fixed. A microstate with energy \(E\) and particle number \(N\) has grand-canonical weight proportional to \(\exp[-\beta(E-\mu N)]\). This follows by applying the thermodynamic entropy differential to a reservoir that loses both \(E\) and \(N\). The chemical potential sets the mean particle number through these statistical weights.

Sum the allowed occupancies of one state

For a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\), define \(z_\epsilon=\exp[-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)]\). An occupancy of \(n\) contributes weight \(z_\epsilon^n\). For fermions the single-state grand partition sum is \(1+z_\epsilon\), so the mean occupancy is \(z_\epsilon/(1+z_\epsilon)\). For bosons the sum runs over all nonnegative integers and equals \(1/(1-z_\epsilon)\), provided \(z_\epsilon<1\). Differentiating the logarithm of each sum with respect to \(\ln z_\epsilon\) gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad \bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.\]

These expressions describe one state, not the total population within an energy interval. To get the latter, multiply by the density of states and integrate. If the exponential is large then both occupancies reduce to \(e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\). The upper sign suppresses double occupancy while the lower sign enhances occupation near the lowest available energy.

Count the occupied sphere of a cold electron gas

In a large cubic volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries, allowed wave vectors fill reciprocal space with one state per volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) for each spin. At zero temperature the noninteracting electron gas fills all states inside a sphere of radius \(k_F\) and leaves higher ones empty. Including two spin states per wave vector gives \(N=2V(4\pi k_F^3/3)/(2\pi)^3\), so \(k_F=(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}\). For electrons of mass \(m_e\), the Fermi energy is \(\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m_e)\).

With density \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(k_F=1.33\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\simeq6.77\) eV. The associated temperature \(T_F=E_F/k_B\) is approximately \(7.9\times10^4\) K. At room temperature \(k_BT\) is small compared with this energy, so only a narrow region around the Fermi surface changes occupation noticeably. A classical equipartition estimate of the electron contribution to heat capacity would ignore this restriction.

Test the dilute limit with a cold atomic gas

A useful scale for translational wave packets is the thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\). For a single internal state the dimensionless product \(n\lambda_T^3\) compares the volume per particle with the thermal wave volume. Small values support the dilute classical approximation. Near unity the occupancy of low-energy states can become appreciable and the statistics of indistinguishability matter.

For particles of mass \(6.6\times10^{-27}\) kg at 1.0 K the thermal wavelength is approximately 0.88 nm. At density \(10^{25}\) m⁻³, \(n\lambda_T^3\simeq0.0068\). Reducing temperature to 0.040 K at unchanged density multiplies that product by \(25^{3/2}=125\), giving about 0.85. This is a degeneracy estimate, not a prediction of the phase of a real interacting gas. Once quantum statistics matter, particle interactions, dimensionality and confinement can also affect which phase the experiment actually finds.

Internal degeneracy enters these comparisons through the density per available internal state. Suppose the same total particle density is spread equally across two spin states with identical spatial dispersion. Each can fill the same momentum levels independently. In a cold ideal Fermi gas the Fermi wavevector at fixed total density is then smaller than in a fully spin-polarized gas. A measured pressure change after altering spin populations can therefore contain a statistical contribution even if the mass, volume and total particle count remain fixed.

Determine the capacity of excited Bose states

Ideal bosons can accumulate macroscopically in one spatial state. Consider a uniform three-dimensional noninteracting gas of identical spinless particles with fixed particle number in a large box. Set the lowest energy to zero. The excited-state occupation at energy \(\epsilon\) is \(1/[\exp((\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT))-1]\) and requires \(\mu\le0\). In the thermodynamic limit, counting momentum states in a sphere gives an excited-state density per volume proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\). Consequently, as the density rises, taking \(\mu\) toward zero from below can increase the excited population only up to a finite value.

Explicitly integrate \(d^3p/(2\pi\hbar)^3\) over positive energies \(\epsilon=p^2/(2m)\). At \(\mu=0\), setting \(x=\epsilon/(k_BT)\) reduces the integral to \(\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}/(e^x-1)dx=\Gamma(3/2)\zeta(3/2)\). The result is

\[n_{\rm ex,max}=\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\lambda_T^3},\qquad \lambda_T=\frac h{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}},\qquad \zeta(3/2)=2.6124\ldots .\]

Below the temperature where this capacity equals the total density, additional particles occupy the lowest state. Solving gives \(T_c=(2\pi\hbar^2/mk_B)[n/\zeta(3/2)]^{2/3}\) and \(N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^{3/2}\). The state count explains why a high phase-space density matters. Reaching this regime does not require individual particles to lose their identity at a sharply defined minimum separation.

Estimate the onset temperature in a box

Take \(n=1.00\times10^{20}\) m⁻³ and particle mass \(1.44\times10^{-25}\) kg. At the ideal transition the thermal wavelength is \((2.6124/n)^{1/3}=297\) nm. Inverting its definition gives \(T_c=h^2/(2\pi m k_B\lambda_T^2)=0.398\) μK. At \(T=0.250\) μK, the excited fraction is \((0.250/0.398)^{3/2}=0.498\) and the condensate fraction is approximately 0.502. Doubling the density raises \(T_c\) by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\) at fixed particle mass.

The predicted fraction tends to zero continuously as \(T\) approaches \(T_c\) from below, and tends to one as temperature approaches zero for the ideal gas. A harmonic trapping potential has a different density of states and hence a different temperature exponent from a uniform box. Finite size smooths the mathematical onset; interactions and inaccurate thermometry can shift its inferred location. Comparing the number in the narrow lowest-momentum component with the thermal-cloud population requires that the analysis resolve these two contributions rather than simply count all particles near the centre of a spatial image.

The dimensionality of the gas is as essential as Bose exchange itself. In a uniform two-dimensional ideal box the single-particle density of states tends to a constant at low energy. At chemical potential approaching zero, Bose occupation varies as \(k_BT/\epsilon\) there. Its energy integral consequently diverges logarithmically rather than approaching a finite excited-state capacity. The three-dimensional derivation of a nonzero transition temperature cannot then be carried across unchanged. A finite box, a confining trap or interparticle interactions can all alter the observed low-energy behaviour and must be identified when comparing different experimental geometries.

Repeat the counting in a harmonic trap

A harmonic trap changes the number of available excited states even for particles with the same mass and statistics. For three oscillator frequencies \(\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z\), measure energy above the lowest level and suppose \(k_BT\) greatly exceeds each \(\hbar\omega_i\). The quantum labels \(n_i\) are nonnegative integers with energy \(\sum_i\hbar\omega_i n_i\). The count below energy \(E\) is approximately the volume of the positive-coordinate tetrahedron bounded by this plane, namely \(E^3/(6\hbar^3\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)\). Differentiating gives an energy density of states proportional to \(E^2\).

At the onset of condensation integrate this density against \(1/(e^{E/k_BT}-1)\). The identity \(\int_0^\infty x^2/(e^x-1)\,dx=2\zeta(3)\) then gives

\[N_{\rm ex,max}=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar\omega}\right)^3,\qquad \bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}.\]

For \(N=1.00\times10^5\) particles in an isotropic 100 Hz trap, \(\hbar\bar\omega/k_B=4.80\) nK and \([N/\zeta(3)]^{1/3}=43.7\), so the predicted transition temperature is \(0.210\) μK. At \(0.150\) μK the condensate fraction is \(1-(0.150/0.210)^3=0.636\). An analysis using the uniform-box exponent at the same temperature ratio would instead give 0.396. The difference comes entirely from the confinement-dependent state count.

Reducing all three trap frequencies at fixed particle number lowers the onset temperature in direct proportion. This differs from enlarging a uniform box at fixed mean density, which leaves its bulk critical temperature unchanged. Specifying how particle number, volume and external confinement are controlled is therefore essential when interpreting a measured condensate fraction.

Chemical equilibrium and reaction extent

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Minimize Gibbs energy along a permitted reaction and predict a composition change when the system is compressed.

From first principles

Build the reaction quotient from chemical potentials

At fixed temperature and pressure, a closed reacting mixture equilibrates by minimizing Gibbs energy subject to conservation of its constituent elements. Let \(\nu_i\) be the balanced stoichiometric coefficient of species \(i\), positive for a product and negative for a reactant. Advancing the reaction by a small extent \(d\xi\) changes each amount by \(dn_i=\nu_i d\xi\). The free-energy change per mole of reaction is then

\[\frac{dG}{d\xi}=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=\Delta_rG .\]

For an interior equilibrium this derivative vanishes. Chemical potentials can be written \(\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln a_i\) using dimensionless activities relative to specified standard states. Collecting all the logarithms gives

\[\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q,\]
\[Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i},\]
\[K=e^{-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)}.\]

The equilibrium composition satisfies \(Q=K\). For an ideal gas, \(a_i=p_i/p^\circ\); nonideal mixtures require activities measured or modeled for the mixture. If \(Q<K\), a small forward reaction lowers \(G\). This thermodynamic preference determines a direction near the present state while leaving the reaction timescale to kinetics.

Derivation and applications

Propagate an equilibrium constant when reaction heat capacity changes the enthalpy

An equilibrium constant quoted at one temperature does not determine the equilibrium composition at another unless the energy change of the reaction is also known. Consider a reversible reaction with a dimensionless standard equilibrium constant \(K(T)\) and unchanged standard-state choices. All reaction quantities below are per mole of reaction as written. At fixed standard pressure, the Gibbs differential \(dG=-S\,dT\), together with \(G=H-TS\), gives \(d(G/T)/dT=-H/T^2\). Apply this identity to the stoichiometric differences of standard molar quantities,

\[\ln K=-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT},\]
\[\frac{d\ln K}{dT}=\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ(T)}{RT^2}.\]

If heat capacities of products and reactants differ, the enthalpy changes with temperature. Over an interval where their stoichiometric difference \(\Delta_r C_p^\circ\) can be treated as constant, integration of \(dH^\circ=C_p^\circ dT\) gives

\[\Delta_rH^\circ(T)=\Delta_rH^\circ(T_1)+\Delta_r C_p^\circ(T-T_1).\]

Insert this linear enthalpy into the derivative for \(\ln K\). The constant part integrates as \(1/T^2\); the term proportional to temperature also produces a logarithm. The two-temperature relation is

\[\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1}=\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ(T_1)}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)+\frac{\Delta_r C_p^\circ}{R}\left[\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+\frac{T_1}{T_2}-1\right].\]

Both terms vanish when the two temperatures coincide. For a small temperature interval the heat-capacity correction begins at second order, consistent with the local slope depending only on the current reaction enthalpy. Phase changes would interrupt the assumed smooth enthalpy law.

Worked example

Estimate a change in an association equilibrium

Take \(K_1=4.00\) at \(300\,\mathrm K\), \(\Delta_rH^\circ(T_1)=-20.0\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\), and \(\Delta_rC_p^\circ=100\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}\). At \(360\,\mathrm K\) the enthalpy term in \(\ln(K_2/K_1)\) is \(-1.336\) and the heat-capacity term is \(+0.188\). Thus \(K_2=4.00e^{-1.148}=1.27\). The association remains exothermic throughout the interval, although its magnitude declines on heating.

Second application

Infer enthalpy from equilibrium measurements

For a different reaction \(K\) rises from \(0.500\) at \(290\,\mathrm K\) to \(0.900\) at \(310\,\mathrm K\). If the reaction heat-capacity difference is negligible here, inversion gives \(\Delta_rH^\circ=R\ln(0.900/0.500)/(1/290-1/310)=22.0\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\). The positive sign is consistent with the increased equilibrium constant on heating. At \(300\,\mathrm K\) the same enthalpy predicts a local logarithmic slope of \(0.0293\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), testable with a more closely spaced measurement.

A reaction has one constrained direction

A closed vessel can change particle species while conserving the total number of atoms of each element. At fixed temperature and pressure the equilibrium state minimizes Gibbs energy. Introduce a reaction extent \(\xi\) in moles and stoichiometric coefficients \(\nu_i\), negative for reactants and positive for products. A small reaction advance changes amounts by \(dn_i=\nu_i\,d\xi\). The Gibbs differential at fixed \(T,p\) therefore gives

\[dG=\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i=\Delta_r G\,d\xi,\qquad \Delta_rG=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i .\]

An interior minimum satisfies \(\Delta_rG=0\). A negative value means that a small positive advance lowers \(G\). Reversibility of the microscopic chemistry is assumed for equilibrium to be accessible. Thermodynamic preference alone does not provide the time needed to reach it.

Activities and the equilibrium constant

For ideal gases \(\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ(T)+RT\ln(p_i/p^\circ)\). The activity is the dimensionless ratio \(a_i=p_i/p^\circ\). Inserting this expression into the reaction Gibbs energy gives \(\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q\), with \(Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}\). The equilibrium value of \(Q\) is \(K=\exp[-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)]\). Standard pressure supplies the reference that makes the logarithm and the equilibrium constant dimensionless even when the total molecular count changes.

For the dimerization \(2A\rightleftharpoons A_2\), begin with one mole of monomer units and define \(\alpha\) as the fraction of units bound into dimers. Then \(n_A=1-\alpha\), \(n_{A_2}=\alpha/2\) and total gas amount is \(1-\alpha/2\) moles on this basis. Partial pressures follow from mole fractions times total pressure. Substitution into the reaction quotient gives

\[Q=\frac{\alpha(2-\alpha)}{4(1-\alpha)^2}\frac{p^\circ}{p}.\]

The ratio of activities is determined by both composition and total pressure. Compression at the original equilibrium composition immediately lowers \(Q\), making the forward reaction favourable. The subsequent association reduces the number of gas particles while conserving monomer units.

A pressure change at fixed temperature

Suppose \(K=0.750\) at the chosen temperature. Let \(y=1-\alpha\). Because \(\alpha(2-\alpha)=1-y^2\), solving \(Q=K\) gives \(y=[1+4Kp/p^\circ]^{-1/2}\). At \(p=p^\circ\) this gives \(\alpha=0.500\), with one half of all units bound and only one third of gas particles being dimers. At \(p=4p^\circ\) the bound-unit fraction is \(1-1/\sqrt{13}=0.723\). The distinction between fractions of conserved units and fractions of gas molecules matters for interpreting a spectroscopic composition measurement.

A temperature change can also move equilibrium. Differentiating \(\ln K=-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)\) and using the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation gives \(d\ln K/dT=\Delta_rH^\circ/(RT^2)\). For an exothermic association increasing temperature decreases \(K\) locally. Pressure changes at fixed temperature leave \(K\) itself fixed under the same standard-state convention. It is the composition-dependent quotient that adjusts to the imposed mechanical conditions.

The distinction between a chemical potential per mole and one per particle is essential in this derivation. Here partial pressures were combined with \(R\) because the extent was measured in moles. Molecular statistical mechanics instead uses \(k_B\) with energies per molecule, but both conventions give the same dimensionless ratio of equilibrium activities. A useful numerical check is to scale every initial amount and the container volume by the same factor at fixed temperature. Mole fractions and partial pressures stay fixed, so the equilibrium fraction is unchanged even though twice as many molecules may react.

Use a membrane to turn chemical equilibrium into pressure

Equality of chemical potential can constrain a species even when other species cannot move. Separate pure solvent at pressure \(p\) from a dilute ideal solution at pressure \(p+\Pi\) by a membrane permeable only to solvent. Hold temperature fixed and assume a nearly incompressible solvent with approximately constant partial molar volume \(\bar v_w\). The solvent can exchange across the membrane; solute cannot. Its equilibrium condition is therefore equality of solvent chemical potentials, even though total pressures and solute concentrations differ.

For an ideal solution the solvent chemical potential relative to pure solvent at the same pressure changes by \(RT\ln x_w\), where \(x_w\) is the solvent mole fraction. Increasing pressure by \(\Pi\) raises the solvent chemical potential by \(\int_p^{p+\Pi}\bar v_w\,dp'\simeq\bar v_w\Pi\). Equating the two sides gives

\[\Pi=-\frac{RT}{\bar v_w}\ln x_w\simeq \frac{RT}{\bar v_w}x_s\simeq RT c_s .\]

The last approximation assumes a small solute mole fraction \(x_s\) and solution volume close to the solvent volume, with \(c_s\) in moles of independently dissolved particles per cubic metre. The pressure difference is positive because mixing lowers the chemical potential of solvent. If pressure is released, solvent flows into the solution, modifying its concentration until a new balance is attained.

Estimate a hydrostatic head and a molar mass

At 298 K dissolve 2.00 g/L of a nonvolatile solute with molar mass 0.200 kg/mol. The mass concentration is 2.00 kg/m³, so \(c_s=10.0\) mol/m³. Predicted osmotic pressure is \(8.314\times298\times10.0=24.8\) kPa. With solution density approximated by \(1000\) kg/m³, an open-column experiment would need height difference \(\Pi/(\rho g)=2.53\) m to balance it. The final concentration must be measured after the column rises, since transferred solvent can appreciably dilute a small sample.

Conversely, for the same 2.00 g/L preparation a pressure reading of 12.4 kPa would imply an apparent molar mass \(RT(2.00)/12400=0.400\) kg/mol. Association of two formula units into one particle is one possible explanation if the chemical analysis gives 0.200 kg/mol. Nonideal activities, leakage and concentration calibration can also change the inferred particle count. Repeating at lower concentrations tests whether \(\Pi/c_s\) approaches the ideal-gas constant times temperature. In the pure-solvent limit \(x_w\to1\) the chemical-potential difference vanishes so no osmotic pressure is required, despite the membrane still being present.

Concentration units can be a major source of error in this example. One mole per litre is a thousand moles per cubic metre, so the gas constant in joules per mole kelvin gives a pressure in pascals only after this conversion. For fully dissociated dilute electrolytes there may also be more than one independently mobile particle per formula unit. Conversely strong ion pairing or aggregation can reduce the count. Measuring a nonvolatile neutral reference solute of known molar mass in the same apparatus provides a way to test both the pressure scale and the effectiveness of the membrane.

How a pair potential changes the gas pressure

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

A low-density gas can measure its own intermolecular forces. At a prescribed temperature the first deviation from ideal behaviour is a volume. Its sign tells us whether overlap exclusion or attraction makes the larger contribution.

Separate independent pairs from correlated pairs

Let \(N\) classical particles occupy volume \(V\), with a total potential energy equal to a sum of short-range pair potentials \(u(r_{ij})\). Momentum integrals give the ideal-gas factors. In the remaining positional integral write each pair's Boltzmann weight as \(e^{-\beta u}=1+f(r)\). The auxiliary function \(f=e^{-\beta u}-1\) vanishes where particles do not interact. In a large vessel the positional integral with one \(f\) is \(V^{N-1}\int f(\boldsymbol r)\,d^3r\). One particle of the pair may be placed anywhere and its neighbour's relative coordinate is sampled against \(f\). There are \(N(N-1)/2\) possible unordered pairs.

In the low-density expansion of \(\ln Z\), independent repetitions of separate pairs cancel against products from the logarithm. The first connected correction is \(N(N-1)\int f/(2V)\). This is a density expansion of the free energy per particle. The total correction need not be small for a macroscopic sample. Differentiating with \(p=k_BT(\partial\ln Z/\partial V)_{T,N}\) supplies a minus sign from the reciprocal volume. In the thermodynamic limit with number density \(\rho=N/V\),

\[\frac{p}{\rho k_BT}=1+B_2(T)\rho+O(\rho^2),\]
\[B_2=-\tfrac12\int f\,d^3r=-2\pi\int_0^\infty f(r)r^2\,dr.\]

An impenetrable core gives \(f=-1\). An attractive annulus gives \(f>0\). The sign of \(B_2\) follows from their integrated volumes, including the spherical shell factor \(r^2\).

Integrate a square well

Set \(u=\infty\) for \(r<d\), \(u=-\epsilon\) for \(d<r<\lambda d\), and \(u=0\) outside. The two nonzero contributions are constants integrated over spherical volumes. With \(B_{\rm core}=2\pi d^3/3\), obtain

\[B_2=B_{\rm core}\{1-(\lambda^3-1)[e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1]\}.\]

A sphere of diameter \(d\) has actual volume \(\pi d^3/6\). The hard-core coefficient is four times that volume. The forbidden positions concern the centre of a second particle, and the factor of one half avoids counting an interacting pair twice. For \(\lambda=1.5\) and \(k_BT/\epsilon=2\), the ratio \(B_2/B_{\rm core}=1-2.375(e^{0.5}-1)\simeq-0.541\). A dilute sample with \(\rho B_{\rm core}=0.02\) consequently has \(p/(\rho k_BT)\simeq0.9892\) at this order.

Locate the temperature at which the slope vanishes

Set \(B_2=0\) and solve for the Boyle temperature. The result is \(k_BT_B/\epsilon=1/\ln[1+1/(\lambda^3-1)]\), or 2.846 for the preceding range. Vary the width of the well. A larger attraction volume can offset the hard core even when the temperature is high. At \(T_B\) the pressure curve has zero first density correction. Three-particle contributions can still produce curvature. At very low temperatures pair binding can become strong enough that even a superficially low density fails to justify truncation.

Why two coexisting phases share a tangent

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

A material prepared with an intermediate composition can separate into two regions of different compositions. Its total composition is conserved. How can a free-energy graph predict both final compositions and the amount of each phase?

Minimise the free energy of a mixture

Work at fixed temperature and volume with a constant density of equivalent sites. Let \(f(c)\) be the Helmholtz free energy per site of a spatially uniform state with fraction \(c\) of species B. If the two regions have compositions \(c_1,c_2\) and the second contains fraction \(x\) of all sites, conservation requires \(\bar c=(1-x)c_1+xc_2\). Neglect interface energy per site in the macroscopic limit. The mixture free energy per site is then the weighted sum of the two \(f\)'s, a point on the chord joining them.

Introduce a multiplier \(\mu\) and minimise \((1-x)f(c_1)+xf(c_2)-\mu[(1-x)c_1+xc_2-\bar c]\). Varying \(c_1,c_2\) when both regions are present gives \(f'(c_1)=f'(c_2)=\mu\). This derivative measures the chemical work to replace an A particle by B. Varying \(x\) supplies the missing condition,

\[f(c_2)-f(c_1)=\mu(c_2-c_1).\]

The common slope therefore equals the slope of the connecting chord. A straight line must touch the free energy at both endpoints and lie below competing homogeneous states. Equivalently \(\int_{c_1}^{c_2}[f'(c)-\mu]\,dc=0\). This equal-area condition is the composition version of Maxwell's construction. For liquids plotted as pressure versus specific volume, the conjugate slope acquires the sign from \(p=-\partial F/\partial V\).

Find the coexistence and stability boundaries separately

Take \(f(c)=A[(c-m)^2-d^2]^2+\mu_0c\), with \(A>0\), \(m=0.5\) and \(0<d<0.5\). Subtracting the linear term tilts all trial chords by the same amount. At \(c=m\pm d\) the remaining term vanishes with zero derivative. Hence the common tangent is \(\mu_0 c\), and the coexistence compositions are \(m-d,m+d\). At \(d=0.30\) a sample of average composition 0.45 separates into 0.20 and 0.80 regions. Conservation gives \(x=(0.45-0.20)/(0.80-0.20)=5/12\) for the B-rich region.

Now perturb a uniform state into two equal parts at \(c\pm\eta\). Their average energy differs from the uniform energy by \(f''(c)\eta^2/2+O(\eta^4)\). Here \(f''=4A[3(c-m)^2-d^2]\). Between \(m-d/\sqrt3\) and \(m+d/\sqrt3\) infinitesimal fluctuations reduce the energy. Between either of these limits and the nearest coexistence endpoint a uniform state resists small fluctuations but a sufficiently large rearrangement can still lower the energy. This accounts for metastability and for the distinction between spinodal and coexistence boundaries. The uniform model does not predict a nucleation time because interfaces carry no energy in this construction.

Get a phase fraction from a graph

The display subtracts \(\mu_0c\) and measures energies in \(A\). Move the mean composition across the whole interval. Outside the coexistence window the stable composition is homogeneous. Inside, the endpoints stay put while the rich-region fraction changes linearly. Compare \(c=0.45\) to \(c=0.55\) for the same gap. Reflection symmetry exchanges the two phase fractions and leaves the free-energy saving unchanged.

When does a polar crystal change its preferred state?

Fields and the response of matter

Derive stable and unstable dielectric responses from a free-energy landscape. Separate a reversible small-signal capacitance from a switching experiment.

Why one voltage can support several measured polarisations

Some insulating crystals develop a spontaneous electric polarisation as temperature is lowered. The high-temperature phase may be symmetric under inversion so that states with polarisation \(+P\) and \(-P\) are equivalent in zero field. Near a continuous transition, treat one component of polarisation as an order parameter. A local free-energy density compatible with the symmetry is \(f(P)=a(T-T_c)P^2/2+bP^4/4\) with \(a,b>0\). The omitted terms limit how far in temperature or field this truncated expansion is reliable. The positive quartic term keeps the energy bounded when the quadratic coefficient is negative.

For a device whose applied electric field \(E\) is controlled, the appropriate potential in this simple model is \(g(P;E)=f(P)-EP\). The linear term records external work; changing what is held fixed changes the energy to minimise. Differentiation gives the constitutive equation \(E=a(T-T_c)P+bP^3\). An algebraic root is an equilibrium candidate. A stable local response also requires positive second derivative \(a(T-T_c)+3bP^2\). A very accurate rootfinder can return an unstable stationary point if this second test is omitted.

Find the equilibrium branches and their response

Introduce scaled polarisation \(p\), field \(e\) and temperature parameter \(s\) such that \(g=p^4/4+sp^2/2-ep\). Then \(e=p^3+sp\). For \(s>0\), the slope \(3p^2+s\) stays positive. Each field has one stable solution. At \(e=0\), \(p=0\) and the small-signal susceptibility \(dp/de=1/s\) increases when approaching the ideal critical point from above.

For \(s=-1,\ e=0\), stationary values are \(-1,0,+1\). The middle root has curvature −1 and is a local maximum. Each outer root has curvature 2 and is a minimum. The susceptibility about a selected outer state is \(1/2\) in the scaled units. The fact that the two domains can carry opposite zero-field polarisation does not make the harmonic response negative. The mere existence of a negative-slope segment in the cubic constitutive curve is insufficient to infer a stable isolated small-signal device with that differential response.

Derive the limit of a metastable homogeneous state

Imagine preparing the negative-polarisation state at \(s=-1\), then increasing the applied field slowly. A positive field lowers the energy of the positive state. The negative state can persist locally behind a barrier even after it ceases to be the global minimum. A model of perfectly homogeneous evolution without early nucleation follows this metastable branch until the curvature vanishes. Solve \(3p^2-1=0\), choose \(p=-1/\sqrt3\), and insert it into \(e=p^3-p\). The positive limiting field is \(e=2/(3\sqrt3)\simeq0.3849\). At the opposite spinodal the signs reverse.

This is a limit of the particular energy model. Real switching can occur earlier through domain walls, nucleation, defects or thermally activated barrier crossing. Observing a smaller coercive field in a specimen is not evidence that the cubic equation was solved inaccurately. To compare an actual hysteresis loop with a calculation, state the sweep rate, boundary circuit, sample preparation and a dynamical or nucleation model in addition to equilibrium free energy.

Control-volume momentum and forces on fittings

Fluid mechanics

Use the velocity vector, gauge pressure and a consistent force boundary to find the support load on a pipe bend.

From first principles

Relate a support reaction to momentum carried by a stream

Newton's second law applies to a fixed set of fluid particles: external force equals the time derivative of their momentum. In a pipe bend it is easier to observe a stationary control volume that particles continually enter and leave. The rate of change for the material particles consists of storage in that volume plus the net rate at which momentum crosses its boundary.

\[\sum\mathbf F=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_V\rho\mathbf u\,dV+\oint_{\partial V}\rho\mathbf u(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,dA .\]

The dot product determines whether mass crosses inwards or outwards, while the other velocity factor retains the direction of the momentum carried. For steady uniform inlet and outlet sections, the flux integral reduces to

\[\mathbf F_{\rm wall}+\mathbf F_{\rm pressure}+\mathbf W=\dot m(\mathbf V_2-\mathbf V_1).\]

Pressure pushes inward normal to each open section, so its contribution must be included before solving for the wall force. The force of the fluid on the bend is opposite to the wall's force on the fluid. Equal inlet and outlet speed can still give a substantial support load when their directions differ.

Derivation and applications

Derive the irreversible head loss across a sudden expansion

A sudden enlargement slows a liquid and partly restores static pressure. Consider steady incompressible flow from area \(A_1\) into area \(A_2>A_1\) in a horizontal pipe. Choose the downstream section after mixing has produced an approximately uniform velocity again. Approximate both station profiles as uniform, neglect wall shear over the short control volume and take the mean pressure on the annular shoulder to be the upstream pressure \(p_1\). Mass conservation makes the two mean speeds \(V_1,V_2\) proportional to inverse area.

\[Q=A_1V_1=A_2V_2,\]
\[p_1A_2-p_2A_2=\rho Q(V_2-V_1).\]

The inlet and shoulder together contribute \(p_1A_2\) to the axial pressure force. Rearranging the momentum balance gives a positive downstream pressure rise, despite the separated mixing region.

\[p_2-p_1=\rho V_2(V_1-V_2).\]

Insert that pressure rise into the mechanical energy balance. The difference between kinetic energy released and pressure recovered is the specific energy dissipated in mixing, expressed below as a head of flowing liquid.

\[h_L=\frac{V_1^2-V_2^2}{2g}-\frac{p_2-p_1}{\rho g}=\frac{(V_1-V_2)^2}{2g}.\]

This result tends to zero quadratically as the area change vanishes. A gradual diffuser would require a different shoulder-pressure and separation model. Pressure recovery can thus coexist with positive mechanical-energy loss and its measurement alone does not provide the loss.

Worked example

Audit an abrupt pipe enlargement

Water moving at 3.00 m/s enters a pipe section with three times the area. Its final speed is 1.00 m/s. Taking \(\rho=1000\) kg/m³, the pressure rises by \(1000(1.00)(2.00)=2.00\) kPa. The kinetic pressure falls by 4.00 kPa; the missing 2.00 kPa is a head loss of 0.204 m. For 6.00 L/s, the rate of mechanical energy dissipation is \(Q\rho g h_L=12.0\) W.

Second application

Recover the enlargement ratio from a head test

Another rig measures \(V_1=2.50\) m/s and \(h_L=0.0800\) m. Taking the positive speed drop yields \(V_2=2.50-\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.0800)}=1.25\) m/s. The inferred area ratio is \(A_2/A_1=V_1/V_2=2.00\) to the stated precision. The expected water pressure rise is about 1.56 kPa, a separately measurable check on the shoulder-pressure assumption.

Momentum crosses the boundary with the mass

A pipe bend can exert a substantial force on its supports even when its inlet and outlet speeds agree. Momentum has direction. Turning a stream requires force and gives the fitting an equal and opposite load. Enclose the fluid inside the fitting in a fixed control volume. The external forces include wall traction, pressure on the control surfaces and body forces such as gravity.

A surface element transports mass per second \(\rho(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)dA\). Multiply by the local velocity to obtain momentum per second. Newton’s second law applied to the control volume reads

\[\sum\mathbf F_{\rm ext}=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\rm CV}\rho\mathbf u\,dV+\int_{\rm CS}\rho\mathbf u(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,dA.\]

For a steady fitting with nearly uniform inlet and outlet velocities this reduces to a sum of outgoing \(\dot m\mathbf u\) minus incoming \(\dot m\mathbf u\). Use one fixed set of axes even as pipe directions change. Pressure always pushes into the control volume, whereas the flux carries the direction of the local velocity, including its sign on the chosen axis.

Include the end pressures explicitly

Take a horizontal bend turning water from the positive \(x\) direction to the positive \(y\) direction. The inlet and outlet areas are each 0.010 m² and \(Q=0.040\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\). Their speeds are both 4.0 m/s and \(\dot m=40\) kg/s. The net outward momentum flux vector is \((-160,+160)\) N. Let the gauge pressures be 90 kPa at inlet and 80 kPa at outlet. Pressure pushes +900 N in \(x\) through the inlet and −800 N in \(y\) through the outlet.

Let \(\mathbf W\) be the fitting’s wall force on the enclosed fluid, with external atmospheric effects accounted for consistently using gauge pressures. Then \(\mathbf W+(900,-800)=(-160,160)\) N, giving \(\mathbf W=(-1060,960)\) N. The fluid’s net gauge load on the fitting is the opposite, \((1060,-960)\) N, of magnitude 1.43 kN. Supports plus connected pipework must provide the opposite force for a stationary fitting, in addition to carrying the weight of hardware and fluid. The strongest contribution here stems from the pressure terms, despite the conspicuous turn in velocity.

Recover momentum from a nonuniform profile

A flow meter usually measures \(Q\), not \(\int u^2dA\). At a plane normal to a unidirectional profile define \(\beta=\int u^2dA/(A\bar u^2)\). Then the axial momentum rate is \(\beta\rho Q\bar u\). Uniform flow gives \(\beta=1\). For a developed laminar circular pipe \(u=2\bar u[1-(r/R)^2]\); inserting \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\) in the integral gives \(\beta=4/3\). The largest velocities carry a disproportionate share of momentum. If one bend cross-section admits strong swirl or backflow, resolve vector fluxes instead of applying a single profile coefficient.

Recognise an open-stream boundary

A free jet at atmospheric pressure strikes a fixed flat plate normal to its axis and turns into a thin sheet that leaves sideways. Neglecting rebound and friction far from the impact, the outgoing axial momentum flux is zero. For water flowing at 10 m/s through a 20 mm diameter nozzle, \(\dot m=3.14\) kg/s, so the axial force on the plate is 31.4 N. Both the incoming and outgoing free-stream control surfaces have zero gauge pressure. The plate force then follows entirely from the change of momentum. A nozzle bolted to a pressurised pipe would require different pressure forces even if it produced exactly the same free jet.

A jet catches a moving vane

Consider a single flat vane moving steadily in the jet direction at speed \(u\). In the vane frame the arriving volume rate is \(A(V-u)\) and the incoming streamwise relative velocity is \(V-u\). If the jet spreads tangentially across the face, the resulting normal force is \(\rho A(V-u)^2\). For water, \(A=2.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), \(V=10\) m/s and \(u=4\) m/s, this gives 7.2 N. Its mechanical power on the moving vane is \(Fu=28.8\,\mathrm W\). Using the stationary volume rate would count fluid parcels that the moving vane never catches.

Derive the two depths of a hydraulic jump

A hydraulic jump converts shallow fast open-channel flow into deeper slow flow over a short distance. Turbulent mixing dissipates mechanical energy, so applying an inviscid energy balance between the two uniform sections would miss its defining loss. Instead choose a control volume bounded by sections before and after the roller, the horizontal bed and the free surface. Approximate the velocity as uniform and the pressure distribution as hydrostatic at the two end sections. Atmospheric pressure cancels, and over the short reach assume the integral of bed friction is small compared with the pressure and momentum forces.

In a rectangular channel per unit width let the volume rate be \(q\), depths \(y_1,y_2\) and speeds \(v_i=q/y_i\). The horizontal pressure resultant per width is \(\rho g y_i^2/2\). Equating pressure force difference to momentum-flux change gives

\[\frac{y_1^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{g y_1}=\frac{y_2^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{g y_2}.\]

One solution is the unchanged depth. Factoring out \(y_2-y_1\) for the nontrivial solution gives \(q^2=g y_1 y_2(y_1+y_2)/2\). With upstream Froude number \(Fr_1=v_1/\sqrt{g y_1}\), the downstream-to-upstream depth ratio becomes \([\sqrt{1+8Fr_1^2}-1]/2\). The deeper downstream branch occurs when \(Fr_1>1\). As \(Fr_1\to1\), the ratio tends to unity and the finite jump disappears.

Calculate the energy dissipated in a channel transition

A horizontal test channel carries water at \(y_1=0.20\,\mathrm m\) and \(v_1=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Boundary layers and momentum thickness

Fluid mechanics

Connect a measured velocity deficit to wall drag and distinguish the physical edge of a boundary layer from its integral thicknesses.

From first principles

Obtain wall drag from the streamwise momentum deficit

A thin boundary layer slows fluid next to a solid wall. Consider steady two-dimensional flow over a smooth flat plate with constant external speed \(U\) and pressure. Use unit span and constant density \(\rho\). Choose a control volume ending at location \(x\), with its upper surface a streamline outside the layer. Mass conservation relates the uniform inlet flow to the downstream velocity profile. Multiplying the discharged mass in a strip by the streamwise speed it has lost gives that strip's contribution to the momentum deficit.

\[dD=\rho\,u(y)[U-u(y)]\,dy,\]
\[D(x)=\rho U^2\theta(x),\]
\[\theta=\int_0^\infty\frac uU\left(1-\frac uU\right)dy .\]

The integrated deficit equals the drag per unit span exerted by the wall on the flow in magnitude. Extending the plate by a small \(dx\) adds wall drag \(\tau_w\,dx\), so differentiating yields

\[\tau_w=\rho U^2\frac{d\theta}{dx},\]
\[C_f=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U^2/2}=2\frac{d\theta}{dx}.\]

These relations assume negligible pressure gradient. The thickness \(\theta\) weights lost velocity by the mass flux actually present in that part of the profile.

Derivation and applications

Relate displacement thickness to boundary-layer entrainment

Mass conservation requires a weak normal flow at the edge of a developing boundary layer. Consider steady, two-dimensional incompressible flow over an impermeable flat plate with constant external speed \(U\). Let \(u(x,y)\) be tangential velocity, \(v(x,y)\) normal velocity and \(y=0\) the wall. Integrate continuity from the wall to a fixed measurement height \(H\) chosen well outside the layer throughout the region of interest. Since \(v(x,0)=0\),

\[\partial_x u+\partial_y v=0,\]
\[v(x,H)=-\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^H u(x,y)\,dy .\]

The integral would equal \(UH\) for a uniform velocity profile. The missing volume flux per unit span defines displacement thickness \(\delta^*\) times \(U\). For constant \(H,U\), differentiating cancels the large uniform part and connects the missing flux directly to the weak external normal velocity.

\[U\delta^*=\int_0^H(U-u)\,dy,\]
\[v(x,H)=U\frac{d\delta^*}{dx}.\]

For an illustrative profile take \(u/U=2\eta-\eta^2\) up to \(\eta=y/\delta=1\) and \(u=U\) above it. Its deficit is \((1-\eta)^2\) and its integral is one third. When \(\delta\) grows as the square root of distance from the leading edge, the normal velocity follows by differentiation.

\[\delta^*=\frac{\delta}{3},\]
\[\delta=C\sqrt x\ \Longrightarrow\ v(x,H)=\frac{U\delta}{6x}.\]

The sign is positive because the low-speed layer occupies an increasing share of the fixed-height section. The value at fixed external height differs from the mass entering a control surface that itself follows the growing edge.

Worked example

Estimate normal flow above a plate

At \(x=0.500\) m, a measured polynomial layer has \(\delta=6.00\) mm and \(U=4.00\) m/s. If it grows locally as \(\sqrt x\), the predicted edge velocity is \(4.00(0.00600)/(6\times0.500)=0.00800\) m/s. Its ratio to the external tangential speed is only 0.00200. The displacement thickness is 2.00 mm, the height by which a uniform-speed stream would need to be displaced to produce the same missing flux.

Second application

Independent surveys at \(x=0.200\) and 0.600 m find displacement thicknesses 1.20 and 2.00 mm. At \(U=3.00\) m/s the interval-average normal velocity is \(3.00(0.000800)/0.400=0.00600\) m/s. Over a plate span of 0.500 m this corresponds to outward volume flow \(0.00600(0.400)(0.500)=1.20\) L/s through the fixed upper face, equal to the growth of the streamwise flux deficit.

Viscosity matters where velocity changes rapidly

Flow past a smooth plate with a large length-based Reynolds number often has nearly uniform speed outside a thin layer next to the wall. No slip requires zero velocity at the stationary surface. Inside the thin region, transverse velocity gradients can be large enough for viscosity to balance streamwise inertia. If the free-stream speed is \(U\), the streamwise distance is \(x\) and the layer thickness is \(\delta\), the leading inertial and viscous scales are \(U^2/x\) and \(\nu U/\delta^2\). Their balance gives \(\delta/x\sim1/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}\), where \(\mathrm{Re}_x=Ux/\nu\).

This scaling predicts how a laminar layer grows. Its numerical factor depends on how the thickness is defined. A common measured edge is the position reaching 99% of the external speed. Two integral measures instead quantify flow displaced by the layer and its lost momentum, and are less sensitive to picking a particular edge point.

\[\delta^*=\int_0^\infty(1-u/U)\,dy,\qquad \theta_m=\int_0^\infty(u/U)(1-u/U)\,dy .\]

The thickness \(\delta^*\) gives the equivalent missing volume flux per unit width divided by \(U\). Momentum thickness \(\theta_m\) weights that deficit by the actual local speed. Fluid right at the wall contributes strongly to displacement thickness but carries almost no streamwise momentum flux.

Relate the momentum deficit to wall shear

A steady control volume surrounding one face of a flat plate at zero incidence receives fluid from the uniform upstream stream. For constant external speed and pressure, the integrated wall force equals the deficit of streamwise momentum flux downstream after accounting for entrained fluid. Per unit plate width this gives \(D'(x)=\rho U^2\theta_m(x)\). Differentiating gives local wall shear \(\tau_w=\rho U^2\,d\theta_m/dx\). A growing deficit implies positive drag on the plate.

Suppose a velocity survey is represented by \(u/U=2\eta-\eta^2\) for \(0\le\eta=y/\delta\le1\), and uniform flow outside. Integrating yields \(\delta^*/\delta=\int_0^1(1-\eta)^2d\eta=1/3\). Since \(\int_0^1(2\eta-\eta^2)d\eta=2/3\) and \(\int_0^1(2\eta-\eta^2)^2d\eta=8/15\), the momentum thickness is \(2\delta/15\).

At the end of a 0.60 m plate of width 0.40 m in air, let \(U=12\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(\delta=5.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) according to the fitted profile. Then \(\theta_m=0.667\,\mathrm{mm}\) and drag on the measured face is \(1.2(12)^2(0.000667)(0.40)=0.0461\,\mathrm N\). The mean shear over that face is \(0.0461/(0.60\times0.40)=0.192\,\mathrm{Pa}\). It need not equal the local shear at the measuring station. An adverse pressure gradient or separated flow changes the momentum balance; surface pressure forces and external acceleration must then be included before interpreting a wake or boundary-layer survey.

Reduce a partial differential equation to one similarity profile

For a laminar zero-pressure-gradient boundary layer along a flat plate, the leading equations are \(u_x+v_y=0\) and \(u u_x+v u_y=\nu u_{yy}\). Assume external speed \(U\) constant, a stationary no-slip surface and a layer thin relative to streamwise distance. Introduce a streamfunction \(\psi\) through \(u=\psi_y,\ v=-\psi_x\) to satisfy continuity identically. The scaling \(\delta\sim\sqrt{\nu x/U}\) suggests \(\eta=y\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\) and \(\psi=\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,f(\eta)\). Differentiation gives \(u=U f'\) and \(v=\tfrac12\sqrt{\nu U/x}(\eta f'-f)\).

When these forms enter the momentum equation, the terms containing \(\eta f'f''\) from longitudinal and transverse advection cancel. Both the remaining inertial and viscous terms carry \(U^2/x\). Dividing through gives the Blasius boundary-value problem

\[f'''+\tfrac12 f f''=0,\qquad f(0)=f'(0)=0,\qquad f'(\infty)=1 .\]

The two wall conditions impose zero normal and tangential velocity. The outer condition matches the uniform stream. The unknown wall slope of the velocity profile, \(f''(0)\), is determined by matching that distant condition; prescribing it independently would overdetermine the third-order equation. Numerical integration gives approximately 0.3321 for this definition of \(\eta\). Conventions that include a factor of two in the similarity coordinate assign a different numerical constant but the same physical wall stress.

Predict a force measurement from the similarity solution

The wall shear is \(\mu U f''(0)\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\), giving \(c_f(x)=\tau_w/(\rho U^2/2)=0.664/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}\). Its integral over a plate of length \(L\) yields mean coefficient \(\bar c_f=1.328/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_L}\). The mean equals twice the local trailing-edge value because the integrable \(x^{-1/2}\) shear is highest near the leading edge. Using a single trailing-edge wall gauge for the whole area would therefore underestimate the force.

Take a smooth plate 0.50 m long and 0.25 m wide in air at \(U=6.0\,\mathrm{m/s},\ \nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s},\ \rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). The trailing-edge Reynolds number is 200,000, and a nominally laminar calculation gives \(c_f(L)=0.00149,\ \bar c_f=0.00297\). The one-face drag is \(0.5(1.20)(6)^2(0.50)(0.25)(0.00297)=0.00802\,\mathrm N\). The trailing-edge wall shear is just 0.0321 Pa, and the 99% thickness estimate \(5L/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_L}\) is 5.59 mm. A support carrying both plate faces would register roughly twice the one-face drag if both flows are comparable, plus fixture and edge drag. A single net force measurement cannot by itself tell whether a discrepancy arose from transition, unequal face conditions or the support geometry.

The similarity solution also predicts how to collapse profiles measured at different stations. At \(x=0.125\) m the same value of \(\eta\) corresponds to half the wall-normal distance it does at 0.50 m, since the distance scales with \(\sqrt{x}\). Meanwhile wall shear doubles and local skin-friction coefficient doubles. A traverse resolving only whole millimetres may describe the outer velocity defect at both positions but badly estimate the near-wall slope at the earlier one. Testing both the profile collapse and the independent integral drag provides a stronger check of the assumed laminar flow than comparing a single velocity at the outer edge.

Measure the momentum transported by turbulent fluctuations

In a smooth plane channel driven by a constant pressure drop, time averaging can hide the dominant transport mechanism. Consider steady statistics and a fully developed mean flow between parallel walls. Let \(y=0\) denote the lower wall and \(h\) the distance to the centreline. Write \(u=U(y)+u'\), \(v=v'\). Impermeability and incompressibility give zero mean transverse velocity. The average of the instantaneous flux of streamwise momentum across a horizontal surface is nevertheless \(\rho\overline{uv}=\rho\overline{u'v'}\). Averaging the product retains the correlation between the two fluctuations.

For constant density and viscosity the mean streamwise balance becomes \(0=-d\bar p/dx+d(\mu\,dU/dy-\rho\overline{u'v'})/dy\). Set \(G=-d\bar p/dx>0\). Integrating from \(y\) to the centreline, where symmetry sets the total shear to zero, gives

\[\tau_{\rm total}(y)=\mu\,\frac{dU}{dy}-\rho\overline{u'v'}=G(h-y),\qquad \tau_w=Gh.\]

The right side is fixed by the driving pressure gradient. At a no-slip wall both fluctuating velocities vanish, so molecular viscosity carries the full shear there. Farther out a parcel moving rapidly downstream can move towards the lower wall (\(u'>0,\ v'<0\)). A slow parcel leaving that wall has the same negative product. Both transfer streamwise momentum towards the wall. With the signs above the turbulent contribution to the shear is positive.

For water with \(\rho=1000\) kg/m³ and \(\mu=0.0010\) Pa s, take \(h=0.020\) m and \(G=100\) Pa/m. The wall stress is 2.00 Pa. At \(y=0.0050\) m the total shear is 1.50 Pa. A measured slope of \(150\) s⁻¹ accounts for 0.150 Pa. The remaining 1.35 Pa implies \(\overline{u'v'}=-1.35\times10^{-3}\) m²/s². The exact force balance gives a quantitative consistency check for velocity measurements or a simulation. Predicting the split from \(G\) alone would require a relation for the velocity correlations.

Compressible nozzles and choking

Fluid mechanics

Relate area change to Mach number and calculate the limiting mass flow through a converging nozzle.

From first principles

Derive the area–velocity relation for an isentropic stream

Consider a steady, quasi-one-dimensional flow through a smooth duct of area \(A\). Assume negligible viscosity, heat exchange and elevation change, and no shocks. Density \(\rho\) and speed \(V\) vary between stations. Mass conservation gives constant \(\rho VA\), so taking logarithmic differentials relates three fractional changes.

\[\frac{d\rho}{\rho}+\frac{dV}{V}+\frac{dA}{A}=0 .\]

Streamwise momentum gives \(dp+\rho V\,dV=0\). For an isentropic change, sound speed satisfies \(a^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\) so the pressure differential can be eliminated in favour of density. With \(M=V/a\) this yields

\[\frac{d\rho}{\rho}=-\frac{V\,dV}{a^2}=-M^2\frac{dV}{V},\]
\[\frac{dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\frac{dV}{V}.\]

Subsonic flow accelerates in a contraction because its density variation is comparatively small. A supersonic stream accelerates in a diverging duct because density falls more rapidly. A smooth transition through \(M=1\) with nonzero velocity gradient requires a stationary area, usually the minimum area of a nozzle. Reservoir state and downstream pressure determine whether that sonic condition is actually reached.

Derivation and applications

Recover subsonic Mach number from stagnation pressure

A forward-facing pressure probe brings a small sample of moving gas nearly to rest. Relating the resulting pressure to speed requires accounting for compressibility. Consider a calorically perfect ideal gas, constant heat-capacity ratio \(\gamma>1\), no heat input or shaft work, and subsonic flow brought reversibly to rest. Neglect the height change. Use \(a^2=\gamma R T\), Mach number \(M=V/a\) and \(c_p=\gamma R/(\gamma-1)\). The steady energy balance gives

\[c_pT_0=c_pT+\tfrac12 V^2,\]
\[\frac{T_0}{T}=1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2.\]

For an isentropic ideal gas, \(p/\rho^\gamma\) is constant. Using the gas law to eliminate density yields a pressure ratio as a power of the temperature ratio. This provides a relation between a stagnation tap and a separate static tap at the same upstream state.

\[\frac{p_0}{p}=\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}=\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}.\]

Solve for the nonnegative speed magnitude. A static temperature measurement is needed to convert dimensionless Mach number into dimensional velocity. Expanding the pressure law for small Mach number recovers the familiar dynamic pressure as its leading term.

\[M=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\frac{p_0}{p}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}-1\right]},\]
\[p_0-p=\tfrac12\rho V^2+O(pM^4).\]

Both pressures in the ratio must be absolute. A supersonic probe can form a shock and its recovered stagnation pressure then differs from the reversible upstream value.

Worked example

Estimate air speed from two pressure readings

For air take \(\gamma=1.40\), \(R=287\) J/(kg K), \(T=290\) K, \(p=90.0\) kPa and \(p_0=106\) kPa. Their ratio is 1.178, giving \(M=\sqrt{5[(106/90)^{2/7}-1]}=0.489\). The sound speed is 341 m/s, so the flow speed is about 167 m/s. The incompressible pressure estimate using static density would be higher because the gas compresses while it decelerates.

Second application

Predict the pressure ratio of a calibration stream

A second air stream has measured speed 102 m/s and static temperature 288 K, yielding \(a=340\) m/s and \(M=0.300\). At static pressure 100 kPa, the stagnation rise is \(100[(1+0.2(0.300)^2)^{3.5}-1]=6.44\) kPa. The leading incompressible expression predicts \(0.5\gamma pM^2=6.30\) kPa. Their 0.14 kPa difference sets a scale for the pressure resolution needed to detect compressibility here.

Incorporate expansion work and mass conservation

Gas accelerates through a nozzle by converting enthalpy into kinetic energy. At sufficiently high speeds, density changes appreciably and a constant-density volume balance fails. For steady one-dimensional adiabatic flow without shaft work or appreciable elevation change, \(h+V^2/2=h_0\). A calorically perfect gas has \(h=c_pT\); its local sound speed is \(a=\sqrt{\gamma RT}\), and Mach number is \(M=V/a\). Evaluate \(a\) at local static temperature, rather than at the upstream reservoir temperature.

Assume the acceleration is reversible, so \(dp=a^2d\rho\) along the isentrope. Differential momentum balance gives \(dp=-\rho VdV\), hence \(d\rho/\rho=-M^2dV/V\). Inserting this in continuity \(d\rho/\rho+dV/V+dA/A=0\) gives the area–velocity relation

\[\frac{dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\frac{dV}{V}.\]

A subsonic stream accelerates through a converging passage. A supersonic stream accelerates through a diverging one. A smooth transition through sonic conditions requires a stationary area, conventionally a minimum-area throat for acceleration from subsonic to supersonic speed. A converging nozzle supplied from rest can first reach Mach one at its smallest area, the exit.

Obtain the throat state from the reservoir

In the upstream reservoir the flow is essentially at rest, so its static pressure and temperature are stagnation values \(p_0,T_0\). Combining \(c_p(T_0-T)=V^2/2\) with \(c_p=\gamma R/(\gamma-1)\) gives \(T/T_0=[1+(\gamma-1)M^2/2]^{-1}\). Apply the ideal-gas isentropic pressure–temperature relation to find the critical pressure and temperature ratios at \(M=1\).

\[T^*/T_0=2/(\gamma+1),\qquad p^*/p_0=[2/(\gamma+1)]^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} .\]

With exit area fixed, lowering back pressure increases mass flow until the exit becomes sonic. Further reduction in back pressure does not change the ideal throat state for fixed reservoir conditions. Expansion downstream can then adjust the emerging jet to its surroundings.

Estimate a choked discharge

An insulated converging air nozzle has throat area \(50\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) and reservoir conditions 300 kPa absolute and 300 K. Use \(R=287\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\) and \(\gamma=1.40\). The critical temperature is \(300(2/2.4)=250\,\mathrm K\), critical pressure \(300(2/2.4)^{3.5}=158.5\,\mathrm{kPa}\), density \(158500/(287\times250)=2.21\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and critical speed \(\sqrt{1.4(287)(250)}=317\,\mathrm{m/s}\).

Atmospheric back pressure of 100 kPa lies below the critical pressure, so the ideal mass flow is \(\rho^*a^*A^*=2.21(317)(50\times10^{-6})=0.0350\,\mathrm{kg/s}\). A back pressure of 210 kPa would leave this converging nozzle unchoked, and the exit state must then be calculated from that back pressure. Real contraction and boundary-layer losses can reduce the flow through a calibrated orifice compared with the ideal nozzle prediction; a measured discharge coefficient accounts for those effects within its tested range.

Predict changes at a choked throat

At fixed reservoir pressure, the critical temperature is proportional to \(T_0\), critical density to \(1/T_0\) and critical speed to \(\sqrt{T_0}\). Their product shows that the choked mass flow scales as \(p_0A^*/\sqrt{T_0}\) for the same gas. Warming the original reservoir from 300 to 360 K at unchanged absolute pressure therefore reduces the ideal mass flow by \(\sqrt{300/360}=0.913\), to about 0.0320 kg/s. Increasing throat area or reservoir pressure has a direct linear effect while the downstream pressure stays low enough to maintain choking.

Derive the admissible jump from conservation laws

Supersonic gas in a duct can decelerate abruptly through a normal shock. The shock is thin compared with the duct dimensions, so treat it as a stationary discontinuity of unchanged area. Take a calorically perfect gas, no heat or shaft work and negligible elevation change. Mass, streamwise momentum and stagnation enthalpy are conserved:

\[\rho_1V_1=\rho_2V_2,\quad p_1+\rho_1V_1^2=p_2+\rho_2V_2^2,\quad c_pT_1+V_1^2/2=c_pT_2+V_2^2/2 .\]

Entropy increases through the shock, so the two end states cannot be joined with the isentropic pressure–temperature relation. Set \(r=\rho_2/\rho_1=V_1/V_2\). Momentum gives \(p_2/p_1=1+\gamma M_1^2(1-1/r)\) and the gas law gives \(T_2/T_1=(p_2/p_1)/r\). Substitution into the energy equation yields two roots, one for unchanged flow and the compression solution

\[r=\frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M_1^2},\qquad M_2^2=\frac{1+(\gamma-1)M_1^2/2}{\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)/2}.\]

For \(M_1>1\) this second state has higher density and lower Mach number. As \(M_1\to1^+\), every state ratio tends to unity. For very large upstream Mach number the compression ratio approaches \((\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)\) in the constant-specific-heat approximation, although real-gas changes eventually limit that approximation.

Calculate a diffuser’s stagnation-pressure loss

Air upstream of a normal shock has \(M_1=2.0,\ p_1=80\,\mathrm{kPa}\) absolute and \(T_1=250\,\mathrm K\). Use \(\gamma=1.4,\ R=287\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\). The density ratio is \(2.4(4)/(2+0.4(4))=2.667\), and \(p_2/p_1=1+1.4(4)(1-1/2.667)=4.50\). Hence \(p_2=360\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and \(T_2=250(4.50/2.667)=421.9\,\mathrm K\). The downstream Mach number is \(\sqrt{1.8/(5.6-0.2)}=0.577\). Upstream speed is approximately 634 m/s and mass conservation gives about 238 m/s downstream.

The stagnation temperature upstream is \(250[1+0.2(4)]=450\,\mathrm K\); downstream it is \(421.9[1+0.2(0.577)^2]=450\,\mathrm K\) as required by energy conservation. Evaluate stagnation pressure separately from each local state by a hypothetical isentropic deceleration on its own side. Upstream \(p_{01}=80(1.8)^{3.5}=626\,\mathrm{kPa}\), whereas downstream \(p_{02}=360(1.0667)^{3.5}=451\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Recovery \(p_{02}/p_{01}=0.721\) shows that a large static pressure rise can accompany a 28% loss of stagnation pressure. For the same stagnation temperature the entropy rise is \(-R\ln(0.721)=94\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\), a positive value consistent with the irreversible deceleration.

A pitot tube facing the upstream supersonic flow forms its own shock ahead of the tip. The final stagnation pressure measured there corresponds ideally to isentropic deceleration after the probe’s shock, about 451 kPa here, rather than to the upstream isentropic stagnation pressure of 626 kPa. A conventional subsonic pitot reduction applied to the reading would therefore infer an incorrect upstream speed. By contrast, a static pressure tap placed sufficiently far upstream can still report the 80 kPa static state. The ratio \(451/80=5.64\) illustrates why the probe configuration must be stated when comparing a pressure ratio with a compressible-flow prediction.

The downstream temperature calculation can also be checked using mass flux. The upstream density is \(80000/[287(250)]=1.12\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and the measured downstream static values imply \(360000/[287(421.9)]=2.97\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Multiplication by the corresponding velocities gives roughly \(707\,\mathrm{kg/(m^2s)}\) on both sides. A calculation conserving stagnation pressure instead would generally fail one of these basic balances even if its pressure trend looked plausible.

The shock also changes the area needed to choke a downstream passage. For a given gas the choked mass flow scales as \(A^*p_0/\sqrt{T_0}\). Stagnation temperature is unchanged but stagnation pressure drops to 72.1% of its upstream value. Passing the same mass flow therefore requires a downstream critical area larger by \(1/0.721=1.39\). This does not mean the physical duct jumps in area at the normal shock. The critical area is the sonic reference area for an isentropic stream tube drawn from each side, and the two hypothetical reference conditions differ because entropy has increased.

A converging test section downstream that was sized from the upstream stagnation pressure may consequently choke earlier than expected. The entropy and critical-area tests point to the same lost capacity from different directions: one uses the gas state, the other its maximum deliverable mass flux. Together with conservation of actual mass flux at the shock, they make it possible to distinguish a real shock loss from a simple mismatch between static and total pressure instrumentation.

Time-dependent diffusion

Numerical partial differential equations

Derive the diffusion time-step restriction from two independent arguments and compare explicit and implicit decay of spatial modes.

From first principles

Derive the explicit heat step and its grid-dependent time scale

Take the heat equation \(T_t=\alpha T_{xx}\) with constant positive diffusivity on a uniform one-dimensional grid. Fourier conduction through each side of a cell produces a temperature update proportional to the difference of the two neighbour gradients. With forward Euler timing, collect those fluxes to obtain

\[T_i^{n+1}=T_i^n+r(T_{i-1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i+1}^n),\]
\[r=\alpha\Delta t/h^2 .\]

When \(0\le r\le1/2\) the new temperature is a convex average of three old ones, preserving bounds on a source-free grid. To check whether a higher step could still be stable for all periodic grid disturbances, substitute \(T_j^n=g^n e^{ij\theta}\), where \(j\) is the node index. The amplification simplifies to

\[g=1+r(e^{-i\theta}-2+e^{i\theta})=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2).\]

The alternating mode at \(\theta=\pi\) gives \(g=1-4r\), so \(|g|\le1\) again requires \(r\le1/2\). Halving spatial spacing therefore quarters the largest explicit time step for this discretization. Implicit stepping changes this restriction but still has to resolve any transient relevant to the measurement.

Derivation and applications

Can an energy-stable heat step create an alternating transient?

A diffusion mode should decay rather than oscillate. To distinguish decay of a norm from preservation of sign, examine the finite-element or finite-volume semidiscrete system \(M\dot u+Ku=0\), with symmetric positive definite mass matrix \(M\) and thermal stiffness \(K\) positive semidefinite. An eigenvector satisfying \(Kv=\lambda Mv\) has scalar amplitude \(y(t)\) obeying \(y'=-\lambda y\). A generalized \(\theta\) step applied to this mode gives

\[[1+\theta z]y^{n+1}=[1-(1-\theta)z]y^n,\]
\[z=\Delta t\,\lambda .\]

For Crank–Nicolson take \(\theta=1/2\). The factor \(G=(1-z/2)/(1+z/2)\) has \(|G|\le1\) for all \(z\ge0\) but changes sign when \(z>2\). An initial grid-scale component can therefore alternate in time. To test a remedy, replace the first full step by two half backward-Euler steps. Their combined factor is

\[G_{\rm start}=(1+z/2)^{-2}.\]

Thereafter return to the second-order Crank–Nicolson update. After \(n\) further full steps the mode amplitudes, with and without the initial damping, are

\[y_{\rm damped}=G_{\rm start}G^ny^0,\]
\[y_{\rm plain}=G^{n+1}y^0 .\]

For small \(z\), both startup strategies agree through first order, while for large \(z\) the two half steps strongly suppress the unresolved mode. This argument explains a startup treatment for nonsmooth initial data; sign preservation of every physical grid value is a stronger matrix property than sign preservation of one mode.

Worked example

Check damping of a near-grid-scale eigenmode

Take \(\lambda=2000\) s⁻¹ and \(\Delta t=0.0100\) s. Then \(z=20\). A single Crank–Nicolson step leaves \(-9/11=-0.818\) of the amplitude; two backward-Euler half steps leave \(1/121=0.00826\). The exact amplitude factor is \(e^{-20}=2.06\times10^{-9}\). Both discrete results are bounded, but the startup treatment removes almost all of a mode that the time grid cannot resolve.

Second application

Check the cost for a physically slow component

For the same mesh and step, a slow mode with \(\lambda=2.00\) s⁻¹ has \(z=0.0200\). Crank–Nicolson gives \(0.99/1.01=0.980198\), the startup gives \(1/1.01^2=0.980296\) and the exact factor is \(0.980199\). The slow component changes by less than \(10^{-4}\) in this comparison. A timestep must still resolve a transient that is itself part of the desired output.

Discretise space and time separately

A heat pulse in a thin insulated rod smooths over time according to \(u_t=D u_{xx}\) for constant positive diffusivity \(D\). Use a uniform grid of spacing \(h\), and initially examine interior points or periodic boundaries so the stencil is unchanged everywhere. Replacing the second derivative by a centred difference gives a system of ordinary differential equations for the nodal temperatures. The time method still has to resolve the fastest rate that this spatial operator can generate.

An explicit Euler step yields \(u_i^{n+1}=r u_{i-1}^n+(1-2r)u_i^n+r u_{i+1}^n\) with \(r=D\Delta t/h^2\). If \(0\leq r\leq1/2\), the update is a convex combination of three temperatures. The maximum cannot rise and the minimum cannot fall in a source-free interior step. Starting with one hot node at 1 and neighbours at zero, \(r=0.4\) gives a centre value 0.2 and neighbours 0.4 after a single step. At \(r=0.6\) the centre would become negative although the continuum heat equation preserves nonnegativity.

Identify the spatial modes that set the limit

Insert \(u_j^n=g^n e^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}\), where \(\mathrm{i}\) is the imaginary unit and \(j\) indexes the grid. The two neighbouring values contribute factors \(e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\theta}\), yielding \(g=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Long waves have \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\simeq\theta^2/4\), so their decay approaches the continuum rate as the mesh is refined. The alternating grid mode \(\theta=\pi\) has the most negative amplification and enforces \(r\leq1/2\) for bounded magnitude.

At the limiting value \(r=1/2\) the alternating mode flips sign without losing amplitude. A strict margin damps it. For \(D=10^{-4}\) m²/s and \(h=2\) mm, the maximum explicit step is \(h^2/(2D)=0.020\) s. Refining to 1 mm reduces that limit to 0.005 s. The mesh gives a sharper temperature profile yet can cost four times more steps over the same physical interval, besides the increased number of spatial degrees of freedom.

Compare backward Euler and Crank–Nicolson

Writing the semidiscrete system as \(u'=-A_hu\), backward Euler solves \((I+\Delta t A_h)u^{n+1}=u^n\). Crank–Nicolson averages the old and new diffusive fluxes, giving \((I+\Delta t A_h/2)u^{n+1}=(I-\Delta t A_h/2)u^n\). For a Fourier mode put \(s=4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Their factors are \(1/(1+s)\) and \((1-s/2)/(1+s/2)\), respectively. Both remain bounded at arbitrarily large \(s\), but Crank–Nicolson may alternate for under-resolved sharp features.

With \(s=20\), backward Euler retains only \(1/21\) per step. Crank–Nicolson yields \(-9/11\), retaining much more of the fast component and reversing its sign. For a smooth low-frequency mode Crank–Nicolson generally gives more accurate temporal decay because it is second order. Decide whether early sharp transients or later smooth behaviour is the relevant physical quantity. To identify spatial and temporal error separately, refine \(\Delta t\) first on a fixed mesh, then refine space with time error kept well below spatial error. Merely halving both at once cannot reveal which term dominated the original discrepancy.

Apply a prescribed heat input without confusing it with temperature

An insulated periodic rod with uniform heating rate \(s\) measured in K/s has an average temperature that must rise by \(s\Delta t\) each step. Sum the centred second differences over the periodic indices: each nodal value appears with coefficients \(1,-2,1\), which cancel. The source alone changes the mean. For \(s=3\) K/s and a step of 0.02 s the numerical average must increase by 0.06 K, independently of the diffusivity and initial profile.

A code can pass this global test while still mishandling short-wave damping, since an alternating error has zero spatial mean. Pair a mean-temperature test with a nonconstant mode-decay test. Use a homogeneous periodic run for isolates of diffusion, and a uniform temperature with prescribed source for the storage term. A failure in one test then identifies a different part of the mathematical balance than a failure in the other.

Prove decay of a discrete thermal energy

Let a closed semidiscrete diffusion model be \(M\dot u+Ku=0\), with \(M\) symmetric positive definite and \(K\) symmetric positive semidefinite. Assume \(K{\bf1}=0\), expressing zero flux for a uniform temperature. Backward Euler satisfies \(M(u^{n+1}-u^n)+\Delta t Ku^{n+1}=0\). Multiplying by the constant vector and using symmetry gives \({\bf1}^TMu^{n+1}={\bf1}^TMu^n\). Thus the capacity-weighted sum, proportional to stored sensible heat for constant capacities and a fixed reference, is conserved for any step size.

Now multiply by \(u^{n+1}\). The identity \(a^TM(a-b)=[\|a\|_M^2-\|b\|_M^2+\|a-b\|_M^2]/2\) yields the exact balance

\[\tfrac12\|u^n\|_M^2-\tfrac12\|u^{n+1}\|_M^2=\Delta t\,(u^{n+1})^TKu^{n+1}+\tfrac12\|u^{n+1}-u^n\|_M^2 .\]

Both terms are nonnegative. The quadratic quantity therefore decays although total sensible heat is conserved. Subtracting the conserved mean first yields the same decay statement for temperature variation around equilibrium. The first term represents spatial smoothing measured at the new time, and the second reflects the numerical time update. This derivation gives a stability certificate on nonuniform grids without assuming Fourier modes.

Audit two cells with different heat capacities

Choose capacities 1 and 3 J/K, conductance 2 W/K, and initial temperature deviations \((4,0)^T\) K. The conserved sensible heat is 4 J, so the final equilibrium in a closed system must be one kelvin above reference. At \(\Delta t=0.5\) s the matrix \(M+\Delta tK\) has rows \((2,-1)\) and \((-1,4)\), and the right-hand side is \((4,0)^T\). The new temperatures are \((16/7,4/7)^T\). Checking their weighted sum gives \(16/7+3(4/7)=4\), as required. The unweighted average changes, which is physically appropriate for unequal capacities.

The quadratic quantity at the start is \(4^2/2=8\). After the step it is \([(16/7)^2+3(4/7)^2]/2=152/49\). Its drop is \(240/49\). The conductive contribution is \((0.5)(2)(12/7)^2=144/49\); the time-update contribution is \([(-12/7)^2+3(4/7)^2]/2=96/49\). Their sum recovers the measured drop exactly. Neither contribution equals a loss of the 4 J of sensible heat. The quadratic measure depends on squared temperatures and has different physical units.

A positive heat supply or a prescribed exterior temperature introduces a boundary or source work term into these identities. Applying the closed-system decay test to an externally heated experiment would therefore diagnose the wrong physical model. Verify which conserved or dissipated quantity is actually implied by the capacity matrix, boundary conditions and source terms before comparing numerical integrators.

The conserved mean suggests another check after many large steps. The steady state must have zero conductance dissipation, which for two connected cells requires equal temperatures. Together with the weighted heat total this fixes both temperatures at one kelvin above reference. A scheme that instead approaches the unweighted initial average of two would be converging to the wrong conserved quantity despite producing a visually smooth, uniform endpoint. The measured quadratic variation about the correct mean tends to zero; the uncentred squared quantity tends to a positive constant because the remaining uniform state still has temperature above the chosen reference.

Interfaces, contact resistance and Robin data

Numerical partial differential equations

Derive a face flux that transports the same heat through two different materials and through a convective boundary.

From first principles

Derive an interface flux from two half-cell temperature drops

Two neighbouring cell centres can lie in materials with sharply different conductivities. Consider a planar interface between them, normal to a one-dimensional steady heat flow. Let \(d_L,d_R\) be the distances from the centres to the interface, and suppose there is no interface source, storage or contact resistance. Steady conservation requires one heat flux \(q\), positive to the right, and perfect contact requires one interface temperature \(T_I\). Applying Fourier’s law to each half gives

\[q=k_L(T_L-T_I)/d_L=k_R(T_I-T_R)/d_R .\]

Solve each expression for its temperature drop, add the two drops and eliminate \(T_I\). The conductance follows from the actual material distances:

\[q=\frac{T_L-T_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R},\]
\[k_{\rm eff}=\frac{d_L+d_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R}.\]

For equal distances this is the harmonic mean; only equal conductivities recover the same result as the arithmetic mean. If one material approaches zero conductivity with positive thickness, the flux approaches zero. A finite thermal contact resistance adds another temperature drop \(qR_c\), while a heat-generating interface alters the flux-continuity condition itself.

Derivation and applications

Enforce the flux jump at a heat-generating interface

Two materials in series carry the same conductive current only if their common interface stores and generates no energy. Consider two homogeneous slabs with an idealized thin heater between them. Let \(T_L,T_R\) be prescribed outer surface temperatures, \(R_L,R_R\) the positive thermal resistances per unit area in m² K/W, and \(s\) the heater release in W/m². Assume perfect thermal contact, steady conduction and negligible thickness of the heater. Define the interface temperature as \(T_I\) and flux \(q\) positive to the right.

Fourier’s law integrated through each slab relates its own constant flux to its temperature drop. The pillbox balance across the heater requires the right flux to exceed the left one by the generated heat:

\[q_L=(T_L-T_I)/R_L,\]
\[q_R=(T_I-T_R)/R_R,\]
\[q_R-q_L=s .\]

Substitute the two face laws into the balance. With \(g_j=1/R_j\) as the two conductances per area, the interface temperature and individual fluxes are

\[T_I=\frac{g_LT_L+g_RT_R+s}{g_L+g_R},\]
\[q_R=g_R(T_I-T_R),\]
\[q_L=g_L(T_L-T_I).\]

Alternatively eliminate \(T_I\) to express the fluxes as through-flow plus a split source. The resistances determine the fraction of added heat directed to each boundary:

\[q_L=\frac{T_L-T_R-sR_R}{R_L+R_R},\]
\[q_R=\frac{T_L-T_R+sR_L}{R_L+R_R}.\]

Setting \(s=0\) restores a single common flux. Equal outside temperatures send more generated heat toward the lower resistance. For an interface at a mesh face the two cells therefore need distinct physical face fluxes linked by the stated jump; assigning exactly the same flux while adding no source anywhere violates energy balance.

Worked example

Determine both heat flows through an instrumented wall

Let \(T_L=320\) K, \(T_R=300\) K, \(R_L=0.100\), \(R_R=0.200\) m² K/W and \(s=150\) W/m². The interface sees conductances \(10.0\) and \(5.00\) W/(m² K) and reaches \(323.33\) K. The left flux is \(-33.3\) W/m² and the right flux \(116.7\) W/m². Heat leaves on both sides, including toward the hotter external surface. Their oriented difference equals \(150\) W/m².

Second application

Select a heater setting that makes one sensor read zero

Keeping the same temperatures and layers, impose \(q_L=0\). Then \(T_I=T_L=320\) K and \(s=(T_L-T_R)/R_R=100\) W/m². All generated heat exits on the right. Reducing \(s\) to zero instead gives \(66.7\) W/m² from left to right through both layers. A zero left-side flux in the heated experiment therefore does not imply an insulating left layer.

One flux through resistances in series

Let \(q=-kT'\) be heat flux in the positive coordinate direction, with unit cross-sectional area and no internal source. Between two cell centres separated by an interface, let the left and right centre-to-interface distances be \(d_L,d_R\). Piecewise constant conductivities \(k_L,k_R>0\) make the equilibrium temperature linear in each segment. If the interface has perfect contact, its temperature \(T_f\) is continuous. Integrating Fourier's law on each side gives \(T_L-T_f=q\,d_L/k_L\) and \(T_f-T_R=q\,d_R/k_R\). Adding eliminates the unknown interface temperature.

\[q=\frac{T_L-T_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R}.\]

For equal distances this amounts to using the harmonic mean conductivity over the full centre spacing. Inserting the same signed \(q\) as the outflow of the left cell and the inflow of the right cell produces cancellation when their balances are added. If a material interface stores energy or supplies heat independently, a flux jump belongs in the balance and this steady series calculation must be modified.

Measure the temperature drop

Take \(d_L=d_R=0.01\) m, \(k_L=2,\ k_R=10\) W/(m K) and \(T_L=30,\ T_R=18\) °C. The series resistance per unit area is \(0.005+0.001=0.006\) m²K/W. The flux is \(2000\) W/m² and the interface temperature is \(30-2000(0.005)=20\) °C. The lower conductivity carries the larger temperature drop despite carrying the same flux. Using the arithmetic mean 6 with the centre difference over 0.02 m instead predicts \(3600\) W/m².

Introduce a contact resistance \(R_c=0.002\) m²K/W. Its temperature jump is \(qR_c\); the total resistance rises to 0.008, reducing the heat flux to 1500 W/m². The two sides of the contact now have temperatures 22.5 and 19.5 °C. Imposing a single interface temperature on this model would remove the physically measured 3 K jump.

Apply a convective boundary

At an exterior face the outward heat flux obeys \(q=h_c(T_s-T_\infty)\), a Robin condition. Eliminate the unknown surface temperature as above to obtain \(q=(T_P-T_\infty)/(d/k+1/h_c)\). If \(h_c\) tends to zero, the boundary becomes insulating. With very large \(h_c\), the surface approaches ambient temperature while the finite centre-to-face resistance remains. For \(d=0.01\) m, \(k=2\), \(h_c=100\) W/(m² K), \(T_P-T_\infty=15\) K, the predicted flux is 1000 W/m² and the surface is 10 K above ambient. Multiplying by face area converts this flux to the power used in a cell energy balance. Mixing up these two quantities makes the result depend incorrectly on mesh area.

A jump located inside a cell

If the materials change at a known position inside a segment, the fractions of that segment matter. Moving the interface so that 75 percent of the length has conductivity 2 and 25 percent has conductivity 10 produces effective conductivity \(1/(0.75/2+0.25/10)=2.5\) W/(m K). Giving each material half the segment instead would give \(10/3\) W/(m K). The harmonic rule depends on actual layer thicknesses. A mesh-refinement study that snaps the interface to different positions at every refinement can mix geometric representation error with flux-discretisation error.

Derive effective transport parallel and normal to layers

A fine laminate of isotropic materials can have a direction-dependent macroscopic conductivity. Consider two perfectly bonded planar layers occupying thickness fractions \(\theta\) and \(1-\theta\), with conductivities \(k_1,k_2\). Assume steady conduction, no internal sources, and boundary conditions that permit a one-dimensional field for the loading orientation being tested. For transport normal to the layers, continuity forces equal flux. Adding their temperature drops across a total thickness \(L\) gives \(k_\perp=[\theta/k_1+(1-\theta)/k_2]^{-1}\). For transport parallel to the layers the imposed longitudinal gradient is common and the area-weighted fluxes add, giving \(k_\parallel=\theta k_1+(1-\theta)k_2\).

The arithmetic and harmonic expressions are thus both correct, but apply to different geometries of imposed transport. Their ordering follows without a conductivity table: multiplying the two positive weighted sums \(\theta k_1+(1-\theta)k_2\) and \(\theta/k_1+(1-\theta)/k_2\) gives \(1+\theta(1-\theta)(k_1-k_2)^2/(k_1k_2)\ge1\). Equality requires identical conductivities or a missing layer. Spatial averaging performed before identifying the flux direction would erase precisely the distinction that determines the coarse physics.

Predict two experiments on the same material sample

Suppose a repeated laminate consists of 40 percent material with conductivity 1 and 60 percent with conductivity 10 W/(m K). Then \(k_\parallel=0.4+6=6.4\) W/(m K) and \(k_\perp=1/(0.4+0.06)=50/23\approx2.174\) W/(m K). A temperature drop of 4 K over 0.02 m imposes gradient magnitude 200 K/m. Perfectly normal loading produces flux magnitude about 434.8 W/m², whereas the same gradient parallel to the layers yields 1280 W/m². The larger flux is consistent with the fast material providing a continuous path in the latter orientation.

For the normal experiment the low-conductivity phase occupies 0.008 m and accounts for a drop \(434.8(0.008)/1\approx3.478\) K. The high-conductivity phase occupies 0.012 m and drops only \(434.8(0.012)/10\approx0.522\) K. Adding recovers the imposed four kelvin. For the parallel experiment individual flux densities are 200 and 2000 W/m². Averaging by their area fractions gives \(0.4(200)+0.6(2000)=1280\) W/m². These local checks use the same two material properties and explain the difference in the observed coarse responses.

Using the effective tensor assumes the observation scale spans representative layers. A sensor within one layer may respond to its local temperature slope instead. Finite contact resistance adds a temperature jump in the normal direction; if the number of interfaces per macroscopic length changes, the effective normal resistance changes even at identical material fractions. Measuring both orientations can distinguish geometric or contact effects that a single scalar fitted conductivity would conceal.

Rotating a laminate specimen through ninety degrees interchanges the roles of these two effective values without changing its volume fractions. This makes an orientation experiment particularly discriminating: a fit that happened to reproduce the first orientation by adjusting a scalar conductivity predicts the same flux for the rotated specimen, whereas the fine-layer balance predicts a factor of about \(6.4/(50/23)=2.94\) between them.

Repeating the test over multiple layer spacings also probes the assumption of negligible contact resistance.

What a periodic grid does to a decaying Fourier mode

Numerical partial differential equations

A periodic diffusion solver predicts the shape of a sampled wave while its amplitude decays too slowly. Fourier analysis separates spatial differentiation error, an unresolved physical mode and errors caused by the time integrator.

Diagonalise the differential operator on the periodic interval

Use a dimensionless coordinate \(0\le x<2\pi\) and solve \(u_t=\kappa u_{xx}\) with periodic values and periodic derivatives. The complex exponentials \(e^{imx}\), with integer m, satisfy both conditions and are eigenfunctions of the second derivative. Put \(u(x,t)=A_m(t)e^{imx}\) and cancel the common exponential. The scalar amplitude equation is \(A'_m=-\kappa m^2 A_m\). Thus every cosine or sine mode decays by \(\exp(-\kappa m^2t)\), with no phase shift, while the m=0 mean is conserved.

On a uniform grid \(x_j=2\pi j/N\), the samples of \(m\) and \(m+\ell N\) agree for every integer \(\ell\). A discrete Fourier transform returns coefficients in one representative band. For the real cosine test with even N we can fold a positive physical mode into \(k=\min(r,N-r)\), \(r=m\bmod N\). A Fourier spectral Laplacian multiplies that coefficient by \(-k^2\). For a resolved mode it differentiates the trigonometric interpolant exactly, up to arithmetic. For unresolved data it differentiates the aliased representative; no operation using only those samples can identify the original m.

Apply the centred difference stencil to a single wave

The standard second difference is \((u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1})/h^2\), \(h=2\pi/N\). Put the grid wave \(u_j=e^{ikjh}\) into the stencil. Factor out the common phase and simplify with \(e^{i\xi}+e^{-i\xi}=2\cos\xi\). This gives the symbol and its small-h expansion:

\[{e^{ikh}-2+e^{-ikh}\over h^2}=-{4\over h^2}\sin^2(kh/2)=-k^2+{k^4h^2\over12}+O(k^6h^4).\]

The effective squared wavenumber is smaller than \(k^2\) for every nonconstant mode in the resolved band, so this spatial approximation decays too slowly if the time equation is integrated exactly. The error is modest when \(kh\ll1\), but at the Nyquist mode \(kh=\pi\) the ratio is \(4/\pi^2=0.4053\). A stable time integrator cannot repair this spatial attenuation error without additional modeling. The experiment exponentiates both discretizations' symbols exactly, isolating space and sampling here.

Measure separate errors with the same sampled initial data

Choose \(\kappa=0.5,\ m=3,\ N=16\). The true squared wavenumber is 9 and the difference stencil gives 8.006. Starting at unit amplitude, at \(t=0.20\) exact and spectral amplitudes are 0.40657; finite differences give 0.44906. Doubling N to 32 changes the difference result to 0.41716. The absolute amplitude error falls from 0.04249 to 0.01059, a ratio of 4.01 consistent with the second-order expansion. This is a spatial-convergence study because time evolution introduces no truncation error.

Now take \(m=12,\ N=16\). Every sampled cosine equals the low mode \(k=4\) at \(t=0\). At \(t=0.02\), the continuum amplitude for the true physical mode is \(e^{-1.44}=0.237\), whereas the spectral grid predicts \(e^{-0.16}=0.852\). A spectral computation can therefore agree exactly with its trigonometric interpolant and disagree strongly with the evolution of the intended signal. Sampling on a finer grid or obtaining prior bandwidth information resolves that ambiguity. The mean mode is particularly revealing when a nonconstant physical cosine aliases to a constant.

Start at mode 3 and successively double N from 8 to 32. Compute two amplitude-error ratios at the same time. Then use mode 12 on 16 points to observe an alias, and increase N until the Fourier grid represents the intended mode.

Measuring magnetic core loss by a hysteresis loop and by calorimetry

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A closed loop on a graph of magnetic induction against field encloses an area with units of energy per unit volume. A thermometer measures kelvins. Bring these two observations onto the same scale, account for the winding heat, and determine when the two experiments actually test the same material loss.

Infer the field and the induction from independent windings

Wind a toroidal specimen with a primary winding of \(N_p\) turns and a lightly loaded pickup of \(N_s\) turns. Let \(A\) be the representative iron cross-sectional area, \(\ell\) the mean magnetic path length and \(V_c=A\ell\) the magnetic volume. Neglect a gap and leakage initially. Ampère's law around a path through the core gives \(H\simeq N_pi_p/\ell\). This relation is accurate when the toroid is slender enough that the spread of path radii is small and the pickup current is negligible. In a thick toroid \(H(r)=N_pi_p/(2\pi r)\), so one point on a nominal loop can actually represent a range of local field values.

Faraday's law supplies the second observable. Choose winding polarity so the reported voltage is \(v_s=N_sA\,dB/dt\). Integrating the voltage gives the change in cross-section-averaged magnetic induction. The pickup cannot determine a constant induction offset, because static flux induces no voltage. A closed loop area is insensitive to adding a constant to \(B\) or \(H\), but a dc offset in the voltage integrates into a time-dependent ramp and prevents the reconstructed trajectory from closing. In steady periodic operation one can subtract the voltage mean over a complete period before integration. Applying that correction to a genuinely evolving magnetisation process would remove part of a real flux change.

Multiply the two measured field quantities in the appropriate order. The field work supplied per unit material volume on a small induction change is \(H\,dB\). The source term associated with the magnetising flux is \(i_p v_{p,{\rm induced}}\,dt\). Using \(v_{p,{\rm induced}}\simeq(N_p/N_s)v_s\), its value is \(i_pN_pA\,dB=V_cH\,dB\). Integrating around a settled cycle gives the magnetic loss

\[W_c=V_c\oint H\,dB,\]
\[P_c=fV_c\oint H\,dB.\]

The field can absorb positive work and return some of it later in the same cycle. The area measures the algebraic remainder. Reversing the polarity of a single signal flips the loop orientation. A passively heated specimen must receive positive net energy when signs are defined for power entering the core. Simply taking the absolute value of all instantaneous products would count reversible storage twice and can grossly overestimate the heat.

Work an elliptical loop from time-domain signals

Suppose a nearly sinusoidal field is \(H=H_p\cos\omega t\) and the induction lags it by a positive angle \(\delta\), \(B=B_p\cos(\omega t-\delta)\). Differentiate the latter and multiply by \(H\). Over one cycle \(\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\) averages to zero, and \(\cos^2\omega t\) averages to one half. The resulting mean power density is \(\omega H_pB_p\sin\delta/2\), giving \(\oint H\,dB=\pi H_pB_p\sin\delta\). With zero lag the two signals trace a line and the area vanishes, even if the peak values are large. A saturation-distorted loop need not be elliptical; the original time integral remains valid with synchronised data.

Take \(H_p=120\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_p=0.200\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta=12^\circ\). The energy per cycle and volume is \(15.68\,{\rm J/m^3}\). A toroid with \(\ell=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(A=2.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\) and \(V_c=40\,{\rm cm^3}\) dissipates \(0.627\,{\rm mJ}\) per cycle. At \(f=200\,{\rm Hz}\) its power is \(0.125\,{\rm W}\). With \(N_p=100\), the necessary current peak is \(0.240\,{\rm A}\). With \(N_s=50\), the pickup voltage has peak \(N_sA(2\pi f)B_p=2.51\,{\rm V}\). These last two predictions allow the raw oscilloscope traces to be checked before a calculated loss in watts is trusted.

For sampled periodic data use \(w=\sum_n(H_n+H_{n+1})(B_{n+1}-B_n)/2\), including the segment from the last point back to the first. This trapezoidal line integral computes an oriented polygon area. Plot the starting and ending points before adding the closing segment. Connecting widely separated endpoints by hand hides drift and can generate a fictitious wedge whose area depends on acquisition timing. Repeat at higher sampling rates to see whether corners or switching features carry appreciable extra area. When a periodic excitation has harmonics the time increment must resolve the highest significant harmonic in both measured signals.

Compare a thermal measurement with the magnetic area

Consider first a calorimeter that receives heat only from the core. Let the heat capacity of specimen, holder and sensor be \(C_{\rm eff}\). Let the leakage conductance to an ambient bath be \(G\), and let \(\Delta T=T-T_a\). The thermal balance \(C_{\rm eff}\dot{\Delta T}=P_c-G\Delta T\) predicts a steady offset \(P_c/G\) and an initial heating slope \(P_c/C_{\rm eff}\). A small thermometer lag or poor thermal contact can make the earliest measured slope misleading even though environmental losses are then small. A separate electrical heater placed where the core deposits its heat can calibrate the effective thermal response.

For \(C_{\rm eff}=20\,{\rm J/K}\), \(G=0.015\,{\rm W/K}\), and a \(2.0\,{\rm K}\) rise above ambient, the magnetic loss \(0.125\,{\rm W}\) would give a slope of about \(0.00477\,{\rm K/s}\). The heat already leaking to the bath is \(0.030\,{\rm W}\), too large to discard in a ten-percent comparison. If the winding is inside the same calorimeter, its Ohmic heat must enter the balance as well. With primary resistance \(1.50\,\Omega\) and the computed sinusoidal current, winding heating is \((0.240/\sqrt2)^2(1.50)=0.0432\,{\rm W}\). The expected slope of the combined package at the same temperature is now \(0.00693\,{\rm K/s}\). Subtraction of winding heat recovers the core result only when the estimated resistance represents the winding at its operating temperature and frequency.

A thermal estimate drawn from a whole interval is often more robust than a derivative from two adjacent temperature samples. Integrating gives \(Q_{\rm deposited}=C_{\rm eff}[\Delta T(t_2)-\Delta T(t_1)]+G\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Delta T\,dt\). Divide by the duration for mean power, then remove other measured heat sources. A drifting ambient must be recorded explicitly because the calorimeter stores energy relative to its own absolute temperature. If the core and coil equilibrate slowly with each other, a single fitted heat capacity can depend on the fitting time window. A two-temperature model or a longer dwell then supplies an independent diagnostic.

Separate reversible exchange from hysteretic and induced currents

A single measured loop at finite frequency gives the total energy dissipated by the core at that prescribed waveform and temperature. The loop can include irreversible domain processes, eddy currents and other rate-dependent effects. It does not by itself split these contributions. To probe their different scaling hold the peak induction and waveform shape fixed, then change frequency. Under a restricted low-frequency model the quasi-static hysteresis energy per cycle is approximately constant, while classical thin-lamination eddy-current power scales as \(f^2B_p^2\). Its energy per cycle therefore scales roughly as \(f\). Plotting the measured loss per cycle and volume against \(f\) tests whether such an intercept-plus-slope description fits the chosen range.

Suppose the area is \(12\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(100\,{\rm Hz}\) and \(20\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(300\,{\rm Hz}\), at the same peak induction. A straight line gives intercept \(8\,{\rm J/m^3}\) and slope \(0.040\,{\rm J\,s/m^3}\). It predicts \(16\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(200\,{\rm Hz}\), near the elliptical-loop calculation. Two points always determine a line. A third and fourth frequency, together with uncertainties, are required to test the law. Different losses can share similar frequency scaling over a short interval, and temperature-dependent conductivity can bend a nominally simple curve.

Use the pickup to reject the reversible part of terminal power

Measuring primary terminal voltage and current with a power analyser provides a separate electrical route to loss, but the wanted number can be a small difference between terms. The applied voltage contains winding resistance, induced magnetising voltage, leakage-inductance voltage and possible parasitic contributions. In steady periodic operation the energy stored in a fixed linear leakage inductance returns after each cycle, while winding resistance dissipates energy permanently. The secondary pickup preferentially measures the core-linked flux. Forming \(p_c(t)=(N_p/N_s)i_p(t)v_s(t)\) and averaging over a full cycle estimates core power without directly including the primary's copper voltage drop. It relies on both windings linking the same relevant flux and on negligible power drawn by the pickup.

For the elliptical example the primary magnetising voltage has peak \(5.03\,{\rm V}\) and leads the induction by ninety degrees. Its phase relative to the current is \(90^\circ-\delta=78^\circ\). The core branch's apparent power is \(V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\approx0.603\,{\rm VA}\); its mean dissipative part is about \(0.125\,{\rm W}\), and the reactive part is about \(0.590\,{\rm var}\). Only the mean part contributes net heating of the magnetic volume. Including the winding voltage adds about \(0.043\,{\rm W}\) of real power to the terminal reading. A simple product of peak current and peak secondary voltage, without the turns ratio, factor one half and phase factor, has no reason to agree with either measured loss.

Consider a nearly ideal core with the same peak signals but a lag of only one degree. Its loss is about \(10.5\,{\rm mW}\), while the much larger reversible power exchange is almost unchanged. A time offset of \(10\,\mu{\rm s}\) at \(200\,{\rm Hz}\) adds \(0.72^\circ\), causing a relative loss error of approximately \(72\%\) for that small lag. Calibrate the relative channel delays using a reference signal through the actual current transducer and voltage chain, including their filters. Adjusting traces on screen until a core loop appears narrow assumes the very loss one is trying to measure. A synchronous measurement over many periods suppresses random noise but does not remove a persistent delay bias.

Test reconstruction on a waveform with a third harmonic

Let \(H(t)=H_1\cos\omega t+H_3\cos3\omega t\) while \(B(t)=B_1\cos(\omega t-\delta_1)+B_3\cos(3\omega t-\delta_3)\). With these definitions \(\delta_n\) is the phase lag of the \(n\)th harmonic in its own sinusoid. Multiplying \(H\) by \(\dot B\) and integrating over an integer number of fundamental periods eliminates products of different harmonics. Each surviving harmonic contributes the number of its own cycles in one fundamental period. The area per fundamental period is therefore \(\pi[H_1B_1\sin\delta_1+3H_3B_3\sin\delta_3]\). This identity tests whether a numerical area calculation tracks the full waveform. It does not assume that a real material is linear; here the two signals are supplied as measured periodic functions.

Use the earlier fundamental values and add \(H_3=20\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_3=0.030\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta_3=30^\circ\). The third harmonic contributes \(2.83\,{\rm J/m^3}\) in each fundamental period. This represents an additional \(0.0226\,{\rm W}\) for the chosen specimen and frequency. A fundamental-only sine fit would miss nearly fifteen percent of the resulting total area. An apparent jagged loop can also arise from asynchronous sampling or an offset. Distinguish genuine harmonics by checking that they are phase locked to the drive and persist under a change in sampling rate and analogue bandwidth.

Sweep phase and read the oriented loop

The live experiment reconstructs the \(H,B\) trajectory and compares it with a zero-lag reference. It uses the same peak field and induction for the two curves. At fixed peaks changing frequency leaves an assigned phase lag and therefore the prescribed energy per cycle unchanged, while watts rise with the number of cycles per second. A real core can change its lag and waveform with frequency; the graph separates this independent variable so that the meaning of the two scaled quantities is clear. Add a timing offset and predict the direction of the area error before checking its reported size. Near zero lag even the direction of an apparent loop can be controlled by a small electronic offset.

A saturated sample shows a small loop during a biased excitation that never crosses zero induction. Does the area formula still apply?

Work through the solution

A settled periodic minor loop encloses an area representing the net cyclic energy under exactly that bias and excursion. Adding a static induction offset does not change \(\oint H\,dB\), but the bias can alter the trajectory, differential permeability, and width of the minor loop itself. Do not infer the full major-loop energy from its area. The pickup returns only changes of induction unless an independent dc measurement or a known magnetic preparation supplies the offset. Thermal radiance, étendue and detector calibration

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A square millimetre of a heated object emits thermal radiation into an entire hemisphere. A detector sees only the rays accepted by its optics and only wavelengths to which it responds. Derive the equilibrium spectrum, follow it through an optical collection system and infer what temperature or emissivity a calibrated reading can actually determine.

Count optical states in a large equilibrium cavity

Take a cubic airless cavity of volume \(V\) in thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\). For its bulk mode density we can use periodic boundary conditions. Allowed wavevectors are separated by \(2\pi/L\) in each coordinate. Count a spherical shell in wavevector space, divide by the volume per state and include two independent transverse polarisations. Substituting \(k=2\pi\nu/c\) gives \(g(\nu)d\nu/V=8\pi\nu^2d\nu/c^3\). Using standing waves in an ideally reflecting large box gives the same leading volume density when directions and polarisations are counted consistently.

One oscillator mode of frequency \(\nu\) can hold \(m=0,1,2,\ldots\) photons. Its thermal occupation has probabilities proportional to \(e^{-mh\nu/k_BT}\). Summing the geometric series and taking its logarithmic derivative gives \(\langle m\rangle=1/(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)\). Multiplying by the density of modes and energy per photon gives spectral energy density \(u_\nu\). The zero-point contribution of a harmonic oscillator is not part of the thermal power exchanged between these black surfaces. At low frequency the result approaches an energy \(k_BT\) per mode; extending that classical limit to arbitrarily high frequency would make the integrated power diverge.

An isotropic distribution of energy travelling at speed \(c\) produces a normal flux through a small aperture weighted by the direction cosine. Per unit solid angle its radiance is \(B_\nu=cu_\nu/(4\pi)\). Transform to equal wavelength intervals using \(B_\lambda d\lambda=B_\nu|d\nu|\). The two useful spectral forms are

\[B_\nu=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac1{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1},\]
\[B_\lambda=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.\]

The wavelength expression has units of watts per square metre per steradian per metre of wavelength. To express it per micrometre multiply by one millionth. At 400 K and 10 micrometres the result is 33.56 W per square metre per steradian per micrometre. At 295 K it is 9.14 in the same units. Equal wavelength and equal frequency bins carry different widths, so their distributions reach their maxima at different converted wavelengths. A spectrum's peak should always name its horizontal variable and spectral-density convention.

Distinguish emitter power from brightness in a chosen direction

For a black surface with angle-independent radiance into its outward hemisphere, integrate \(\cos\theta\,d\Omega\). The result is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\). The emitted spectral power per unit area is \(\pi B_\lambda\). Integrate over frequency using \(x=h\nu/(k_BT)\). The remaining integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) follows by expanding \(1/(e^x-1)\) as a sum of exponentials and integrating term by term. This yields Stefan–Boltzmann exitance \(M=\sigma T^4\) and identifies the constant \(\sigma=2\pi^5k_B^4/(15h^3c^2)\).

A black patch of 1.0 square millimetre at 400 K emits about 1.45 mW into its entire outward hemisphere over all wavelengths. The same patch at 295 K emits about 0.429 mW. Replacing one by the other changes all-wavelength hemispherical power by about 1.02 mW. This is not the power a small infrared receiver sees through a narrow filter. The integrated emitter number is valuable for energy conservation, but by itself contains no receiver area, direction, passband or optical transmission.

The surface spectrum of a body in thermal equilibrium has directional spectral emissivity equal to its absorptivity in the corresponding reciprocal optical modes under the usual reciprocal-material conditions. An opaque diffuse grey surface with emissivity \(\epsilon\) in an isotropically illuminated background can be modelled by \(\epsilon B_\lambda(T)+(1-\epsilon)B_\lambda(T_{\rm bg})\). It emits less than a black surface but also reflects background. A shiny object viewed in a warm room may consequently display a radiance temperature nearer the reflected scene than its own physical temperature. Specular surfaces require explicit directional knowledge of that scene.

Derive the geometric acceptance without counting the angle twice

An area element \(dA\) normal to the collection axis emits into \(d\Omega\) at angle \(\theta\) a spectral power \(B_\lambda\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\). For a uniformly filled cone of half-angle \(\alpha\) and a uniform patch of area \(A\), integrating gives \(A\pi\sin^2\alpha\). This is its geometrical throughput in air when there is no vignetting. Optical étendue more generally uses \(n^2dA\cos\theta\,d\Omega\). A lossless passive optical relay in geometrical optics preserves this phase-space measure; correspondingly \(L/n^2\) is conserved along a ray in transparent media under the usual stationary conditions.

A direct lens example makes the restriction tangible. An image relay enlarging a uniformly bright patch by a factor of two in each transverse direction forms an image four times the area. In a lossless paraxial design filling the original pupil, each image point then occupies one quarter the solid angle. A detector covering the whole enlarged image and accepting that cone can receive the original power. Placing a fixed small sensor at the image plane intercepts less area instead. Higher magnification has not created additional radiance, even if it makes a small detail easier to resolve.

For the same 1.0 square millimetre patch seen within ten degrees, the projected acceptance is \(9.47\times10^{-8}\) square metre steradian. A wavelength-independent combined transmission and detector absorption of 0.65 leaves effective acceptance \(6.16\times10^{-8}\) square metre steradian. Integrating the Planck curve from 8 to 12 micrometres gives source and background band radiances 133.74 and 35.43 W per square metre per steradian. Their difference yields only 6.05 microwatts in the receiver. Keeping a single midpoint radiance times four micrometres happens to be fairly close here, but there is no fixed universal midpoint correction across temperatures or filters.

Convert received power to calibrated voltage or counts

A thermal detector may respond to absorbed power through a temperature rise and an electrical responsivity. A photon-counting detector measures absorbed photons, so its count rate contains a factor \(\lambda/(hc)\) multiplying spectral power before integration. Two receivers with identical geometric acceptance and nominal quantum efficiency can respond differently to a changing source temperature if one output is proportional to energy and the other to photon number. Calibrate the actual electrical transfer and make the time interval explicit for accumulated counts.

For an ideal linear power detector let \(V=V_0+gP\), with offset \(V_0\) and gain \(g\). Two calibrated sources spanning the use range determine both. A black reference port at the same ambient temperature can also reject a common background in a chopped measurement. A filter, window or warm lens that absorbs thermal radiation also emits it. If these optics remain unchanged and stable, part of their own emission cancels in a reference difference. A reference traversing a different path generally leaves a residual input that is not captured by an electronic dark measurement.

Suppose effective source-minus-ambient powers of 2.00 and 8.00 microwatts yield 0.120 and 0.420 V. The fitted slope is 0.0500 V per microwatt with an offset of 0.020 V. A reading of 0.320 V implies 6.00 microwatts after offset correction. Substituting the same reading directly into a single-point gain with zero assumed offset would bias the inferred temperature. Rechecking a third reference near six microwatts tests linearity, while repeated zero-input readings at realistic instrument temperature test offset drift.

Invert the radiance and propagate uncertainties

A narrowband blackbody radiance measurement can be inverted analytically by solving Planck's expression for \(T\). In a broad passband use numerical quadrature across the calibrated spectral response and solve a scalar monotone equation when emissivity and background are known. The derivative at a single wavelength is particularly informative for estimating precision. With \(x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)\),

\[\frac{\partial\log B_\lambda}{\partial\log T}=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}},\]
\[T=\frac{hc/(\lambda k_B)}{\log[1+2hc^2/(\lambda^5B_\lambda)]}.\]

At 10 micrometres and 400 K the logarithmic sensitivity is 3.70. A one-percent relative radiance uncertainty therefore gives about 1.08 K temperature uncertainty if all other quantities are exact. An unknown five-percent emissivity error can dominate that estimate. A two-colour temperature ratio may reduce uncertainty from an emissivity that is constant across the two narrow bands, but cannot cancel an arbitrary spectral emissivity or a large reflected ambient component.

As an example consider a grey object at 400 K and ambient 295 K with emissivity 0.70. At ten micrometres its outgoing radiance is \(0.70(33.56)+0.30(9.14)=26.24\) in the earlier spectral units. Inverting as a perfect blackbody gives a brightness temperature of about 375 K. The difference is a physical radiance–temperature degeneracy. Repeat at known ambient temperatures while keeping the object thermally stabilised to constrain the reflected fraction. If heating the background changes the actual object temperature, a second independent temperature sensor or a transient thermal model is necessary.

When detector heat capacity sets the measured signal

Consider a thermal receiver of heat capacity \(C_d\) coupled to its surroundings with thermal conductance \(G_d\). For small excursions its temperature obeys \(C_d\dot\theta+G_d\theta=P_{\rm abs}(t)\), where \(\theta\) is the excess above its operating point. A voltage thermometer with responsivity \(r_T\) converts this into output. Its frequency-dependent power responsivity is \(r_T/(G_d-i\omega C_d)\). The geometric and spectral calculations determine absorbed optical power, while this thermal equation determines how much of its modulation appears at the electrical output.

Take \(C_d=2.0\) microjoules per kelvin and \(G_d=0.10\) milliwatt per kelvin. The thermal time constant is 20 ms and ordinary corner frequency is 7.96 Hz. A sinusoidal absorbed-power amplitude of 6.0 microwatts would give a static temperature rise of 0.060 K. At 20 Hz the amplitude is reduced by \(1/\sqrt{1+(2\pi f\tau)^2}=0.370\), leaving about 22.2 mK with a phase lag magnitude near 68 degrees. A receiver calibrated with a steady optical power is not automatically calibrated for this chopped experiment.

A square-wave chopper also changes the relation between time-domain peak differences and a lock-in's fundamental reading. For equal hot and reference dwell times, a square wave switching between powers \(P_1,P_0\) has fundamental sinusoidal peak amplitude \(2(P_1-P_0)/\pi\), with different factors for rms and peak reports. The thermal transfer attenuates this component at the chopper frequency and the odd harmonics at their respective frequencies. Substituting the full hot-minus-reference power directly into the first-harmonic response would overestimate the expected signal.

Measure a frequency sweep on a stable reference and test whether amplitude and phase share a single time constant. A geometrical error rescales the amplitude but ordinarily leaves this thermal phase unchanged. A second thermal mass or an electronic filter can introduce another pole. Lowering chop frequency increases thermal signal until the static limit but can expose low-frequency environmental drift. Choose the frequency by comparing the calibrated response with the measured noise spectrum.

Finally, a reference and an unknown source must illuminate the same active area if responsivity varies across the receiver. Imaging one source as a small spot and the other as a filled patch can create a calibration bias even when total spectral powers agree. Translating a reference spot across the detector or imaging the same aperture successively at both temperatures tests that spatial uniformity assumption.

If the sensor is used on a source much smaller than the calibrated reference aperture, record an image or a field-stop scan to determine its filling fraction. Otherwise even exact knowledge of filter transmission and thermodynamic temperature leaves an unknown geometrical scale factor. Aperture filling is part of the measurement equation, not an electronic gain setting.

Tutorial · Design a numerical spectral-integration check

An engineer tabulates spectral radiance per micrometre on a wavelength mesh. Another rewrites the detector model on an equally spaced frequency mesh, obtaining a signal that differs by several percent after independent mesh refinement. Explain what must transform, what stays dimensionless and how to compare a photon-counting channel with a power channel.

Transform the complete measurement integral

The differential power \(B_\lambda\,d\lambda\) must equal \(B_\nu\,|d\nu|\), with \(|d\lambda/d\nu|=c/\nu^2\). A dimensionless transmission curve becomes the same physical transmission at \(\lambda=c/\nu\); it acquires no additional Jacobian. The integration bounds reverse when increasing wavelength is replaced by increasing frequency. A response specified per unit wavelength, or a calibrated gain integrating a particular finite bin, must instead be interpreted from how it was defined.

Refine each grid independently, treating sharp filter edges consistently and converting per-micrometre densities before using metres. For a photon counter divide each spectral power contribution by photon energy before summation. Replacing that energy by a single central-wavelength value is an approximation that should be checked against the full band and source temperature. Close agreement between two different meshes using the same incorrect density is not a valid units check.
