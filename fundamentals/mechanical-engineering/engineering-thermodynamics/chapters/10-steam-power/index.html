From first principles

Obtain Rankine-cycle efficiency by following one kilogram

Consider a simple steady steam loop with a pump, boiler, turbine and condenser. Number states \(1\) at pump inlet, \(2\) at pump outlet, \(3\) at turbine inlet and \(4\) at turbine exhaust. Neglect kinetic and potential changes and external heat leaks. The steady-flow energy balance for one kilogram through the adiabatic pump and turbine converts their enthalpy changes directly into shaft work; in the boiler and condenser there is no shaft work:

\[w_p=h_2-h_1,\]
\[w_t=h_3-h_4,\]
\[q_{\rm in}=h_3-h_2,\]
\[q_{\rm out}=h_4-h_1 .\]

Subtract the pump input from the turbine output and divide by heat supplied. Rearrangement gives a second expression that checks cycle energy closure:

\[\eta_{\rm th}=\frac{(h_3-h_4)-(h_2-h_1)}{h_3-h_2}=1-\frac{h_4-h_1}{h_3-h_2}.\]

These identities use actual enthalpies. An idealized isentropic turbine supplies \(h_{4s}\) from its outlet pressure and inlet entropy; isentropic efficiency then estimates actual \(h_4\). Liquid pump work often admits the incompressible estimate \(v(p_2-p_1)\), explaining why its enthalpy rise can be much smaller than the turbine enthalpy drop.

Derivation and applications

Close a steam-cycle balance using the condenser cooling-water measurement

The condenser often transfers more heat than the plant delivers in shaft work. Its cooling-water measurement supplies an independent check on assumed steam flow and moisture fraction. Choose a steady control volume enclosing both sides of a surface condenser, with no mixing, negligible heat leaks or pressure losses, and negligible kinetic and potential energy changes. Steam leaves as saturated liquid at the condenser pressure. Cooling water remains a single-phase liquid with constant \(c_w\). Denote steam and cooling-water mass rates by \(\dot m_s,\dot m_w\).

Separate mass balances keep both flow rates constant across their respective sides. The steady energy balance couples the enthalpy lost by steam to the energy gained by water:

\[\dot m_s(h_{\rm ex}-h_f)=\dot m_w c_w(T_{w,o}-T_{w,i}).\]

If the turbine exhaust is a saturated mixture of quality \(x\), then \(h_{\rm ex}=h_f+xh_{fg}\). Solving for quality gives a quantity inferred independently of a turbine efficiency assumption:

\[x=\frac{\dot m_w c_w(T_{w,o}-T_{w,i})}{\dot m_s h_{fg}} .\]

To connect this measurement to the complete cycle, write the boiler heat per kilogram as \(q_{\rm in}=h_3-h_2\) and the condenser rejection as \(q_{\rm out}=xh_{fg}\). Over a full ordinary Rankine cycle the working fluid stores no net energy:

\[w_{\rm net}=q_{\rm in}-q_{\rm out},\]
\[\eta_{\rm th}=1-\frac{xh_{fg}}{h_3-h_2}.\]

These relations assume no extraction, reheating or additional heat exchange elsewhere. If the exhaust is superheated or the condensate is significantly subcooled, the first energy balance remains valid but the latent-heat-only expression must be replaced by actual endpoint enthalpies.

Worked example

Infer exhaust quality and plant output

Change the inputs and test this calculation.

Suppose \(\dot m_s=10.0\) kg/s, \(\dot m_w=500\) kg/s, \(c_w=4.18\) kJ/(kg K), water temperature rise \(10.0\) K and \(h_{fg}=2393\) kJ/kg. The water carries off \(20.9\) MW, giving \(x=20\,900/(10.0\times2393)=0.873\). If boiler heat is \(3000\) kJ/kg, the steam receives \(30.0\) MW and the cycle delivers \(9.10\) MW net, corresponding to efficiency \(30.3\%\).

The larger value suggests checking water flow, sensor bias or an assumption such as superheated inlet steam before accepting the inferred shaft output.

Follow a kilogram through the loop

A simple steam plant pumps condensed water to boiler pressure, heats it to steam, expands it through a turbine and condenses it at low pressure. Water is nearly incompressible in the pump, while vapour occupies a much greater specific volume during expansion. A reversible steady adiabatic device satisfies \(dh=v\,dp\) because \(Tds=dh-v\,dp\) and \(ds=0\). Pressure increase therefore costs little work per kilogram when the fluid is liquid. This is one reason for condensing the working fluid before pumping it.

Number the saturated liquid leaving the condenser as state 1, the pump outlet as 2, the turbine inlet as 3 and the exhaust as 4. Neglect changes in kinetic and potential energy. Using positive magnitudes for pump work input and turbine work output gives

\[w_p\simeq v_1(p_2-p_1),\]
\[w_t=h_3-h_4,\]
\[q_{\rm in}=h_3-h_2,\]
\[\eta_{\rm th}=(w_t-w_p)/q_{\rm in}.\]

The exhaust pressure is set by the low-pressure side. An ideal turbine exit at that pressure has \(s_{4s}=s_3\); a real adiabatic turbine delivers a smaller enthalpy drop. Its isentropic efficiency is \(\eta_t=(h_3-h_4)/(h_3-h_{4s})\).

Calculate an ideal and an actual expansion

Use illustrative rounded state data. Condenser pressure is 10 kPa, with \(h_1=191.8\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), \(v_1=0.00101\,\mathrm{m^3/kg}\), \(s_f=0.649,\ s_{fg}=7.50\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) and \(h_{fg}=2393\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Boiler pressure is 4000 kPa and superheated inlet properties are

\[h_3=3214\,\mathrm{kJ/kg},s_3=6.77\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}.\]

The ideal pump requires \(0.00101(4000-10)=4.03\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), so \(h_2=195.8\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\).

At the low pressure, the ideal exhaust quality is \(x_{4s}=(6.77-0.649)/7.50=0.816\), giving \(h_{4s}=191.8+0.816(2393)\simeq2145\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). With turbine efficiency 0.85 the actual exit enthalpy is

\[3214-0.85(3214-2145)=2305\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}.\]

The corresponding quality is

\[(2305-191.8)/2393=0.883.\]

At fixed inlet and exhaust pressure this less efficient turbine leaves more enthalpy in the exhaust, and therefore a higher quality inside the two-phase region.

Interpret the condenser and generator duties

The actual turbine delivers about 909 kJ/kg. Subtracting pump work leaves approximately 905 kJ/kg net shaft work. Boiler heat input is \(3214-195.8=3018\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving thermal efficiency about 30.0%. The condenser rejects \(2305-191.8=2113\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), which closes the cycle energy balance within the rounding of the property data. At 2.0 kg/s, the idealised net mechanical output is about 1.81 MW before generator and auxiliary losses.

A lower condenser pressure generally increases the available expansion work for a fixed turbine inlet. It can also lower exhaust quality in the ideal expansion, increasing liquid droplet loading on the later blades. Evaluating such a change requires new saturation properties and a fresh state calculation. Treating the exhaust as an ideal gas would omit the phase fraction precisely where it affects both the energy balance and turbine operating requirements.

Estimate the consequence of a pump loss

If the pump’s isentropic efficiency falls to 0.70, actual input rises from 4.03 to \(4.03/0.70=5.76\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The turbine work remains the same under the specified inlet and outlet assumptions. Boiler duty falls by 1.73 kJ/kg because its feed arrives with more enthalpy. Both effects should enter the revised efficiency, even though pump work is small compared with the turbine output.

Derive the extraction fraction of an open feedwater heater

In a regenerative steam cycle, part of the flow is withdrawn from the turbine to preheat the boiler feedwater. This raises the average temperature of external heat input but reduces the mass continuing through the low-pressure turbine. Draw the control volumes per kilogram entering the boiler. Let fraction \(y\) be extracted at intermediate pressure and enthalpy \(h_b\), and let the remaining \(1-y\) pass to the condenser. A first pump returns the condensed fraction to the extraction pressure at enthalpy \(h_c\). In an insulated open heater the streams mix at the same pressure to a single liquid outlet of enthalpy \(h_f\).

Mass and energy balances reduce to \(y h_b+(1-y)h_c=h_f\), hence \(y=(h_f-h_c)/(h_b-h_c)\). This fraction must lie between zero and one for passive mixing at the stated states. A second pump then raises the full boiler flow to boiler pressure. If turbine inlet and final exhaust enthalpies are \(h_3,h_4\), work per kilogram admitted to the boiler is

\[w_t=(h_3-h_b)+(1-y)(h_b-h_4),\]
\[w_p=(1-y)w_{p1}+w_{p2}.\]

The first stage always carries the whole flow, as does the second pump. Only the low-pressure turbine, condenser and first pump carry the reduced mass. Applying the factor \(1-y\) to the entire turbine drop is a common bookkeeping mistake. Actual component efficiencies can be included through the enthalpies used in the same balances.

Quantify the exchange between specific work and thermal efficiency

Use an illustrative cycle with \(h_3=3300,\ h_b=2780,\ h_4=2300\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Condensate leaves at 192.0 kJ/kg and requires \(w_{p1}=0.5\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) to reach the heater pressure, so \(h_c=192.5\). Mixed feedwater leaves at \(h_f=640\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) and the second pump requires 5.0 kJ/kg. The extraction fraction is

\[(640-192.5)/(2780-192.5)=0.1730.\]

About 83% of the boiler flow therefore reaches the low-pressure exhaust.

Turbine work is

\[520+0.8270(480)=917.0\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}.\]

Pump input is \(0.8270(0.5)+5=5.41\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving net 911.5 kJ/kg. Boiler input is \(3300-(640+5)=2655\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), or 34.3% thermal efficiency. Condenser duty, calculated independently from the remaining stream, is \(0.8270(2300-192)=1743.3\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), matching the difference between heat input and net work after rounding.

As a simplified comparison, let all steam expand to the same exhaust state and let a single pump require 5.5 kJ/kg with no regeneration. Net work rises to 994.5 kJ/kg but heat input rises to \(3300-197.5=3102.5\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\); efficiency falls to 32.1%. A boiler with a fixed heat supply favours the more efficient cycle, while a turbine limited by inlet mass flow may deliver more shaft power without extraction. The appropriate selection depends on which physical resource sets the constraint.

For a boiler limited to 10 MW of heat supply, the regenerative cycle can process \(10000/2655=3.77\,\mathrm{kg/s}\) of boiler flow and supply about 3.43 MW net. Without extraction the same heat limit permits only \(10000/3102.5=3.22\,\mathrm{kg/s}\), yielding approximately 3.21 MW net. These power outputs reverse the ranking of work per kilogram because the limiting quantity has changed from mass flow to heat input.
