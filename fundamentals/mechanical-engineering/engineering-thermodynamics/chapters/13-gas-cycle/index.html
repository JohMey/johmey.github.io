Choosing a pressure ratio for a gas-turbine cycle

Why does a bigger pressure ratio eventually reduce the work from a gas turbine? Follow one kilogram of gas round four devices, keep separate accounts for heat and work, and see what changes when the compressor and turbine are imperfect.

Label four states before comparing designs

Use an air-standard Brayton cycle. A compressor takes gas from state 1 to 2. A constant-pressure heater raises it to the maximum allowed temperature at 3. A turbine expands the gas to the original pressure at 4. A cooler returns it to 1. Neglect kinetic and potential energy changes and pressure losses in the heat exchangers. The heater and cooler replace combustion and exhaust in this closed model. Use a calorically perfect gas with constant \(c_p\) and \(\gamma=c_p/c_v\). For an ideal adiabatic reversible device the entropy change vanishes. Integrating the ideal-gas entropy differential gives

\[0=c_p\ln(T_{2s}/T_1)-R\ln r,\]
\[x=r^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma},\]
\[T_{2s}=xT_1,\]
\[T_{4s}=T_3/x .\]

The pressure ratio \(r=p_2/p_1=p_3/p_4\) is dimensionless. The subscript s identifies a state at the specified exit pressure reached along an isentrope. The turbine and compressor compare different inlet states. A common bookkeeping mistake is to use the compressor inlet temperature to calculate turbine work.

Subtract the compressor demand before optimizing output

Write positive turbine output and positive compressor consumption separately. The first law then gives the net delivered work \(w\) and the heat input \(q\) per kilogram. Maximizing \(w\) at fixed \(T_1,T_3\) amounts to differentiating with respect to x.

\[{w\over c_p}=T_3(1-1/x)-T_1(x-1),\]
\[{q\over c_p}=T_3-xT_1,\]
\[{1\over c_p}{dw\over dx}=T_3/x^2-T_1 .\]

The maximum occurs at \(x=\sqrt{T_3/T_1}\). The second derivative is negative. There the compressor exit and turbine exit have the same temperature. Dividing work by heat also gives the ideal efficiency \(1-1/x\), which increases throughout the range that admits positive heat input. A design chosen for maximum shaft work and a design chosen for maximum thermal efficiency can therefore have different pressure ratios. At the extreme \(x=T_3/T_1\), both heat input and net work vanish.

A design point and its sensitivity to losses

At 300 K and 1200 K with \(\gamma=1.4,\ c_p=1.005\) kJ/(kg K), maximum ideal work occurs at \(x=2\), hence

\[r=2^{3.5}=11.31.\]

Both intermediate temperatures are 600 K. The compressor consumes 301.5 kJ/kg and the turbine produces 603.0 kJ/kg. The delivered 301.5 kJ/kg for 603.0 kJ/kg heating gives 50% efficiency.

Now set the compressor isentropic efficiency to 0.80 and the turbine efficiency to 0.90. The actual compressor rise is divided by its efficiency, while the ideal turbine drop is multiplied by its efficiency. At the same pressure ratio the actual temperatures are 675 K and 660 K, the net work is 165.8 kJ/kg, and heat input is 527.6 kJ/kg. The corresponding work derivative vanishes at \(x=\sqrt{\eta_t\eta_c T_3/T_1}\). This moves the optimum to a smaller pressure ratio. Use the curves to locate both maxima before calculating their positions.

Start with both device efficiencies set to one, record the pressure ratio at maximum work, then reduce only compressor efficiency. Predict which side of the old optimum becomes more costly and compare your reasoning to the shifted peak.
