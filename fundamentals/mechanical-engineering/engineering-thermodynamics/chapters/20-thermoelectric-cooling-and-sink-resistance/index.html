Where a thermoelectric cooler rejects its heat

A detector has to operate ten kelvin below the laboratory air temperature. A thermoelectric module beneath it can transport heat against that temperature difference when an electric current flows. Increasing the current also generates heat inside the module and warms its heat sink. Build an energy balance that predicts when additional current helps, when it costs more than it delivers and why a nominal cooling-capacity figure alone cannot specify an operating point.

Identify the two faces and the power supply

Treat the detector plate as an isothermal cold face at \(T_c\) and the face in contact with the heat sink as an isothermal hot face at \(T_h\). The laboratory air is at temperature \(T_a\). All three temperatures enter the calculation in kelvin. Temperature differences in kelvin and degrees Celsius have the same numerical value, but the Peltier term contains an absolute temperature. A calculation using a cold-side value of 15 instead of 288 would suppress its principal heat-transport mechanism.

Define \(Q_c\) as the steady heat absorbed from the cold plate and \(Q_h\) as the steady heat given to the sink, both in watts. The electrical power entering the module is \(IV\). The complete module has no net energy storage in steady state, hence \(Q_h=Q_c+IV\). For a detector dissipating 15 W, its refrigeration requirement may be larger than this dissipation if wiring or insulation admits additional environmental heat. The load balance belongs to the detector, while the module characteristic supplies the deterministic prediction of what it can remove at specified temperatures.

Use effective parameters for the assembled device: Seebeck coefficient \(\alpha\) in V/K, electrical resistance \(R\) in ohms and thermal conductance \(K\) in W/K. Current flows through the series electrical elements, while the thermoelectric legs supply largely parallel paths for heat conduction. An effective module coefficient cannot be silently replaced by the coefficient of a single leg. Manufacturer data can vary with temperature, mechanical mounting and electrical contact. The constant-property approximation used here permits an exact algebraic check before introducing fitted temperature-dependent coefficients.

Separate transported heat, back conduction and ohmic heating

A reversible thermoelectric contact transports heat \(\Pi I\) where its Peltier coefficient is

\[\Pi=\alpha T.\]

This relation follows from the Kelvin connection between the reversible Peltier and Seebeck effects. With the sign of current chosen for cooling, the cold junction removes \(\alpha T_c I\) from the plate. Heat conducts down the temperature gradient from the hot face toward the cold one at a rate \(K(T_h-T_c)\), reducing the net amount the module can absorb from the detector.

Resistance generates \(I^2R\) of heat throughout the legs. The familiar one-half allocation can be derived rather than treated as bookkeeping by convention. In a homogeneous leg of constant thermal conductivity and equal cross section, a uniform volumetric heat source produces a parabolic correction to the imposed linear temperature profile. The source-driven correction vanishes at both isothermal ends and its slopes there have equal and opposite signs. Each face therefore receives half of the internally generated heat. A different material arrangement or strongly temperature-dependent coefficients can change that split.

\[Q_c=\alpha T_c I-\tfrac12 I^2R-K(T_h-T_c),\]
\[Q_h=\alpha T_h I+\tfrac12 I^2R-K(T_h-T_c).\]

Subtracting the two equations gives

\[Q_h-Q_c=I[\alpha(T_h-T_c)+IR].\]

Thus the electrical terminal voltage compatible with these heat flows is \(V=IR+\alpha(T_h-T_c)\). The second term is the back electromotive force associated with the imposed temperature difference. Omitting it underestimates the input power and inflates the reported coefficient of performance. As a sign check, set \(I=0\). Heat still leaks through a module with \(T_h>T_c\); \(Q_c\) is then negative.

Close the hot-side balance and solve for temperature

A heat sink with thermal resistance \(R_s\) must satisfy

\[T_h=T_a+R_sQ_h.\]

Insert the hot-face expression above, collect every occurrence of \(T_h\) on the left, and solve before evaluating the cold-face capacity. The result is

\[T_h=\frac{T_a+R_s(KT_c+I^2R/2)}{1+R_s(K-\alpha I)}.\]

When \(R_s=0\), the hot junction stays at ambient temperature. For finite \(R_s\) it responds to the rejected heat, which includes the electrical input. A steady-state candidate also requires a positive stabilising slope in the hot-face balance. With a finite hot-side heat capacity the linear coefficient of a temperature perturbation is proportional to \(\alpha I-K-1/R_s\). The denominator in the formula is positive precisely when this perturbation decays, for \(R_s>0\). Extremely large currents can invalidate constant material properties well before a formal singularity is reached.

For \(T_c=288\) K, \(T_a=298\) K, \(\alpha=0.050\) V/K, \(R=2.0\) ohms, \(K=0.60\) W/K, \(R_s=0.45\) K/W and \(I=2.5\) A, the hot face settles at 311.90 K. The cold-side terms are 36.0 W of transported heat, 6.25 W of returning ohmic heat and 14.34 W of thermal leakage. Their net is 15.41 W. The required voltage is approximately 6.195 V. The sink has to dissipate about 30.90 W, consistent with its 13.90 K temperature rise above ambient. Check both balances independently before using a single net wattage in a larger thermal model.

Find the feasible current interval for a prescribed load

With a perfect sink, hold \(T_c\) and \(T_h\) fixed. The capacity is a concave quadratic in \(I\). Differentiation gives the peak at \(I=\alpha T_c/R\), or 7.2 A for the example. At this point the cold-junction transported heat still grows with current, but its marginal gain is exactly cancelled by the marginal ohmic loss. If a positive target is below the maximum available capacity, the same target may be reached at a low-current and a high-current operating point. At fixed face temperatures the low-current point uses less electrical power.

For a finite sink there is an additional derivative \(-K\,dT_h/dI\) in \(dQ_c/dI\). The hot junction becomes warmer as the supply current rises in the operating band. As a result the capacity curve in the notebook reaches its maximum close to 4.32 A. The first current on its finite sweep that meets a 15 W load is about 2.43 A. Refining the current grid or solving \(Q_c(I)-15=0\) gives a sharper estimate. A point chosen just at the load threshold has essentially no margin for an unexpectedly warm laboratory.

The refrigeration coefficient of performance is \(Q_c/(IV)\) only where \(Q_c>0\) and \(IV>0\). At 2.5 A it is about 0.995. The ideal reversible upper bound for heat pumping between the actual two face temperatures is \(T_c/(T_h-T_c)\simeq12.1\). Using room air in place of \(T_h\) yields another bound for a differently defined system whose losses include the heat exchanger. Neither comparison supplies a substitute for the heat-flow balances. They independently warn about an implausibly optimistic calculated performance.

Estimate the sink resistance from an operating test

Imagine that the laboratory supply measures 2.50 A and 6.20 V while two thermometers indicate 311.9 K at the hot face and 298.0 K in the entering air. A calibrated electrical heater on the cold plate supplies 15.4 W. With negligible other heat leaks the module lifts the heater power. The sink receives \(15.4+2.50(6.20)=30.9\) W. Dividing the measured temperature difference by this load gives an effective sink resistance of 0.450 K/W. A measurement of the sink temperature alone would be insufficient for this estimate. The electrical input must be measured at the device terminals, and the two thermal measurements must refer to the endpoints of the resistance being inferred.

Consider instead that an unmeasured heat leak of 2 W also enters the cold plate. The true hot-side load rises to 32.9 W at the stated electrical readings. Assigning the full temperature rise to only 30.9 W overestimates the thermal resistance by a factor \(32.9/30.9\), or about 6.5%. Repeating the measurement with a second heater power at the same cold temperature can help separate an approximately constant parasitic load from a change in resistance. Plot hot-face rise against heater power plus measured electrical power for both points. The slope estimates the resistance and an intercept can reveal the omitted heat load. Changes in fan speed or ambient temperature during the test would add another explanation for that intercept.

The first feasible current in a tabulated sweep depends on the grid spacing. The notebook refines the lower crossing with OneSimulation’s bracketed inverse in a local installation. It chooses two adjacent currents on the increasing branch. A bracket spanning the entire current interval could include a second solution on the decreasing branch.

Exercises on operating point and experimental interpretation

1. Repeat the 2.5 A calculation with the hot face clamped to room temperature. How much cooling does an ideal heat sink recover?

Work through the solution

With \(T_h-T_c=10\) K the cold capacity is

\[36.0-6.25-6.0=23.75\,\mathrm{W}.\]

Compared with 15.41 W, the ideal sink recovers about 8.34 W by reducing back conduction. Its terminal voltage is also smaller, \(5+0.05(10)=5.50\) V, since the Seebeck contribution falls with the temperature difference.

2. A technician sizes the heat sink for 15.4 W because that is the measured cooling capacity. What temperature prediction results and what balance does it violate?

Work through the solution

The assumed temperature rise is \(0.45(15.4)=6.93\) K, about half the actual rise. The electrical supply adds approximately \(2.5(6.195)=15.49\) W. The correct hot-face load includes both contributions. A cooled detector can warm its surrounding air by more heat than the detector generates because the power supply is part of the complete energy balance.

3. Which measurement can disentangle electrical resistance from the Seebeck coefficient when both are uncertain?

Work through the solution

At a fixed measured difference between face temperatures, sweep small currents and measure terminal voltage. The slope gives \(R\) and the zero-current voltage gives \(\alpha(T_h-T_c)\), provided the face temperatures remain controlled and the leads contribute negligible voltage offsets. Reverse the temperature difference or calibrate the leads to test that offset. A current sweep without measuring both face temperatures mixes the slope of the ohmic term with the change in hot-face temperature.

Check the module definitions against an engineering reference

The Ferrotec reference on mathematical modelling of modules gives performance equations and temperature-dependent parameter descriptions. When moving from this example to a particular module, check the property definitions and the temperatures at which the supplier evaluated them.
