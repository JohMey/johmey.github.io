Separate the conserved and relaxing coordinates

Let the capacities of initially hot and cold bodies be \(C_h=120\) and \(C_c=300\) J/K. Initial temperatures are 70 and 20 degrees Celsius, respectively. Their contact conducts \(H=2.5\) W/K. Assume capacities and conductance are constant and heat losses initially negligible. Derive two independent combinations of temperature and identify which one a thermometer difference measures.

Add the balances and subtract the temperature rates

The contact heat rate is \(H(T_h-T_c)\), entering the cold body and leaving the hot body. Their balances are \(C_h\dot T_h=-H(T_h-T_c)\) and \(C_c\dot T_c=H(T_h-T_c)\). Add them to obtain conservation of \(C_hT_h+C_cT_c\). An arbitrary Celsius reference cancels from changes in this energy; absolute temperature will be necessary if entropy is required. Subtract the equations after dividing by the respective capacities to obtain

\[\bar T=\frac{C_hT_h+C_cT_c}{C_h+C_c},\]
\[T_h-T_c=(T_{h0}-T_{c0})e^{-t/\tau},\]
\[\tau=\frac{C_hC_c}{H(C_h+C_c)}.\]

The equilibrium temperature is 34.286 degrees Celsius and the relaxation time 34.286 seconds. Their matching numerical values are coincidental and their dimensions differ. Changing the contact conductance changes only the relaxation time, while changing a body's capacity changes both. The arithmetic temperature mean is not conserved for unequal capacities.

Predict an intermediate reading and irreversible entropy production

Compute both thermometer readings at 40 seconds. Does energy conservation imply zero total entropy change? Use the complete final equilibration to obtain a numerical entropy check and explain why using temperatures in degrees Celsius would give a spurious result.

Reconstruct both temperatures and integrate the entropy changes

The difference after 40 seconds is

\[50e^{-40/34.286}=15.57\,\mathrm{K}.\]

Solving the weighted-mean and difference equations gives \(T_h=\bar T+C_c(T_h-T_c)/(C_h+C_c)=45.41\) degrees Celsius and \(T_c=29.84\) degrees Celsius. Substitution into the original energy balance gives equal and opposite heat transfer. The instantaneous contact heat current is about 38.9 W, below its initial value of 125 W.

At any instant, \(dS_{\rm total}/dt=H(T_h-T_c)(1/T_c-1/T_h)\ge0\) using kelvin. Integrating each body's reversible state-function differential \(C\,dT/T\) between its initial and final equilibrium temperatures gives \(C_h\ln(T_f/T_{h0})+C_c\ln(T_f/T_{c0})\), about 1.09 J/K. Temperature differences in conduction may use either Celsius or kelvin, but a reciprocal temperature or ratio in an entropy calculation requires an absolute scale. Internal irreversibility produces entropy while the isolated system conserves energy.

Recover a contact from data with one independently known capacity

Suppose the hot capacity is known and the cold capacity is to be calibrated. The equilibrium and temperature-difference measurements are as predicted. Show how to recover \(C_c\) and \(H\), then demonstrate a family of parameter sets producing identical temperatures if neither capacity is known.

Use one temperature ratio and one time constant

The final energy balance gives \(C_c/C_h=(T_{h0}-T_f)/(T_f-T_{c0})=2.50\), so \(C_c=300\) J/K. The slope of a log difference against time equals \(-1/\tau\); substitution gives \(H=2.50\) W/K. Fit the signed temperature difference before taking a logarithm near its noise floor. Two-channel gain or lag mismatch can make the measured difference turn negative late in the record even while actual contact heat flow has not reversed.

Multiplying both capacities and the conductance by the same positive number leaves both differential equations for temperature unchanged. No temperature trace from this isolated preparation can fix their absolute scale without additional information. A known electrical heat pulse delivered to one isolated body can determine its capacity if heat loss and thermometer lag are controlled. Changing the initial temperatures alone supplies more data but cannot remove this exact scaling degeneracy.

Diagnose a warm enclosure and a delayed thermometer

After the fast transient, the two nearly equal temperatures cool slowly toward an enclosure at 20 degrees Celsius, with common slope initially minus 0.010 K/s near 34.3 degrees Celsius. Estimate total external thermal conductance. Can the same trace determine which body leaks the heat?

Form the whole-system balance and design a separate perturbation

When internal equilibration is fast, a common-temperature approximation gives

\[G_{\rm loss}\simeq-(C_h+C_c)\dot T/(\bar T-T_{\rm room}).\]

The measured slope implies about 0.294 W/K. Its external cooling time is roughly 1430 s, much longer than the contact relaxation. A slow external loss may still bias a final-temperature estimate taken very late. Fit the full two-temperature model or extrapolate the common cooling trajectory back toward the end of internal equilibration.

The common slope identifies the sum of conductances to the enclosure, not their allocation when both temperatures are equal. Heat one body separately with the link removed or swap external insulation while keeping contact unchanged. A sensor's own heat capacity and time lag can introduce another apparent time constant. Calibrating both thermometers on one body during a known step tests that alternative. Those independent checks decide whether a second observed decay belongs to contact physics, environmental loss or the measurement itself.
