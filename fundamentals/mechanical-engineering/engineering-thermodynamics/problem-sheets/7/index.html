Budgeting cooling power for a freezing experiment

A cold plate grows a planar ice layer while a metal support fin conducts heat into the cold enclosure. Construct an energy budget that distinguishes the rate at which the front moves, the total latent heat extracted, and the electrical input of the refrigerator.

Problem sheet 7 · Integrative project

1 · Predict the completion time and distinguish two resistances2 · Convert front position into a heat-load history3 · Include a parasitic fin connected to a warm mount4 · Test a thermodynamic bound

1 · Predict the completion time and distinguish two resistances

Water starts at its melting point. Use \(\rho=917\) kg/m³, latent heat 334 kJ/kg and solid conductivity 2.2 W/(m K). Coolant is 5 K below the melting point with film coefficient 200 W/(m² K). Find the time to grow 15 mm on 0.0100 m². Use the small-Stefan-number model and explain why doubling area does not change this time.

Integrate the local flux balance

The heat flux magnitude is \(5/(1/h+s/k)\). Equating it to \(\rho L_f\dot s\) gives

\[t=(\rho L_f/5)[s/h+s^2/(2k)].\]

Film and solid contributions are about 4594 and 3132 s, respectively. Total time is 7727 s, or 2.15 h. Both extracted heat and interface mass scale with area, so area cancels from the local balance for a planar front. The plate and coolant must deliver the corresponding total power over the chosen footprint.

Check the finite-film and fixed-wall growth curves at 15 mm

2 · Convert front position into a heat-load history

Determine latent heat extracted and mean latent heat rate over the run. Calculate initial and final heat rates and explain why the simple average of the endpoint rates differs from the integral mean.

Close the integral and instantaneous budgets

Ice mass is \(917(0.0100)(0.015)=0.13755\) kg, hence \(Q=45.94\) kJ and mean rate 5.95 W. Initially the finite film limits flux to \(h\Delta T=1000\) W/m² or 10.0 W. At 15 mm it falls to \(5/[0.005+0.015/2.2]=423\) W/m² or 4.23 W. The average of these endpoints is 7.12 W. Heat rate varies nonlinearly with time and spends longer near its lower values. Integrating through the latent energy gives the correct time average.

3 · Include a parasitic fin connected to a warm mount

An aluminium support strip 20 mm wide, 2 mm thick and 50 mm long has a base 25 K warmer than the cold enclosure. Take \(k=200\) W/(m K), \(h=25\) W/(m² K), all four long faces exposed, insulated tip. Calculate the extra steady heat deposited in the enclosure and the mean electrical power at a measured refrigerator COP of 2.2.

Evaluate the distributed heat leak

With \(A=4.0\times10^{-5}\) m² and \(P=0.044\) m, \(m=\sqrt{hP/(kA)}=11.73\) m⁻¹. Fin heat is \(kAm(25)\tanh(mL)=1.24\) W. During freezing the enclosure therefore requires mean heat removal \(5.95+1.24=7.19\) W. Electrical power is \(7.19/2.2=3.26\) W using unrounded numbers. The chapter's fin study uses a 50 K root excess, so its heat rate for the same geometry is twice this value by linearity. The peak electrical requirement under the constant-COP assumption is about 5.11 W at startup.

Check the support-strip heat rate and use its linear temperature scaling

4 · Test a thermodynamic bound

The refrigerator rejects heat to a room at 20 °C while its cold reservoir is at −5 °C. Compute the maximum reversible COP and its minimum possible mean work input at the preceding load. Would a claimed 0.50 W mean input be compatible with these two reservoirs?

Use absolute temperatures and both energy streams

The reversible COP is \(T_c/(T_h-T_c)=268.15/25=10.73\), so even a reversible refrigerator needs about \(7.18/10.73=0.670\) W. A reported 0.50 W would imply COP 14.4. Check whether other cooling paths, stored cold or an incorrect averaging interval entered that measurement. The warm side must reject the sum of extracted heat and electrical work, giving about 10.45 W for the measured COP.
