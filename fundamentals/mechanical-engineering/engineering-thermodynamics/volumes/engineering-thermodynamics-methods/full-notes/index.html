Thermal processes and transport in engineering · 1. Staged compression with an imperfect intercooler

A laboratory compressed-air supply has two compressor stages and an intercooler. Equal pressure ratios minimise work only under particular assumptions. Derive the optimum, compare it with a single-stage machine and identify which temperature and power measurements reveal imperfect cooling or unequal efficiency.

Work follows the actual enthalpy rise

Model dry air as an ideal gas with constant \(c_p=1.005\) kJ per kilogram kelvin and \(\gamma=1.40\). Assume steady adiabatic compressor stages, negligible changes in kinetic and potential energy, and negligible intercooler pressure drop initially. For a fixed inlet temperature \(T_i\) and pressure ratio \(r_i\), an isentropic reference ends at \(T_{os}=T_i r_i^a\), \(a=(\gamma-1)/\gamma\). Isentropic compressor efficiency compares reference enthalpy rise with actual rise, so actual shaft work per unit mass is \(w_i=c_pT_i(r_i^a-1)/\eta_i\).

An intercooler reduces the second-stage inlet temperature by rejecting heat to a colder stream. It does not undo the pressure work already delivered by the first stage. For overall ratio \(R\) and first-stage ratio \(r\), assume the measured second-stage inlet \(T_2\) can be held fixed across the comparison. Adding both work contributions gives

\[w(r)=c_p\left[\frac{T_1}{\eta_1}(r^a-1)+\frac{T_2}{\eta_2}((R/r)^a-1)\right].\]

Direct differentiation with respect to \(\log r\) balances the incremental work costs of transferring pressure ratio between stages. The stationary split is

\[r_{\rm opt}=\sqrt R\left(\frac{T_2\eta_1}{T_1\eta_2}\right)^{1/(2a)} .\]

Both exponential-power terms have positive second derivatives in \(\log r\), giving a unique interior minimum when the root lies between one and \(R\). Otherwise the constrained optimum is a boundary under this simplified model. A hot or inefficient second stage favours assigning more ratio to the first. Perfect intercooling with identical efficiencies gives \(r=\sqrt R\), the geometric mean rule for intermediate absolute pressure.

Use pressure, temperature and electrical power together

For \(R=9,T_1=T_2=300\) K and efficiencies 0.85, each stage ideally uses ratio three. Each consumes 130.79 kJ/kg and the combined work is 261.59 kJ/kg. The otherwise identical single stage uses 309.82 kJ/kg. At 0.020 kg/s, two-stage shaft power is 5.23 kW before motor losses. The first-stage discharge temperature is about 430.1 K; cooling it back to 300 K rejects 130.8 kJ/kg, numerically equal to that first adiabatic stage's work under these property assumptions.

Open the live experiment

Raise the second inlet temperature or reduce its efficiency. The minimum now moves toward a higher first pressure ratio. The graph compares total specific work, keeping final absolute pressure fixed. It does not guarantee that a real compressor can operate at every displayed split: stage flow maps, surge, choke, motor limits and heat exchanger capacity may restrict the admissible point. Measure actual mass flow and pressure drop at each tested condition. A wattmeter upstream of a motor reports more than shaft power; use measured or justified motor efficiency when comparing with a gas enthalpy rise.

Close the work and rejected-heat balances separately

The two-stage minimum is a statement about shaft work per kilogram of ideal gas. An experimenter can test it by weighing delivered gas over a timed interval and measuring electrical energy or shaft torque. Converting an electrical reading to specific shaft work requires the motor efficiency at the measured operating point. A small motor often operates at a different efficiency when the intermediate pressure changes. Dividing the same electrical input by two different mass-flow readings can obscure a compressor change behind a motor change.

For an illustrative mass flow of 0.10 kg/s, a computed 262 kJ/kg corresponds to 26.2 kW at the shaft. At 90 percent motor efficiency the electrical input is about 29.1 kW. If the first compressor delivers at 430 K and the intercooler returns the gas to 300 K, its separate steady heat duty at the stated constant heat capacity is approximately 13.1 kW. A flow of cooling water warming by 10 K at 4.18 kJ per kilogram kelvin would need about 0.31 kg/s. These two measurements test different assumptions. Work compares both compressor stages; the cooling-water enthalpy rise compares only the intermediate exchanger, including any loss through its casing.

When measuring intake and discharge temperatures, distinguish stagnation from static temperature if velocity changes appreciably in connecting ducts. A thermocouple close to a wall may also respond to conduction along its sheath. Repeat each setting after allowing the intercooler and casing to approach steady temperatures. Heat retained in a warming machine changes the short-time coolant balance while leaving the algebraic optimum calculated for fully steady operation unchanged.

Tutorial · Find the new optimum for incomplete cooling

Suppose \(R=9,T_1=300\) K, \(T_2=330\) K and both efficiencies remain 0.85. Predict the optimal first ratio and decide whether it is physically sensible. Estimate its work advantage over equal ratios using the graph or direct substitution.

Balance the two marginal temperature-weighted costs

Since \(1/(2a)=1.75\), \(r_{\rm opt}=3(1.10)^{1.75}=3.545\), so the second ratio is about 2.54. The hotter second stage now receives less compression work. Direct substitution gives approximately 273.5 kJ/kg versus 274.7 kJ/kg at equal ratios, an improvement only around one kJ/kg. Imperfect cooling itself costs much more relative to the original 261.6 kJ/kg. A noisy power sweep can therefore locate a rather broad minimum poorly even when cooling quality clearly matters.

Tutorial · Include an intercooler pressure loss

The cooler outlet pressure is a fraction \(1-\delta\) of first-stage discharge pressure. For fixed compressor inlet and final discharge pressures, what happens to the product of the actual compressor-stage ratios? Compute the equal-efficiency, fully cooled optimum at \(R=9\), \(\delta=0.05\).

Use absolute pressures on both sides of the exchanger

With \(p_2=(1-\delta)p_1r_1\), final pressure \(p_f=p_2r_2\), so \(r_1r_2=R/(1-\delta)=9.474\). Under identical stage inlet temperatures and efficiencies the optimum actual compressor ratios are equal to \(\sqrt{9.474}=3.078\). Thus the pressures around the cooler straddle the ideal loss-free geometric-mean intermediate pressure. A pressure tap only at first discharge cannot determine the second-stage ratio when exchanger loss is appreciable.

Tutorial · A temperature measurement conflicts with the power estimate

A trial records 0.020 kg/s, first-stage inlet 300 K and discharge 430 K. The drive wattmeter reports 3.25 kW for that stage. Estimate gas enthalpy power and explain two possible sources of the difference that have distinguishable signatures.

Draw separate drive and gas control volumes

The gas enthalpy gain is \(0.020(1.005)(130)=2.61\) kW. Relative to electrical input this gives an apparent conversion fraction of 0.804. Electrical and mechanical motor losses are candidates, but heat rejected by a nonadiabatic compressor, temperature probe recovery errors, flow calibration and auxiliary equipment can also enter. Compare shaft torque times angular speed with electrical power to isolate drive losses. Measure casing heat rejection or obtain an energy balance around compressor plus coolant if shaft power remains above the gas gain. A single inferred efficiency cannot identify which physical boundary carries the missing 0.64 kW.

Thermal processes and transport in engineering · 2. How a gravity-drained condensate film limits heat transfer

Saturated vapour condenses on a cool vertical plate. A liquid film thickens as it drains, adding thermal resistance toward the bottom. Derive the local heat-transfer coefficient from coupled momentum and energy balances, then determine which observations would invalidate the smooth-film model.

Find the liquid flow for a specified film thickness

Let \(x\) increase downward along a vertical isothermal wall, \(y\) normal to it, and \(\delta(x)\) be the liquid thickness. Assume a steady laminar film with negligible inertia, constant liquid properties, negligible interfacial shear from slow vapour, saturation temperature at the interface, and a wall uniformly below saturation. Neglect sensible cooling of the condensate relative to latent heat and heat conduction along the plate. The common vapour pressure gradient is approximately hydrostatic, so net downward liquid driving per unit volume is \((\rho_l-\rho_v)g\).

Solve \(\mu_lu_{yy}=-(\rho_l-\rho_v)g\) with no slip at \(y=0\) and zero shear at \(y=\delta\). The velocity is a downward parabola \(u=(\rho_l-\rho_v)g(\delta y-y^2/2)/\mu_l\). Integrating gives mass flow per unit width \(\Gamma=\rho_l(\rho_l-\rho_v)g\delta^3/(3\mu_l)\). The velocity increases from the wall to its interface maximum; assigning the interface a second no-slip condition would substantially underpredict gravity drainage.

A growing mass flow changes the conductive resistance

If transverse heat conduction dominates, approximately linear temperature through the film gives wall heat flux \(q''=k_l(T_{\rm sat}-T_w)/\delta\). New condensate produced per unit wall length adds \(d\Gamma/dx=q''/h_{fg}\). Substitute the cubic film-flow relation and integrate from an ideal leading edge of zero film thickness:

\[\delta(x)=\left[\frac{4\mu_lk_l\Delta T\,x}{\rho_l(\rho_l-\rho_v)g h_{fg}}\right]^{1/4},\]
\[h_x=\frac{k_l}{\delta(x)},\]
\[\bar h_L=\frac43h_L.\]

The leading coefficient diverges at \(x=0\) in this idealisation, but its integral is finite. A real leading edge, substrate conduction and contact-angle effects regularise the immediate entrance. The factor \(4/3\) comes from integrating \(x^{-1/4}\), not averaging endpoint values. A plate made twice as long gains heat transfer area but increases film resistance downstream; total heat per unit width rises only as \(L^{3/4}\).

As an illustrative water-like fluid take \(\rho_l=960,\rho_v=0.6\) kg/m³, \(k_l=0.68\) W/(m K), \(\mu_l=2.9\times10^{-4}\) Pa s, \(h_{fg}=2.26\) MJ/kg, \(\Delta T=5.0\) K and \(L=0.20\) m. The outlet film is roughly 79 micrometres and the outlet coefficient about 8.6 kW per square metre kelvin. The average coefficient is about 11.5 kW per square metre kelvin; a 0.10 m wide plate removes about 1.15 kW and yields roughly 0.51 g/s condensate. Calculate film Reynolds number with the specified convention before accepting the assumed smooth regime.

Return to the compressor and intercooler calculation to compare this thermal coefficient with an actual heat duty

Test the quarter-power prediction at several stations

A film-thickness measurement is easiest away from the leading edge. There the ideal formula predicts a thin film and formally unbounded heat transfer coefficient. Real entrance geometry, surface condition, contact-line physics and measurement resolution take over. In the study the curves begin at a small positive fraction of plate length. This preserves the finite integral of the singular ideal heat flux yet avoids assigning a physical measured value at the mathematical edge.

Open the live experiment

For an independent test place two thermal sensors along the downstream wall and estimate local heat flux by conduction across a thin backing of known thickness and conductivity. At two stations whose distances differ by a factor sixteen, the ideal prediction halves the local coefficient. A common wall heat loss can distort this ratio much more at the downstream station, where the useful heat flux is smaller. Repeat with vapour absent to estimate the backing loss and use an unheated reference section to check temperature gradients in the support.

The liquid mass collected at the bottom integrates all upstream condensation. Doubling plate length increases total condensation by a factor of two raised to three quarters under fixed conditions, even as the mean coefficient falls. If the collector instead finds exactly twice the condensate and local sensors show small axial variation, test for liquid dripping onto the plate from above, a nonuniform vapour temperature or turbulent mixing. Keep track of condensate retained on the surface when using short timed collection intervals; intermittent release into the collector can dominate the apparent uncertainty.

Tutorial · Reconstruct a heat balance from collected mass

A plate of the stated size produces 0.48 g/s of condensate in a steady timed collection. Estimate latent heat removal and inferred mean coefficient. Does disagreement with the wall-coolant calorimeter necessarily imply an incorrect latent heat?

Convert the collected mass into a rate of thermal energy

Latent duty is \(0.00048(2.26\times10^6)=1085\) W. The effective mean coefficient is \(1085/(0.020\cdot5)=10.85\) kW per square metre kelvin. Disagreement with a coolant energy balance can arise from heat leaks, condensate subcooling, liquid holdup or drainage that the collection misses. Weigh over a sufficiently long stationary interval and measure condensate outlet temperature. A transient wetting front stores liquid and invalidates equating instantaneous formation and collection.

Tutorial · Compare two uses of the same plate area

Two plates can be placed vertically in series so liquid feeds from the upper into the lower, or arranged side by side with independently drained leading edges. Each plate has length \(L\) and width \(b\). Estimate the ideal heat-duty ratio at fixed temperatures.

Compare fresh versus pre-existing films

Side-by-side total duty is \(2Q(L)\). A continuous vertically stacked film behaves like one length \(2L\), giving \(Q(2L)=2^{3/4}Q(L)\). Independently drained plates therefore give a factor \(2^{1/4}=1.189\) more ideal duty for the same area. Additional vapour routing, wall conduction and edge drainage can change real performance. The advantage arises from repeatedly beginning with a thin film, not from more total geometric area.

Tutorial · Diagnose a vapour-side resistance

A nominally identical plate shows dramatically smaller heat flux after a small amount of noncondensable gas enters the vapour volume, while liquid film thickness measurements barely change. Why can changing only liquid conductivity in a fit give a misleading result?

Identify a missing interface boundary condition

The model assumed saturated pure vapour at an interface of known temperature. A noncondensable component can accumulate at the interface and require vapour diffusion through a gas layer. Local vapour partial pressure and interface saturation temperature can differ from estimates based on total chamber pressure. Heat and species transport on the vapour side then add resistance. Measure composition and partial pressure, sweep the vapour flow or purge the noncondensable fraction and compare flux with an independent film profile. Fitting a different liquid conductivity can mimic the overall flux but would generally predict the wrong response to those controls.

Thermal processes and transport in engineering · 3. Radiative exchange in a grey enclosure

A thin radiation shield does not need to absorb all incoming radiation to reduce heat transfer. Multiple reflections connect every surface to every other visible surface. Build the radiosity equations, recover the two-plate result and infer what can be learned from a measured shield temperature.

Separate emission, reflection and net heat flux

For opaque diffuse-grey isothermal surface \(i\), let \(G_i\) be irradiation arriving from the enclosure and \(J_i\) be all radiative power leaving per unit area. The outgoing consists of thermal emission plus reflected irradiation. With emissivity \(\epsilon_i\), reflectivity \(1-\epsilon_i\) and blackbody emissive power \(E_{bi}=\sigma T_i^4\), one obtains \(J_i=\epsilon_i E_{bi}+(1-\epsilon_i)G_i\). Net outward heat is \(Q_i=A_i(J_i-G_i)\).

Geometry enters through view factors: \(G_i=\sum_jF_{ij}J_j\) for a closed nonparticipating enclosure of diffuse surfaces, provided reciprocity \(A_iF_{ij}=A_jF_{ji}\) has been used consistently. Each closed-enclosure row sums to one, and summing \(Q_i\) over all surfaces gives zero. A concave surface can see itself, so setting every diagonal view factor to zero would break some real enclosure models. For an opening to surroundings include an explicit environmental surface or known irradiance source.

Eliminate the unknown reflected fields

Two large parallel plates of equal area see only one another. Thus \(G_1=J_2,G_2=J_1\). Eliminate both radiosities, or add a surface resistance for each plate and a unit space resistance in the diffuse network. Net flux from hot to cold becomes

\[q''=\frac{\sigma(T_1^4-T_2^4)}{1/\epsilon_1+1/\epsilon_2-1}.\]

The denominator is not simply the reciprocal of the product of emissivities because photons can reflect repeatedly before absorption. In the black limit both emissivities are one and the cold plate's own emission remains in the numerator. At equal temperature flux vanishes even when emissivities differ. Measuring only outgoing hot-side radiance without reflected incoming light cannot determine heat loss.

For \(T_1=500\) K, \(T_2=300\) K and emissivities 0.80 and 0.60, the blackbody power difference is 3085 W/m² and the denominator 1.9167, giving about 1609 W/m². Place an opaque thermally floating shield between the plates, with both faces of emissivity 0.10 and negligible conduction to its supports. The same steady flux crosses both gaps. The two gap resistances add in series, with total dimensionless denominator \(1/\epsilon_1+2/\epsilon_s+1/\epsilon_2-2=20.9167\), leaving about 147.5 W/m².

Check the underlying blackbody emission and view-factor balances in the radiation chapter

Determine its temperature from the steady flux

For the above example the first gap denominator is \(1/0.80+1/0.10-1=10.25\) and the second \(10.6667\). Write \(E_{bs}=E_{b1}-q''D_1\) or \(E_{bs}=E_{b2}+q''D_2\). The shield temperature is about 435 K. It is a weighted average of fourth powers, not the arithmetic average of temperatures. A measured shield temperature without net heat flux generally constrains resistance ratios, while the flux supplies their sum.

Use a floating shield as a two-sided calorimeter

Insert an opaque isothermal sheet between the two parallel walls. At steady state its net accumulation vanishes, so energy transferred across the hot gap equals energy leaving the cold gap. For identical external surface emissivities and identical shield-face emissivities, the two radiative resistances of the gaps are the same. Equality of the fourth-power temperature drops then places the shield temperature at the fourth-power mean of the two plate temperatures. It is generally above their arithmetic mean. Lower shield emissivity changes the size of the common heat flow while leaving the intermediate temperature fixed in this symmetric problem.

Open the live experiment

Make a paired test using a matte shield and a polished shield of the same dimensions. A heat-flux transducer should respond strongly, while an ideal shield-temperature measurement would indicate the same equilibrium value. That contrast provides a cleaner test than relying on a single fitted emissivity. A thermocouple lead can conduct heat away from the shield and change its temperature, particularly for the polished sheet when radiative conductance is small. A radiation thermometer aimed at that sheet can receive reflected hot-wall radiation. Use a conducting contact sensor with estimated lead loss and a separately characterised radiance measurement to investigate discrepancies.

If the shield faces have different coatings, reverse it without changing either plate. The series resistance and net heat flow stay the same under the simple diffuse-grey model because the two face resistances enter as a sum. The equilibrium shield temperature changes, however, because the allocation of resistance to the hot and cold gaps changes. This distinguishes an orientation-dependent temperature from an orientation-dependent heat flux.

Tutorial · Can two identical black plates benefit from a black shield?

Keep the same temperatures but make both plates and both shield faces black. Calculate flux with and without one floating shield. Would replacing one shield by two double the benefit again?

Count separate spaces and solve their fluxes

Without a shield the net flux is about 3085 W/m². With one black shield two identical gap resistances divide it in half, about 1542 W/m². With two black shields there are three equal resistances and flux is one third of the unshielded value, about 1028 W/m². Each plate emits and absorbs on its own faces even when black. Equal changes in the count of shields add equal resistances in this ideal case rather than multiplying the previous flux by one half each time.

Tutorial · Test a view-factor proposal by reciprocity

Two surfaces have areas 0.020 and 0.050 square metres. A ray calculation gives \(F_{12}=0.70\). What should \(F_{21}\) be and why cannot the uncovered row sum automatically be assigned to the same neighbouring surface?

Use the area-weighted symmetry

Reciprocity gives \(F_{21}=0.020(0.70)/0.050=0.28\). Row remainders are respectively 0.30 and 0.72. They can describe additional walls, an opening or a surface's self-view if geometry permits. Two convex finite unequal plates alone do not form a closed enclosure with unit rows. Give their surrounding environment a temperature and surface properties or prescribe incoming irradiation before computing the net powers.

Tutorial · Infer an omitted conductive heat leak

An instrument with the example shield and plate area 0.10 square metres reports net heat input of 19 W at the hot wall after other losses are removed. Assume the ideal radiation estimate holds. Estimate the parallel conductance directly connecting hot and cold walls and propose a second test.

Compare absolute powers and their different temperature scaling

Predicted radiation is about 14.75 W, leaving 4.25 W not explained. At a 200 K temperature difference a direct linear bypass would require \(0.0213\) W/K. This could represent support conduction but is not unique: emissivity errors, finite plate-edge view to surroundings or residual gas conduction can also contribute. Repeat at a different mean absolute temperature while holding the same wall-temperature difference. Linear solid conduction with weak property variation remains similar, whereas radiative transfer grows strongly with mean temperature. Monitor the floating shield temperature too; direct bypass need not heat it, whereas a support attached to the shield changes its equilibrium.

Thermal processes and transport in engineering · 4. Periodic heating as a measurement of thermal diffusivity

Two buried thermometers see the same applied surface temperature oscillation with different amplitudes and phases. Derive how a solid filters that oscillation, recover diffusivity independently from both observations and identify when a rear boundary or a poor sensor contact changes the inference.

Choose the decaying branch of a complex thermal wave

A homogeneous solid fills \(x>0\) and has constant conductivity \(k\), density \(\rho\) and heat capacity \(c_p\). For small one-dimensional disturbances its temperature excess obeys \(\partial_t\theta=\alpha\partial_x^2\theta\), \(\alpha=k/(\rho c_p)\). Impose sinusoidal surface temperature with amplitude \(A\) and angular frequency \(\omega\), and wait until the initial transient has decayed. With a positive-exponential time convention look for \(\theta=\operatorname{Re}[Ae^{-sx+i\omega t}]\). Then \(s^2=i\omega/\alpha\), and boundedness far from the wall selects

\[s=(1+i)/\delta,\]
\[\delta=\sqrt{2\alpha/\omega},\]
\[\theta(x,t)=Ae^{-x/\delta}\cos(\omega t-x/\delta).\]

One penetration depth produces both an amplitude factor \(e^{-1}\) and a phase lag of one radian. This is diffusive propagation: it is not an undamped travelling wave and its phase velocity depends on frequency. Temperature is continuous at the surface condition, while required input heat flux has complex amplitude \(k s A\). Its magnitude is \(kA\sqrt{\omega/\alpha}\) and its phase leads surface temperature by 45 degrees. A known heat flux and surface amplitude can therefore supply information about thermal effusivity \(\sqrt{k\rho c_p}\), distinct from diffusivity.

Extract a complex slope from two thermometer depths

For two sensors separated by known \(\Delta x\), the amplitude ratio gives \(\log(A_1/A_2)=\Delta x/\delta\) and unwrapped phase difference gives \(\Delta\phi=\Delta x/\delta\). Either yields \(\alpha=\omega(\Delta x)^2/(2\Delta\phi^2)\) when amplitude log is substituted for phase if desired. A common unknown drive amplitude and common timing phase cancel. Different sensor gains or unequal sensor time constants do not cancel automatically.

For a solid with \(\alpha=1.0\times10^{-5}\) square metres per second heated at 0.10 Hz, \(\delta=5.64\) mm. Sensors 3.0 mm apart should display amplitude ratio \(A_2/A_1=0.588\) and lag 0.532 rad, about 30.5 degrees and 0.847 s. Increasing frequency by a factor four halves penetration depth and doubles the phase lag at the same separation. The signal at depth falls more strongly and may eventually become noise-dominated although the theoretical phase difference grows.

Compare time-dependent wall-temperature inference with a steady-stage energy balance

A finite slab creates a returning thermal disturbance

The half-space solution is valid only if the far boundary is several penetration depths beyond the region of interest and if lateral heat losses or heat spreading remain small. In a slab of thickness \(L\) with its back face held at zero temperature excess, an exact periodic solution satisfying both faces is proportional to \(\sinh[s(L-x)]/\sinh(sL)\). An insulating back instead gives \(\cosh[s(L-x)]/\cosh(sL)\). Both reduce to the decaying exponential far from the back at sufficiently high frequency. Fitting the half-space law at very low frequency in a thin specimen can bias diffusivity even with perfect temperature readings.

Use the time traces to separate a material delay from a sensor delay

In the experiment the two displayed signals share a clock and a known drive amplitude. Read off the time difference between successive maxima, multiply it by angular frequency, and compare with the logarithm of the two amplitudes. Both dimensionless readings estimate depth divided by penetration depth. Increase drive frequency while keeping all geometry fixed. Thermal propagation predicts the same square-root frequency scaling for both quantities. An electronic timing offset instead contributes a phase proportional to frequency and leaves amplitude unchanged.

Open the live experiment

A thermistor embedded in a drilled hole may exchange heat with adhesive and its own leads. A simple first-order sensor with time constant one second has gain magnitude about 0.847 and phase lag about 32 degrees at one tenth hertz. That is already comparable with the nominal three-millimetre material phase in the example. Exchange the two sensors at the same holes after a baseline run. A phase shift which follows the sensor points to unequal time response; one that stays attached to the location is more consistent with diffusion or a location-dependent contact layer.

To compare a finite solid with the semi-infinite model, estimate the reflected thermal contribution from the far boundary. A disturbance travelling a distance equal to five penetration depths decays in amplitude by a factor near 0.0067. A boundary much nearer than that may noticeably alter phase and attenuation, particularly for a low drive frequency. The relevant distance for a reflected disturbance includes both the outward and return journey. Repeating the measurement at two thicknesses can reveal this effect while preserving material properties and sensor spacing.

Tutorial · Check two independent diffusivity estimates

A run at 0.20 Hz with 2.0 mm separation gives amplitude ratio 0.600 and lag 0.500 rad. Infer diffusivity separately from the two measurements and decide whether a simple phase-wrap error is likely.

Invert the attenuation and lag laws

The amplitude logarithm is 0.5108. Its estimate is \(\alpha=2\pi(0.20)(0.002)^2/[2(0.5108)^2]=9.63\times10^{-6}\) square metres per second. The phase estimate is \(1.01\times10^{-5}\) in the same units. The small difference is plausible with roughly percent-scale amplitude or phase uncertainty. A full missed wrap would add \(2\pi\) and change the inferred diffusivity dramatically. Repeat at adjacent frequencies to establish a continuous unwrapped phase rather than selecting a branch from a single fit.

Tutorial · Separate conductivity and volumetric heat capacity

A second measurement of imposed surface heat-flux amplitude and temperature identifies effusivity \(e=1.50\times10^4\) in SI units. Diffusivity is \(1.0\times10^{-5}\) square metres per second. Recover conductivity and volumetric heat capacity.

Combine the product and ratio of material properties

Write \(e=\sqrt{kC}\), \(\alpha=k/C\), where \(C=\rho c_p\). Then \(k=e\sqrt{\alpha}=47.4\) W/(m K) and \(C=e/\sqrt{\alpha}=4.74\times10^6\) J/(m³ K). Neither a temperature attenuation nor a phase slope alone identifies both quantities. Surface convection, heater thermal mass and contact resistance can distort the inferred heat actually entering the specimen, so an electrical heater input must be corrected before assigning the effusivity.

Tutorial · Design a frequency window for a finite coupon

A 20 mm specimen has its second sensor at depth 5 mm. A designer wants at least three penetration depths from that sensor to the back wall and a sensor-depth amplitude exceeding one tenth of the surface amplitude. Find an approximate interval of allowable penetration depths, then convert it to frequencies for the example diffusivity.

Satisfy attenuation and boundary constraints simultaneously

The back-wall criterion gives \(\delta\le(20-5)/3=5.0\) mm. The signal criterion gives \(e^{-5{\rm mm}/\delta}>0.10\), or \(\delta>2.17\) mm. Since \(f=\alpha/(\pi\delta^2)\), the interval is approximately 0.127 to 0.675 Hz. These are design heuristics for the stated geometry and signal requirement, not universal boundaries of diffusion theory. A finite-slab calculation at both endpoints can quantify actual back-boundary error before committing to the measurement.
