    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Choose first a closed system, then a flowing control volume. Energy balances determine work and heat, entropy restricts their conversion, and returning to the initial state creates a cycle whose performance can be measured.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      A thermal resistance sets a steady heat rate. Adding storage makes temperatures time-dependent. Phase change and moist air then require tracking composition as well as sensible energy.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Use a reference environment to measure available work. Follow water around a steam cycle, transfer energy between two moving streams, and close the course with radiative exchange between surfaces.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. Choosing a pressure ratio for a gas-turbine cycle

      Why does a bigger pressure ratio eventually reduce the work from a gas turbine? Follow one kilogram of gas round four devices, keep separate accounts for heat and work, and see what changes when the compressor and turbine are imperfect.

    2. Why a real gas cools or warms across a throttle

      A gas passes through an insulated porous plug. Its pressure drops and its measured temperature changes. Start with the energy flowing through the plug and obtain a prediction from the equation of state.

    3. When another centimetre of cooling fin stops helping

      A fin adds surface area, but heat must travel down its length before it can reach that area. Derive the temperature profile from an energy balance and quantify how much of the nominal area contributes to cooling.

    4. A moving boundary in a freezing layer

      A flat cold surface grows a layer of ice from water initially at its melting temperature. Estimate when it will reach a specified thickness, then discover how a finite heat-transfer resistance changes its early growth.

    5. Diffusion with a stationary carrier gas

      A solvent evaporates into a gas layer. Its molecules diffuse relative to the mixture, while evaporation also induces a bulk molar flow. Work through the species balances to see why the flux ceases to scale linearly with surface concentration.

    6. Reading a vapour-compression refrigerator from its four enthalpies

      A cooling machine circulates refrigerant through a compressor, condenser, valve and evaporator. Follow one kilogram around the circuit and see why compressor efficiency and liquid subcooling change the coefficient of performance by different mechanisms.

    7. Finding the vapour fraction of an equilibrium flash

      A mixture enters a separator at given overall composition, pressure and temperature. Some components prefer the vapour more strongly than others. Derive a scalar equation for the fraction that evaporates and test for a two-phase solution before iterating.

    8. Where a thermoelectric cooler rejects its heat

      A detector has to operate ten kelvin below the laboratory air temperature. A thermoelectric module beneath it can transport heat against that temperature difference when an electric current flows. Increasing the current also generates heat inside the module and warms its heat sink. Build an energy balance that predicts when additional current helps, when it costs more than it delivers and why a nominal cooling-capacity figure alone cannot specify an operating point.

    Final synthesis problem · Close the energy and exergy balances of a heat exchanger

    Applied study

    Size a heat exchanger from energy balances and determine the temperature limits of the design.

    Follow energy through an open system

    A heat exchanger recovers energy from a warm process stream to preheat an incoming cold stream. A turbine also cools a stream, yet delivers shaft work. A steady-flow control volume distinguishes the mechanisms. Neglect changes in kinetic and potential energy and let heat into the control volume and shaft work out be positive. For one inlet and one outlet the first law reads \(\dot Q-\dot W=\dot m(h_{\rm out}-h_{\rm in})\), where \(h\) is specific enthalpy. For an ideal gas with constant specific heat, or a liquid stream with constant specific heat and negligible pressure change,

    \[h_{\rm out}-h_{\rm in}=c_p(T_{\rm out}-T_{\rm in}).\]

    Draw a boundary around an insulated exchanger containing both streams. With no shaft work, any heat given up by the hot stream must appear in the cold stream. Define heat-capacity rates \(C_h=\dot m_hc_{p,h}\) and \(C_c=\dot m_cc_{p,c}\), measured in W/K. The positive rate of internal heat transfer is

    \[\dot Q_{\rm ex}=C_h(T_{h,i}-T_{h,o})=C_c(T_{c,o}-T_{c,i}).\]

    The smaller capacity rate limits the possible transfer to \(\dot Q_{\max}=C_{\min}(T_{h,i}-T_{c,i})\). Energy balance alone gives one equation for the two outlet temperatures. The missing information is how much surface and thermal conductance connect the streams.

    Derive the counterflow temperature profile

    Let \(x\) increase in the hot-stream direction. The cold stream enters at the other end. Per unit length, a strip of wall of width \(w\) transfers \(Uw(T_h-T_c)\). Assume steady operation, constant overall coefficient \(U\), no external losses and negligible axial conduction. A slice balance gives

    \[dT_h/dx=-Uw(T_h-T_c)/C_h,\]
    \[dT_c/dx=-Uw(T_h-T_c)/C_c.\]

    Both slopes are negative in this coordinate. A cold parcel warms as it moves towards decreasing \(x\).

    Take \(C_h=C_{\min}\), \(C_r=C_h/C_c\) and \(\mathrm{NTU}=UA/C_h\), with transfer area \(A=wL\). Subtract the slice balances. The local temperature difference at \(L\) divided by its value at zero is \(\exp[-\mathrm{NTU}(1-C_r)]\). Substitute the two stream balances above to eliminate the outlet temperatures. The resulting effectiveness is

    \[\varepsilon=\frac{\dot Q_{\rm ex}}{\dot Q_{\max}}=\frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{NTU}(1-C_r)}}{1-C_r e^{-\mathrm{NTU}(1-C_r)}}.\]

    With equal capacity rates the difference is constant along the wall, and the limiting effectiveness is \(\mathrm{NTU}/(1+\mathrm{NTU})\). With a vanishing area there is no exchange. These limits provide simple checks on the algebra.

    Worked example · Two water channels

    Use \(T_{h,i}=350\) K, \(T_{c,i}=290\) K, \(C_h=0.0054\) W/K and \(C_c=0.0090\) W/K. The wall and fluid films together give \(U=90\) W/(m² K) over \(A=0.045(0.002)=9.0\times10^{-5}\) m². Thus \(C_r=0.60\), \(\mathrm{NTU}=1.50\), and the exponential factor is

    \[e^{-0.60}=0.5488.\]

    The effectiveness is 0.6727 and \(\dot Q_{\rm ex}=0.6727(0.0054)(60)=0.2180\) W.

    The hot outlet is \(350-0.2180/0.0054=309.64\) K, and the cold outlet is \(290+0.2180/0.0090=314.22\) K. These outlets lie at opposite ends. The local hot-to-cold differences at the ends are \(350-314.22=35.78\) K and \(309.64-290=19.64\) K, both positive. For a body cooling in time against a reservoir, the same conductance enters a different energy balance. Study transient cooling of a solid.

    Transfer problem · A compressor and turbine

    In an ideal gas-turbine cycle, a compressor raises the pressure by a factor of 8, heat is supplied at constant pressure and the turbine expands the stream back to its starting pressure. Let air enter the compressor at 300 K and the turbine at 1200 K. Assume ideal-gas air with constant \(\gamma=1.4\) and \(c_p=1.005\) kJ/(kg K), reversible adiabatic compression and expansion, and negligible pressure losses. For either isentropic machine \(T_{\rm out}/T_{\rm in}=(p_{\rm out}/p_{\rm in})^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\).

    The pressure factor raised to the temperature exponent is

    \[8^{0.4/1.4}=1.811.\]

    The compressor therefore discharges at 543.4 K and the turbine at 662.5 K. By the steady-flow first law the turbine delivers \(c_p(1200-662.5)=540.2\) kJ/kg of work, while compression requires \(c_p(543.4-300)=244.6\) kJ/kg. The net output is 295.6 kJ/kg. Dividing by the heat input \(c_p(1200-543.4)=659.9\) kJ/kg gives an ideal thermal efficiency of 44.8%. The exhaust is hotter than the compressed air by about 119 K, so those two streams offer an opportunity for recuperation before further heat is supplied.

    Exercises

    1. Size the exchanger at fixed flow

    Keep both original capacity rates and \(U\) fixed. What area gives \(\varepsilon=0.80\)? How long would the 2 mm wide wall need to be?

    Show the sizing calculation

    Inverting the effectiveness formula gives

    \[\mathrm{NTU}=\ln[(1-\varepsilon C_r)/(1-\varepsilon)]/(1-C_r)=\ln(2.6)/0.4=2.389.\]

    Then

    \[A=\mathrm{NTU}\,C_h/U=1.43\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2},\]
    \[L=A/w=71.7\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

    The extra area supplies diminishing gains as the end temperatures approach one another.

    2. Check the second law

    For the original insulated exchanger assume negligible pressure drops and incompressible streams. Use the logarithmic entropy change of each stream to find the entropy-generation rate.

    Show the second-law balance

    At steady state \(\dot S_{\rm gen}=C_h\ln(T_{h,o}/T_{h,i})+C_c\ln(T_{c,o}/T_{c,i})\). The hot stream loses entropy and the cold stream gains more. Substitution of 309.638 K and 314.217 K gives \(6.02\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{W/K}\). The positive result is consistent with transfer through a finite temperature difference.

In this code the geometry and conductance are fixed. Changing ntu changes the computed capacity rates and inlet speeds.

    1. Set ntu=3.0 at the original capacity ratio. Both flows halve. The one-dimensional model predicts an effectiveness of 0.853 and a hot outlet near 298.8 K. The heat rate should fall, despite the greater effectiveness, since there is now less hot water to cool every second.
    2. Restore ntu=1.5 and set capacity_ratio=0.3. The hot capacity stays fixed and the cold capacity doubles. Expect a lower cold outlet (about 303.1 K) and an increased heat rate (about 0.235 W). Check both energy balances against the numerical solution.
