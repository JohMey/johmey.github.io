Cross-sectional averaging leaves a correlated flux

Consider steady fully developed laminar Poiseuille flow of mean speed \(U\) in a circular tube of radius \(a\). The axial profile is \(u(r)=2U(1-r^2/a^2)\). A dilute passive solute of molecular diffusivity \(D\) is carried by this velocity and diffuses in all directions, with no solute flux at the wall. Section-averaging its advection–diffusion equation leaves a flux \(\overline{uc}\). Splitting into mean and deviation gives \(U\bar c+\overline{u'c'}\). It is the latter covariance that disperses the pulse beyond its molecular diffusion.

At times long compared with a transverse mixing scale of order \(a^2/D\), and for a slowly varying axial average, take \(c'\simeq f(r)\partial_z\bar c\). Substituting into the leading radial balance gives

\[D r^{-1}(rf')'=u-U=U(1-2r^2/a^2).\]

Regularity at the centre and zero wall derivative give

\[f'=(U/D)(r/2-r^3/(2a^2)).\]

Integrate again and choose a constant so \(\overline f=0\). The section-integrated correlation is most easily evaluated by multiplying the radial equation by \(f\) and integrating by parts:

\[\overline{u'f}= -D\overline{(f')^2}=-\frac{U^2a^2}{48D},\]
\[D_{\rm eff}=D+\frac{U^2a^2}{48D}.\]

The negative sign means an average concentration gradient drives a net down-gradient dispersive flux. The molecular diffusivity in the denominator is not a paradox. Faster radial diffusion lets a tracer sample slow and fast streamlines more frequently and reduces long-time axial velocity memory. Taking \(D\) arbitrarily close to zero at a fixed observation time violates the long transverse-mixing requirement before the formula could diverge.

Predict a pulse width and its applicability window

For \(a=100\) micrometres, \(U=0.50\) mm/s and \(D=1.0\times10^{-9}\) square metres per second, \(Ua/D=50\) and \(D_{\rm eff}=5.31\times10^{-8}\) square metres per second. Radial mixing time \(a^2/D\) is about 10 s, corresponding to an advected length 5 mm. After 100 s, under long-tube and narrow-initial-pulse assumptions, the axial rms width is \(\sqrt{2D_{\rm eff}t}=3.26\) mm while the mean pulse has travelled 50 mm. A velocity measured from width alone is ambiguous without an independent molecular diffusivity and a check of the time regime.

A detector at a fixed axial station records concentration versus time. Converting a small spatial width to a temporal width by division by \(U\) is a local approximation when the pulse is narrow compared with distance travelled and continues broadening slowly during its passage. An absorbing detector or outlet collecting solute flux can weight the distribution differently from a nonintrusive optical section-average. State whether the instrument measures local intensity, section-average concentration or total mass flux.

Background · How does a finite injection pulse change the inferred dispersion?

A tracer valve introduces a plug of finite axial width. In the long-time regime with linear transport, the measured concentration is the convolution of the released profile with the spreading kernel. After normalising both to unit mass their variances add, just as variances add for independent displacements.

\[\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_{x,0}^2+2D_{\rm eff}t,\]
\[D_{\rm eff}=\frac{\sigma_x^2(t_2)-\sigma_x^2(t_1)}{2(t_2-t_1)}.\]

A uniform 12 mm plug has initial variance \(12^2/12=12\) mm². A one-time estimate forced through zero at \(t=10\) s therefore overestimates \(D_{\rm eff}\) by 0.60 mm²/s. Two complete spatial images can eliminate the intercept if both are taken in the Taylor-dispersion regime. A camera that clips one tail does not record the full variance.

Read dispersion from the second moment of an image

After a short pulse has entered the straight observation tube, a camera integrates fluorescence across its diameter. Subtract a background frame and flat-field the illumination, integrate across the complete width, then compute the axial centroid and variance with concentration as weights. A constant gain cancels in both normalised moments. Saturating the brightest central pixels does not cancel: it reduces the measured peak more than the tails and biases the width. A window too narrow to capture the tails produces the opposite error. Shift and widen the analysis window as the pulse travels.

Tutorial · Predict what a second pulse station should see

Two nonintrusive imaging stations observe the same well-mixed pulse after mean transit times 60 and 120 seconds under the example conditions. Estimate the increase in spatial variance. Why does differencing help with uncertainty in initial injection width?

Use the long-time variance slope

The difference is \(2D_{\rm eff}(120-60)=6.37\times10^{-6}\) square metres, or 6.37 square millimetres. An initial injection variance and any common Gaussian imaging blur add approximately constant offsets to the spatial variance and cancel in this difference. If the image point spread differs between stations or the first station is still within the shear-dominated entrance transient, cancellation fails. Check more than two stations to test linear variance growth before inferring a dispersion coefficient.

Tutorial · Compare a thinner tube at fixed volumetric rate

Halve the radius while keeping volumetric flow rate and molecular diffusivity fixed, assuming fully developed laminar flow remains valid. What happens to mean velocity, transverse mixing time, dispersive enhancement and required pressure gradient?

Keep the imposed quantity fixed

Mean speed increases by four because area is quartered. Transverse mixing time falls by four. The enhancement \(U^2a^2/(48D)\) increases by four. For Poiseuille flow at fixed volume rate the required axial pressure gradient scales as \(a^{-4}\), hence increases by sixteen. At fixed mean velocity, in contrast, the enhancement would decrease by four. A claim that narrower tubes always improve or always suppress dispersion is incomplete without naming the operating constraint.

Tutorial · Interpret a non-Gaussian short-time profile

At a distance reached in only one second the optical image shows a long leading filament and a concentration tail near the wall. Does this invalidate the measured molecular diffusivity? Sketch the limiting motion if radial diffusion were absent during that interval.

Compare the observation time with the radial mixing time

One second is only a tenth of the example transverse diffusion scale. Material near the centre moves near \(2U\), while material close to a no-slip wall is almost stationary. An initially uniform cross-section pulse therefore separates by streamline velocity, covering a broad range of axial positions. A single effective Gaussian diffusion law has not yet emerged. Compare direct advection–diffusion with an independently measured initial profile, then check the long-time variance at larger distances. Wall adsorption would create additional lag even after radial mixing and can be tested by changing wall chemistry or solute type.
