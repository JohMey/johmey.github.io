Gravity, capillarity and instability at a moving interface

A flat interface can carry waves restored by gravity and surface tension. If its two liquids or gases move at different speeds, a band of those waves can grow. Derive the ideal threshold, identify the most vulnerable wavelength at onset and distinguish growth of an instability from advection of an otherwise stable disturbance.

Impose kinematic conditions on both fluids

Consider two immiscible inviscid incompressible fluids of effectively infinite depth. The lower fluid has density \(\rho_1>\rho_2\); base velocities \(U_1,U_2\) are uniform and parallel to a horizontal interface at \(z=0\). Let its small displacement be \(\eta e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\) with \(k>0\). Assume irrotational disturbances within each layer, so their potentials satisfy Laplace's equation. Decay away from the interface gives forms \(A_1e^{kz}\) below and \(A_2e^{-kz}\) above. An interfacial vortex sheet is still allowed because tangential base velocities differ.

A material point on either side stays on the perturbed interface in the normal direction. Applying \((\partial_t+U_j\partial_x)\eta=\partial_z\phi_j\) gives

\[A_1=-i(\omega-kU_1)\eta/k,\]
\[A_2=+i(\omega-kU_2)\eta/k.\]

Pressure perturbations from linear Bernoulli include both the unsteady potential and hydrostatic change at the displaced surface. Equating pressure jump to the surface-tension curvature force yields

\[\rho_1(\omega-kU_1)^2+\rho_2(\omega-kU_2)^2=(\rho_1-\rho_2)gk+\sigma k^3 .\]

Gravity restores long waves for the stated stable density ordering, while surface tension restores short waves. Check the equal-velocity limit: adding the same velocity to both layers merely Doppler-shifts the wave frequency and cannot make a stable interface unstable.

Complete the square and locate the first unstable wavelength

Define density-weighted velocity \(\bar U=(\rho_1U_1+\rho_2U_2)/(\rho_1+\rho_2)\). The squared frequency relative to \(k\bar U\) becomes the restoring right-hand side divided by total density minus \(\rho_1\rho_2 k^2(U_1-U_2)^2/(\rho_1+\rho_2)^2\). A negative value gives one exponentially growing and one decaying branch. In that regime the real frequency still advects with \(k\bar U\), while growth rate is the square root of the negative discriminant's magnitude. A moving pattern alone is insufficient evidence of instability.

For each \(k\), the squared critical relative speed is proportional to \((\rho_1-\rho_2)g/k+\sigma k\). Differentiating gives the minimum at \(k_*=\sqrt{(\rho_1-\rho_2)g/\sigma}\). For densities 1000 and 1.2 kg/m³ and tension 0.072 N/m the onset wavelength is 17.0 mm, with ideal threshold 6.66 m/s. At 7.0 m/s the growth rate at this same wavelength is about 27.6 per second, an e-fold interval of 36 ms. The wavelength giving maximum growth above threshold can differ from the onset wavelength.

Make a quantitative observation under controlled assumptions

A suitable numerical experiment starts from the same small disturbance amplitude for a set of Fourier modes and compares early exponential growth with the dispersion relation. If an initial ripple amplitude doubles from 0.10 to 0.20 mm in 25 ms, its estimated growth is \(\log2/0.025=27.7\) per second. Measure several early time windows and ensure amplitude remains small compared with wavelength. Nonlinear saturation, finite channel depth, viscosity, actual shear-layer thickness and wave generation at an inlet all change the late or measured growth. The ideal air–water threshold is not a prediction for wind-wave onset with an arbitrary atmospheric velocity profile.

Distinguish a convected disturbance from growth at one place

An unstable wave in the temporal calculation has a real wavelength and a complex frequency. Its visible crests are carried along by the density-weighted mean flow while its envelope grows. A fixed camera viewing a short section may see a larger wave on one side than on the other without observing a temporal increase at a fixed material phase. An inlet wavemaker, reflection, a varying channel depth and convective transport can each change the spatial pattern. Follow the same crest group with a time-stamped image sequence and inspect its early amplitude before the interface becomes strongly distorted.

As a simple measurement example, suppose a tracked narrow band grows from 0.20 mm to 0.40 mm in 0.10 seconds. The inferred temporal exponent is the natural logarithm of two divided by the elapsed time, or 6.93 per second. If noise adds a constant apparent amplitude floor, fitting logarithms of the earliest images can suppress the inferred slope. Measure a quiet reference sequence and repeat the estimate over several windows. Once the slope falls substantially as amplitude increases, nonlinear saturation may have begun. Extrapolating the early exponential through the late stage then overestimates growth.

At the theoretical onset the predicted growth rate vanishes. This is the least convenient location for a finite observation interval because a weakly unstable wave may scarcely grow before it leaves the field of view. Compare a sequence of speeds on both sides of threshold and plot the squared measured growth rate against squared relative velocity at one chosen wavelength. The ideal dispersion relation gives a locally linear relation and suggests a more stable intercept estimate than judging the first visibly wavy frame.

Tutorial · Recover surface tension from the onset wavelength

At the same densities, an ideal calculation with a different liquid predicts the minimum threshold at wavelength 12.0 mm. Estimate surface tension. What independent measurement checks the model without reusing the wavelength?

Invert the onset condition

With \(k_*=2\pi/0.012=524\) per metre, \(\sigma=(998.8)(9.81)/k_*^2=0.0357\) N/m. Insert that value into the threshold-speed equation and compare with a measured critical relative velocity. Wavelength alone fixes the ratio of buoyancy and tension terms in this model; matching a second independent onset observable is a stronger test. Verify that the observed wavelength corresponds to first onset rather than the fastest mode at a substantially larger speed.

Tutorial · Distinguish finite growth from a short observation

A mode has predicted growth 5.0 per second but is visible in a camera field for only 30 ms as it advects. Estimate its amplitude increase. Could an apparently constant amplitude establish stability?

Compare residence and growth times

The predicted factor is \(e^{5(0.03)}=1.162\), just sixteen percent. If amplitude noise, illumination variation or uncertainty in initial conditions is comparable, such a record cannot distinguish weak growth from neutral propagation. Measure along the convecting packet or extend the observable path, then fit log amplitude with calibrated spatial gain. A lack of measurable growth over one short camera region places only an upper bound on its rate.

Tutorial · Suppress tension in the mathematical model

Set surface tension to zero while retaining a sharp velocity discontinuity and stable gravity. What happens to the high-wavenumber threshold and why would finite-mesh simulations disagree as they are refined?

Examine the highest resolved modes

The required squared speed then falls as \(1/k\), so any nonzero velocity jump can destabilise sufficiently short waves. In the vortex-sheet idealisation their high-\(k\) growth rate scales with \(k\). Refining a numerical mesh allows faster modes previously excluded by the grid. A physically smooth shear layer, surface tension, viscosity or other physical regularisation must be specified if the small-scale evolution is important. Numerical smoothing can control a calculation but its length scale is then part of the assumed model, not a measured material parameter.
