Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 1. Gravity, capillarity and instability at a moving interface

A flat interface can carry waves restored by gravity and surface tension. If its two liquids or gases move at different speeds, a band of those waves can grow. Derive the ideal threshold, identify the most vulnerable wavelength at onset and distinguish growth of an instability from advection of an otherwise stable disturbance.

Impose kinematic conditions on both fluids

Consider two immiscible inviscid incompressible fluids of effectively infinite depth. The lower fluid has density \(\rho_1>\rho_2\); base velocities \(U_1,U_2\) are uniform and parallel to a horizontal interface at \(z=0\). Let its small displacement be \(\eta e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\) with \(k>0\). Assume irrotational disturbances within each layer, so their potentials satisfy Laplace's equation. Decay away from the interface gives forms \(A_1e^{kz}\) below and \(A_2e^{-kz}\) above. An interfacial vortex sheet is still allowed because tangential base velocities differ.

A material point on either side stays on the perturbed interface in the normal direction. Applying \((\partial_t+U_j\partial_x)\eta=\partial_z\phi_j\) gives

\[A_1=-i(\omega-kU_1)\eta/k,\]
\[A_2=+i(\omega-kU_2)\eta/k.\]

Pressure perturbations from linear Bernoulli include both the unsteady potential and hydrostatic change at the displaced surface. Equating pressure jump to the surface-tension curvature force yields

\[\rho_1(\omega-kU_1)^2+\rho_2(\omega-kU_2)^2=(\rho_1-\rho_2)gk+\sigma k^3 .\]

Gravity restores long waves for the stated stable density ordering, while surface tension restores short waves. Check the equal-velocity limit: adding the same velocity to both layers merely Doppler-shifts the wave frequency and cannot make a stable interface unstable.

Complete the square and locate the first unstable wavelength

Define density-weighted velocity \(\bar U=(\rho_1U_1+\rho_2U_2)/(\rho_1+\rho_2)\). The squared frequency relative to \(k\bar U\) becomes the restoring right-hand side divided by total density minus \(\rho_1\rho_2 k^2(U_1-U_2)^2/(\rho_1+\rho_2)^2\). A negative value gives one exponentially growing and one decaying branch. In that regime the real frequency still advects with \(k\bar U\), while growth rate is the square root of the negative discriminant's magnitude. A moving pattern alone is insufficient evidence of instability.

For each \(k\), the squared critical relative speed is proportional to \((\rho_1-\rho_2)g/k+\sigma k\). Differentiating gives the minimum at \(k_*=\sqrt{(\rho_1-\rho_2)g/\sigma}\). For densities 1000 and 1.2 kg/m³ and tension 0.072 N/m the onset wavelength is 17.0 mm, with ideal threshold 6.66 m/s. At 7.0 m/s the growth rate at this same wavelength is about 27.6 per second, an e-fold interval of 36 ms. The wavelength giving maximum growth above threshold can differ from the onset wavelength.

Make a quantitative observation under controlled assumptions

A suitable numerical experiment starts from the same small disturbance amplitude for a set of Fourier modes and compares early exponential growth with the dispersion relation. If an initial ripple amplitude doubles from 0.10 to 0.20 mm in 25 ms, its estimated growth is \(\log2/0.025=27.7\) per second. Measure several early time windows and ensure amplitude remains small compared with wavelength. Nonlinear saturation, finite channel depth, viscosity, actual shear-layer thickness and wave generation at an inlet all change the late or measured growth. The ideal air–water threshold is not a prediction for wind-wave onset with an arbitrary atmospheric velocity profile.

Distinguish a convected disturbance from growth at one place

An unstable wave in the temporal calculation has a real wavelength and a complex frequency. Its visible crests are carried along by the density-weighted mean flow while its envelope grows. A fixed camera viewing a short section may see a larger wave on one side than on the other without observing a temporal increase at a fixed material phase. An inlet wavemaker, reflection, a varying channel depth and convective transport can each change the spatial pattern. Follow the same crest group with a time-stamped image sequence and inspect its early amplitude before the interface becomes strongly distorted.

As a simple measurement example, suppose a tracked narrow band grows from 0.20 mm to 0.40 mm in 0.10 seconds. The inferred temporal exponent is the natural logarithm of two divided by the elapsed time, or 6.93 per second. If noise adds a constant apparent amplitude floor, fitting logarithms of the earliest images can suppress the inferred slope. Measure a quiet reference sequence and repeat the estimate over several windows. Once the slope falls substantially as amplitude increases, nonlinear saturation may have begun. Extrapolating the early exponential through the late stage then overestimates growth.

At the theoretical onset the predicted growth rate vanishes. This is the least convenient location for a finite observation interval because a weakly unstable wave may scarcely grow before it leaves the field of view. Compare a sequence of speeds on both sides of threshold and plot the squared measured growth rate against squared relative velocity at one chosen wavelength. The ideal dispersion relation gives a locally linear relation and suggests a more stable intercept estimate than judging the first visibly wavy frame.

Tutorial · Distinguish finite growth from a short observation

A mode has predicted growth 5.0 per second but is visible in a camera field for only 30 ms as it advects. Estimate its amplitude increase. Could an apparently constant amplitude establish stability?

Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 2. Settling spheres, Stokes drag and a wall correction

A falling-sphere viscometer infers viscosity from terminal speed. Derive the drag on a no-slip sphere, distinguish creeping flow from merely steady motion and use radius, vessel width and orientation checks to decide what the speed experiment actually measures.

Balance pressure and viscosity around a sphere

Let a rigid sphere of radius \(a\) move with small relative speed \(U\) in a Newtonian fluid. In the sphere frame impose a uniform far flow \(U\mathbf e_z\), no slip at its surface and incompressibility. If particle Reynolds number \(\rho U(2a)/\mu\) is small, drop convective inertia, obtaining \(-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u=0\). Dimensional reasoning predicts a drag proportional to \(\mu aU\) but cannot determine its prefactor. That prefactor depends on the no-slip surface and three-dimensional geometry.

The axisymmetric Stokes solution has \(u_r=U\cos\theta[1-3a/(2r)+a^3/(2r^3)]\),

\[u_\theta=-U\sin\theta[1-3a/(4r)-a^3/(4r^3)],\]
\[p-p_\infty=-3\mu Ua\cos\theta/(2r^2).\]

Substitution verifies zero surface velocity and the uniform far limit. The pressure contribution in the streamwise direction integrates as the surface integral of \(-(p-p_\infty)\cos\theta\), giving \(2\pi\mu aU\). Tangential viscous traction supplies \(4\pi\mu aU\). Their sum gives

\[F_D=6\pi\mu aU,\]
\[C_D=\frac{24}{Re_D},\]
\[Re_D=\frac{2\rho aU}{\mu}.\]

A seemingly simpler potential-flow solution satisfying only impermeability misses the viscous traction and cannot reproduce the drag. Conversely a calculation that integrates just wall shear misses one third of this drag even at negligible inertia. The signs depend on whether one computes force of fluid on sphere or sphere on fluid; the magnitude above resists relative motion.

Balance buoyancy and find the terminal motion scale

A sphere of density \(\rho_s>\rho\) has buoyancy-corrected weight \((4\pi a^3/3)(\rho_s-\rho)g\). Equating drag and this weight yields

\[U_t=2a^2(\rho_s-\rho)g/(9\mu).\]

Take \(a=0.50\) mm, density difference 1200 kg/m³ and viscosity 0.50 Pa s. The unbounded terminal speed is 1.31 mm/s. For fluid density 1000 kg/m³, \(Re_D=0.00262\), supporting a steady creeping-flow approximation. A 30 mm timed distance then takes about 22.9 s.

A coarse transient model uses sphere inertia alone, \(m_s\dot U+6\pi\mu a U=W_{\rm eff}\), predicting relaxation time \(m_s/(6\pi\mu a)\). Actual transient hydrodynamics also contains added mass and a viscous-history contribution. Measuring a steady late portion is much safer than fitting the initial release with the simple exponential when extracting viscosity. Measure speed over successive equal intervals and check that it has become constant, away from both the release and vessel bottom.

A slow particle can still strongly interact with the container

A stationary no-slip cylindrical vessel increases drag on a sphere translating along its centreline. For small size ratio \(\lambda=a/R_{\rm tube}\), the standard leading mobility correction is \(U_{\rm tube}/U_\infty\simeq1-2.1044\lambda+\cdots\). At \(\lambda=0.05\) the measured speed is already roughly ten percent smaller. Inserting that diminished speed into an unbounded drag law biases inferred viscosity upward. A small Reynolds number does not make walls irrelevant; it only addresses inertia relative to viscosity.

Measure terminal speed from a recorded trajectory

Choose a transparent column wide enough that the sphere remains many radii from its wall in the analysed window. Convert pixel distances to physical length using a scale in the same plane as the particle centre. Refraction through a curved vessel can otherwise give different apparent distances at the wall and centre. Fit position against time over several windows beginning after release. A stable slope with changing window length supports the terminal-speed interpretation; a trend in that slope can indicate acceleration, a fluid current or proximity to the bottom.

For example, if particle and liquid densities are 1020 and 1000 kilograms per cubic metre, an absolute uncertainty of two kilograms per cubic metre in each input is small relative to either density yet large relative to their difference. Tutorial · Brownian jitter or sustained background flow?

A much smaller tracer shows fluctuations in falling displacement and an unexpected common horizontal drift. How would you estimate its settling speed without attributing every displacement increment to gravity?

Separate first and second displacement moments

Brownian motion adds variance increasing with lag in a diffusive interval but no mean drift in a homogeneous quiescent bath. Background flow adds a mean vector common to passive tracers. Measure many trajectories, estimate the fluid's mean velocity with a nearly neutrally buoyant marker and subtract it before fitting mean vertical drift. Fit the residual displacement variance separately to estimate diffusion and compare with the Einstein–Stokes prediction. A static temperature gradient can generate convection or thermophoresis, so repeat at several positions or orientations before assigning a gravity-only terminal balance.

Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 3. Why shear disperses a tracer faster than molecular diffusion

A short dye pulse in a straight tube first stretches into a filament and much later resembles a broadened one-dimensional cloud. Derive the long-time Taylor–Aris coefficient, predict the width of a measured pulse and identify the mixing time needed before fitting the familiar one-dimensional approximation.

Cross-sectional averaging leaves a correlated flux

Consider steady fully developed laminar Poiseuille flow of mean speed \(U\) in a circular tube of radius \(a\). The axial profile is \(u(r)=2U(1-r^2/a^2)\). A dilute passive solute of molecular diffusivity \(D\) is carried by this velocity and diffuses in all directions, with no solute flux at the wall. Section-averaging its advection–diffusion equation leaves a flux \(\overline{uc}\). Splitting into mean and deviation gives \(U\bar c+\overline{u'c'}\). It is the latter covariance that disperses the pulse beyond its molecular diffusion.

At times long compared with a transverse mixing scale of order \(a^2/D\), and for a slowly varying axial average, take \(c'\simeq f(r)\partial_z\bar c\). Substituting into the leading radial balance gives

\[D r^{-1}(rf')'=u-U=U(1-2r^2/a^2).\]

Regularity at the centre and zero wall derivative give

\[f'=(U/D)(r/2-r^3/(2a^2)).\]

Integrate again and choose a constant so \(\overline f=0\). The section-integrated correlation is most easily evaluated by multiplying the radial equation by \(f\) and integrating by parts:

\[\overline{u'f}= -D\overline{(f')^2}=-\frac{U^2a^2}{48D},\]
\[D_{\rm eff}=D+\frac{U^2a^2}{48D}.\]

The negative sign means an average concentration gradient drives a net down-gradient dispersive flux. The molecular diffusivity in the denominator is not a paradox. Faster radial diffusion lets a tracer sample slow and fast streamlines more frequently and reduces long-time axial velocity memory. Taking \(D\) arbitrarily close to zero at a fixed observation time violates the long transverse-mixing requirement before the formula could diverge.

Predict a pulse width and its applicability window

For \(a=100\) micrometres, \(U=0.50\) mm/s and \(D=1.0\times10^{-9}\) square metres per second, \(Ua/D=50\) and \(D_{\rm eff}=5.31\times10^{-8}\) square metres per second. Radial mixing time \(a^2/D\) is about 10 s, corresponding to an advected length 5 mm. After 100 s, under long-tube and narrow-initial-pulse assumptions, the axial rms width is \(\sqrt{2D_{\rm eff}t}=3.26\) mm while the mean pulse has travelled 50 mm. A velocity measured from width alone is ambiguous without an independent molecular diffusivity and a check of the time regime.

A detector at a fixed axial station records concentration versus time. Converting a small spatial width to a temporal width by division by \(U\) is a local approximation when the pulse is narrow compared with distance travelled and continues broadening slowly during its passage. An absorbing detector or outlet collecting solute flux can weight the distribution differently from a nonintrusive optical section-average. State whether the instrument measures local intensity, section-average concentration or total mass flux.

Background · How does a finite injection pulse change the inferred dispersion?

A tracer valve introduces a plug of finite axial width. In the long-time regime with linear transport, the measured concentration is the convolution of the released profile with the spreading kernel. After normalising both to unit mass their variances add, just as variances add for independent displacements.

\[\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_{x,0}^2+2D_{\rm eff}t,\]
\[D_{\rm eff}=\frac{\sigma_x^2(t_2)-\sigma_x^2(t_1)}{2(t_2-t_1)}.\]

A uniform 12 mm plug has initial variance \(12^2/12=12\) mm². A one-time estimate forced through zero at \(t=10\) s therefore overestimates \(D_{\rm eff}\) by 0.60 mm²/s. Two complete spatial images can eliminate the intercept if both are taken in the Taylor-dispersion regime. A camera that clips one tail does not record the full variance.

Read dispersion from the second moment of an image

After a short pulse has entered the straight observation tube, a camera integrates fluorescence across its diameter. Subtract a background frame and flat-field the illumination, integrate across the complete width, then compute the axial centroid and variance with concentration as weights. A constant gain cancels in both normalised moments. Saturating the brightest central pixels does not cancel: it reduces the measured peak more than the tails and biases the width. A window too narrow to capture the tails produces the opposite error. Shift and widen the analysis window as the pulse travels.

Tutorial · Predict what a second pulse station should see

Two nonintrusive imaging stations observe the same well-mixed pulse after mean transit times 60 and 120 seconds under the example conditions. Estimate the increase in spatial variance. Why does differencing help with uncertainty in initial injection width?

Use the long-time variance slope

Tutorial · Compare a thinner tube at fixed volumetric rate

Halve the radius while keeping volumetric flow rate and molecular diffusivity fixed, assuming fully developed laminar flow remains valid. What happens to mean velocity, transverse mixing time, dispersive enhancement and required pressure gradient?

Keep the imposed quantity fixed

Mean speed increases by four because area is quartered. Transverse mixing time falls by four. The enhancement \(U^2a^2/(48D)\) increases by four. For Poiseuille flow at fixed volume rate the required axial pressure gradient scales as \(a^{-4}\), hence increases by sixteen. At fixed mean velocity, in contrast, the enhancement would decrease by four. A claim that narrower tubes always improve or always suppress dispersion is incomplete without naming the operating constraint.

Tutorial · Interpret a non-Gaussian short-time profile

At a distance reached in only one second the optical image shows a long leading filament and a concentration tail near the wall. Does this invalidate the measured molecular diffusivity? Sketch the limiting motion if radial diffusion were absent during that interval.

Compare the observation time with the radial mixing time

One second is only a tenth of the example transverse diffusion scale. Material near the centre moves near \(2U\), while material close to a no-slip wall is almost stationary. An initially uniform cross-section pulse therefore separates by streamline velocity, covering a broad range of axial positions. A single effective Gaussian diffusion law has not yet emerged. Compare direct advection–diffusion with an independently measured initial profile, then check the long-time variance at larger distances. Wall adsorption would create additional lag even after radial mixing and can be tested by changing wall chemistry or solute type.

Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 4. Estimate wall drag by integrating a boundary-layer profile

A velocity probe can resolve the momentum missing from a boundary layer more easily than the wall shear itself. Derive an integral momentum relation, estimate drag using a polynomial profile and determine when a pressure gradient or a measurement too far from the wall changes the answer.

Define displacement and momentum thickness separately

For steady incompressible two-dimensional flow over a smooth wall, let \(U_e(x)\) denote the local speed just outside a thin attached boundary layer and \(u(x,y)\) the tangential speed inside it. Displacement thickness \(\delta^*=\int_0^\infty(1-u/U_e)dy\) measures missing volume flow per unit outer speed. Momentum thickness \(\theta=\int_0^\infty (u/U_e)(1-u/U_e)dy\) measures a different deficit. Both have dimensions of length, while their ratio \(H=\delta^*/\theta\) characterises profile shape. Neither thickness is defined by a particular percentage edge speed.

Integrating the streamwise boundary-layer momentum equation and using continuity to eliminate the edge-normal flux yields

\[\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U_e^2}=\frac{d\theta}{dx}+(2+H)\frac{\theta}{U_e}\frac{dU_e}{dx}.\]

The edge pressure gradient follows outer inviscid flow through \(dp_e/dx=-\rho U_e\,dU_e/dx\). Under uniform outer speed the equation reduces to

\[d\theta/dx=C_f/2,\]
\[C_f=\tau_w/(\rho U_e^2/2).\]

With an adverse pressure gradient the second term is negative, and an increasingly thickening layer can still have very small or negative wall shear. Applying the uniform-speed relation outside its conditions would overestimate shear near separation.

Derive an approximate flat-plate law

Assume a laminar zero-pressure-gradient layer with \(u/U=2\eta-\eta^2\) for \(0<\eta=y/\delta<1\) and outer speed beyond. The profile satisfies no slip at the wall, reaches the edge speed and has zero first derivative at its nominal outer edge. Integrating gives \(\delta^*=\delta/3\), \(\theta=2\delta/15\), while the wall gradient gives

\[\tau_w=2\mu U/\delta.\]

Substitute into the momentum integral and integrate from the leading edge:

\[\delta=\sqrt{\frac{30\nu x}{U}},\]
\[C_{f,x}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{30Re_x}},\]
\[Re_x=\frac{Ux}{\nu}.\]

The coefficient 0.730 from this approximate profile differs from the exact Blasius laminar result 0.664 while reproducing the square-root scaling. Profile choice closes an otherwise exact integrated balance and creates a measurable modelling error. It also treats the local leading-edge region outside its proper thin-layer asymptotics. Refining numerical integration of the same polynomial cannot eliminate the profile approximation.

For air with \(U=5.0\) m/s, \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\) square metres per second, \(x=0.20\) m and \(\rho=1.2\) kg/m³, \(Re_x=6.67\times10^4\) and predicted \(\delta=4.24\) mm. The approximate local \(C_f=0.00283\) gives \(\tau_w=0.0424\) Pa. If a pressure-gradient or transition changes the profile appreciably, measure the actual shape and its \(x\) variation rather than applying this laminar estimate.

Use a probe traverse to measure a momentum deficit

A small velocity probe is moved away from a smooth plate in increments of known height. Calibrate zero height and measure outer velocity independently. Convert the measured values to ratios with the outer velocity, integrate the displacement and momentum kernels using the same station coordinates, and repeat downstream. Even if the probe misses the point where the speed first reaches 99 percent of the outer value, the two integrals can remain informative. A contaminated outer-velocity estimate, however, leaves a persistent offset in the integrands across the entire outer part of the traverse.

Tutorial · Check an apparent separation claim

At one location \(H=2.5,\theta=1.0\) mm, \(U_e=4.0\) m/s, \(dU_e/dx=-8.0\) per second and \(d\theta/dx=0.010\). Infer wall shear sign. Would the same momentum-thickness growth guarantee that sign in zero pressure gradient?

The dimensionless right side is \(0.010+4.5(0.001/4)(-8)=0.0010\), still positive but only one tenth of the zero-gradient estimate. In this example both cases have positive shear, yet a slightly stronger adverse gradient would reverse the indicated sign. Tutorial · Detect a probe that misses the outer deficit

A traverse stops at the first point where measured speed reaches 97 percent of the apparent outer value. How might this bias displacement and momentum thickness, and how would you design a convergence check?

Integrate the remaining positive tail

For an attached monotone profile both integrands are positive below the true outer speed, so prematurely truncating usually underestimates both thicknesses. Near the edge their integrands become similar because \(u/U_e\simeq1\); even a small speed defect over a broad tail can contribute appreciable area. Extend the traverse and compare integrals as the cutoff rises. Independently estimate true undisturbed \(U_e\): a slight bias in that denominator can create a false nonzero tail over the entire outer integration region. Repeat at finer near-wall spacing too, separately testing quadrature and physical domain coverage.

Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 5. How long does a moving wall take to set a fluid layer in motion?

Start the top of a narrow gap suddenly and follow the velocity, volume flow and wall stresses as viscous diffusion builds a Couette profile.

Remove the final state before separating variables

Take two large, parallel plates separated by \(H\), with \(y=0\) at the fixed plate. The liquid starts from rest. At \(t=0\) the upper plate begins to move at constant speed \(U\). For an incompressible Newtonian liquid with no imposed pressure gradient, the unidirectional velocity \(u(y,t)\) has zero convective acceleration. The streamwise momentum balance on a thin slice then reduces to

\[\rho\,\partial_tu=\partial_y(\mu\,\partial_yu).\]

With constant viscosity, kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) is the momentum diffusivity.

Use \(\eta=y/H\) and \(F=\nu t/H^2\). The linear profile \(u/U=\eta\) solves the eventual steady equation and both wall conditions. Subtract it by setting \(w=u/U-\eta\). Both boundaries now require \(w=0\), which selects eigenfunctions \(\sin(n\pi\eta)\) for \(w_F=w_{\eta\eta}\). Each amplitude decays as \(\exp(-n^2\pi^2 F)\). The interior initially has \(w=-\eta\). Multiplying by a sine and integrating from zero to one gives

\[b_n=-2\int_0^1\eta\sin(n\pi\eta)\,d\eta=2(-1)^n/(n\pi).\]

Combining the parts gives

\[\frac{u}{U}=\eta+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\sin(n\pi\eta)e^{-n^2\pi^2 F}.\]

For any positive time, higher modes decay rapidly. At the initial top corner the sudden change in surface velocity conflicts with the initially resting liquid. The mathematical wall gradient there diverges as \(t\downarrow0\). A finite starting acceleration would regularize that prediction. Fluid well away from that wall sees little change until the diffusion distance, of order \(\sqrt{\nu t}\), becomes comparable with its distance to the moving surface.

Distinguish stored momentum from transmitted shear

Integrating the profile over the gap eliminates the even sine modes. The dimensionless volume flow per unit span becomes

\[q/(UH)=1/2-(4/\pi^2)\sum_{n\ {\rm odd}} e^{-n^2\pi^2 F}/n^2.\]

Differentiating at the walls gives

\[\tau_\mathrm{top}/(\mu U/H)=1+2\sum_n e^{-n^2\pi^2F},\]
\[\tau_\mathrm{bottom}/(\mu U/H)=1+2\sum_n(-1)^n e^{-n^2\pi^2 F}.\]

Here both symbols report \(\mu u_y\) evaluated in the positive \(y\) direction. The forces on the fluid act in opposite directions, so the net streamwise force per unit plate area is \(\tau_\mathrm{top}-\tau_\mathrm{bottom}\).

Set \(H=4\) mm, \(\nu=1.6\times10^{-5}\) m\(^2\)/s, \(\rho=900\) kg/m\(^3\) and \(U=0.20\) m/s. The gap diffusion time is 1 s. At 0.1 s the flow is \(0.34894 UH\), already 69.8% of its final value. The wall gradients still differ sharply, measuring 1.7843 and 0.2929 times the eventual shear of 0.72 Pa. Integrating the original momentum equation over the layer verifies that their difference accelerates the stored streamwise momentum, \(\rho\,dq/dt\).

Problem 1 · Predict the flow-settling time

Estimate when the flow reaches 99% of its final value. Why is one mode enough for this estimate?

Work through the solution

Divide the missing flow by its final value to obtain \((8/\pi^2)\sum_{n\ {\rm odd}}e^{-n^2\pi^2 F}/n^2\). Its leading term reaches 0.01 at \(F=\ln[8/(0.01\pi^2)]/\pi^2=0.44532\), or 0.44532 s with the given gap. At this time the next contributing mode contains \(e^{-9\pi^2F}/9\), below \(10^{-17}\). At much earlier times several modes reconstruct the thin moving layer.

Problem 2 · Double the gap

The plate speed and liquid stay fixed while \(H\) doubles. Compare the 99% settling time, steady shear, and steady flow per unit width in the two gaps.

Work through the solution

The dimensionless solution reaches the same settling criterion at the same \(F\). Since \(t=FH^2/\nu\), the wider gap takes four times as long, about 1.78 s. The steady gradient is \(U/H\), so its shear halves to 0.36 Pa. The steady flow is the area under a triangle of height \(U\) and width \(H\), namely \(UH/2\). It doubles to \(8.0\times10^{-4}\) m\(^2\)/s.

Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 6. How far upstream does a downstream water level matter?

A depth gauge downstream of a change in bed level measures a local state. Predict the depth one kilometre upstream by combining energy, cross-section geometry and a resistance law, then fit the roughness against independent uniform-flow readings.

Separate normal depth and critical depth

Consider steady flow at discharge \(Q\) in a long prismatic rectangular channel of width \(b\). Depth \(y\) sets area \(A=by\), wetted perimeter \(P=b+2y\) and hydraulic radius \(R=A/P\). For a long reach of uniform depth, weight down the slope balances wall shear over the wetted perimeter. If \(S_0=-dz_b/dx\) is the bed slope, the force balance gives \(\tau_0=\rho g R S_0\). A resistance relation from measurement closes this balance. In SI units the Manning convention is

\[Q=\frac{1}{n}AR^{2/3}\sqrt{S_f},\]
\[S_f=\left(\frac{nQ}{AR^{2/3}}\right)^2 .\]

Normal depth \(y_n\) solves \(S_f=S_0\). Roughness \(n\) has units s m⁻¹ᐟ³. The equivalent Chézy form \(U=C\sqrt{R S_f}\) has \(C=R^{1/6}/n\) for the same prediction. Critical depth follows from specific energy \(E=y+Q^2/(2gA^2)\) at fixed discharge. Differentiating gives \(1-Q^2 b/(gA^3)=0\) at \(y_c\). The critical condition contains no roughness. For \(b=4\) m, \(Q=6\) m³/s, \(S_0=0.001\) and \(n=0.030\), \(y_n=1.556\) m while \(y_c=0.612\) m. This bed is mild for the specified discharge.

Derive an evolution equation in space

Assume hydrostatic pressure, negligible local losses, unit velocity-energy coefficient and a sufficiently gentle surface slope. Along increasing downstream distance the energy head drops by \(S_f\) per metre. Differentiating \(H=z_b+y+Q^2/(2gA^2)\) and using \(dA/dy=b\) gives

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{S_0-S_f(y)}{1-Fr^2(y)},\]
\[Fr^2=\frac{Q^2 b}{g A^3}.\]

A downstream weir may impose a subcritical stage. For \(y>y_n>y_c\) we have \(S_f<S_0\) and \(Fr<1\), so \(dy/dx>0\). The water is deeper toward the control and approaches normal depth upstream. Integrate toward decreasing \(x\) starting at the measured stage. Replacing \(R\) by \(y\) assumes a very wide channel, which the 4 m section does not justify at these depths. Close to \(Fr=1\) the denominator signals the limit of a smooth slowly varying profile. A jump requires its own momentum treatment.

Separate resistance calibration from the boundary condition

The notebook first fits one roughness to four synthetic uniform discharge measurements and then holds it fixed for two downstream controls. The fitted \(n=0.03001\) gives depths of 1.605 m and 1.745 m one kilometre upstream of outlet depths of 1.8 and 2.2 m. The downstream 0.4 m difference has decayed substantially and still leaves an observable upstream effect.

OneSimulation experiment · Two downstream stages produce different upstream profiles at the same calibrated roughness

Run the notebook with OneSimulation 0.2.0, NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib. Change the input data and compare your run with the recorded result.

"""Infer Manning roughness from uniform flow, then predict a subcritical backwater."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from onesim.numerics.nonlinear import LeastSquares

width, slope, discharge, gravity = 4.0, 0.001, 6.0, 9.81
measured_depth = np.array([0.7,0.85,1.0,1.15])  # m
def section(depth):
    area = width*depth
    return area, area/(width+2*depth)
def uniform(depth, roughness):
    area, radius = section(depth)
    return area*radius**(2/3)*np.sqrt(slope)/roughness
measured_flow = uniform(measured_depth,0.030) + np.array([0.02,-0.03,0.02,-0.01])
fit = LeastSquares().solve(lambda n: uniform(measured_depth,n[0])-measured_flow,
                           [0.025], bounds=([0.010],[0.08]))
roughness = fit.x[0]
def depth_gradient(x, y):
    area,radius = section(y)
    friction = (roughness*discharge/(area*radius**(2/3)))**2
    froude_squared = discharge**2*width/(gravity*area**3)
    return (slope-friction)/(1-froude_squared)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4,3.7), layout="constrained")
print(f"fitted Manning n = {roughness:.5f} s/m^(1/3)")
for boundary in [1.8, 2.2]:
    x = np.linspace(0,-1000,250)
    sol = solve_ivp(depth_gradient,[0,-1000],[boundary],t_eval=x,rtol=1e-9,atol=1e-10)
    assert sol.success
    print(f"outlet {boundary:.1f} m: depth 1 km upstream = {sol.y[0,-1]:.3f} m")
    ax.plot(-sol.t,sol.y[0],label=f"Outlet depth {boundary:.1f} m")
ax.set(xlabel="Distance upstream from control (m)",ylabel="Flow depth (m)")
ax.legend()
plt.show()

Why two depth measurements do not always identify roughness

Near normal depth, the water surface changes slowly. Bed-slope bias, discharge error and roughness can produce similar residuals over a short reach. Calibrating against uniform measurements at more than one discharge gives an independent constraint before fitting the backwater.

Momentum, motion and transport in fluids · 7. How can a constant-area duct choke?

A nozzle reaches a sonic state by changing area. A straight pipe can approach the same Mach number through friction or through heating. The two cases conserve different combinations of stagnation properties.

Choose the wall process before eliminating variables

Take a steady one-dimensional calorically perfect gas with \(G=\rho u\) constant, no shaft work and constant pipe area. Write \(M=u/\sqrt{\gamma RT}\), \(h_0=c_pT+u^2/2=c_pT_0\). An adiabatic wall fixes \(T_0\) even when wall friction creates entropy. A frictionless wall which supplies heat changes \(T_0\) while preserving the axial momentum flux \(p+\rho u^2\). Measure static and stagnation variables independently to distinguish these processes.

Integrate the frictional momentum loss

For hydraulic diameter \(D\) define the Darcy factor by \(\tau_w=f_D\rho u^2/8\). A slice has perimeter-to-area ratio \(4/D\), so momentum balance is \(dp+\rho u\,du+f_D\rho u^2dx/(2D)=0\). Differentiate constant \(G\) and \(T_0\) to obtain \(d\rho/\rho=-du/u\) and \(dT/T=-(\gamma-1)M^2du/u\). Substitute in \(dp/p=d\rho/\rho+dT/T\) and \(dM/M=du/u-dT/(2T)\):

\[\frac{f_D dx}{D}=\frac{4(1-M^2)}{\gamma M^3[2+(\gamma-1)M^2]}\,dM .\]

Integrate from inlet \(M_1\) to outlet \(M_2\). Defining the remaining distance to sonic conditions by \(F(1)=0\) gives

\[\frac{f_D L}{D}=F(M_1)-F(M_2),\]
\[F(M)=\frac{1-M^2}{\gamma M^2}+\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\ln\!\frac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}.\]

For air with \(\gamma=1.4\), \(M_1=0.4\), \(M_2=0.7\), the two function values are 2.30849 and 0.20814. With \(D=0.050\) m and constant \(f_D=0.020\), the required length is 5.25 m. The maximum length at the stated inlet condition is 5.77 m. A longer duct requires different inlet conditions or mass flow. A quoted Fanning coefficient is one quarter of this Darcy coefficient.

Remove friction and allow heat transfer

The constant momentum flux becomes \(p(1+\gamma M^2)=p^*(1+\gamma)\), where the star denotes the sonic state on this same momentum line. Use mass conservation and the ideal-gas law to get \(T/T^*=[(1+\gamma) M/(1+\gamma M^2)]^2\). Multiply by the ratio between stagnation and static temperatures at each state to obtain

\[\frac{T_0}{T_0^*}=\frac{2(1+\gamma)M^2[1+(\gamma-1)M^2/2]}{(1+\gamma M^2)^2}.\]

For the same inlet and outlet Mach numbers this ratio rises from 0.52903 to 0.90850. If \(T_{01}=350\) K, the outlet stagnation temperature is 601.1 K. The heat added per unit mass for \(c_p=1005\) J/(kg K) is approximately 252 kJ/kg. The sonic-line maximum \(T_0^*=661.6\) K places an upper bound on additional heat at the fixed inlet state. In the friction case the stagnation temperature remained at 350 K throughout.

Why both subsonic cases accelerate

Mass flux per area is fixed. The change of pressure and temperature required by momentum and wall conditions reduces density on these subsonic paths. Velocity rises. Supersonic and subsonic branches require explicit tracking when inverting a sonic-reference relation.

Exercises on distinguishing mechanisms

A frictional pipe with the worked inlet already carries gas at Mach 0.7 at a pressure tap. How much more straight pipe reaches the mathematical choking limit?

Worked solution

The remaining friction length is \(D F(0.7)/f_D=0.520\) m. This calculation assumes the same Darcy factor up to the sonic state.

A well-insulated pipe test gives a large stagnation-pressure loss but nearly identical measured stagnation temperatures. Could a frictionless Rayleigh model with steady net heating explain both quantities?

Worked solution

Net heating necessarily raises \(h_0\). The nearly unchanged stagnation temperature supports adiabatic frictional loss or another adiabatic irreversible process. Static temperatures alone do not distinguish acceleration from wall heat.
