A capillary that fills horizontally and climbs vertically

A transparent tube draws a wetting liquid from a large reservoir. Combine a static meniscus balance with a viscous pressure-drop law to predict a transient, recover a property from timing and determine where the simplest startup model fails.

Problem sheet 7 · Integrative project

1 · Predict the final height before timing the rise2 · Turn a pressure drop into a filling-time law3 · Predict a measurable fraction of the vertical rise4 · Recover viscosity independently of contact angle

1 · Predict the final height before timing the rise

The inner radius is 0.200 mm, surface tension 0.072 N/m, contact angle 30°, density 1000 kg/m³ and dynamic viscosity 0.010 Pa s. Neglect gas viscosity and assume the radius is small relative to the capillary length. Calculate the pressure head supplied by the meniscus and the final vertical rise.

Calculate pressure and static elevation

A spherical-cap meniscus gives

\[\Delta p=2\gamma\cos\theta/r=624\,\mathrm{Pa}.\]

Hydrostatic pressure balances it at \(H=\Delta p/(\rho g)=63.6\) mm. Viscosity does not enter this equilibrium balance. A liquid with the same density and surface properties but twice the viscosity therefore arrives at the same final level under these assumptions.

Read the 0.20 mm capillary radius on the equilibrium curve

2 · Turn a pressure drop into a filling-time law

First lay the tube horizontally. Denote the filled length by x. In developed laminar flow the mean speed is \(r^2\Delta p/(8\mu x)\). With constant tube area, the speed of the meniscus is that mean speed. Predict the time to fill 50 mm and the effect of doubling viscosity.

Integrate the resistance that grows with the filled length

Equating \(\dot x\) to the mean liquid speed gives

\[x\dot x=r^2\Delta p/(8\mu).\]

Integrating from negligible initial length gives

\[x^2=\gamma r\cos\theta\,t/(2\mu).\]

The time for 50 mm is

\[2(0.010)(0.050)^2/[0.072(0.00020)\cos30^\circ]=4.01\,\mathrm{s}.\]

Doubling viscosity doubles the filling time. At x=25 mm the prediction gives about 0.0125 m/s, with Reynolds number based on diameter about 0.50. Laminar resistance is then plausible. Extrapolating its infinite startup speed to zero length would neglect entrance inertia and the formation of the velocity profile.

3 · Predict a measurable fraction of the vertical rise

Turn the tube upright. Only \(\Delta p-\rho g x\) remains available for viscous losses. Derive a time formula for \(0<x<H\). Find the time to H/2 and compare with the time needed to reach that same length horizontally.

Solve the separable moving-front equation

Write \(K=\rho gr^2/(8\mu)=0.004905\) m/s, which gives

\[\dot x=K(H/x-1).\]

Integration of \(dt=x\,dx/[K(H-x)]\) yields

\[t(x)={1\over K}\left[H\ln{H\over H-x}-x\right].\]

At half height this becomes

\[(H/K)(\ln2-1/2)=2.50\,\mathrm{s}.\]

Horizontal filling over the same 31.8 mm takes 1.62 s. The logarithm diverges as \(x\to H\), so a laboratory definition such as “within 1 mm of the final level” is necessary when reporting an arrival time.

4 · Recover viscosity independently of contact angle

Suppose a fresh tube of the same radius gives static rise 60.0 mm and fills 40.0 mm horizontally in 3.00 s. Infer the viscosity without fitting a contact angle. Which pair of measurements lets you test whether static and dynamic wetting agree?

Eliminate the unknown capillary pressure

The vertical rise yields

\[\Delta p=\rho gH=588.6\,\mathrm{Pa}.\]

Horizontal integration gives \(\mu=r^2\Delta p\,t/(4x^2)=0.0110\) Pa s. Compare this inferred value with independent viscometry and record the meniscus angle during travel. A moving contact angle different from the static value changes the pressure available during the horizontal run. A time trace at several lengths also tests the assumed \(x^2\propto t\) scaling.
