Add inlet pre-swirl

An impeller has \(U_1=9,\ U_2=18\) m/s, exit swirl 12 m/s and positive inlet swirl 3 m/s. Fluid mass flow is 4 kg/s. Find Euler work, power transfer and head. What changes if inlet swirl is reversed at unchanged exit conditions?

Reveal the worked solution

Specific work is \(18(12)-9(3)=189\,\mathrm{J/kg}\); ideal power transfer is 756 W and head 19.27 m. With reversed inlet swirl the term becomes positive in the work difference, giving 243 J/kg, 972 W and 24.77 m. The change reflects a larger increase in the angular momentum of the fluid.

Reconstruct the velocity triangle

At an ideal radial impeller exit the blade speed is 24 m/s, radial fluid velocity 4 m/s and backward blade angle 45°. Calculate exit swirl, absolute speed magnitude and work at zero inlet swirl. If delivered head is 40 m, find the ratio of measured to ideal head.

Reveal the worked solution

The blade-relative tangential component is \(-4\cot45^\circ=-4\,\mathrm{m/s}\), giving \(V_{\theta2}=20\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The absolute speed is

\[\sqrt{20^2+4^2}=20.40\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

Work is \(24(20)=480\,\mathrm{J/kg}\), or 48.93 m ideal head. The measured head ratio is

\[40/48.93=0.818.\]

Compare two back pressures

Air at 400 kPa absolute and 360 K discharges through an ideal converging nozzle of exit area 30 mm². Use \(\gamma=1.4\), \(R=287\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\). Are back pressures 180 and 250 kPa choked? Find mass flow in the choked case and exit speed in the unchoked case.

Reveal the worked solution

Critical pressure is \(400(2/2.4)^{3.5}=211.3\,\mathrm{kPa}\); 180 kPa chokes, 250 kPa does not. At choking \(T^*=300\,\mathrm K\), \(\rho^*=2.45\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(a^*=347.2\,\mathrm{m/s}\), hence \(\dot m=0.0256\,\mathrm{kg/s}\). At 250 kPa,

\[T=360(250/400)^{0.4/1.4}=314.8\,\mathrm K.\]

The energy balance with \(c_p=1004.5\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\) gives

\[V=\sqrt{2c_p(360-314.8)}=301\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

Find the operating point and account for the lost head

A pump between two open tanks supplies head Hₚ = 24 − 0.020q² metres, where q is measured in litres per second. The upper surface is 4.0 m higher, and pipe losses give hL = 0.030q² metres. With water density 1000 kg/m³ and pump efficiency 0.75, find volume flow, electrical input assuming an ideal motor, and the share used to raise the water. Then throttle the discharge by adding 0.030q² of valve loss.

Worked solution

Equalising pump and system head gives 0.050q² = 20, or q = 20.0 L/s. The head is 16.0 m, split into 4.0 m elevation and 12.0 m pipe loss. The fluid receives ρgQH = 3.14 kW and the assumed motor supplies 4.19 kW. Only 0.785 kW raises gravitational potential. It is one quarter of the fluid power and about 18.8% of the input.

With the valve, 0.080q² = 20 gives 15.81 L/s and a pump head of 19.0 m. Pipe and valve each dissipate 7.50 m. The new fluid power is 2.95 kW and useful lift power is 0.620 kW. Increasing the system resistance reduces flow even as the operating head rises.

An energy check sums elevation, pipe and valve contributions at the new flow. Extrapolating a fixed 75% efficiency beyond this example would need a measured efficiency curve. Also, tank surface velocities are negligible here; a discharge jet carrying appreciable kinetic energy would add an endpoint term.
