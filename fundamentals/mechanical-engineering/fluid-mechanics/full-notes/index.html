Chapter 1 · Velocity fields, flow rates and conservation of mass

Read local acceleration from a velocity field and use an integral mass balance to connect inlet, outlet and storage.

Look at a point or follow a parcel

At a contraction in an otherwise steady pipe, a parcel of water speeds up. A probe fixed at the throat reports a constant speed throughout the experiment. Both observations are compatible because a field can vary along the path even when it has no explicit time dependence. Eulerian measurements describe velocity at a place. Following a marked parcel gives its changing position and velocity.

Write the velocity field as \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\). During \(dt\) the parcel advances through \(d\mathbf x=\mathbf u\,dt\). The chain rule along that trajectory gives \(\mathbf a=\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\). The first term is local acceleration and the second is convective acceleration. In steady one-dimensional flow \(a_x=u\,du/dx\). A streamline is tangent to the instantaneous velocity. In a steady flow pathlines and streamlines coincide, whereas in an unsteady field the curve traced by a parcel can differ from a single snapshot’s streamlines.

Count the mass crossing a surface

Enclose part of a fluid system in a fixed control volume. A small surface element with outward normal \(\mathbf n\) passes volume \((\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)dA\,dt\) in time \(dt\). Multiply by density and add over the whole boundary. Normal inflow is negative, reducing the net outward flux. Conservation of mass is

\[\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\rm CV}\rho\,dV+\int_{\rm CS}\rho\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=0.\]

In steady operation the mass flow rate entering all inlets equals that leaving all outlets. A one-inlet, one-outlet reduction with uniform cross-sectional density gives \(\rho_1 A_1\bar u_1=\rho_2 A_2\bar u_2\). The mean velocity is the area integral of the normal component divided by area. A centreline speed is seldom that mean. For variable density the volume rate can change along a duct while mass rate remains constant.

Accelerate water in a contraction

Water of density 1000 kg/m³ enters a straight circular nozzle 60 mm in diameter with mean speed 1.50 m/s. The outlet is 30 mm in diameter. The area ratio is four, giving \(Q=1.50\pi(0.060)^2/4=4.24\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\), \(\dot m=4.24\) kg/s and \(\bar u_2=6.00\) m/s. These speeds already determine the change in streamwise momentum flux if both profiles are nearly uniform. The pressure and wall forces responsible for that change require a momentum balance.

Suppose along a centreline segment of length 0.30 m the speed is approximately linear from 1.50 to 6.0 m/s. Its gradient is 15 s⁻¹. At a local speed of 3.0 m/s the convective acceleration is 45 m/s². The transit time through the segment is \(\int dx/u=\ln(6/1.5)/15=0.0924\) s. Dividing distance by the arithmetic mean of the two speeds gives a different answer because a particle spends a larger fraction of its time in the slower region.

Let the control volume fill

A vented tank receives 4 L/s and initially delivers 2 L/s through a controlled outlet. In a constant horizontal tank section of 0.50 m² the water level rises at \((0.004-0.002)/0.50=0.0040\) m/s. The storage term remains essential even if both inlet and outlet flow meters have steady readings. A free-surface tank can therefore act as an independent calibration: the measured volume increase over a timed interval should equal the integrated difference between inflow and outflow. If the outlet is driven by the changing hydrostatic head, its rate will cease to be constant and the balance becomes a differential equation for the depth.

Check continuity directly in a velocity field

A planar test field \(u=ax,\ v=-ay\) with constant \(a\) stretches a fluid square in one direction and shortens it in the other. Since \(\partial_xu+\partial_yv=a-a=0\), its area is preserved locally. A particle released at \(x_0,y_0\) follows \(x=x_0e^{at},\ y=y_0e^{-at}\); the product \(xy\) remains constant. This explicit trajectory shows how an incompressible parcel can deform and accelerate even when the velocity at every fixed point is independent of time.

Count mass through a control surface that moves

A flow meter on a translating piston does not see the same volume rate as a stationary meter at the same instant. Start with a material system, whose total mass is constant. Over a short time \(dt\), a patch of control surface of area \(dA\) moves a distance \(\mathbf v_s\cdot\mathbf n\,dt\) in its outward normal direction. Fluid moves a distance \(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n\,dt\) in that direction. The volume swept through the moving patch is the difference times \(dA\). Converting this volume into mass gives the moving-control-volume form of continuity:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\int_{CV(t)}\rho\,dV+\int_{CS(t)}\rho(\mathbf v-\mathbf v_s)\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=0 .\]

The storage term differentiates the mass inside the actual changing volume. A boundary following a parcel has zero relative normal velocity there, even when the parcel and the boundary move quickly in the laboratory. On an impermeable wall this no-penetration condition applies to its normal movement; the mass balance alone imposes no constraint on tangential slip. Setting \(\mathbf v_s=0\) recovers the fixed-volume formula. Making the entire surface material removes the flux term and recovers conservation of the system mass.

For constant density, differentiating the geometric volume and using the outward convention gives \(dV/dt=\int\mathbf v_s\cdot\mathbf n\,dA\). Substitution shows how the familiar \(\int\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=0\) can hold for an instantaneous surface even while its enclosed volume is growing. Relative flux and volume storage must be paired consistently.

Interpret a flow reading on a moving piston

A horizontal cylinder of area \(0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}\) has an inlet in its fixed left end and a small outlet passing through a piston at its right end. The piston moves to the right at 0.040 m/s while the cylinder stays full of incompressible water. A fixed inlet meter measures \(0.00070\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\). The volume occupied by water grows at \(0.010(0.040)=0.00040\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\). Therefore the relative outlet flux through the piston is \(0.00070-0.00040=0.00030\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\), or 0.30 L/s.

The outlet area is \(2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) and flow exits to the right. Its mean velocity relative to the piston is 1.50 m/s, while its laboratory velocity at the moving outlet is \(1.50+0.040=1.54\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Multiplying that laboratory velocity directly by the outlet area gives 0.308 L/s, too high for the outlet flow measured by an instrument carried on the piston. If the piston moved to the right at 0.070 m/s, the entire inlet rate would fill the new volume and relative outlet discharge would be zero. These conclusions assume no leakage past the piston rim and that the outlet area is part of the moving boundary; a fixed downstream collection nozzle would require its own control surface and storage terms.

A downstream weighing tank that travels with the piston would collect 0.30 kg per second at water density 1000 kg/m³. This is the discharge relative to that apparatus. The distinction is measurable and does not depend on any pressure-loss assumption for the outlet.

Can a steady flow accelerate a fluid particle?

Let the cross-sectional area in a straight nozzle be \(A(x)/A_0=1-a x/L\). In an ideal steady quasi-one-dimensional incompressible flow, continuity gives \(u=U_0/(1-a x/L)\). The material acceleration is \(u\,du/dx\) although every stationary probe measures a constant velocity.

Chapter 2 · Hydrostatic pressure and forces on submerged surfaces

Derive pressure from a body-force balance and locate the resultant force on a vertical gate.

Balance a stationary fluid element

A diver experiences pressure from all directions, while a sluice gate feels a net force mainly from one side. Pressure is a scalar state variable. The force on a surface element is directed opposite its outward normal and has magnitude \(p\,dA\). The net force on a real gate also depends on what presses on its back face. For a fluid at rest there is no viscous shear. Take a thin horizontal slab of area \(A\) and thickness \(dz\), with \(z\) upward. Its vertical force balance is \(p(z)A-p(z+dz)A-\rho gA\,dz=0\), or \(dp/dz=-\rho g\).

For constant density this integrates to \(p=p_s+\rho gh\) at depth \(h\) below a free surface of pressure \(p_s\). A vented water surface has atmospheric absolute pressure. Gauge pressure subtracts that same atmospheric pressure at every point. It is convenient for net forces when the other face is exposed to the same atmosphere. A sealed gas space with appreciable excess pressure adds a uniform load component to the hydrostatic triangle.

Use two separate integrals

For a flat vertical gate, add horizontal pressure forces to get \(F=\int_A p_g\,dA\), where \(p_g\) is the pressure difference across it. To find the force’s depth \(h_R\), match its moment: \(Fh_R=\int_A h p_g\,dA\). With a vented free surface on one side and air on the other, this gives \(F=\rho gA\bar h\) and \(h_R=\int_A h^2dA/(A\bar h)\). Decomposing the second moment into centroidal and parallel-axis pieces gives

\[h_R=\bar h+\frac{I_G}{A\bar h}.\]

Here \(I_G\) is the area’s second moment about its horizontal centroidal axis. Its SI unit is m⁴. The second term is positive: lower parts of a vertical gate carry more force per unit area, moving the resultant below the area centroid.

Calculate hinge moment before actuator force

A 1.20 m wide rectangular gate extends from the water surface to depth 1.80 m. Its horizontal hinge is at the surface. At the bottom the gauge pressure is \(1000(9.81)(1.80)=17.7\) kPa. The triangular pressure profile integrates to \(F=\rho g bH^2/2=19.1\) kN. The resultant lies at \(2H/3=1.20\) m beneath the hinge, producing a moment of 22.9 kN·m. A horizontal actuator at the bottom must pull with magnitude \(22.9/1.80=12.7\) kN to balance this water moment. This excludes gate weight and friction at the hinge.

If the whole gate is submerged another metre with both ends still separated from the same air pressure on the back, its pressure diagram gains a uniform rectangle. That additional resultant acts at the gate centroid. It increases hinge moment, while the centre of pressure of the total load moves closer to the centroid measured relative to the gate. Simply adding one metre to the original lever arm would confuse a depth measured from the surface with a moment arm measured from the hinge.

Include stratification and buoyancy

In layered immiscible liquids, integrate \(\rho g\) through each layer. Pressure remains continuous across a flat interface when capillary effects are negligible, while its depth gradient changes. For a closed submerged object the resultant pressure force is upward with magnitude equal to the displaced fluid’s weight. To see this, replace the body by the fluid that would occupy its volume. In static equilibrium surface pressure must balance that fluid’s weight; the same surface pressure acts on the original object. Buoyancy concerns the net force on a closed surface. It cannot replace a hinge-moment calculation on an open gate.

Read a two-fluid differential manometer

Two water taps at the same elevation connect to opposite legs of a manometer containing a heavier immiscible liquid of density 2500 kg/m³. If the heavy-fluid interfaces differ in height by 40 mm, following a hydrostatic path gives a pressure difference \((2500-1000)g(0.040)=589\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The tap above the lower heavy-fluid interface has the larger pressure. The water columns matter even though both taps sit at the same elevation.

Derive a restoring moment for a floating body

Equality of weight and buoyancy establishes vertical equilibrium of a floating body, but does not determine whether a small roll is restored. Consider a long prismatic pontoon with vertical sides floating at rest in a liquid of uniform density. Let the displaced volume be \(\nabla\), its centroid the centre of buoyancy \(B\), and the centre of gravity \(G\) on the upright symmetry axis. For a small heel angle \(\theta\), a strip of waterplane area \(dA\) at transverse coordinate \(y\) gains a submerged slice of volume approximately \(y\theta\,dA\) on one side and loses one on the opposite side.

The first moment of these changed wedges shifts the centre of buoyancy sideways by \(\Delta y_B=\theta\int y^2dA/\nabla=\theta I_{\rm wp}/\nabla\). Thus the line of buoyancy in the tilted configuration intersects the original symmetry axis at a point \(M\), the transverse metacentre, located \(BM=I_{\rm wp}/\nabla\) above \(B\) to first order. The initial righting lever is \(GM\sin\theta\simeq GM\,\theta\), and the corresponding restoring moment magnitude is \(WGM\theta\) when \(GM>0\).

The waterplane second moment here is taken about the longitudinal heel axis; it is not the structural bending second moment of the vessel wall. A wide waterplane can increase stability without changing the weight because its outer strips contribute strongly to \(I_{\rm wp}\). A centre of gravity above the centre of buoyancy can still be stable if it remains below the metacentre.

Measure a pontoon’s centre of gravity by shifting a weight

A rectangular pontoon 4.0 m long and 2.0 m wide floats with 0.40 m draft in freshwater. Its displaced volume is \(4(2)(0.4)=3.20\,\mathrm{m^3}\), so the total supported mass is 3200 kg. The centre of buoyancy lies 0.20 m above the keel. Its waterplane moment is \(4(2^3)/12=2.667\,\mathrm{m^4}\), giving \(BM=2.667/3.20=0.833\,\mathrm m\) and \(KM=1.033\,\mathrm m\) above the keel.

Shift a pre-existing 100 kg test weight transversely by 0.40 m without changing its height. A settled heel of 2.0° balances the imposed heeling moment against buoyancy. For small heel the more accurate inclination-test expression is \(GM=md/(M_{\rm tot}\tan\theta)=100(0.40)/[3200\tan2^\circ]=0.358\,\mathrm m\). The inferred unshifted vertical centre of gravity is \(KG=1.033-0.358=0.675\,\mathrm m\). Raising a 400 kg installed component by 0.80 m would raise the combined centre by \(400(0.80)/3200=0.100\) m and reduce initial \(GM\) to 0.258 m. A slack liquid surface inside the pontoon or partial emergence of its deck edge changes the small-heel model; either can invalidate an extrapolation of the measured initial restoring moment to large roll angles.

For the original centre of gravity the initial rotational stiffness is \(WGM=3200(9.81)(0.358)=11.24\,\mathrm{kN\,m/rad}\). A loading estimate from a small known heeling moment can therefore serve as a separate check on the shifted-weight experiment. This stiffness is hydrostatic; rolling inertia and hydrodynamic damping are still required to predict a free-roll period or decay.

Place the hinge on a fully submerged vertical gate

A rectangular vertical gate is 2 m tall and of constant width. Integrate hydrostatic gauge pressure over its area. The centre of pressure is \(h_c+I_c/(A h_c)\), so the offset below the centroid shrinks as the gate is submerged more deeply.

Problem sheet 1 · Mass balances and hydrostatic loading

Determine the correct area, depth and system boundary before evaluating the force or volume flow.

1. Audit a branching pipeline

A 100 mm pipe supplies water at mean speed 1.00 m/s. One 60 mm branch carries 2.50 L/s; the remaining water leaves through a 50 mm branch. (a) Find the inlet flow and both branch speeds. (b) If discharge from the smaller branch is actually 4.00 L/s, infer an accumulation or leak rate. (c) What observation distinguishes temporary storage from a persistent leak?

Close the volume budget

The inlet carries \(0.25\pi(0.10)^2(1)=7.85\) L/s. The nominal branch speeds are \(0.00250/[\pi(0.06)^2/4]=0.884\) m/s and \(0.005354/[\pi(0.05)^2/4]=2.73\) m/s. At the measured discharge the unaccounted rate is \(7.85-2.50-4.00=1.35\) L/s. A rising storage level or an expanding system volume could temporarily account for it. At constant inventory a persistent imbalance points to another flow path or meter offset.

2. Locate pressure with two liquids

A vented tank has 0.40 m of oil of density 800 kg/m³ over 0.80 m of water. (a) Find gauge pressure at the interface and at the base. (b) Find the net horizontal force on a 0.50 m wide vertical wall segment extending only over the water depth. (c) Why is a single triangular pressure diagram across the water segment insufficient?

Add the uniform and triangular loads

Interface pressure is \(800(9.81)(0.40)=3.14\) kPa. At the base add \(1000(9.81)(0.80)=7.85\) kPa to obtain 10.99 kPa. Assuming atmospheric pressure on the back, the average pressure on the water segment is \((3.14+10.99)/2=7.06\) kPa. On 0.40 m² this gives 2.83 kN. Oil adds the uniform 3.14 kPa across the water segment. Using only the water triangle would omit 1.26 kN.

3. Move a gate hinge

A vertical gate is 1.0 m wide, extending from 0.50 to 1.50 m below a vented water surface. There is air behind it. (a) Find resultant force and its depth. (b) Find the water moment about a hinge at its lower edge. (c) Compare with a force applied at the centroid.

Match the force and its moment

The centroid depth is 1.0 m and the area is 1.0 m², giving 9.81 kN. The centroidal second moment is \(1(1)^3/12=0.0833\) m⁴. The resultant acts at \(1.0833\) m depth, with lower-hinge arm \(1.50-1.0833=0.4167\) m, giving 4.09 kN·m. Using the centroid would give 4.91 kN·m, too large because the greater pressure near the lower edge shifts the resultant towards that hinge.

Chapter 3 · Control-volume momentum and forces on fittings

Use the velocity vector, gauge pressure and a consistent force boundary to find the support load on a pipe bend.

Momentum crosses the boundary with the mass

A pipe bend can exert a substantial force on its supports even when its inlet and outlet speeds agree. Momentum has direction. Turning a stream requires force and gives the fitting an equal and opposite load. Enclose the fluid inside the fitting in a fixed control volume. The external forces include wall traction, pressure on the control surfaces and body forces such as gravity.

A surface element transports mass per second \(\rho(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)dA\). Multiply by the local velocity to obtain momentum per second. Newton’s second law applied to the control volume reads

\[\sum\mathbf F_{\rm ext}=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\rm CV}\rho\mathbf u\,dV+\int_{\rm CS}\rho\mathbf u(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,dA.\]

For a steady fitting with nearly uniform inlet and outlet velocities this reduces to a sum of outgoing \(\dot m\mathbf u\) minus incoming \(\dot m\mathbf u\). Use one fixed set of axes even as pipe directions change. Pressure always pushes into the control volume, whereas the flux carries the direction of the local velocity, including its sign on the chosen axis.

Include the end pressures explicitly

Take a horizontal bend turning water from the positive \(x\) direction to the positive \(y\) direction. The inlet and outlet areas are each 0.010 m² and \(Q=0.040\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\). Their speeds are both 4.0 m/s and \(\dot m=40\) kg/s. The net outward momentum flux vector is \((-160,+160)\) N. Let the gauge pressures be 90 kPa at inlet and 80 kPa at outlet. Pressure pushes +900 N in \(x\) through the inlet and −800 N in \(y\) through the outlet.

Let \(\mathbf W\) be the fitting’s wall force on the enclosed fluid, with external atmospheric effects accounted for consistently using gauge pressures. Then \(\mathbf W+(900,-800)=(-160,160)\) N, giving \(\mathbf W=(-1060,960)\) N. The fluid’s net gauge load on the fitting is the opposite, \((1060,-960)\) N, of magnitude 1.43 kN. Supports plus connected pipework must provide the opposite force for a stationary fitting, in addition to carrying the weight of hardware and fluid. The strongest contribution here stems from the pressure terms, despite the conspicuous turn in velocity.

Recover momentum from a nonuniform profile

A flow meter usually measures \(Q\), not \(\int u^2dA\). At a plane normal to a unidirectional profile define \(\beta=\int u^2dA/(A\bar u^2)\). Then the axial momentum rate is \(\beta\rho Q\bar u\). Uniform flow gives \(\beta=1\). For a developed laminar circular pipe \(u=2\bar u[1-(r/R)^2]\); inserting \(dA=2\pi r\,dr\) in the integral gives \(\beta=4/3\). The largest velocities carry a disproportionate share of momentum. If one bend cross-section admits strong swirl or backflow, resolve vector fluxes instead of applying a single profile coefficient.

Recognise an open-stream boundary

A free jet at atmospheric pressure strikes a fixed flat plate normal to its axis and turns into a thin sheet that leaves sideways. Neglecting rebound and friction far from the impact, the outgoing axial momentum flux is zero. For water flowing at 10 m/s through a 20 mm diameter nozzle, \(\dot m=3.14\) kg/s, so the axial force on the plate is 31.4 N. Both the incoming and outgoing free-stream control surfaces have zero gauge pressure. The plate force then follows entirely from the change of momentum. A nozzle bolted to a pressurised pipe would require different pressure forces even if it produced exactly the same free jet.

A jet catches a moving vane

Consider a single flat vane moving steadily in the jet direction at speed \(u\). In the vane frame the arriving volume rate is \(A(V-u)\) and the incoming streamwise relative velocity is \(V-u\). If the jet spreads tangentially across the face, the resulting normal force is \(\rho A(V-u)^2\). For water, \(A=2.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), \(V=10\) m/s and \(u=4\) m/s, this gives 7.2 N. Its mechanical power on the moving vane is \(Fu=28.8\,\mathrm W\). Using the stationary volume rate would count fluid parcels that the moving vane never catches.

Derive the two depths of a hydraulic jump

A hydraulic jump converts shallow fast open-channel flow into deeper slow flow over a short distance. Turbulent mixing dissipates mechanical energy, so applying an inviscid energy balance between the two uniform sections would miss its defining loss. Instead choose a control volume bounded by sections before and after the roller, the horizontal bed and the free surface. Approximate the velocity as uniform and the pressure distribution as hydrostatic at the two end sections. Atmospheric pressure cancels, and over the short reach assume the integral of bed friction is small compared with the pressure and momentum forces.

In a rectangular channel per unit width let the volume rate be \(q\), depths \(y_1,y_2\) and speeds \(v_i=q/y_i\). The horizontal pressure resultant per width is \(\rho g y_i^2/2\). Equating pressure force difference to momentum-flux change gives

\[\frac{y_1^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{g y_1}=\frac{y_2^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{g y_2}.\]

One solution is the unchanged depth. Factoring out \(y_2-y_1\) for the nontrivial solution gives \(q^2=g y_1 y_2(y_1+y_2)/2\). With upstream Froude number \(Fr_1=v_1/\sqrt{g y_1}\), the downstream-to-upstream depth ratio becomes \([\sqrt{1+8Fr_1^2}-1]/2\). The deeper downstream branch occurs when \(Fr_1>1\). As \(Fr_1\to1\), the ratio tends to unity and the finite jump disappears.

Calculate the energy dissipated in a channel transition

A horizontal test channel carries water at \(y_1=0.20\,\mathrm m\) and \(v_1=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Its unit-width discharge is \(q=0.80\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) and Froude number is \(4/\sqrt{9.81(0.20)}=2.86\). The depth ratio is about 3.57, giving \(y_2=0.714\,\mathrm m\) and \(v_2=0.80/0.714=1.12\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The downstream Froude number is about 0.42. These are conjugate depths for the specified momentum balance; the tailwater has to support the downstream depth for a stationary jump to sit at the chosen location.

Using specific mechanical head \(E=y+v^2/(2g)\), the inlet and outlet give 1.015 and 0.778 m respectively. The loss is approximately 0.237 m of water, or \(1000(9.81)(0.80)(0.237)=1.86\,\mathrm{kW}\) dissipated per metre of channel width. Substitution of the momentum relation into the energy difference gives \(\Delta E=(y_2-y_1)^3/(4y_1y_2)\), which is positive for the physical jump and approaches zero rapidly for a weak jump. This is a useful cross-check on a measured depth pair. If measurements are taken within the roller, pressure may be nonhydrostatic and velocity markedly nonuniform; using those sections in the simple end-state formula can produce an apparent energy or momentum imbalance.

Another discrimination comes from comparing the two depths with an upstream energy curve. At \(q=0.80\) m²/s, a downstream depth giving exactly the upstream energy would have to satisfy \(y+0.03262/y^2=1.015\). The deeper root is about 0.981 m, appreciably deeper than the momentum-predicted 0.714 m. Pairing those equal-energy depths would require an additional horizontal force or a different channel geometry to satisfy momentum. A smooth reversible change over a channel hump can conserve specific head while changing velocity and depth; a jump on a flat bed instead conserves the appropriate momentum function while dissipating head. Laboratory measurements of both levels are sufficient to distinguish the two idealisations.

Chapter 4 · Bernoulli head, losses and pump duty

Build a mechanical-energy balance between two stations and distinguish a recoverable pressure change from irreversible loss.

Integrate the acceleration along a streamline

A nozzle can lower static pressure while increasing speed. A valve can also lower pressure, yet recover little useful speed downstream. To separate the mechanisms, start from a steady, inviscid streamline in constant-density flow. Project Newton’s equation along its tangent \(s\), with gravity acting downward. The acceleration in that direction is \(u\,du/ds\), giving \(\rho u\,du=-dp-\rho g\,dz\). Integration between points on that streamline gives \(p/\rho+u^2/2+gz=\) constant.

The three terms have units J/kg. Dividing by \(g\) measures the same energies as equivalent lengths of fluid column, called pressure head, velocity head and elevation head. A rise in speed at constant elevation can be paid for by a drop in static pressure even in lossless flow. Bringing such a stream to rest along a short, nearly lossless streamline would convert velocity head back into stagnation pressure. An irreversible loss reduces that recoverable total.

Add distributed losses and machinery

For a steady incompressible stream between stations with approximately uniform profiles, a pump supplies head \(H_p\) and a turbine extracts head \(H_t\). Viscous losses \(h_L\) appear as mechanical energy converted into internal energy. With endpoints numbered in the flow direction the energy balance is

\[\frac{p_1}{\rho g}+\frac{\bar u_1^2}{2g}+z_1+H_p=\frac{p_2}{\rho g}+\frac{\bar u_2^2}{2g}+z_2+H_t+h_L .\]

For a noticeably nonuniform profile, multiply each velocity-head term by the kinetic-energy correction \(\alpha=\int_Au^3dA/(A\bar u^3)\). It differs from the momentum correction because energy flux weights the cube of velocity. Long straight pipes are commonly modelled with the Darcy loss \(f_D(L/D)\bar u^2/(2g)\) and fittings with \(K\bar u^2/(2g)\). State which pipe diameter supplies the reference velocity for each coefficient.

Lift water between open tanks

Two large vented reservoirs differ in elevation by 8.0 m. Water flows at \(Q=0.010\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) through 40 m of 80 mm diameter pipe. Use a supplied Darcy friction factor 0.025 and total fitting coefficient 3.0, including entrance and exit losses. Both free surfaces have atmospheric pressure and negligible velocity. The pipe area is \(5.03\times10^{-3}\) m², giving mean speed 1.99 m/s and velocity head 0.202 m. Friction and fittings dissipate \([0.025(40/0.080)+3.0]0.202=3.13\) m. The pump therefore supplies 11.13 m head.

The hydraulic power is \(\rho gQH_p=1.09\) kW for \(\rho=1000\) kg/m³. A pump with efficiency 0.70 at this duty requires 1.56 kW of shaft power. Elevation work alone would predict 0.785 kW hydraulic, and electrical input would also require the motor and drive efficiencies. These differences reflect which boundary is used for the energy balance.

Find the intersection with the pump characteristic

If coefficients are treated as fixed, the system head here has the form \(H_{\rm sys}=8+BQ^2\). Doubling the flow quadruples the losses, not the elevation rise. A real pump produces a head that depends on flow rate and rotational speed. Its steady operating point is where available head equals required head. Partially closing a valve increases \(B\); it does not directly prescribe a particular flow. Readjusting pump speed changes its characteristic instead. In a real design recheck friction factor and efficiency at the new rate. At low Reynolds number, where laminar friction factor varies inversely with speed, the distributed loss becomes linear rather than quadratic in flow.

Check the absolute pressure on a pump inlet

A pump inlet sits 3.0 m above an open water reservoir. At a particular duty the suction-line speed is 2.0 m/s and the loss between reservoir and inlet is 0.60 m. Atmospheric pressure is 101 kPa. Applying the mechanical energy balance upstream of the impeller gives absolute inlet pressure \(101000-1000(9.81)(3.0+0.60)-1000(2.0)^2/2=63.7\,\mathrm{kPa}\). If the supplied vapour pressure is 2.3 kPa, this tapping remains above it. Pressure may fall further inside the impeller. Evaluating cavitation margin requires the pump’s suction requirement at the actual duty and an absolute pressure reference; a negative gauge pressure at the tapping alone does not establish cavitation.

Locate the critical depth from a constrained energy minimum

In open-channel flow the atmosphere fixes the free-surface pressure, while the depth and speed can both adjust. Consider a rectangular channel of constant width with slowly changing bed elevation \(z_b\). Define the discharge per unit width \(q=vy\) and use the bed as the local elevation datum. With nearly hydrostatic pressure and approximately uniform section velocity, the mechanical head relative to the bed is \(E(y)=y+q^2/(2gy^2)\). This specific energy is finite at a given depth but diverges both for a very shallow stream at fixed discharge and for a very deep stream.

At fixed \(q\), differentiate with respect to \(y\): \(dE/dy=1-q^2/(gy^3)\). The stationary depth is \(y_c=(q^2/g)^{1/3}\), its Froude number is one, and the second derivative \(3q^2/(gy^4)\) is positive. The minimum value is \(E_c=3y_c/2\). For any specific energy above this minimum two positive depths are possible. One is deep and slow and the other shallow and fast. One must know the upstream flow regime and the physical control to choose a branch.

Across a smooth low hump with negligible loss the total head \(z_b+E\) remains constant. Raising the bed reduces the specific energy available at the crest. On the deep branch depth decreases towards critical, whereas on the shallow branch depth increases towards it. These opposite trends follow from the sign of \(dE/dy\). Neither branch can pass below \(E_c\) at the prescribed discharge.

Predict when a raised bed changes the upstream water level

Water enters a wide rectangular flume at \(y=0.80\,\mathrm m\) and \(v=1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), giving \(q=0.80\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) and upstream specific energy \(E_1=0.8510\,\mathrm m\). The critical depth is \((0.64/9.81)^{1/3}=0.4025\,\mathrm m\), so \(E_c=0.604\,\mathrm m\). The largest ideal bed rise that leaves the upstream state unchanged is \(0.851-0.604=0.247\,\mathrm m\). For a smaller 0.15 m bed rise the crest must have \(E=0.7010\,\mathrm m\). Solving \(y+0.03262/y^2=0.7010\) on the deep branch gives approximately 0.614 m, which can be checked by substitution. Speed increases to about 1.30 m/s while the stream remains subcritical.

Raise the crest instead by 0.30 m. The unchanged upstream level would provide only 0.551 m specific energy, less than the minimum. If discharge stays fixed, the upstream surface must rise until at least \(E_1=0.30+0.604=0.904\,\mathrm m\) is available. The subcritical solution upstream is then approximately 0.860 m depth, a rise of about 60 mm. The physical limitation is the minimum section energy for the prescribed flow, not a loss of mass. Adding appreciable crest losses would require an even larger upstream head to sustain the same discharge.

A depth gauge right at a critical crest is relatively insensitive as an energy measurement: the first derivative \(dE/dy\) vanishes there. Conversely, inferring depth from a noisy measured head becomes sensitive near the minimum because the inverse slope becomes large. Measurements upstream and downstream help identify which of the two nearby depth branches is physically present.

Chapter 5 · Viscous stresses, duct resistance and wall-driven flow

Derive a developed velocity profile from momentum balance and distinguish a moving-wall contribution from a pressure-driven contribution.

Transport momentum between neighbouring layers

Push two parallel plates past one another with liquid in the gap. Even at constant velocity the moving plate requires force. In a Newtonian fluid the tangential shear stress on a plane parallel to the motion is \(\tau_{xy}=\mu\,du/dy\). Dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) has units Pa·s. Divide it by density to obtain kinematic viscosity \(\nu\), an indicator of how rapidly momentum spreads through the fluid. Viscous resistance depends on a velocity gradient; uniform translation of the entire fluid would not create such stress.

Take two long horizontal plates separated by \(H\). Far from inlet and side walls assume constant density, constant viscosity and steady fully developed laminar motion \(u=u(y)\). The lower plate is fixed and the upper moves at speed \(U\). Represent the constant driving pressure gradient by \(G=-dp/dx\), positive for pressure falling downstream. A differential slab balances the downstream pressure force with the change in shear, giving \(0=G+d\tau_{xy}/dy=G+\mu u''\).

Impose the two wall velocities

The first integration gives \(\mu u'=-Gy+C_1\). Integrating again and imposing no slip \(u(0)=0,\ u(H)=U\) determines the constants. The result is the sum of a straight wall-driven profile and a parabolic pressure-driven one:

\[u(y)=U\frac{y}{H}+\frac{G}{2\mu}y(H-y),\qquad \frac{Q}{b}=\int_0^H u\,dy=\frac{UH}{2}+\frac{GH^3}{12\mu}.\]

Superposition here follows because the reduced equation and boundary conditions are linear in these inputs. It is not a universal property of the full nonlinear fluid equations. A negative \(G\) represents an adverse pressure rise and can produce a reverse-flow layer close to the stationary wall even when the moving wall drags fluid forward elsewhere.

Predict the pressure a moving surface can hold

An oil film has \(\mu=0.050\) Pa·s, \(H=1.0\) mm and \(U=0.20\) m/s. The wall-driven flux per unit width is \(UH/2=1.0\times10^{-4}\) m²/s. An adverse pressure gradient sufficient to reduce net flux to zero must satisfy \(G=-6\mu U/H^2=-60\,000\) Pa/m. Over 50 mm, pressure rises by 3.0 kPa. Zero net transport does not make the oil stationary.

Set \(s=y/H\) and insert the zero-flux gradient. The velocity is \(u/U=3s^2-2s\), which changes sign at \(s=2/3\) inside the film. The velocity gradient at the upper moving wall is \(4U/H=800\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so the wall supplies shear of magnitude 40 Pa. Even though the pressure-driven and wall-driven volume rates cancel, the moving surface still performs work, dissipated through the internal shear field.

Connect a local solution to a network

In a horizontal circular tube with fixed wall, axisymmetry changes the area that carries shear. Balancing forces on a cylinder of radius \(r\) gives \(|\tau|=Gr/2\). Integrating the velocity gradient with no slip yields \(u=G(R^2-r^2)/(4\mu)\) and \(Q=\pi R^4\Delta p/(8\mu L)\). Thus a straight developed pipe behaves as a hydraulic resistance \(8\mu L/(\pi R^4)\) in Pa·s/m³. Series resistances carry the same \(Q\) and their pressure drops add. Parallel branches share pressure drop and their flows add. The analogy requires consistent units and a laminar regime; short entrance sections and strongly accelerating junctions may add losses outside the fully developed solution.

Express pipe resistance as a friction factor

For a circular pipe of diameter \(D=2R\), the developed laminar solution gives \(\Delta p=32\mu L\bar u/D^2\). Define the Darcy friction factor by \(\Delta p=f_D(L/D)\rho\bar u^2/2\). Equating these expressions produces \(f_D=64/\mathrm{Re}_D\). For 2 m of 10 mm pipe with mean speed 0.10 m/s, water viscosity 0.001 Pa s and density 1000 kg/m³, the Reynolds number is 1000 and the pressure drop is 64 Pa. A friction factor listed in the Fanning convention is one quarter of the Darcy value, so identify the convention before substituting a tabulated number into a loss calculation.

Derive the profile of a shear-thinning liquid in a pipe

A Newtonian viscosity inferred in one duct may give poor predictions for a suspension or polymer solution at another rate. In a simple power-law model the shear stress magnitude is \(\tau=K|\dot\gamma|^n\), with consistency \(K\) in Pa sⁿ and index \(n\). A liquid with \(n<1\) has apparent viscosity decreasing with shear rate. Assume this law is supported over the shear range being sampled, flow is steady, axisymmetric, laminar and fully developed, and the circular pipe has no wall slip. The axial force balance is independent of rheology: at radius \(r\), \(\tau=Gr/2\) for driving gradient \(G=-dp/dx>0\).

Since the positive downstream velocity decreases towards the wall, write \(-du/dr=(Gr/2K)^{1/n}\). Integrate from radius \(r\) to the stationary wall at \(R\), then integrate the result over the circular cross-section to obtain

\[u(r)=\frac{n}{n+1}(G/2K)^{1/n}(R^{1+1/n}-r^{1+1/n}),\qquad Q=\frac{\pi n}{3n+1}(G/2K)^{1/n}R^{3+1/n}.\]

Setting \(n=1,\ K=\mu\) gives the parabolic profile and \(Q=\pi GR^4/(8\mu)\). For \(n<1\) the velocity is flatter towards the centre and the relationship between pressure gradient and flow rate is nonlinear. The ideal power law can imply unrealistic apparent viscosity near zero shear at the axis; a fitted model with finite limiting viscosities is preferable when measurements resolve those regimes.

Infer a pressure demand from wall shear

A model resin follows \(n=0.50,\ K=0.80\,\mathrm{Pa\,s^{0.5}}\) in the operating range. A 2.0 m pipe of radius 5.0 mm experiences a pressure drop of 8.0 kPa, giving \(G=4000\,\mathrm{Pa/m}\) and wall shear \(\tau_w=GR/2=10.0\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The wall shear rate is \((10/0.80)^2=156.25\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Writing the mean speed in terms of this wall value gives \(\bar u=nR|\dot\gamma_w|/(3n+1)=0.15625\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The corresponding flow is \(\pi(0.005)^2(0.15625)=1.227\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\), about 0.736 L/min.

To double flow in this same pipe, the power-law relation requires the gradient to rise by \(2^n=\sqrt2\). The new pressure drop is 11.3 kPa, and wall shear rate doubles to 312.5 s⁻¹. A linear resistance model extrapolated from the original measurement would predict 16 kPa. Conversely at fixed gradient a 10% radius increase multiplies rate by \(1.1^{3+1/n}=1.61\), a stronger dependence than the Newtonian fourth power. Verify the resulting shear rate still falls in the tested rheological range, and check entrance length and temperature. Temperature changes produced by viscous dissipation can otherwise mimic a shear-dependent viscosity.

Two well-spaced steady pressure tests can independently infer the unknown exponent. At fixed pipe geometry, \(\ln Q=(1/n)\ln\Delta p+\text{constant}\). If pressure drop rises from 8.0 to 12.0 kPa while flow rises from 0.736 to 1.66 L/min, the log slope is \(\ln(1.66/0.736)/\ln(12/8)\simeq2.00\), giving \(n\simeq0.50\). Repeating the higher-pressure point after cooling checks whether heating caused hysteresis between runs. Using an inlet pressure transducer alone would also include a changing downstream pressure; both end pressures or a differential gauge are needed to identify the flow law from this experiment.

The pressure taps should bracket the same developed length at every setting because entrance losses may change differently with flow.

How does a local pressure gradient accelerate a fluid particle?

A duct solution generally begins by cancelling terms in the momentum balance. To decide which terms can be cancelled, follow a small volume of fluid. Its acceleration includes the change in the velocity at its present location and the change as it moves into a different part of the velocity field. Applying the chain rule along its trajectory gives \(D\mathbf u/Dt=\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\).

Consider the two faces normal to the first coordinate axis. Their net surface force per unit volume in direction \(i\) approaches \(\partial_x\sigma_{ix}\) as the face separation tends to zero. Summing the three face pairs and adding weight gives \(\rho Du_i/Dt=\sum_j\partial_j\sigma_{ij}+\rho g_i\). For an incompressible Newtonian liquid of constant viscosity \(\mu\), insert \(\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\mu(\partial_j u_i+\partial_i u_j)\). The cross derivative sums to \(\partial_i(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)=0\). The remaining terms are the local equations

\[\rho[\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u]=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u+\rho\mathbf g,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.\]

Take a horizontal slice through an ideal planar stagnation region, away from confining walls. The steady field \(u=ax,\ v=-ay\) satisfies continuity. Every velocity component is linear, so its Laplacian vanishes. Nevertheless the material acceleration is \((a^2x,a^2y)\). Integrating the two horizontal gradients gives \(p(x,y)=p_0-\rho a^2(x^2+y^2)/2\), where \(p_0\) is the stagnation-point pressure at this elevation. For water at \(1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \(a=4\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(y=0.10\,\mathrm m,\ x=0\), pressure is 80 Pa below \(p_0\). Fluid entering along positive \(y\) moves towards the origin but accelerates in positive \(y\): it slows as pressure rises. At the stagnation point itself both velocity and acceleration vanish. Specifying \(p_0\) fixes the pressure datum. The velocity gradients determine the spatial change.

Chapter 6 · Dynamic similarity, boundary layers and model tests

Choose dimensionless groups for a fluid experiment and determine which full-scale predictions a scale model can support.

Compare competing mechanisms

A scale model can reproduce a shape exactly and still display a different flow pattern. A fast oil stream and a slow water stream may behave similarly if the relevant dimensionless groups match. To see which groups enter, choose a typical length \(L\), speed \(U\), time \(L/U\) and pressure change \(\rho U^2\) in the incompressible Newtonian momentum equation. Divide by the inertial scale \(\rho U^2/L\). The coefficient multiplying viscous diffusion becomes \(1/Re\), where \(Re=\rho UL/\mu\).

If gravity must be retained, its coefficient is \(1/Fr^2\), with \(Fr=U/\sqrt{gL}\). A free water surface makes this particularly important because gravity restores disturbances. For a fully submerged shape in a constant-density fluid, gravity can often be absorbed into hydrostatic pressure, leaving the Reynolds number dominant. Extra effects can introduce more groups, for example roughness divided by length or oscillation frequency times \(L/U\). Choose groups to match the phenomenon being measured.

Derive a near-wall length scale

Consider a smooth flat plate parallel to a uniform stream with a negligible pressure gradient. In a laminar boundary layer, momentum is advected downstream on a scale \(U^2/x\) and diffuses across the shear layer on a scale \(\nu U/\delta^2\). Balancing them gives \(\delta\sim\sqrt{\nu x/U}\). The ratio \(\delta/x\sim Re_x^{-1/2}\) may be small even though the tangential shear force from the layer matters to the machine. The similarity solution places the 99% speed level near \(5x/\sqrt{Re_x}\), and the local skin-friction coefficient \(\tau_w/(\rho U^2/2)\) near \(0.664/\sqrt{Re_x}\).

Doubling speed at a fixed station multiplies the local Reynolds number by two. The dimensionless friction coefficient falls by \(1/\sqrt2\), but dynamic pressure grows fourfold. The dimensional shear therefore rises by \(2^{3/2}\). This distinction matters when reading plots of nondimensional test results: a lower plotted coefficient can accompany a larger actual force.

Scale a submerged component

A streamlined full-size probe of length 0.60 m moves at 2.0 m/s in water with \(\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). A geometrically similar third-scale model in the same water needs 6.0 m/s for equal Reynolds number \(1.2\times10^6\). Assume matching surface finish in relative terms, sufficiently small blockage, the same flow regime and no free-surface effect. Then the dimensionless drag \(C_D=F_D/(\rho U^2A/2)\) should agree. Since the model’s reference area is one ninth and its speed squared is nine times as large, its drag is equal to the prototype drag in this example.

If only 2.0 m/s can be reached in the test tank, the model Reynolds number is one third of full size. An observed transition, separation point or drag coefficient may then shift. Document this mismatch rather than scaling the raw measured drag by area alone. Matching a single group does not fix every boundary condition, especially the upstream disturbance level that can trigger transition.

Scale a different experiment with the same geometry

For a one-ninth length model of a surface vessel, matching Froude number requires \(U_m=U_p/3\). In the same water its Reynolds number relative to the prototype is \((1/3)(1/9)=1/27\). Exact Froude and Reynolds similarity cannot both hold at that length and speed with the same fluid. Gravity-wave resistance can be compared at matched Froude number, while viscous skin friction needs a separate correction. Report both dimensionless numbers with the measured force and its reference area. That allows a reader to distinguish the physical part of the scaling argument from assumptions used for extrapolation.

Recover a dimensional frequency from a model

Matching a dimensionless frequency can transfer an unsteady effect. For an oscillation of frequency \(f\) in a stream of speed \(U\) past a length \(L\), the Strouhal number is \(fL/U\). A 0.10 m model in a 3 m/s stream oscillating at 6 Hz gives 0.20. If relevant geometry and governing dimensionless parameters are matched, a 0.50 m prototype at 2 m/s would oscillate at \(0.20(2)/0.50=0.80\) Hz. Frequency alone cannot be rescaled by the geometric factor when the reference speeds differ.

Determine when surface tension prevents gravity-wave similarity

A reduced-scale flume can match gravity and inertia by matching Froude number. The air–water interface also has surface tension \(\sigma\). A disturbance with characteristic length \(L\) and speed \(U\) has inertial pressure scale \(\rho U^2\), gravity scale \(\rho gL\), and capillary pressure scale \(\sigma/L\). The ratios produce \(\mathrm{Fr}^2=U^2/(gL)\) and \(\mathrm{We}=\rho U^2L/\sigma\). Dividing the latter by the former gives the Bond number \(\mathrm{Bo}=\rho gL^2/\sigma\). Choosing model speed to match Froude number leaves Bond number determined by geometry and material properties. With the same liquid and gravity, reducing length by ten reduces Bond number by one hundred.

For a more precise test use the small-amplitude, inviscid deep-water dispersion relation \(\omega^2=gk+\sigma k^3/\rho\) for wave number \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). The gravity term arises from surface elevation, while the capillary pressure perturbation is proportional to surface curvature \(k^2\eta\). The ratio of capillary to gravity restoring contributions is \(\sigma k^2/(\rho g)\). Equal contributions occur at wavelength \(2\pi\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho g)}\). The appropriate length in this ratio is \(1/k\); omitting the \(2\pi\) when using wavelength changes the inferred correction substantially.

Check a wave-frequency prediction at two geometric scales

For water use \(\sigma=0.072\,\mathrm{N/m},\ \rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). The crossover wavelength is \(2\pi\sqrt{0.072/9810}=0.0170\,\mathrm m\). Consider a prototype wave of wavelength 0.40 m. Its wave number is 15.71 m⁻¹ and the capillary-to-gravity ratio is \(0.072(15.71)^2/9810=0.00181\). Its frequency is approximately \(\sqrt{9.81(15.71)(1.00181)}/(2\pi)=1.98\,\mathrm{Hz}\), essentially the gravity prediction.

A geometrically similar one-tenth model uses wavelength 0.040 m, wave number 157.1 m⁻¹ and capillary-to-gravity ratio 0.181. Pure gravity scaling would predict frequency \(\sqrt{10}(1.98)=6.26\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Including capillarity gives \(\sqrt{9.81(157.1)(1.181)}/(2\pi)=6.79\,\mathrm{Hz}\), about 8.5% higher. A frequency discrepancy need not indicate a clock error or a viscosity error. The model has moved into a region where a restoring force previously negligible is perceptible. Deep water also requires \(kh\) large at both scales; shallow flume depth adds a separate \(\tanh(kh)\) factor. Enlarging the model or choosing a different measured observable may be preferable to enforcing incompatible similarity conditions.

A useful comparison can be made by timing wave packets as well as crests. The phase speed is \(c_p=\omega/k\). The group speed of a narrow wave packet follows from differentiating the dispersion relation, \(c_g=[g+3\sigma k^2/\rho]/(2\omega)\). Their ratio is \([1+3B]/[2(1+B)]\), where \(B=\sigma k^2/(\rho g)\). In the prototype example it is 0.502, close to the deep-water gravity value of one half. In the short model it is 0.653. Thus scaling time to match one measured crest frequency still would not make packets travel relative to their crests in exactly the same way. A video showing both individual crest motion and arrival of a packet envelope provides experimental evidence for the altered mechanism.

Surface contamination is another experimental variable: an altered surface tension changes the capillary correction without changing either geometric length or gravity-wave Froude number.

Chapter 7 · Vorticity, circulation and swirling flow

Distinguish rotation of a fluid element from an orbiting streamline and use radial momentum balance to predict a free-surface shape.

Distinguish a particle orbit from local spin

Drop a small paddle wheel into a swirling tank. Its centre can travel around the tank axis whether or not the wheel itself spins relative to distant reference axes. To measure local rotation, consider two short perpendicular material lines through the same fluid element. In a planar velocity field \((u,v)\), an initially horizontal line rotates at \(\partial v/\partial x\), and an initially vertical line rotates at \(-\partial u/\partial y\), both with counterclockwise positive. Their mean is the local angular velocity, one half the out-of-plane vorticity.

\[\omega_z=\partial v/\partial x-\partial u/\partial y,\qquad \Gamma=\oint_C\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf l=\iint_A\omega_z\,dA .\]

The second relation is Stokes’ theorem. The curve encloses \(A\) with consistent orientation and the velocity must be regular throughout that surface. Circulation is measured in square metres per second. In a region with zero vorticity a closed curve can still enclose a singular or otherwise excluded core; then the area needed for the usual zero-circulation argument does not lie entirely in that region.

Compare two angular velocity distributions

In axisymmetric swirl with only tangential velocity \(v_\theta(r)\), circulation on a circular path is \(2\pi r v_\theta\). Dividing its change between neighbouring rings by the annular area gives \(\omega_z=(1/r)\,d(rv_\theta)/dr\). A forced vortex rotates like a rigid body, \(v_\theta=\Omega r\), with vorticity \(2\Omega\). At radius 0.10 m and angular speed 8 rad/s, its velocity is 0.80 m/s and the circular-path circulation is 0.503 m²/s.

In a free-vortex exterior \(v_\theta=K/r\); the vorticity is zero at every regular point there although the streamlines are circular. If its velocity at the same radius is also 0.80 m/s, then \(K=0.080\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). At radius 0.20 m its speed falls to 0.40 m/s, while in the forced vortex the speed rises to 1.60 m/s. Both flows have the same circulation at the original radius. A second measuring circle distinguishes them without an instrument that directly senses local shear.

Recover pressure and free-surface rise

For steady axisymmetric circular streamlines without radial or vertical motion the radial acceleration points inward with magnitude \(v_\theta^2/r\). Radial momentum balance requires \(dp/dr=\rho v_\theta^2/r\). Vertical equilibrium gives \(\partial p/\partial z=-\rho g\). Along a free surface at uniform ambient pressure their contributions cancel, so \(dz/dr=v_\theta^2/(gr)\). For rigid rotation, integration gives \(z(r)-z(0)=\Omega^2r^2/(2g)\).

A cylindrical open tank of radius 0.20 m rotating at 8 rad/s has a predicted wall-to-centre rise of \(64(0.20)^2/[2(9.81)]=0.1305\,\mathrm m\). If no water spills, volume conservation places its former flat level halfway between the new centre and rim elevations for this paraboloid. Each shifts by about 65 mm in opposite directions. A nearly full tank will spill instead, and the average-level constraint then changes. A free vortex produces a different surface profile and its singular axis must be replaced by a physical core.

Construct irrotational flow around a cylinder

Outside a vortex core or boundary layer, a two-dimensional incompressible flow can often be approximated as irrotational. In a simply connected region irrotationality permits a velocity potential with \(\mathbf v=\nabla\phi\), and continuity then requires \(\nabla^2\phi=0\). For a circular cylinder of radius \(a\) in a uniform stream \(U\) along the positive \(x\) direction, try \(\phi=(Ar+B/r)\cos\theta\). The two radial factors solve Laplace’s equation for the angular first harmonic. Matching the distant uniform potential \(Ur\cos\theta\) gives \(A=U\). No penetration requires radial velocity \(\partial_r\phi=0\) at \(r=a\), giving \(B=Ua^2\).

\[v_r=U(1-a^2/r^2)\cos\theta,\qquad v_\theta=-U(1+a^2/r^2)\sin\theta .\]

The boundary has zero normal speed but a nonzero tangential speed. This is consistent with an inviscid slip condition. It does not satisfy the no-slip condition for an actual viscous wall, so predicted surface pressure is meaningful only insofar as outer flow remains attached and nearly irrotational. In a real cylinder wake, separation breaks the fore–aft symmetry of this ideal solution.

Applying steady Bernoulli relative to the uniform upstream stream gives the surface pressure coefficient \(C_p=1-4\sin^2\theta\). Its values change considerably around the cylinder even though integrating the pressure forces yields zero net drag and lift by symmetry. Zero potential-flow drag does not imply uniform pressure.

Add circulation and predict unequal side pressures

A mathematical cylinder excludes its interior from the fluid. Superpose a free-vortex velocity \(\Gamma/(2\pi r)\) in the positive counterclockwise direction; this preserves the no-penetration condition while changing circulation around the body. Its sign adds to the negative surface velocity on one side and opposes it on the other. For a 50 mm radius cylinder in air at \(U=10\,\mathrm{m/s}\), choose \(\Gamma=0.50\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) counterclockwise. The added surface speed is \(0.50/[2\pi(0.050)]=1.59\,\mathrm{m/s}\). At the top the signed tangential speed is −18.41 m/s, and at the bottom it is +21.59 m/s.

For air density 1.20 kg/m³ the dynamic pressure is 60 Pa. The top and bottom coefficients \(1-(v_\theta/U)^2\) are −2.39 and −3.66, respectively. The lower pressure is now at the bottom, producing downward lift. Integrate \(-p\,n_y a\,d\theta\) around the circumference: the terms even between top and bottom cancel, leaving signed lift per span \(L'=-\rho U\Gamma=-6.0\,\mathrm{N/m}\) for these axes. A rigid-body rotation of the entire fluid would give a different vorticity field; the superposed free vortex has zero local vorticity outside the excluded body. Physical circulation in an experiment depends on how the flow and surface boundary layers are established, and cannot be selected by the potential solution alone.

The stagnation points satisfy \(\sin\theta=\Gamma/(4\pi aU)\), or 0.0796 here. They shift by about 4.6° from their zero-circulation positions without changing the fact that pressure is highest where speed vanishes. This offers a second observable for estimating circulation, independent of integrating the measured lift, provided the observed surface points reasonably follow the attached outer-flow model.

Chapter 8 · Boundary layers and momentum thickness

Connect a measured velocity deficit to wall drag and distinguish the physical edge of a boundary layer from its integral thicknesses.

Viscosity matters where velocity changes rapidly

Flow past a smooth plate with a large length-based Reynolds number often has nearly uniform speed outside a thin layer next to the wall. No slip requires zero velocity at the stationary surface. Inside the thin region, transverse velocity gradients can be large enough for viscosity to balance streamwise inertia. If the free-stream speed is \(U\), the streamwise distance is \(x\) and the layer thickness is \(\delta\), the leading inertial and viscous scales are \(U^2/x\) and \(\nu U/\delta^2\). Their balance gives \(\delta/x\sim1/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}\), where \(\mathrm{Re}_x=Ux/\nu\).

This scaling predicts how a laminar layer grows. Its numerical factor depends on how the thickness is defined. A common measured edge is the position reaching 99% of the external speed. Two integral measures instead quantify flow displaced by the layer and its lost momentum, and are less sensitive to picking a particular edge point.

\[\delta^*=\int_0^\infty(1-u/U)\,dy,\qquad \theta_m=\int_0^\infty(u/U)(1-u/U)\,dy .\]

The thickness \(\delta^*\) gives the equivalent missing volume flux per unit width divided by \(U\). Momentum thickness \(\theta_m\) weights that deficit by the actual local speed. Fluid right at the wall contributes strongly to displacement thickness but carries almost no streamwise momentum flux.

Relate the momentum deficit to wall shear

A steady control volume surrounding one face of a flat plate at zero incidence receives fluid from the uniform upstream stream. For constant external speed and pressure, the integrated wall force equals the deficit of streamwise momentum flux downstream after accounting for entrained fluid. Per unit plate width this gives \(D'(x)=\rho U^2\theta_m(x)\). Differentiating gives local wall shear \(\tau_w=\rho U^2\,d\theta_m/dx\). A growing deficit implies positive drag on the plate.

Suppose a velocity survey is represented by \(u/U=2\eta-\eta^2\) for \(0\le\eta=y/\delta\le1\), and uniform flow outside. Integrating yields \(\delta^*/\delta=\int_0^1(1-\eta)^2d\eta=1/3\). Since \(\int_0^1(2\eta-\eta^2)d\eta=2/3\) and \(\int_0^1(2\eta-\eta^2)^2d\eta=8/15\), the momentum thickness is \(2\delta/15\).

Infer drag from a measured layer

At the end of a 0.60 m plate of width 0.40 m in air, let \(U=12\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(\delta=5.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) according to the fitted profile. Then \(\theta_m=0.667\,\mathrm{mm}\) and drag on the measured face is \(1.2(12)^2(0.000667)(0.40)=0.0461\,\mathrm N\). The mean shear over that face is \(0.0461/(0.60\times0.40)=0.192\,\mathrm{Pa}\). It need not equal the local shear at the measuring station. An adverse pressure gradient or separated flow changes the momentum balance; surface pressure forces and external acceleration must then be included before interpreting a wake or boundary-layer survey.

Predict a boundary-layer thickness before viewing a simulation

At the same measuring station the laminar scaling from the first section gives \(\delta\propto\sqrt{\nu/U}\). Starting from a measured 35.84 mm layer at \(x=0.50\) m, predict its thickness after separately doubling free-stream speed and doubling kinematic viscosity. Which change should raise the dimensional wall stress if density and viscosity are held fixed while speed increases? Compare your thickness ratios with the two computed runs and distinguish the measured skin-friction coefficient from wall stress in pascals. Compare the flat-plate runs.

Reduce a partial differential equation to one similarity profile

For a laminar zero-pressure-gradient boundary layer along a flat plate, the leading equations are \(u_x+v_y=0\) and \(u u_x+v u_y=\nu u_{yy}\). Assume external speed \(U\) constant, a stationary no-slip surface and a layer thin relative to streamwise distance. Introduce a streamfunction \(\psi\) through \(u=\psi_y,\ v=-\psi_x\) to satisfy continuity identically. The scaling \(\delta\sim\sqrt{\nu x/U}\) suggests \(\eta=y\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\) and \(\psi=\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,f(\eta)\). Differentiation gives \(u=U f'\) and \(v=\tfrac12\sqrt{\nu U/x}(\eta f'-f)\).

When these forms enter the momentum equation, the terms containing \(\eta f'f''\) from longitudinal and transverse advection cancel. Both the remaining inertial and viscous terms carry \(U^2/x\). Dividing through gives the Blasius boundary-value problem

\[f'''+\tfrac12 f f''=0,\qquad f(0)=f'(0)=0,\qquad f'(\infty)=1 .\]

The two wall conditions impose zero normal and tangential velocity. The outer condition matches the uniform stream. The unknown wall slope of the velocity profile, \(f''(0)\), is determined by matching that distant condition; prescribing it independently would overdetermine the third-order equation. Numerical integration gives approximately 0.3321 for this definition of \(\eta\). Conventions that include a factor of two in the similarity coordinate assign a different numerical constant but the same physical wall stress.

Predict a force measurement from the similarity solution

The wall shear is \(\mu U f''(0)\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\), giving \(c_f(x)=\tau_w/(\rho U^2/2)=0.664/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_x}\). Its integral over a plate of length \(L\) yields mean coefficient \(\bar c_f=1.328/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_L}\). The mean equals twice the local trailing-edge value because the integrable \(x^{-1/2}\) shear is highest near the leading edge. Using a single trailing-edge wall gauge for the whole area would therefore underestimate the force.

Take a smooth plate 0.50 m long and 0.25 m wide in air at \(U=6.0\,\mathrm{m/s},\ \nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s},\ \rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). The trailing-edge Reynolds number is 200,000, and a nominally laminar calculation gives \(c_f(L)=0.00149,\ \bar c_f=0.00297\). The one-face drag is \(0.5(1.20)(6)^2(0.50)(0.25)(0.00297)=0.00802\,\mathrm N\). The trailing-edge wall shear is just 0.0321 Pa, and the 99% thickness estimate \(5L/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}_L}\) is 5.59 mm. A support carrying both plate faces would register roughly twice the one-face drag if both flows are comparable, plus fixture and edge drag. A single net force measurement cannot by itself tell whether a discrepancy arose from transition, unequal face conditions or the support geometry.

The similarity solution also predicts how to collapse profiles measured at different stations. At \(x=0.125\) m the same value of \(\eta\) corresponds to half the wall-normal distance it does at 0.50 m, since the distance scales with \(\sqrt{x}\). Meanwhile wall shear doubles and local skin-friction coefficient doubles. A traverse resolving only whole millimetres may describe the outer velocity defect at both positions but badly estimate the near-wall slope at the earlier one. Testing both the profile collapse and the independent integral drag provides a stronger check of the assumed laminar flow than comparing a single velocity at the outer edge.

Measure the momentum transported by turbulent fluctuations

In a smooth plane channel driven by a constant pressure drop, time averaging can hide the dominant transport mechanism. Consider steady statistics and a fully developed mean flow between parallel walls. Let \(y=0\) denote the lower wall and \(h\) the distance to the centreline. Write \(u=U(y)+u'\), \(v=v'\). Impermeability and incompressibility give zero mean transverse velocity. The average of the instantaneous flux of streamwise momentum across a horizontal surface is nevertheless \(\rho\overline{uv}=\rho\overline{u'v'}\). Averaging the product retains the correlation between the two fluctuations.

For constant density and viscosity the mean streamwise balance becomes \(0=-d\bar p/dx+d(\mu\,dU/dy-\rho\overline{u'v'})/dy\). Set \(G=-d\bar p/dx>0\). Integrating from \(y\) to the centreline, where symmetry sets the total shear to zero, gives

\[\tau_{\rm total}(y)=\mu\,\frac{dU}{dy}-\rho\overline{u'v'}=G(h-y),\qquad \tau_w=Gh.\]

The right side is fixed by the driving pressure gradient. At a no-slip wall both fluctuating velocities vanish, so molecular viscosity carries the full shear there. Farther out a parcel moving rapidly downstream can move towards the lower wall (\(u'>0,\ v'<0\)). A slow parcel leaving that wall has the same negative product. Both transfer streamwise momentum towards the wall. With the signs above the turbulent contribution to the shear is positive.

For water with \(\rho=1000\) kg/m³ and \(\mu=0.0010\) Pa s, take \(h=0.020\) m and \(G=100\) Pa/m. The wall stress is 2.00 Pa. At \(y=0.0050\) m the total shear is 1.50 Pa. A measured slope of \(150\) s⁻¹ accounts for 0.150 Pa. The remaining 1.35 Pa implies \(\overline{u'v'}=-1.35\times10^{-3}\) m²/s². The exact force balance gives a quantitative consistency check for velocity measurements or a simulation. Predicting the split from \(G\) alone would require a relation for the velocity correlations.

Reveal the worked solution

Chapter 9 · Pressure integration, lift and drag

Resolve pressure and shear into integrated forces and infer drag independently from a far-wake velocity survey.

Build the resultant from surface measurements

A wind tunnel balance reports force components along and across the incoming flow. A pressure tap reports only local pressure. To connect the two, draw the surface outward normal from the solid into the fluid. A pressure load then acts on the solid in direction \(-n\); fluid wall shear acts tangentially. Integrate pressure traction and shear traction over the body and project the result onto drag and lift axes. A uniform ambient pressure integrates to zero on a closed surface, so gauge pressure is sufficient for this calculation.

For a thin wing at small incidence the upper and lower surfaces are almost horizontal. To leading order the upward pressure force is the planform integral of \(p_{\rm lower}-p_{\rm upper}\). With dynamic pressure \(q_\infty=\rho U^2/2\), define \(C_p=(p-p_\infty)/q_\infty\). For rectangular planform and a pressure difference independent of span, the lift coefficient is approximately

\[C_L=\frac{L}{q_\infty S}\simeq \int_0^1(C_{p,l}-C_{p,u})\,d(x/c).\]

The reference area \(S\) must be stated. A coefficient based on wing planform and a bluff-body coefficient based on frontal area cannot be compared as if they used the same force scale.

Integrate a pressure distribution

A wing panel has chord 0.30 m and span 0.80 m. Take the pressure-coefficient difference as a triangle, rising linearly from zero at the leading edge to 1.20 at \(x/c=0.25\) and decreasing linearly to zero at the trailing edge. The coefficient integral is the area under this triangle, \(C_L=0.60\). At \(U=20\,\mathrm{m/s}\) in air of density 1.20 kg/m³, \(q_\infty=240\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The estimated lift is \(240(0.24)(0.60)=34.56\,\mathrm N\).

For this spanwise-uniform distribution the centre of pressure follows from the first moment of the triangle. Its chord fraction is the mean of the three vertex abscissae, \((0+0.25+1)/3=0.4167\). The resultant acts about 0.125 m aft of the leading edge. The moment magnitude about that edge is \(34.56(0.125)=4.32\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Two panels with the same total lift can therefore transmit different support moments if their pressure distributions differ.

Recover a drag force without surface taps

For a survey far enough downstream that static pressure has returned to the external value, integrate the streamwise momentum deficit across the wake. With essentially parallel flow, constant density and negligible outer boundary shear, drag per unit span is \(D'=\rho\int u(U-u)\,dy\). This momentum survey includes the effect of both body pressure and surface shear.

For a simplified wake 40 mm thick with uniform \(u=16\,\mathrm{m/s}\) inside it and \(U=20\,\mathrm{m/s}\) outside, the inferred drag per span is \(1.20(16)(4)(0.040)=3.07\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Replacing the product by \(\rho U(U-u)\) overestimates the deficit because it assigns the undisturbed mass flux to the wake. A survey close to the trailing edge often also needs a static pressure correction; insufficient lateral coverage can miss part of the deficit.

Relate finite-span lift to a second contribution to drag

A pressure integration on one two-dimensional section neglects a feature of every finite lifting surface. Bound circulation changes along the span and trailing vorticity produces downwash behind the wing. For attached lifting-line flow at small angles, downwash tilts the effective incoming stream downward by induced angle \(\alpha_i\). The section’s aerodynamic force is approximately perpendicular to that local stream, so its projection along the original incoming direction creates induced drag \(D_i\simeq L\alpha_i\). This contribution can remain even for an inviscid outer flow because momentum is carried away in a three-dimensional trailing wake.

For elliptical span loading the ideal lifting-line result is \(\alpha_i=C_L/(\pi AR)\), where aspect ratio \(AR=b^2/S\), span \(b\) and planform area \(S\). A span efficiency factor \(e\) accounts approximately for a non-elliptic distribution at a given lift, giving \(C_{D,i}=C_L^2/(\pi e AR)\). With a locally constant parasite coefficient \(C_{D,0}\), total drag at fixed required lift \(W\) becomes

\[D=qS C_{D,0}+\frac{W^2}{\pi e AR\,qS},\qquad q=\rho U^2/2.\]

At low speed the necessary lift coefficient rises and induced drag dominates; at high speed parasite drag rises with dynamic pressure. Differentiating \(D\) with respect to \(qS\) gives minimum drag where the two terms are equal. Thus \(C_{L,\rm opt}=\sqrt{\pi e AR C_{D,0}}\). This optimum maximises lift-to-drag ratio for the adopted polar. Minimum power \(DU\) is a different operating point.

Select a glide speed and check the angle assumptions

A small unpowered survey glider has weight 120 N, area 1.20 m² and span 3.0 m. Use density 1.20 kg/m³, \(e=0.80\) and \(C_{D,0}=0.025\). Assume shallow steady glide so lift is approximately weight. Aspect ratio is \(3^2/1.2=7.50\) and \(\pi e AR=18.85\). The optimum lift coefficient is \(\sqrt{18.85(0.025)}=0.686\). From \(W=qSC_L\) the dynamic pressure is 145.8 Pa and the airspeed is \(\sqrt{2(145.8)/1.20}=15.6\,\mathrm{m/s}\).

At this condition total drag coefficient is \(0.025+0.686^2/18.85=0.0500\). Drag is 8.75 N, giving \(L/D=13.7\) and glide angle approximately \(\arctan(1/13.7)=4.17^\circ\). The assumption \(L\simeq W\) is consistent to better than one percent. Doubling speed at unchanged weight reduces \(C_L\) by four, so induced drag falls by four while parasite drag rises by four. The original 4.38 N contributions become about 1.09 and 17.5 N. If an estimated optimum lies at or beyond the measured stall coefficient, the attached-flow polar and lifting-line argument no longer support that speed decision.

Banking the same glider changes the required lift. In a coordinated near-level turn with bank angle \(\phi\), the vertical lift component must support the weight, so \(L=W/\cos\phi\). At 45° bank the required lift rises by \(\sqrt2\). At unchanged speed the induced portion of drag then doubles, while the parasite portion is approximately unchanged under the same polar. This transfer calculation explains why a straight-flight drag estimate cannot be inserted unchanged into a turning-flight performance prediction.

The required lift coefficient at the higher bank angle must still remain inside the measured unstalled polar.

Settling in the creeping-flow limit

A small tracer bead in a viscous liquid can reach a regime in which inertial forces on the surrounding liquid are negligible. Estimate their ratio to viscous forces using \(Re_d=\rho U(2a)/\mu\), with radius \(a\). For \(Re_d\ll1\), the steady incompressible equations outside a sphere reduce to \(\nabla p=\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0\). Apply no slip at the sphere and uniform speed \(U\) far away. Linearity then requires drag proportional to \(U\).

The coefficient comes from surface traction. Fix the sphere in flow directed along positive \(z\), and measure polar angle \(\theta\) from that axis. The Stokes solution gives surface pressure relative to the far field \(-3\mu U\cos\theta/(2a)\) and tangential shear \(-3\mu U\sin\theta/(2a)\). The normal viscous stress vanishes there. Project pressure and shear onto the flow direction and use the area of a ring, \(2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\). The pressure integral uses \(\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=2/3\), while the shear integral uses \(\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=4/3\). The two contributions are \(2\pi\mu aU\) and \(4\pi\mu aU\). Even in this highly viscous regime, pressure accounts for one third of the drag.

A spherical calibration bead settles far from the container walls. At terminal speed its apparent weight equals \(6\pi\mu aU_t\), so

\[U_t=\frac{2(\rho_s-\rho)ga^2}{9\mu}.\]

For \(a=0.200\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\rho_s=1250\), \(\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), and \(\mu=0.100\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\), \(U_t=0.218\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}\). The resulting \(Re_d=0.000872\) justifies dropping convective inertia. After terminal speed is reached, 10 mm takes about 45.9 s. Doubling the radius at the same density difference quadruples speed and multiplies \(Re_d\) by eight. A size change therefore merits a fresh check on the approximation.

Chapter 10 · Pump curves, pipe networks and operating points

Construct a hydraulic system curve and solve the operating point of a pump connected to serial or parallel flow paths.

Why a pump does not prescribe a unique flow

A centrifugal pump at fixed speed can deliver different combinations of flow and head. The connected network selects an operating point by requiring the head gained through the machine to match the static lift and losses elsewhere. For two large reservoirs, surface speeds are negligible and equal surface pressures cancel. A steady mechanical energy balance then gives \(H_{\rm pump}=\Delta z+h_L\). With turbulent pipe resistance represented locally by constant loss coefficients, the losses can be written \(h_L=KQ^2\) for positive flow.

This quadratic law is an approximation over a specified operating range. It follows because fittings and pipe friction have head losses proportional to velocity squared when the relevant coefficients vary little. In laminar pipe flow, the pressure loss at fixed viscosity is instead proportional to flow. A system curve extrapolated far outside its underlying Reynolds-number range can give a misleading operating point.

Combine resistances by their physical constraints

In series the same flow passes through each component and head losses add. Thus two quadratic loss coefficients combine as \(K_s=K_1+K_2\). Parallel branches between the same nodes have equal head loss while their flow rates add. For two branches with forward flow, \(Q_i=\sqrt{h/K_i}\), giving

\[Q=\sqrt h\,(K_1^{-1/2}+K_2^{-1/2}),\qquad K_{\parallel}=(K_1^{-1/2}+K_2^{-1/2})^{-2}.\]

A second identical branch divides the flow equally and reduces the quadratic coefficient of that section to one quarter of the single-branch value. Any upstream or downstream common pipe is still in series with the equivalent section and carries total flow.

Open a parallel return line

Use \(Q\) in litres per second and head in metres. A pump has \(H_p=30-0.20Q^2\); two open reservoirs differ in elevation by 6 m. Initially one pipe connects them with loss \(0.40Q^2\). Equating the heads gives \(30-0.20Q^2=6+0.40Q^2\), so \(Q=\sqrt{40}=6.32\,\mathrm{L/s}\) and \(H_p=22.0\,\mathrm m\). Hydraulic power delivered to water is \(\rho gQ H_p=1000(9.81)(0.00632)(22)=1.36\,\mathrm{kW}\).

Now add a second identical parallel pipe over the whole route. The system curve becomes \(6+0.10Q^2\). The new flow is \(\sqrt{80}=8.94\,\mathrm{L/s}\) and pump head 14.0 m. Each pipe takes only 4.47 L/s, less than the original single-pipe flow. Total delivery increases by about 41% while hydraulic power falls to 1.23 kW. The input shaft power also depends on the efficiency at each operating point. If a flow controller holds the original delivery after the second branch opens, its valve or speed controller changes the operating curve and the free-running comparison no longer predicts the duty.

Determine which side of a branch controls the flow

A check valve on a return line permits only positive branch flow. If an added elevated reservoir imposes a static threshold of 5 m on that branch, the forward branch law becomes \(Q_b=\sqrt{(H-5)/K_b}\) only for driving head \(H>5\) m. Below that threshold the flow is zero. The resulting system curve changes form as the valve opens, so a single constant equivalent coefficient cannot describe both regimes.

Derive the first pressure change in a rapidly closed line

The operating point of a pump and pipe network is a steady state. A valve closure can generate a much higher transient pressure while the flow far upstream has barely changed. Consider a straight horizontal liquid-filled pipe of area \(A\) and initially uniform forward speed \(V\). A rapid downstream closure sends a compression wave upstream at wave speed \(a\). During a short interval \(dt\), the wave reaches approximately \(a\,dt\) of pipe, bringing the fluid behind it to rest. Neglect friction over this short wavefront and take \(a\gg V\).

The stopped mass is \(\rho A a\,dt\), losing momentum \(\rho A a\,dt\,V\). The pressure difference across the front supplies an impulse \(A\Delta p\,dt\). Balancing magnitudes yields the Joukowsky rise \(\Delta p=\rho a V\); for a partial rapid speed change use its magnitude \(\rho a|\Delta V|\) with sign determined by whether the wave compresses or relieves the pipe. Small fluid compressibility and wall compliance both enter the actual wave speed. In a rigid tube \(a\simeq\sqrt{K/\rho}\), but an expanding elastic pipe stores some of the volume change and lowers that speed.

Reflections matter after the wave has travelled to a boundary and back. For a reservoir a distance \(L\) away the first round-trip time is approximately \(2L/a\). A valve that completes closure before the returning wave acts can produce the full first rise. A much slower closure allows incoming reflections to alter the pressure during the manoeuvre and requires a time-dependent network calculation.

Compare closure schedules against a pressure allowance

A water line is 300 m long with measured wave speed \(a=1000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), initial speed 1.5 m/s, density 1000 kg/m³ and initial valve gauge pressure 0.40 MPa. Rapid full closure predicts \(\Delta p=1000(1000)(1.5)=1.50\,\mathrm{MPa}\), bringing the first local peak to about 1.90 MPa gauge. A valve closing in 0.15 s completes its action well inside the 0.60 s round-trip interval. A static energy estimate based only on \(\rho V^2/2=1.13\,\mathrm{kPa}\) would miss the pipe-length momentum transient by orders of magnitude.

Suppose an operating specification allows a first-wave rise of 0.45 MPa. The maximum abrupt decrement in line speed consistent with it is \(450000/[1000(1000)]=0.45\,\mathrm{m/s}\). This provides a useful bound on a single rapid speed change. Dividing the full stop into several identical steps does not automatically satisfy the total pressure limit: reflected waves can add if the steps are poorly timed. A proposed 3 s controlled closure is slow compared with \(2L/a\), so the full instantaneous formula no longer estimates its actual peak. Model the valve characteristic, boundary reflections, pump coast-down and possible sub-atmospheric pressure before choosing a schedule. Measuring pressure at the valve and a second station allows wave speed to be identified from the arrival-time difference.

Calculate a pipe resistance from roughness and flow rate

For fully developed turbulent flow in a round pipe the pressure gradient depends on both the roughness height and the viscous scale of the near-wall flow. Force balance on a length \(L\) gives \(\Delta p\,\pi D^2/4=\tau_w\pi DL\). Define the Darcy factor by \(f_D=8\tau_w/(\rho U^2)\), where \(U\) is the cross-sectional mean velocity. Combining the equations gives \(h_f=f_D(L/D)U^2/(2g)\). Use the same Darcy convention throughout. The Fanning factor is \(f_D/4\).

For a straight, uniform, turbulent pipe the empirical Colebrook equation relates \(f_D\) to \(Re=UD/\nu\) and equivalent sand roughness \(\epsilon/D\). Setting \(x=1/\sqrt{f_D}\) turns the implicit equation into a simple iteration:

\[x_{n+1}=-2\log_{10}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{3.7D}+\frac{2.51x_n}{Re}\right).\]

When roughness dominates the second term, the friction factor becomes nearly independent of Reynolds number. The smooth limit retains a Reynolds-number dependence. For fully developed laminar flow the separate law \(f_D=64/Re\) applies. Near transition a roughness estimate and a single turbulent curve alone cannot resolve the flow regime.

A building cooling loop carries \(Q=4.00\,\mathrm{L\,s^{-1}}\) of water through \(L=40.0\,\mathrm m\) of straight pipe with \(D=50.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\nu=1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\) and \(\epsilon=0.050\,\mathrm{mm}\). Continuity gives \(U=4Q/(\pi D^2)=2.037\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(Re=1.019\times10^5\). Starting at \(x_0=7\), iteration converges to \(x=6.721\), hence \(f_D=0.02214\) and \(h_f=3.75\,\mathrm m\). If deposits raise equivalent roughness to \(0.200\,\mathrm{mm}\) at unchanged bore and flow, the same calculation gives \(f_D=0.02948\) and \(h_f=4.99\,\mathrm m\). Thus the pump needs 1.24 m additional head for this pipe segment alone. At fixed pump speed the operating flow would generally change. That requires solving the pump and system curves together.

Chapter 11 · Angular momentum and work in a rotor

Use absolute and blade-relative velocities to derive the ideal head developed by a rotating impeller.

Convert a change of swirl into shaft torque

A fluid element can gain energy in a rotating impeller even if its inlet and outlet are at the same elevation. The rotor exerts a torque that changes the angular momentum carried by the flow. Draw a fixed control volume around the rotor. In a steady, single-inlet, single-outlet approximation, with representative mass-flow-weighted tangential velocities \(V_{\theta1},V_{\theta2}\), the axial torque on the fluid is \(\dot m(r_2V_{\theta2}-r_1V_{\theta1})\). Positive tangential velocity points in the direction of shaft rotation.

Multiplying by shaft angular speed converts torque to power. Divide by the mass flow to obtain the Euler specific work. Writing blade speeds \(U_i=\omega r_i\) avoids confusion between the angular velocity of the hardware and the velocity components of the fluid.

\[w_E=U_2V_{\theta2}-U_1V_{\theta1},\qquad H_E=w_E/g .\]

A pump adding energy has positive \(w_E\) under this convention. A turbine removes energy from the fluid and develops shaft output corresponding to a decrease in the fluid’s angular momentum. A stationary guide vane may change swirl while doing no shaft work because its angular speed is zero.

Relate the blade angle to the exit flow

At each station absolute velocity is the vector sum of local blade velocity \(U\) and velocity \(W\) relative to the moving blade. The throughflow component of \(V\) equals that of \(W\) when the blade motion is purely tangential. For an ideal radial impeller with backward-curved blades, define outlet angle \(\beta_2\) from the backward tangent towards the radial throughflow. Then \(W_{\theta2}=-V_{r2}\cot\beta_2\) and \(V_{\theta2}=U_2-V_{r2}\cot\beta_2\). State this angle convention before reading a drawing; other conventions change the algebraic appearance.

Predict work and distinguish hydraulic delivery

Let the outer radius be 0.10 m, rotor speed 1800 rpm, outlet radial velocity 3.0 m/s, exit blade angle 30°, and inlet swirl zero. The tip speed is 18.85 m/s; the ideal absolute exit swirl is \(18.85-3\cot30^\circ=13.65\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Hence specific work is 257.4 J/kg and head \(257.4/9.81=26.24\,\mathrm m\). For water at 8.0 L/s the mass flow is about 8.0 kg/s. Rotor torque on the fluid is \(8(0.10)(13.65)=10.92\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), and work rate is 2.06 kW.

If measurements show an actual delivered head of 21 m at this flow, hydraulic output is \(8(9.81)(21)=1.65\,\mathrm{kW}\). The ratio of actual head to the ideal blade-following estimate is 0.800. It combines departures from the ideal exit triangle with hydraulic losses in this simplified comparison. Mechanical bearing loss would additionally enter a comparison with measured shaft input power. If inlet guide vanes add positive inlet swirl, the subtracted \(U_1V_{\theta1}\) term reduces specific work for the same exit conditions.

Connect the triangle to pump affinity

For geometrically similar operation at unchanged impeller diameter, a fixed ratio \(V_r/U\) holds the ideal outlet triangle shape constant. Flow then scales with shaft speed, head with speed squared and hydraulic power with speed cubed. A real installation with static lift need not move between similar operating points when speed changes.

Derive work from a jet in a moving bucket

A radial pump uses an impeller to add angular momentum to a confined flow. An impulse turbine reverses the sequence: a stationary nozzle first converts pressure head to a high-speed free jet, which transfers momentum to buckets at nearly ambient pressure. Let jet speed \(V_j\) be tangential at the runner and bucket speed \(U\) positive in that direction. Relative inlet speed is \(V_j-U\). Suppose the bucket turns this relative stream through angle \(\pi-\beta\) and its exit relative speed magnitude is \(k(V_j-U)\), with \(k\le1\) accounting for relative-flow loss. The exit tangential absolute speed is \(V_{\theta2}=U-k(V_j-U)\cos\beta\).

A succession of buckets intercepts the full continuous nozzle mass flow \(\dot m=\rho Q\), unlike one isolated vane travelling away from a jet. Neglect leakage, windage and mechanical losses. Tangential runner force is \(\dot m(V_j-V_{\theta2})\), hence shaft work per kilogram and hydraulic efficiency relative to incoming jet kinetic energy are

\[w=U(V_j-U)(1+k\cos\beta),\qquad \eta_j=\frac{2U(V_j-U)(1+k\cos\beta)}{V_j^2}.\]

Differentiating with respect to speed at fixed inlet jet and exit geometry gives \(U=V_j/2\) for maximum power. At zero bucket speed force can be large but shaft power is zero. At bucket speed equal to jet speed there is no relative inlet stream to turn and therefore no useful momentum transfer. These two limits expose an incorrectly drawn exit velocity triangle.

Compare torque at the design and off-design speeds

A laboratory nozzle delivers 12 L/s of water at 30 m/s to a runner of pitch radius 0.20 m. Adopt \(k=0.85\) and \(\beta=15^\circ\), so \(1+k\cos\beta=1.821\). At optimum speed \(U=15\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\omega=75\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), about 716 rpm. The runner force is \(12(30-15)(1.821)=328\,\mathrm N\), torque 65.6 N m, and power \(15(328)=4.92\,\mathrm{kW}\). The jet supplied \(12(30^2)/2=5.40\,\mathrm{kW}\), giving efficiency 91.0% under these idealised runner assumptions.

Load the shaft until bucket speed drops to 9 m/s. The force rises to \(12(21)(1.821)=459\,\mathrm N\) and torque rises to 91.8 N m, but power drops to \(9(459)=4.13\,\mathrm{kW}\). Increased measured torque therefore does not demonstrate improved conversion without the speed measurement. The exit tangential velocity becomes \(9-0.85(21)\cos15^\circ=-8.24\,\mathrm{m/s}\), indicating the bucket now reverses some absolute tangential momentum. Maintaining the assumed jet flow requires sufficient nozzle head independently of shaft loading; a restricted supply may change both jet velocity and flow rate during the test.

At ideal design speed the leaving water still has a radial or transverse velocity because \(\beta\) is nonzero. Its relative exit transverse component is \(k(V_j-U)\sin\beta=3.30\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The absolute tangential component is \(15-0.85(15)\cos15^\circ=2.69\,\mathrm{m/s}\), giving residual jet kinetic energy about 9.1 J/kg. The rest of the gap between inlet kinetic energy and shaft work includes dissipation associated with \(k<1\). It would be incorrect to attribute the entire efficiency shortfall to measured tangential exit velocity alone. Traversing velocity components or collecting the discharge separately provides a diagnostic of losses within the bucket.

Chapter 12 · Compressible nozzles and choking

Relate area change to Mach number and calculate the limiting mass flow through a converging nozzle.

Incorporate expansion work and mass conservation

Gas accelerates through a nozzle by converting enthalpy into kinetic energy. At sufficiently high speeds, density changes appreciably and a constant-density volume balance fails. For steady one-dimensional adiabatic flow without shaft work or appreciable elevation change, \(h+V^2/2=h_0\). A calorically perfect gas has \(h=c_pT\); its local sound speed is \(a=\sqrt{\gamma RT}\), and Mach number is \(M=V/a\). Evaluate \(a\) at local static temperature, rather than at the upstream reservoir temperature.

Assume the acceleration is reversible, so \(dp=a^2d\rho\) along the isentrope. Differential momentum balance gives \(dp=-\rho VdV\), hence \(d\rho/\rho=-M^2dV/V\). Inserting this in continuity \(d\rho/\rho+dV/V+dA/A=0\) gives the area–velocity relation

\[\frac{dA}{A}=(M^2-1)\frac{dV}{V}.\]

A subsonic stream accelerates through a converging passage. A supersonic stream accelerates through a diverging one. A smooth transition through sonic conditions requires a stationary area, conventionally a minimum-area throat for acceleration from subsonic to supersonic speed. A converging nozzle supplied from rest can first reach Mach one at its smallest area, the exit.

Obtain the throat state from the reservoir

In the upstream reservoir the flow is essentially at rest, so its static pressure and temperature are stagnation values \(p_0,T_0\). Combining \(c_p(T_0-T)=V^2/2\) with \(c_p=\gamma R/(\gamma-1)\) gives \(T/T_0=[1+(\gamma-1)M^2/2]^{-1}\). Apply the ideal-gas isentropic pressure–temperature relation to find the critical pressure and temperature ratios at \(M=1\).

\[T^*/T_0=2/(\gamma+1),\qquad p^*/p_0=[2/(\gamma+1)]^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} .\]

With exit area fixed, lowering back pressure increases mass flow until the exit becomes sonic. Further reduction in back pressure does not change the ideal throat state for fixed reservoir conditions. Expansion downstream can then adjust the emerging jet to its surroundings.

Estimate a choked discharge

An insulated converging air nozzle has throat area \(50\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) and reservoir conditions 300 kPa absolute and 300 K. Use \(R=287\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\) and \(\gamma=1.40\). The critical temperature is \(300(2/2.4)=250\,\mathrm K\), critical pressure \(300(2/2.4)^{3.5}=158.5\,\mathrm{kPa}\), density \(158500/(287\times250)=2.21\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and critical speed \(\sqrt{1.4(287)(250)}=317\,\mathrm{m/s}\).

Atmospheric back pressure of 100 kPa lies below the critical pressure, so the ideal mass flow is \(\rho^*a^*A^*=2.21(317)(50\times10^{-6})=0.0350\,\mathrm{kg/s}\). A back pressure of 210 kPa would leave this converging nozzle unchoked, and the exit state must then be calculated from that back pressure. Real contraction and boundary-layer losses can reduce the flow through a calibrated orifice compared with the ideal nozzle prediction; a measured discharge coefficient accounts for those effects within its tested range.

Predict changes at a choked throat

At fixed reservoir pressure, the critical temperature is proportional to \(T_0\), critical density to \(1/T_0\) and critical speed to \(\sqrt{T_0}\). Their product shows that the choked mass flow scales as \(p_0A^*/\sqrt{T_0}\) for the same gas. Warming the original reservoir from 300 to 360 K at unchanged absolute pressure therefore reduces the ideal mass flow by \(\sqrt{300/360}=0.913\), to about 0.0320 kg/s. Increasing throat area or reservoir pressure has a direct linear effect while the downstream pressure stays low enough to maintain choking.

Derive the admissible jump from conservation laws

Supersonic gas in a duct can decelerate abruptly through a normal shock. The shock is thin compared with the duct dimensions, so treat it as a stationary discontinuity of unchanged area. Take a calorically perfect gas, no heat or shaft work and negligible elevation change. Mass, streamwise momentum and stagnation enthalpy are conserved:

\[\rho_1V_1=\rho_2V_2,\quad p_1+\rho_1V_1^2=p_2+\rho_2V_2^2,\quad c_pT_1+V_1^2/2=c_pT_2+V_2^2/2 .\]

Entropy increases through the shock, so the two end states cannot be joined with the isentropic pressure–temperature relation. Set \(r=\rho_2/\rho_1=V_1/V_2\). Momentum gives \(p_2/p_1=1+\gamma M_1^2(1-1/r)\) and the gas law gives \(T_2/T_1=(p_2/p_1)/r\). Substitution into the energy equation yields two roots, one for unchanged flow and the compression solution

\[r=\frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M_1^2},\qquad M_2^2=\frac{1+(\gamma-1)M_1^2/2}{\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)/2}.\]

For \(M_1>1\) this second state has higher density and lower Mach number. As \(M_1\to1^+\), every state ratio tends to unity. For very large upstream Mach number the compression ratio approaches \((\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)\) in the constant-specific-heat approximation, although real-gas changes eventually limit that approximation.

Calculate a diffuser’s stagnation-pressure loss

Air upstream of a normal shock has \(M_1=2.0,\ p_1=80\,\mathrm{kPa}\) absolute and \(T_1=250\,\mathrm K\). Use \(\gamma=1.4,\ R=287\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\). The density ratio is \(2.4(4)/(2+0.4(4))=2.667\), and \(p_2/p_1=1+1.4(4)(1-1/2.667)=4.50\). Hence \(p_2=360\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and \(T_2=250(4.50/2.667)=421.9\,\mathrm K\). The downstream Mach number is \(\sqrt{1.8/(5.6-0.2)}=0.577\). Upstream speed is approximately 634 m/s and mass conservation gives about 238 m/s downstream.

The stagnation temperature upstream is \(250[1+0.2(4)]=450\,\mathrm K\); downstream it is \(421.9[1+0.2(0.577)^2]=450\,\mathrm K\) as required by energy conservation. Evaluate stagnation pressure separately from each local state by a hypothetical isentropic deceleration on its own side. Upstream \(p_{01}=80(1.8)^{3.5}=626\,\mathrm{kPa}\), whereas downstream \(p_{02}=360(1.0667)^{3.5}=451\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Recovery \(p_{02}/p_{01}=0.721\) shows that a large static pressure rise can accompany a 28% loss of stagnation pressure. For the same stagnation temperature the entropy rise is \(-R\ln(0.721)=94\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\), a positive value consistent with the irreversible deceleration.

A pitot tube facing the upstream supersonic flow forms its own shock ahead of the tip. The final stagnation pressure measured there corresponds ideally to isentropic deceleration after the probe’s shock, about 451 kPa here, rather than to the upstream isentropic stagnation pressure of 626 kPa. A conventional subsonic pitot reduction applied to the reading would therefore infer an incorrect upstream speed. By contrast, a static pressure tap placed sufficiently far upstream can still report the 80 kPa static state. The ratio \(451/80=5.64\) illustrates why the probe configuration must be stated when comparing a pressure ratio with a compressible-flow prediction.

The downstream temperature calculation can also be checked using mass flux. The upstream density is \(80000/[287(250)]=1.12\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and the measured downstream static values imply \(360000/[287(421.9)]=2.97\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Multiplication by the corresponding velocities gives roughly \(707\,\mathrm{kg/(m^2s)}\) on both sides. A calculation conserving stagnation pressure instead would generally fail one of these basic balances even if its pressure trend looked plausible.

The shock also changes the area needed to choke a downstream passage. For a given gas the choked mass flow scales as \(A^*p_0/\sqrt{T_0}\). Stagnation temperature is unchanged but stagnation pressure drops to 72.1% of its upstream value. Passing the same mass flow therefore requires a downstream critical area larger by \(1/0.721=1.39\). This does not mean the physical duct jumps in area at the normal shock. The critical area is the sonic reference area for an isentropic stream tube drawn from each side, and the two hypothetical reference conditions differ because entropy has increased.

A converging test section downstream that was sized from the upstream stagnation pressure may consequently choke earlier than expected. The entropy and critical-area tests point to the same lost capacity from different directions: one uses the gas state, the other its maximum deliverable mass flux. Together with conservation of actual mass flux at the shock, they make it possible to distinguish a real shock loss from a simple mismatch between static and total pressure instrumentation.

Compare two back pressures

At choking \(T^*=300\,\mathrm K\), \(\rho^*=2.45\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(a^*=347.2\,\mathrm{m/s}\), hence \(\dot m=0.0256\,\mathrm{kg/s}\). At 250 kPa, \(T=360(250/400)^{0.4/1.4}=314.8\,\mathrm K\). The energy balance with \(c_p=1004.5\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\) gives \(V=\sqrt{2c_p(360-314.8)}=301\,\mathrm{m/s}\).

Find the operating point and account for the lost head

A pump between two open tanks supplies head Hₚ = 24 − 0.020q² metres, where q is measured in litres per second. The upper surface is 4.0 m higher, and pipe losses give hL = 0.030q² metres. With water density 1000 kg/m³ and pump efficiency 0.75, find volume flow, electrical input assuming an ideal motor, and the share used to raise the water. Then throttle the discharge by adding 0.030q² of valve loss.

Worked solution

Equalising pump and system head gives 0.050q² = 20, or q = 20.0 L/s. The head is 16.0 m, split into 4.0 m elevation and 12.0 m pipe loss. The fluid receives ρgQH = 3.14 kW and the assumed motor supplies 4.19 kW. Only 0.785 kW raises gravitational potential. It is one quarter of the fluid power and about 18.8% of the input.

With the valve, 0.080q² = 20 gives 15.81 L/s and a pump head of 19.0 m. Pipe and valve each dissipate 7.50 m. The new fluid power is 2.95 kW and useful lift power is 0.620 kW. Increasing the system resistance reduces flow even as the operating head rises.

An energy check sums elevation, pipe and valve contributions at the new flow. Extrapolating a fixed 75% efficiency beyond this example would need a measured efficiency curve. Also, tank surface velocities are negligible here; a discharge jet carrying appreciable kinetic energy would add an endpoint term.

How does a surface slow down a moving fluid?

Upstream of a smooth plate, every parcel in a uniform stream moves at speed \(U\). At the plate the no-slip condition brings the fluid to rest. Momentum diffuses across the flow while the stream carries it downstream, so the region slowed by the wall thickens with distance. The wall shear determines how much power is needed to move a plate through the fluid.

Consider steady two-dimensional incompressible laminar flow at constant density \(\rho\), dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) and zero streamwise pressure gradient. Measure \(x\) from the leading edge and \(y\) normal to the plate. When the viscous layer is thin relative to \(x\), the streamwise momentum and continuity equations reduce to

\[u u_x+v u_y=\nu u_{yy},\qquad u_x+v_y=0,\qquad \nu=\mu/\rho.\]

At the wall \(u=v=0\), while \(u\) approaches \(U\) far above it. Balancing \(U^2/x\) with \(\nu U/\delta^2\) gives the thickness scale \(\delta\sim\sqrt{\nu x/U}\). The thickness increases downstream even though \(\delta/x\) falls. Doubling the speed gives the fluid less time to diffuse momentum before it reaches the observation point.

Relate a measured velocity slope to drag

Profiles at different stations have nearly the same shape when plotted against \(\eta=y\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\). The laminar Blasius solution gives a wall slope \(\partial_y u|_0=0.332\,U\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\). Therefore, with \(Re_x=Ux/\nu\),

\[\tau_w=\mu\,\partial_yu|_0,\qquad c_f(x)=\frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U^2}=\frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad \delta_{99}\simeq\frac{5.0x}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.\]

The last thickness is the height at which \(u=0.99U\). Integrating \(\tau_w\) from the leading edge to length \(L\) gives the drag on one face per unit width, \(D/b=0.664\rho U^2\sqrt{\nu L/U}\). The mean friction coefficient over the plate is twice the local coefficient at its trailing edge. Surface roughness, transition to turbulence and strong pressure gradients change this result.

Worked example · Sample a plate halfway along

Use \(U=1.0\) m/s, \(\rho=1.0\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(\mu=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\) and \(x=0.50\) m. Then \(\nu=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(Re_x=5000\). The 99% thickness is 35.4 mm, the friction coefficient is 0.00939, and the shear stress is \(0.5(1)(1)^2(0.00939)=0.00470\) Pa. At \(x=1.00\) m the layer has grown to 50 mm and the wall shear has fallen to 0.00332 Pa. The amount of fluid affected has increased while the near-wall velocity gradient has weakened.

Transfer problem · Use a pressure drop to infer pipe flow

In a sufficiently long straight pipe of radius \(R\), the viscous regions growing from the walls merge and the velocity profile stops changing downstream. For a fluid cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(L\), the pressure force \(\Delta p\,\pi r^2\) balances the resisting shear force \(2\pi r L|\tau|\). Hence \(|\tau|=\Delta p\,r/(2L)\). Since \(du/dr=-|\tau|/\mu\), integration with \(u(R)=0\) gives \(u(r)=\Delta p(R^2-r^2)/(4\mu L)\). Integrating over circular rings yields \(Q=\pi R^4\Delta p/(8\mu L)\).

A water line has \(R=5.0\) mm, \(L=2.0\) m, \(\mu=0.0010\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\) and \(\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Delivering \(Q=1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) requires \(\Delta p=8.15\) Pa over the developed portion. The mean speed is \(Q/(\pi R^2)=0.0127\) m/s and the diameter-based Reynolds number is 127, consistent with laminar flow. Halving the pipe radius at fixed flow multiplies the required pressure by sixteen. Compare the derivation for a parallel-plate channel.
