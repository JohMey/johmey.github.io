Pump curves, pipe networks and operating points

From first principles

Find pump discharge by matching added and required head

A pump supplies head that depends on its discharge and speed. The connected pipework requires head to overcome both terminal elevation or pressure differences and losses. Consider steady incompressible flow between large reservoirs with negligible endpoint velocities. For a fixed pump speed, conservation of mechanical energy at the operating point gives

\[H_p(Q)=H_{\rm static}+h_L(Q),\]
\[H_{\rm static}=z_2-z_1+(p_2-p_1)/(\rho g).\]

For fittings and pipe sections with approximately constant loss coefficients, each loss varies as \(V^2/(2g)\). In a single series path all sections carry the same \(Q\), with \(V_j=Q/A_j\). Adding the individual losses gives a system coefficient \(K\).

\[h_L=\sum_j\frac{\zeta_j}{2gA_j^2}Q^2=KQ^2 .\]

If the measured pump characteristic is approximated by \(H_p=H_0-aQ^2\), solve the intersection explicitly on the positive-flow branch.

\[Q=\sqrt{\frac{H_0-H_{\rm static}}{a+K}} .\]

This solution needs \(H_0>H_{\rm static}\). Branches in parallel share an endpoint head difference and their discharges add; those constraints must be imposed before using an equivalent system curve.

Derivation and applications

Find the sensitivity of a pump operating point to a valve

A pump curve and a system curve determine their intersection. Their local slopes also determine how far that intersection moves when a valve setting changes. Model a fixed-speed pump by \(H_p=H_0-aQ^2\) and a network by \(H_s=H_z+bQ^2\) in the intended flow direction. Here \(H_z\) is a static elevation or pressure head, \(a,b>0\) have units of head per squared discharge, and \(H_0>H_z\). The loss model assumes an approximately constant turbulent loss coefficient in the examined flow range.

\[H_0-aQ^2=H_z+bQ^2,\]
\[Q=\sqrt{\frac{H_0-H_z}{a+b}} .\]

Differentiate implicitly with respect to the network coefficient at fixed speed and static head. A larger loss coefficient raises the system curve and reduces discharge. The fractional sensitivity is weaker when the pump itself has a steep characteristic.

\[\frac{dQ}{db}=-\frac{Q}{2(a+b)},\]
\[\frac{d\ln Q}{d\ln b}=-\frac{b}{2(a+b)} .\]

The actual head delivered by the pump changes as the flow moves along its curve. Differentiating the pump head with the result just obtained gives a positive slope with respect to \(b\), an effect distinguishable by measuring both flow and pressure.

\[\frac{dH_p}{db}=-2aQ\frac{dQ}{db}=\frac{aQ^2}{a+b}.\]

These derivatives apply to small changes. Near zero available head a fractional uncertainty in the difference \(H_0-H_z\) can dominate, even if the separate head measurements are quite accurate.

Worked example

Predict the effect of throttling a circulation line

Measure \(Q\) in L/s and head in metres. Take \(H_0=20.0\), \(H_z=4.00\), \(a=0.250\) and \(b=0.750\) in consistent units. The flow is 4.00 L/s and pump head is 16.0 m. A ten-percent increase in \(b\) predicts \(-3.75\) percent flow by the derivative, or 3.85 L/s. The exact new flow is \(\sqrt{16/1.075}=3.86\) L/s, close to the linear estimate.

Second application

Infer which curve limits adjustability

At a separate operating point the measured logarithmic flow sensitivity to the total network coefficient is \(-0.100\). This implies \(b/(a+b)=0.200\) and therefore \(a=4b\). A reduction of \(b\) by 20.0 percent at fixed available head changes flow by the exact factor \(\sqrt{5/4.8}=1.0206\). The small 2.06 percent increase quantifies why a noticeable valve change can have little effect on discharge.

Why a pump does not prescribe a unique flow

A centrifugal pump at fixed speed can deliver different combinations of flow and head. The connected network selects an operating point by requiring the head gained through the machine to match the static lift and losses elsewhere. For two large reservoirs, surface speeds are negligible and equal surface pressures cancel. A steady mechanical energy balance then gives

\[H_{\rm pump}=\Delta z+h_L.\]

With turbulent pipe resistance represented locally by constant loss coefficients, the losses can be written \(h_L=KQ^2\) for positive flow.

This quadratic law is an approximation over a specified operating range. It follows because fittings and pipe friction have head losses proportional to velocity squared when the relevant coefficients vary little. In laminar pipe flow, the pressure loss at fixed viscosity is instead proportional to flow. A system curve extrapolated far outside its underlying Reynolds-number range can give a misleading operating point.

OneSimulation · When does reducing pump speed close the check valve?

An ideal check valve connects a pump to an open reservoir above its inlet. Let \(n\) denote the speed divided by its reference value and let \(Q\) be measured in litres per second. The pump supplies \(20n^2-0.025Q^2\) metres of head. The pipe and reservoir require \(H_s+0.075Q^2\) metres. Forward flow requires a positive intersection.

\[0.100Q^2=20n^2-H_s.\]

OneSimulation inverts the total dynamic loss over the forward-flow interval. With a 5 m static lift, half speed just balances gravity at zero delivery. At reference speed the pump delivers 12.25 L/s. At three-quarter speed it delivers 7.91 L/s, considerably below three quarters of the reference delivery. Beneath the threshold the closed valve carries the pressure difference; the forward-flow equation no longer applies.

"""Intersect a pump curve with a pipe-system head curve at two lifts."""
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from onesim.numerics.nonlinear.kernels.local_solvers.inversion.bracketed_inverse import bisect_increasing

speed = np.linspace(0.25, 1.2, 181)  # fraction of reference speed
shutoff = 20. * speed**2             # pump shutoff head in m
pump_loss, pipe_loss = 0.025, 0.075   # m per (L/s)²
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4), constrained_layout=True)
for lift, colour in [(0., "#19596a"), (5., "#a5442e")]:
    available = shutoff - lift
    delivery = np.zeros_like(speed)
    active = available > 0
    def total_loss(flow):
        return (pump_loss + pipe_loss) * flow**2
    delivery[active] = bisect_increasing(total_loss, available[active], 0., 30.)
    assert np.allclose(shutoff[active] - pump_loss*delivery[active]**2,
                       lift + pipe_loss*delivery[active]**2)
    ax.plot(speed, delivery, color=colour, label=f"Static lift {lift:g} m")

ax.axvline(0.5, color="0.45", linestyle=":", label="5 m lift threshold")
ax.set(xlabel="Pump speed / reference speed", ylabel="Forward delivery (L/s)")
ax.legend()
fig.savefig("fluid-mechanics.png", dpi=170)

Combine resistances by their physical constraints

In series the same flow passes through each component and head losses add. Thus two quadratic loss coefficients combine as \(K_s=K_1+K_2\). Parallel branches between the same nodes have equal head loss while their flow rates add. For two branches with forward flow, \(Q_i=\sqrt{h/K_i}\), giving

\[Q=\sqrt h\,(K_1^{-1/2}+K_2^{-1/2}),\]
\[K_{\parallel}=(K_1^{-1/2}+K_2^{-1/2})^{-2}.\]

A second identical branch divides the flow equally and reduces the quadratic coefficient of that section to one quarter of the single-branch value. Any upstream or downstream common pipe is still in series with the equivalent section and carries total flow.

Open a parallel return line

Use \(Q\) in litres per second and head in metres. A pump has \(H_p=30-0.20Q^2\); two open reservoirs differ in elevation by 6 m. Initially one pipe connects them with loss \(0.40Q^2\). Equating the heads gives \(30-0.20Q^2=6+0.40Q^2\), so \(Q=\sqrt{40}=6.32\,\mathrm{L/s}\) and \(H_p=22.0\,\mathrm m\). Hydraulic power delivered to water is

\[\rho gQ H_p=1000(9.81)(0.00632)(22)=1.36\,\mathrm{kW}.\]

Now add a second identical parallel pipe over the whole route. The system curve becomes \(6+0.10Q^2\). The new flow is \(\sqrt{80}=8.94\,\mathrm{L/s}\) and pump head 14.0 m. Each pipe takes only 4.47 L/s, less than the original single-pipe flow. Total delivery increases by about 41% while hydraulic power falls to 1.23 kW. The input shaft power also depends on the efficiency at each operating point. If a flow controller holds the original delivery after the second branch opens, its valve or speed controller changes the operating curve and the free-running comparison no longer predicts the duty.

Determine which side of a branch controls the flow

A check valve on a return line permits only positive branch flow. If an added elevated reservoir imposes a static threshold of 5 m on that branch, the forward branch law becomes \(Q_b=\sqrt{(H-5)/K_b}\) only for driving head \(H>5\) m. Below that threshold the flow is zero. The resulting system curve changes form as the valve opens, so a single constant equivalent coefficient cannot describe both regimes.

Derive the first pressure change in a rapidly closed line

The operating point of a pump and pipe network is a steady state. A valve closure can generate a much higher transient pressure while the flow far upstream has barely changed. Consider a straight horizontal liquid-filled pipe of area \(A\) and initially uniform forward speed \(V\). A rapid downstream closure sends a compression wave upstream at wave speed \(a\). During a short interval \(dt\), the wave reaches approximately \(a\,dt\) of pipe, bringing the fluid behind it to rest. Neglect friction over this short wavefront and take \(a\gg V\).

The stopped mass is \(\rho A a\,dt\), losing momentum \(\rho A a\,dt\,V\). The pressure difference across the front supplies an impulse \(A\Delta p\,dt\). Balancing magnitudes yields the Joukowsky rise \(\Delta p=\rho a V\); for a partial rapid speed change use its magnitude \(\rho a|\Delta V|\) with sign determined by whether the wave compresses or relieves the pipe. Small fluid compressibility and wall compliance both enter the actual wave speed. In a rigid tube \(a\simeq\sqrt{K/\rho}\), but an expanding elastic pipe stores some of the volume change and lowers that speed.

Reflections matter after the wave has travelled to a boundary and back. For a reservoir a distance \(L\) away the first round-trip time is approximately \(2L/a\). A valve that completes closure before the returning wave acts can produce the full first rise. A much slower closure allows incoming reflections to alter the pressure during the manoeuvre and requires a time-dependent network calculation.

Compare closure schedules against a pressure allowance

A water line is 300 m long with measured wave speed \(a=1000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), initial speed 1.5 m/s, density 1000 kg/m³ and initial valve gauge pressure 0.40 MPa. Rapid full closure predicts \(\Delta p=1000(1000)(1.5)=1.50\,\mathrm{MPa}\), bringing the first local peak to about 1.90 MPa gauge. A valve closing in 0.15 s completes its action well inside the 0.60 s round-trip interval. A static energy estimate based only on \(\rho V^2/2=1.13\,\mathrm{kPa}\) would miss the pipe-length momentum transient by orders of magnitude.

Suppose an operating specification allows a first-wave rise of 0.45 MPa. The maximum abrupt decrement in line speed consistent with it is

\[450000/[1000(1000)]=0.45\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

This provides a useful bound on a single rapid speed change. Dividing the full stop into several identical steps does not automatically satisfy the total pressure limit: reflected waves can add if the steps are poorly timed. A proposed 3 s controlled closure is slow compared with \(2L/a\), so the full instantaneous formula no longer estimates its actual peak. Model the valve characteristic, boundary reflections, pump coast-down and possible sub-atmospheric pressure before choosing a schedule. Measuring pressure at the valve and a second station allows wave speed to be identified from the arrival-time difference.

Calculate a pipe resistance from roughness and flow rate

For fully developed turbulent flow in a round pipe the pressure gradient depends on both the roughness height and the viscous scale of the near-wall flow. Force balance on a length \(L\) gives

\[\Delta p\,\pi D^2/4=\tau_w\pi DL.\]

Define the Darcy factor by \(f_D=8\tau_w/(\rho U^2)\), where \(U\) is the cross-sectional mean velocity. Combining the equations gives

\[h_f=f_D(L/D)U^2/(2g).\]

Use the same Darcy convention throughout. The Fanning factor is \(f_D/4\).

For a straight, uniform, turbulent pipe the empirical Colebrook equation relates \(f_D\) to \(Re=UD/\nu\) and equivalent sand roughness \(\epsilon/D\). Setting \(x=1/\sqrt{f_D}\) turns the implicit equation into a simple iteration:

\[x_{n+1}=-2\log_{10}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{3.7D}+\frac{2.51x_n}{Re}\right).\]

When roughness dominates the second term, the friction factor becomes nearly independent of Reynolds number. The smooth limit retains a Reynolds-number dependence. For fully developed laminar flow the separate law \(f_D=64/Re\) applies. Near transition a roughness estimate and a single turbulent curve alone cannot resolve the flow regime.

A building cooling loop carries \(Q=4.00\,\mathrm{L\,s^{-1}}\) of water through \(L=40.0\,\mathrm m\) of straight pipe with \(D=50.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\nu=1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\) and \(\epsilon=0.050\,\mathrm{mm}\). Continuity gives \(U=4Q/(\pi D^2)=2.037\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(Re=1.019\times10^5\). Starting at \(x_0=7\), iteration converges to \(x=6.721\), hence \(f_D=0.02214\) and \(h_f=3.75\,\mathrm m\). If deposits raise equivalent roughness to \(0.200\,\mathrm{mm}\) at unchanged bore and flow, the same calculation gives \(f_D=0.02948\) and \(h_f=4.99\,\mathrm m\). Thus the pump needs 1.24 m additional head for this pipe segment alone. At fixed pump speed the operating flow would generally change. That requires solving the pump and system curves together.
