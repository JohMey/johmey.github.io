Balance the surface force against a pressure difference

Let \(a\) be the tube radius and \(\theta\) the advancing contact angle measured through the liquid. The vertical component of surface tension along a circular contact line is \(2\pi a\sigma\cos\theta\). Dividing by the cross-sectional area equates this force to a capillary pressure magnitude \(p_c=2\sigma\cos\theta/a\). The liquid pressure immediately below a wetting meniscus is below the gas pressure. An equivalent derivation uses a spherical cap and the Young–Laplace pressure jump with radius of curvature \(a/\cos\theta\). Both derivations require a meniscus whose transverse shape is set mainly by capillarity, so \(a\) should be small compared with the capillary length \(\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho g)}\).

Take \(z\) to be the height of the meniscus above an effectively infinite open reservoir. The bottom and the gas above the meniscus share the same external atmospheric pressure. A static column can sustain the hydrostatic head \(\rho g z\). Equating that head with \(p_c\) gives the equilibrium height \(H=2\sigma\cos\theta/(\rho g a)\). A wider tube has a smaller equilibrium rise. Viscosity does not enter this statement: after flow stops, there is no viscous pressure drop.

This equilibrium construction implicitly neglects the small volume in the curved meniscus, any entrance flow below the reservoir surface, and changes in the contact angle as the wall wets. These corrections matter when the rise is only a few tube radii. They can also matter in comparing two nominally identical capillaries cleaned in different ways. A practical measurement should define whether the microscope tracks the meniscus centre, a contact line or an optical threshold. An inconsistent definition looks like an offset in the inferred height.

Relate the instantaneous capillary force to wall shear

Farther inside a long circular tube the local pressure gradient sets a parabolic velocity profile for steady, fully developed laminar flow. Applying this relation quasi-steadily to a column of length \(z\), the pressure loss is \(8\mu z\,\bar u/a^2\). Conservation of liquid volume in a tube of uniform cross-section identifies \(\bar u=\dot z\). The pressure remaining to drive viscous motion is the capillary jump less the hydrostatic head. Therefore

\[\frac{8\mu z}{a^2}\frac{dz}{dt}=p_c-\rho g z=\rho g(H-z).\]

The driving pressure falls as the column grows, and its hydraulic resistance grows at the same time. These two effects account for the long final approach to equilibrium. Introduce \(B=\rho g a^2/(8\mu)\), which has dimensions of velocity. The resulting first-order equation is

\[\dot z=B(H-z)/z.\]

Close to the entrance it predicts an arbitrarily large speed when \(z\) tends to zero. The approximation has discarded fluid inertia, entrance losses and moving contact-line dynamics. Treat the early viscous law as an intermediate asymptotic once a wetted column exists. A good fit late in the record is no evidence for that divergent initial prediction.

To integrate the differential equation it is simplest to regard time as a function of height. Set \(s=z/H\) and \(\tau=H/B\). Then \(dt/\tau=s\,ds/(1-s)\). Integrate from zero to \(s\), choosing the nominal clock origin at vanishing length. The result is

\[\frac{t}{\tau}=-s-\ln(1-s),\qquad 0\le s<1.\]

The right side is increasing. The notebook uses bisection to invert it for height at a specified laboratory time. The two bracket endpoints have clear physical meanings, zero rise and the equilibrium rise. Near \(s=0\), direct subtraction of \(s\) from \(-\ln(1-s)\) can discard significant digits. Evaluating the logarithm with log1p and using the power series for very small \(s\) preserve the small positive elapsed time.

Read two distinct time laws from one expression

Expanding the logarithm gives

\[t/\tau=s^2/2+s^3/3+\cdots.\]

To leading order \(z^2=2HBt=a\sigma\cos\theta\,t/(2\mu)\). At a common early time, a wider tube therefore has a larger height proportional to \(\sqrt a\). It has less capillary pressure but its resistance per unit length falls rapidly with radius. An experiment that accelerates the initial uptake by increasing radius can simultaneously reduce the final available lifting height.

Near equilibrium write \(s=1-\epsilon\). The implicit solution reads \(t/\tau=-1+\epsilon-\ln\epsilon\). To leading order \(\epsilon\simeq\exp[-1-t/\tau]\). The final height is approached exponentially and is never reached in finite time by this idealisation. Fitting a straight line in \(z^2\) against \(t\) across this late part of the record will overestimate the viscosity if gravity is omitted, because it attributes the slower rise entirely to viscous resistance. A semilog plot of \(H-z\) is a more appropriate late-time diagnostic, although uncertainty in \(H\) becomes more important as the difference shrinks.

For \(a=100\) µm, \(\mu=0.012\) Pa s, \(\rho=1000\) kg/m³, \(\sigma=0.060\) N/m and \(\theta=30^\circ\), the predicted static height is 105.94 mm. The time scale is about 103.7 s. Reaching 25%, 50% and 90% of that height takes approximately 3.91, 20.02 and 145.40 s. The time to half height is not half the time to a specified near-equilibrium height. At half height the exact dimensionless time is

\[\ln2-1/2=0.19315.\]

The leading square-root estimate gives only 0.125 and underestimates the time by about 35% relative to the exact result.

Use a static observation and a moving observation together

In a high-viscosity sample a single short-time image constrains only the combination \(\sigma\cos\theta/\mu\) if all you have is the early growth rate. A change of surface chemistry and a change of viscosity can produce the same slope. A static-height measurement independently fixes \(\sigma\cos\theta\) for a known radius, density and gravity. Combining it with the time to half height gives a viscosity estimate \(\mu=\rho ga^2t_{1/2}/[8H(\ln 2-1/2)]\). The two measurements have different parameter dependences, which is what makes the inverse calculation possible.

Consider the limitations of this estimate. A slowly falling reservoir shifts the elevation origin and changes the effective head. Evaporation from the front can drive a persistent flow even with an apparently stationary meniscus. A wall with contact-angle hysteresis can hold a range of static heights. Repeating the experiment after prewetting the tube changes which contact angle is relevant. Observing both the growing profile and the final plateau helps locate these effects. Reducing the residual of a single two-parameter fit can otherwise conceal them.

Estimate viscosity using only passage times at two marks

A camera need not capture the instant of immersion if the tube carries two calibrated height marks. Select marks at \(z_1=0.25H\) and \(z_2=0.50H\) using an independent long-time reading of \(H\). If the camera clock has an unknown constant offset, subtract the recorded times. The offset cancels exactly. With \(F(s)=-s-\ln(1-s)\) the model gives

\[t_2-t_1=\tau[F(0.50)-F(0.25)].\]

The bracket is about 0.15547. A measured interval of 16.11 s therefore gives \(\tau=103.6\) s. Substituting this value into \(\mu=\rho g a^2\tau/(8H)\) gives approximately 0.0120 Pa s for the stated dimensions. The two-mark construction does not cancel a time-varying camera rate or an error in the static height.

Where should the marks be placed? Two marks very close together make the absolute timing uncertainty large relative to the measured interval. Setting the second mark extremely near equilibrium can also be troublesome because a small height uncertainty then produces a large uncertainty in passage time. Differentiate the dimensionless time to obtain

\[F'(s)=s/(1-s).\]

At half height its slope is one, while at ninety percent it is nine. The same one-percent error in fractional height produces approximately nine times as much time uncertainty at the latter mark as at half height. Exercises on scaling and interpretation

1. Enlarge the tube radius by 50% while holding all material parameters fixed. Predict the ratios of final height, initial square-root coefficient and time to half of the new height.

Work through the solution

The static height is inversely proportional to radius, so the height ratio is \(2/3\). The coefficient in \(z=C\sqrt t\) scales as \(\sqrt a\), giving \(C_{\mathrm{new}}/C_{\mathrm{old}}=\sqrt{1.5}=1.225\). The time scale behaves as \(\tau\propto a^{-3}\). Reaching half of the smaller final height now takes about \(20.02/(1.5)^3=5.93\) s. The two experiments reach different absolute heights at their respective half-height times.

2. In a tube of known radius the static rise is unchanged after a fluid is replaced, but the time to 50% height doubles. What quantity changes in this model?

Work through the solution

At fixed density, gravity and radius the observed \(H\) fixes \(\sigma\cos\theta\). The dimensionless time at 50% is fixed, so a doubling of \(t_{1/2}\) doubles \(\tau\) and hence doubles the viscosity. If density also changes, the measurements constrain the kinematic combination \(\mu/\rho\) and the capillary combination \(\sigma\cos\theta/\rho\), so an independent density measurement is needed to identify dynamic viscosity.

3. A plot of \(z^2\) versus time is linear over the first recorded interval. Does that identify the contact angle uniquely?

Work through the solution

Its slope is \(a\sigma\cos\theta/(2\mu)\) in the gravity-free viscous regime. Knowing only the radius leaves several unknown factors. Independent measurements of viscosity and surface tension would give \(\cos\theta\). Even then, a dynamic advancing angle could differ from the static angle, so compare with the plateau and inspect whether the inferred angle varies with the chosen fit interval.

Read the original capillary-flow argument

Washburn’s paper The Dynamics of Capillary Flow develops the connection between viscous motion and capillary pressure. In comparing radii, first decide whether the experimental observable is a penetration length, a transported volume or a fraction of equilibrium height.
