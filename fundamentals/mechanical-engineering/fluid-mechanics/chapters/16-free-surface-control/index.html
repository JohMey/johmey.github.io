Critical depth and the hydraulic jump

In a wide shallow channel the same discharge can pass as a shallow fast stream or as a deeper slow stream. A gradual hump and an abrupt jump connect depths through different conservation laws.

Minimise the energy needed to carry a discharge

Use a broad rectangular channel and let \(q=Q/b\) be its volume flow per unit width. At a section with approximately uniform speed \(u\) and water depth \(h\), mass conservation gives \(u=q/h\). Take pressure to be hydrostatic over the depth and neglect losses between adjacent smooth sections. Measured relative to the local channel bottom, specific energy in metres of head is

\[E(h)=h+\frac{q^2}{2gh^2},\]
\[\frac{dE}{dh}=1-\frac{q^2}{gh^3}=1-\mathrm{Fr}^2 .\]

The derivative vanishes at \(h_c=(q^2/g)^{1/3}\), where \(\mathrm{Fr}=u/\sqrt{gh}=1\). The second derivative \(3q^2/(gh^4)\) is positive. Critical depth is the unique minimum, with \(E_c=3h_c/2\). The speed \(\sqrt{gh}\) is the speed of small long gravity waves relative to shallow water. When \(u\) exceeds it, even a disturbance travelling upstream relative to the liquid is carried downstream relative to the bed.

Background · Froude number
\[\mathrm{Fr}=U/\sqrt{gh}\]

For shallow rectangular flow with gravity waves this compares material speed to wave speed. Critical depth occurs when the two are equal.

Worked example · decide if a smooth rise can pass the flow

A channel carries \(q=0.40\) m²/s at depth 0.50 m. Speed is 0.80 m/s and specific energy is

\[0.50+0.80^2/(2g)=0.5326\,\mathrm{m}.\]

Critical depth is 0.2536 m and the minimum specific energy is 0.3804 m. Since total head includes bed elevation, a local rise of up to about 0.152 m can be accommodated in the ideal fixed-upstream-state model. A higher rise requires the upstream water level or discharge to adjust. For a small rise on the subcritical branch the available specific energy decreases and depth decreases toward the critical value.

Keep momentum across a short rapid transition

A hydraulic jump is turbulent and strongly dissipative. Place control surfaces far enough upstream and downstream for pressure to be hydrostatic again. Across a short horizontal reach, neglect net bed shear compared with the momentum and end-pressure forces. The hydrostatic force on an end section is \(\rho g h^2/2\) per unit width, obtained by integrating a triangular pressure profile. Momentum flux is \(\rho q^2/h\). Requiring equal momentum functions gives

\[\frac{h_1^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{g h_1}=\frac{h_2^2}{2}+\frac{q^2}{g h_2}.\]

Factor out \(h_2-h_1\) and take the distinct-depth root. With \(r=h_2/h_1\), the remaining relation is

\[r(r+1)=2\mathrm{Fr}_1^2.\]

The positive solution and associated energy drop are

\[r=\frac{\sqrt{1+8\mathrm{Fr}_1^2}-1}{2},\]
\[E_1-E_2=\frac{(h_2-h_1)^3}{4h_1h_2}.\]

The loss is positive for a jump from shallow to deep flow. Reversing the depths would require a gain in mechanical energy without a source, which excludes that continuation of the same algebraic equation.

Worked example · locate the conjugate depth

At a flume section let \(h_1=0.100\) m and \(u_1=2.00\) m/s. Then \(q=0.200\) m²/s and \(\mathrm{Fr}_1=2.02\). The depth ratio is 2.40, giving \(h_2\simeq0.240\) m and \(u_2\simeq0.835\) m/s. Upstream energy is 0.304 m and downstream energy about 0.275 m. Momentum balance predicts a head drop near 0.0285 m. Its location along the channel also depends on the imposed tailwater level; the two-section balance itself contains no position variable.

Follow the head over the bump until its horizontal line just touches the energy curve. Estimate the maximum admissible bed rise from the two vertical readings. Predict how changing discharge moves that tangency before changing the input.

Tutorial · infer discharge from two depth gauges

Two gauges straddle a stationary jump and report 0.12 and 0.30 m. Use the momentum relation to recover the discharge per unit width, determine which side is upstream and calculate the specific mechanical-energy loss.

Recover the flow and check its direction

Factoring the momentum equality gives \(q^2=g h_1 h_2(h_1+h_2)/2\), hence \(q=0.2724\) m²/s. The shallow side has speed 2.27 m/s and Froude number 2.09. The deeper side has speed 0.908 m/s and Froude number 0.529. Taking the shallow side upstream yields energy loss \(0.18^3/[4(0.12)(0.30)]=0.0405\) m. Both discharge and the sign of dissipation support this ordering.
