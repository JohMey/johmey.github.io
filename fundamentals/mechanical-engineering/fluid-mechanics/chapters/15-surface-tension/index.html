Curvature pressure, wetting and capillary rise

The water height in a narrow glass tube is a measurement of an interface force. Starting from a surface energy explains both its direction and its dependence on tube radius.

Convert a surface-energy change into a pressure difference

A clean liquid–gas interface of fixed composition and temperature requires reversible work \(\gamma\,dA\) to increase its area by \(dA\). The surface tension \(\gamma\) therefore has equivalent units J/m² and N/m. Imagine a spherical drop changing radius by \(dR\). Pressure inside does work while the outside pressure resists. Equating net pressure work to the increase in interface energy gives

\[(p_i-p_o)\,dV=\gamma\,dA,\]
\[p_i-p_o=\gamma\frac{8\pi R}{4\pi R^2}=\frac{2\gamma}{R}.\]

For a local patch with two principal radii the same argument gives \(\Delta p=\gamma(1/R_1+1/R_2)\) with signed curvature and consistently chosen normal. A cylinder has only one nonzero curvature, so the spherical factor of two is inappropriate there. A thin soap bubble has two nearly equal interfaces and doubles the pressure jump for a given radius.

Background · Capillary pressure
\[\Delta p=\gamma(1/R_1+1/R_2)\]

Principal curvatures carry signs set by the chosen normal. A cylindrical interface contributes only one finite principal curvature.

Close the balance at the wall

Put a vertical tube of inner radius \(r\) into a bath and let \(\theta\) be the contact angle measured through the liquid. Surface tension along the circular contact line has upward component \(2\pi r\gamma\cos\theta\). The excess column over the external free surface has effective weight \((\rho_l-\rho_g)g\pi r^2 h\). Neglect meniscus volume relative to the column and assume equilibrium, a broad bath, a uniform tube and a well-defined contact angle.

\[h=\frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{(\rho_l-\rho_g)gr}.\]

The sign of \(h\) changes when \(\theta\) exceeds 90°. A smaller tube gives more contact-line length per unit cross-sectional area and therefore supports a taller column. The same equation follows from the pressure jump for a nearly spherical cap of radius \(r/\cos\theta\) in magnitude and a hydrostatic pressure change. If gravity distorts the local cap considerably, the full interface shape must be solved. The capillary length \(\sqrt{\gamma/(\Delta\rho g)}\) indicates when tube radius is small on the relevant scale.

Worked example · inspect a level reading

Take water with \(\gamma=0.072\) N/m, density difference 1000 kg/m³, radius 0.400 mm and contact angle 30°. The rise is

\[2(0.072)\cos30^\circ/[1000(9.81)(0.0004)]=31.8\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The hydrostatic pressure difference is 312 Pa. The capillary length is about 2.71 mm, comfortably larger than the radius. A reader who assumes perfect wetting would infer 36.7 mm. The uncertainty in surface condition can therefore exceed a millimetre-scale ruler uncertainty.

Worked example · use a second liquid column as a test

In a tube of radius 0.500 mm a liquid of density 850 kg/m³ rises 10.0 mm. The product determined by this experiment is

\[\gamma\cos\theta=\Delta\rho g r h/2=0.02085\,\mathrm{N/m}.\]

If an independent measurement gives \(\gamma=0.0300\) N/m, then \(\cos\theta=0.695\), or \(\theta=46.0^\circ\). A second tube of half the radius should approximately double the height on identically prepared walls. It does not by itself separate contact angle from surface tension because the same product appears in both balances.

At a fixed radius rotate the contact angle through 90 degrees. Estimate the sign and magnitude just on either side of the crossing. Compare the effect of halving surface tension with doubling radius in the complete-wetting comparison.

Tutorial · compare a drop and a bubble

A clean water drop of radius 0.60 mm has \(\gamma=0.072\) N/m. Find its excess pressure. A spherical soap film of the same radius has tension 0.030 N/m at each of its two interfaces. Which object has the larger excess pressure? State a radius at which the soap bubble would match the drop.

Count each interface

The drop has one interface and excess pressure \(2(0.072)/0.00060=240\) Pa. The bubble has two interfaces and \(4(0.030)/0.00060=200\) Pa. To match 240 Pa its radius must be 0.500 mm. The liquid volume between the bubble interfaces is treated as small enough for both interface radii to coincide.
