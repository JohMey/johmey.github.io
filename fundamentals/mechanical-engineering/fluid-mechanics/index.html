    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      First specify how fluid moves and how pressure supports a stationary column. Apply momentum and mechanical-energy balances to a control volume. The force on a bend and the head lost in a pipe require different terms.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Resolve viscous shear in a duct, then use similarity to identify equivalent flows. Vorticity tracks local rotation. At a wall, balancing inertia with viscous diffusion predicts the growth of a thin boundary layer.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Integrate pressure and shear over a surface to obtain drag or lift. Couple pressure losses across networks and rotating machines, then include density changes to study compressible flow.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. How a moving wall sends momentum into a liquid

      A plate oscillating horizontally in a deep bath has no average velocity. It nevertheless dissipates energy continuously. A velocity measurement at two heights can reveal the fluid’s viscosity.

    2. A cylinder in potential flow and the absent drag

      A pressure field can vary by several dynamic-pressure heads around a body while producing exactly zero resultant drag in an ideal flow. Derive the field and identify what a real measurement must add.

    3. Curvature pressure, wetting and capillary rise

      The water height in a narrow glass tube is a measurement of an interface force. Starting from a surface energy explains both its direction and its dependence on tube radius.

    4. Critical depth and the hydraulic jump

      In a wide shallow channel the same discharge can pass as a shallow fast stream or as a deeper slow stream. A gradual hump and an abrupt jump connect depths through different conservation laws.

    5. A viscous squeeze film as a rate-dependent support

      Bring two smooth circular disks slowly together with a thin layer of liquid between them. The force needed depends strongly on gap size even when neither surface is elastic.

    6. How turbulent momentum transport produces a logarithmic velocity profile

      The time-averaged velocity next to a wall looks smooth, even when individual velocity records fluctuate rapidly. Decompose the momentum flux, derive the wall scaling, and recover a shear stress from two measurements in the logarithmic region.

    7. How gravity and surface tension select different wave speeds

      A long crest and a millimetre-scale ripple on the same deep liquid can travel at different speeds. Derive their dispersion relation from two surface boundary conditions and distinguish the motion of a crest from the motion of a narrow packet.

    8. Watch a wetting front approach the capillary height

      Dip two clean glass tubes of different inner radii into the same wetting liquid. A microscope following only the first few millimetres of rise can give a different impression of which tube fills more readily than a photograph taken at equilibrium. Start with the force on one meniscus, derive a transient relation including gravity, and learn which measurements can distinguish a change of viscosity from a change of surface condition.

    Applied study

    Connect a growing boundary layer to wall shear, momentum loss and drag.

    How does a surface slow down a moving fluid?

    Upstream of a smooth plate, every parcel in a uniform stream moves at speed \(U\). At the plate the no-slip condition brings the fluid to rest. Momentum diffuses across the flow while the stream carries it downstream, so the region slowed by the wall thickens with distance. The wall shear determines how much power is needed to move a plate through the fluid.

    Consider steady two-dimensional incompressible laminar flow at constant density \(\rho\), dynamic viscosity \(\mu\) and zero streamwise pressure gradient. Measure \(x\) from the leading edge and \(y\) normal to the plate. When the viscous layer is thin relative to \(x\), the streamwise momentum and continuity equations reduce to

    \[u u_x+v u_y=\nu u_{yy},\]
    \[u_x+v_y=0,\]
    \[\nu=\mu/\rho.\]

    At the wall \(u=v=0\), while \(u\) approaches \(U\) far above it. Balancing \(U^2/x\) with \(\nu U/\delta^2\) gives the thickness scale \(\delta\sim\sqrt{\nu x/U}\). The thickness increases downstream even though \(\delta/x\) falls. Doubling the speed gives the fluid less time to diffuse momentum before it reaches the observation point.

    Relate a measured velocity slope to drag

    Profiles at different stations have nearly the same shape when plotted against \(\eta=y\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\). The laminar Blasius solution gives a wall slope \(\partial_y u|_0=0.332\,U\sqrt{U/(\nu x)}\). Therefore, with \(Re_x=Ux/\nu\),

    \[\tau_w=\mu\,\partial_yu|_0,\]
    \[c_f(x)=\frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U^2}=\frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\]
    \[\delta_{99}\simeq\frac{5.0x}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.\]

    The last thickness is the height at which \(u=0.99U\). Integrating \(\tau_w\) from the leading edge to length \(L\) gives the drag on one face per unit width,

    \[D/b=0.664\rho U^2\sqrt{\nu L/U}.\]

    The mean friction coefficient over the plate is twice the local coefficient at its trailing edge. Surface roughness, transition to turbulence and strong pressure gradients change this result.

    Worked example · Sample a plate halfway along

    Use \(U=1.0\) m/s, \(\rho=1.0\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(\mu=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\) and \(x=0.50\) m. Then \(\nu=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(Re_x=5000\). The 99% thickness is 35.4 mm, the friction coefficient is 0.00939, and the shear stress is

    \[0.5(1)(1)^2(0.00939)=0.00470\,\mathrm{Pa}.\]

    At \(x=1.00\) m the layer has grown to 50 mm and the wall shear has fallen to 0.00332 Pa. The amount of fluid affected has increased while the near-wall velocity gradient has weakened.

    Transfer problem · Use a pressure drop to infer pipe flow

    In a sufficiently long straight pipe of radius \(R\), the viscous regions growing from the walls merge and the velocity profile stops changing downstream. For a fluid cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(L\), the pressure force \(\Delta p\,\pi r^2\) balances the resisting shear force \(2\pi r L|\tau|\). Hence \(|\tau|=\Delta p\,r/(2L)\). Since \(du/dr=-|\tau|/\mu\), integration with \(u(R)=0\) gives

    \[u(r)=\Delta p(R^2-r^2)/(4\mu L).\]

    Integrating over circular rings yields

    \[Q=\pi R^4\Delta p/(8\mu L).\]

    A water line has \(R=5.0\) mm, \(L=2.0\) m, \(\mu=0.0010\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\) and \(\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Delivering \(Q=1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}\) requires \(\Delta p=8.15\) Pa over the developed portion. The mean speed is \(Q/(\pi R^2)=0.0127\) m/s and the diameter-based Reynolds number is 127, consistent with laminar flow. Halving the pipe radius at fixed flow multiplies the required pressure by sixteen. Compare the derivation for a parallel-plate channel.

    Exercises

    1. Choose an appropriate force measurement

    A 0.20 m wide plate is exposed on one face from its leading edge to \(L=1.00\) m under the conditions above. What force should a balance measure? What bias would follow from using the trailing-edge wall shear across the entire plate?

    Show the drag estimate

    The integrated force is

    \[0.20(0.664)(1)(1)^2\sqrt{10^{-4}(1)/1}=0.00133\,\mathrm{N}.\]

    Using the final local shear gives only \(0.00332(1)(0.20)=0.000664\) N. Wall shear varies as \(x^{-1/2}\), so the leading part contributes more drag per unit length.

    2. Distinguish dimensionless and dimensional changes

    At the same station, double the free-stream speed without changing the fluid. Predict the changes in \(\delta_{99}\), \(c_f\), \(\tau_w\) and power lost to skin friction at fixed plate length.

    Show the scaling argument

    Both thickness and friction coefficient scale as \(U^{-1/2}\), giving a factor 0.707. Dimensional shear includes \(U^2\), hence grows by \(2^{3/2}=2.83\). Drag has the same speed exponent and required power is \(DU\), which grows by \(2^{5/2}=5.66\).
