Why does a magnetic field flatten duct flow?

Balance viscous and magnetic forces to find the length scale of a Hartmann layer.

Prerequisites Viscous shear, electric current and scaling.

Follow the current across a duct

With a fixed pressure gradient, a liquid between two stationary plates flows fastest at the centre. Apply a magnetic field across a conducting liquid and the profile develops a broad, nearly uniform core. We can predict where the remaining velocity change takes place.

Take two wide plates at \(z=\pm b\), steady laminar flow \(u(z)\) in the \(x\) direction, and a uniform field \(B\) in the \(z\) direction. Assume the induced magnetic field is small relative to the applied field. Away from the side walls, Ohm’s law gives transverse current density \(j_y=\sigma(E_y-uB)\), where \(\sigma\) is the electrical conductivity. An open circuit has zero net transverse current. In the parallel-plate model \(E_y\) is uniform, so \(E_y=B\bar u\), with \(\bar u\) the gap-average speed.

The streamwise magnetic force per volume is \(j_y B=\sigma B^2(\bar u-u)\). It slows fluid moving faster than the mean and accelerates slower fluid. No slip still fixes \(u=0\) at both walls. These two effects concentrate the velocity gradient close to each wall.

Derive the wall length scale

Write \(G=-dp/dx\) for the positive driving gradient and \(\mu\) for dynamic viscosity. Axial momentum balance reads \(0=G+\mu u''+\sigma B^2(\bar u-u)\). Set \(U=\bar u+G/(\sigma B^2)\). The velocity deficit \(w=U-u\) then satisfies

\[w''=w/\delta^2,\]

with

\[\delta=\frac{1}{B}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\sigma}},\]
\[\mathrm{Ha}=\frac{b}{\delta}.\]

Symmetry requires \(w'(0)=0\). No slip requires \(w(b)=U\). Hence \(u(z)=U[1-\cosh(z/\delta)/\cosh(b/\delta)]\). When \(\mathrm{Ha}\gg1\), a distance \(d\) inward from the wall gives \(u/U\simeq1-e^{-d/\delta}\). At \(d=\delta\) the fluid has reached 63% of the core speed. This is the Hartmann layer at a wall perpendicular to the field. Hartmann’s parallel-plate analysis (1937, PDF).

Locate the layer in a liquid-metal channel

Take a 20 mm full gap, \(\mu=2.16\times10^{-3}\) Pa·s, \(\sigma=3.3\times10^6\) S/m and \(B=0.128\) T. Use the half-gap \(b=10\) mm to compute \(\mathrm{Ha}=Bb\sqrt{\sigma/\mu}\simeq50\). The layer thickness is

\[b/50=0.200\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

A probe 0.60 mm from the wall is three layer thicknesses inward and reads about \(1-e^{-3}=95.0\%\) of the core speed.

At \(G=10\) Pa/m the speed scale also follows from force balance. Integrate momentum from the centre to the wall. The magnetic term integrates to zero because the open-circuit current averages to zero, giving \(\mu u'(b)=-Gb\). For a thin layer \(u'(b)\simeq-U/\delta\), so \(U\simeq Gb\delta/\mu=9.26\) mm/s. A thinner layer gives greater wall drag at any given core speed.

Tutorial exercises

Move the probe through the layer

At the field and pressure gradient of the example, where should a probe be placed to reach 90% of core speed? Predict how that distance and the core speed change when \(B\) doubles.

Show the solution

Solve \(0.9=1-e^{-d/\delta}\), giving \(d=\delta\ln 10=0.461\) mm. Doubling \(B\) halves \(\delta\), so the new distance is 0.230 mm. At the same \(G\), \(Gb\delta/\mu\) also halves to about 4.63 mm/s. These estimates require well-separated wall layers.

Switch the magnetic field off

With \(G\), \(\mu\) and \(b\) unchanged, derive the zero-field profile and its centre speed from the original momentum equation.

Show the solution

At \(B=0\), \(\mu u''=-G\). Integrating with \(u'(0)=0\) and \(u(b)=0\) gives

\[u=G(b^2-z^2)/(2\mu).\]

The reference centre speed is 0.231 m/s. The profile is parabolic across the entire gap, so extrapolating the thin-layer estimate to zero field would give an unphysical result.

Optional numerical comparison

The OneSimulation cross-section uses a 140 mm by 20 mm rectangular duct with insulating, no-slip walls and \(G=10\) Pa/m. Read its mid-width traverse at \(\mathrm{Ha}=50\). The reported distance from the wall to 63.2% of the centre speed is 0.202 mm, against the parallel-plate prediction of 0.200 mm. This comparison uses the centre of the wide duct, where the narrow side walls have the least influence. Hartmann duct calculation.
