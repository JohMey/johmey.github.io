How far does a voltage change spread along a passive fibre?

Balance axial current against membrane leakage and calculate the distance over which a steady voltage decays.

Prerequisites Ohm’s law and second-order differential equations.

Balance axial current and membrane leak

Imagine holding one end of a thin passive fibre 25 mV above its resting potential. A distant electrode reads a smaller change. To predict what it reads, picture a conducting core surrounded by a uniformly leaky membrane. Take the outside potential as constant. Work at steady state, with a constant cross-section and a membrane leak proportional to the voltage change.

Let \(v(x)=V(x)-E_L\) be voltage relative to the leak reversal potential. In the core, Ohm’s law gives axial current \(I=-\sigma A\,dv/dx\), with conductivity \(\sigma\) and cross-sectional area \(A\). A segment of length \(dx\) has membrane area \(P\,dx\), where \(P\) is its perimeter. Its outward leak current is \(g_LvP\,dx\), where \(g_L\) is conductance per unit membrane area.

The difference between current entering and leaving the segment supplies that leak. Thus \(dI/dx=-g_LPv\). Substituting Ohm’s law gives

\[\lambda^2 v''=v,\]
\[\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\beta g_L}},\]
\[\beta=\frac{P}{A}.\]

The length \(\lambda\) grows when axial conduction improves and shrinks when membrane leakage increases. Membrane capacitance affects the charging transient. Once the voltage is steady the capacitive current is zero.

What changes at a sealed end?

A voltage clamp fixes \(v(0)=v_0\). Sealing the far end at \(x=L\) forces \(I(L)=0\), so \(v'(L)=0\). The voltage at that end is free to settle to a nonzero value.

Write the two independent solutions of the cable equation as \(\cosh((L-x)/\lambda)\) and \(\sinh((L-x)/\lambda)\). At \(x=L\), the first has zero slope and the second has nonzero slope. The sealed condition therefore removes the second term. The clamp sets the remaining coefficient, giving

\[v(x)=v_0\,\frac{\cosh((L-x)/\lambda)}{\cosh(L/\lambda)}.\]

On a semi-infinite fibre, bounded voltage far from the clamp instead selects \(v=v_0e^{-x/\lambda}\). At one length constant the signal on that long fibre has fallen to about 37% of its initial value.

Further study · Gerstner et al. derive the time-dependent cable equation and explain how branching changes its boundary conditions in Spatial Structure: The Dendritic Tree.

Predict two electrode readings

Take \(L=60\) mm, \(\sigma=1.0\) S/m, \(\beta=2000\) m⁻¹ and \(g_L=2.0\) S/m². Then \(\lambda=\sqrt{1/(2000\times 2)}\) m \(=15.81\) mm. With \(E_L=-65\) mV and \(v_0=25\) mV the clamp holds \(V(0)=-40\) mV.

At the midpoint, \(v=25\cosh(30/15.81)/\cosh(60/15.81)=3.83\) mV. At the sealed tip the numerator becomes 1, giving \(v(L)=1.12\) mV. An electrode measuring voltage relative to the outside would read \(-61.17\) mV at the midpoint and \(-63.88\) mV at the tip.

Tutorial exercises

Shorten the fibre

Cut the fibre to length \(L=\lambda\), seal its new tip and apply the same clamp. Predict the tip depolarisation. How would using the semi-infinite expression change the prediction?

Show the solution

The finite cable gives

\[v(L)=25/\cosh(1)=16.2\,\mathrm{mV}.\]

The long-fibre expression would give \(25/e=9.20\) mV. At a sealed tip all incoming axial current must have leaked out upstream. A fibre continuing past the electrode carries additional current beyond it and has a steeper local voltage drop.

Change the leak

Return to the 60 mm fibre and multiply \(g_L\) by four. Find the new length constant and predict the tip depolarisation.

Show the solution

The square-root law gives

\[\lambda'=15.81/2=7.91\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The tip is now 7.59 length constants from the clamp, so \(v(L)=25/\cosh(7.59)\simeq0.0253\) mV. Halving the length constant produces a much larger fractional reduction in the distant signal.

Optional numerical comparison
