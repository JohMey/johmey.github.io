Why does a beam bend less when turned on edge?

Use moment balance to predict how far the tip of a cantilever moves when you rotate its cross-section.

Prerequisites Force and moment balance, elementary integration.

Take a cut through a loaded beam

Clamp a rectangular ruler to a bench and hang a weight from its free end. Turn the ruler on edge and hang the same weight again. Its length, mass and material are unchanged. Which property of the cross-section controls the motion of the tip?

Let \(x\) measure distance from the clamp, \(L\) be the span and \(F\) the downward end force. Cut the beam at \(x\) and take moments of the free segment about the cut. Its internal bending moment has magnitude \(M(x)=F(L-x)\). The largest moment occurs at the clamp. This moment stretches the upper fibres and shortens the lower ones. The beam can carry these internal stresses while it is at rest.

For a slender, homogeneous beam, assume that each cross-section stays plane and perpendicular to the bent centreline. A fibre at distance \(z\) from the neutral axis has strain of magnitude \(|z|\kappa\), where \(\kappa\) is the local curvature. Hooke’s law converts strain to stress. Integrating the moment of that stress over the section gives

\[M=E\kappa I,\]
\[I=\int_A z^2\,dA=\frac{bh^3}{12}.\]

Here \(E\) is Young’s modulus, \(b\) is width and \(h\) is vertical depth. Material further from the neutral axis contributes more to the bending stiffness \(EI\).

Use the clamp to fix slope and displacement

Measure deflection \(w\) positively downwards. For small slopes \(\kappa\simeq w''\). The clamp fixes \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\). Integrating \(EIw''=F(L-x)\) from the clamp first gives \(EIw'=F(Lx-x^2/2)\), then

\[w(x)=\frac{Fx^2(3L-x)}{6EI},\]
\[w(L)=\frac{FL^3}{3EI}.\]

This is a centreline prediction for small elastic deflection. Short beams may also deform appreciably in shear, and the three-dimensional stress close to the clamp depends on how it grips the beam.

Further study · David Roylance develops the slope and deflection of beams under different loads in Beam Displacements (MIT, PDF).

Turn a 120 mm aluminium beam on edge

Take \(F=30\) N, \(E=70\) GPa and an 18 mm wide, 12 mm deep section. In metres, \(I=0.018(0.012)^3/12=2.592\times10^{-9}\) m⁴. The tip moves down by \(30(0.120)^3/[3(70\times10^9)I]=0.0952\) mm.

Turn the same section through 90°. With 18 mm now vertical, \(I\) grows by \((18/12)^2=2.25\). The same force gives \(0.0952/2.25=0.0423\) mm of deflection. The square in this ratio appears because width and depth both change during the rotation.

Tutorial on bending stiffness

1. Move the load inwards

Place the 30 N load halfway along the original flat beam. How far does the point under the load move? Does the free tip move by the same amount?

Solution to the load-position problem

Write \(a=L/2\). The loaded portion behaves as a cantilever of length \(a\), so

\[w(a)=Fa^3/(3EI)=0.0119\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Beyond the load the bending moment is zero. The remaining length continues at slope \(w'(a)=Fa^2/(2EI)\), giving \(w(L)=w(a)+(L-a)w'(a)=0.0298\) mm. A straight segment can have a nonzero slope.

2. Compare stresses after rotation

Return the 30 N load to the free end. Find the nominal bending stress magnitude at the outer fibre halfway along the beam in each orientation. Explain why the stress ratio differs from the tip-deflection ratio.

Solution to the stress comparison

At midspan \(M=30(0.060)=1.80\) N·m. The stress \(M(h/2)/I\) is \(4.17\) MPa with 12 mm vertical. Turning the section on edge multiplies \(I\) by 2.25 and \(h/2\) by 1.5, so the stress falls to \(2.78\) MPa. Deflection depends on \(I\) alone for a given material and load, while stress also depends on the fibre’s distance from the neutral axis.

Optional numerical comparison

Sample the vertical displacement at the centre of the loaded end face. For the original orientation, compare its magnitude with 0.0952 mm and check that the relative difference is below 1% on the supplied mesh.
