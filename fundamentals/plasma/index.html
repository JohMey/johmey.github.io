What sets the rhythm of an electron plasma?

Derive the restoring force on displaced electrons and predict how oscillation frequency depends on density.

A restoring force from charge separation

Compress a small group of electrons in a plasma and let go. Their motion sets up an electric field that pushes them back. They overshoot because they carry momentum, and the process repeats. Why does this oscillation measure the electron density?

Begin with a neutral plasma of uniform electron density \(n_0\). Take singly charged ions as stationary over one electron oscillation. Ignore collisions, external magnetic fields and thermal pressure for now. Label each electron fluid element by its equilibrium position \(x\), and let \(\xi(x,t)\) be a small longitudinal displacement. Two nearby elements initially \(dx\) apart become separated by \((1+\partial_x\xi)dx\). Conservation of particle number then gives the density change \(n_1=-n_0\partial_x\xi\) to first order. A region where displacements converge gathers excess electrons.

Closing the equations for one spatial mode

Take a sinusoidal disturbance in a periodic region with no imposed mean field. The net charge perturbation is \(-en_1\), where \(e\) is the positive elementary charge. Gauss’s law gives

\[\partial_x E=-en_1/\epsilon_0=(en_0/\epsilon_0)\partial_x\xi.\]

Integrate over \(x\). For this zero-mean mode the constant vanishes, leaving \(E=en_0\xi/\epsilon_0\). An electron with positive displacement thus feels a force \(-eE\) in the opposite direction.

\[m_e\ddot{\xi}=-\frac{n_0 e^2}{\epsilon_0}\xi,\]
\[\omega_{pe}=\sqrt{\frac{n_0 e^2}{m_e\epsilon_0}}.\]

This is the harmonic oscillator equation. In the cold model each nonzero wave number oscillates at the same angular frequency. For a standing disturbance released from rest, twice during each period the density perturbation vanishes and the electrons pass through equilibrium at their greatest speed.

From density to a measurable period

At \(n_0=1.00\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), use \(e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C\), \(m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(\epsilon_0=8.85\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}\). Substitution gives \(\omega_{pe}^2=3.18\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}\) and \(\omega_{pe}=5.64\times10^9\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). A probe counting cycles measures

\[f=\omega_{pe}/(2\pi)=898\,\mathrm{MHz}.\]

Neighbouring positive peaks at a fixed probe are \(T=1/f=1.11\,\mathrm{ns}\) apart.

Tutorial · Reading a plasma oscillation

  1. A similar cold plasma gives consecutive positive field peaks separated by 2.00 ns. Infer the density. Predict how peak spacing changes if this density is then doubled.

    Worked solution

    Use \(\omega=2\pi/T=3.142\times10^9\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(n_0=m_e\epsilon_0\omega^2/e^2=3.10\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). A longer measured period implies a weaker restoring field at the same displacement. Doubling this density decreases the period by \(1/\sqrt 2\), to 1.41 ns.

  2. Return to the worked density. Suppose the electrons have thermal energy \(k_BT_e=1.00\,\mathrm{eV}\) and the wave has wavelength 5.00 mm. For a long wave in a Maxwellian plasma the leading pressure correction is \(\omega^2\simeq\omega_{pe}^2+3k^2 v_{\rm th}^2\), with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\) and \(v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{k_BT_e/m_e}\). Estimate the fractional shift and explain its sign.

    Worked solution

    Converting the energy to \(1.602\times10^{-19}\) J gives \(v_{\rm th}=4.19\times10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Then \(k=1.257\times10^3\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and \(kv_{\rm th}/\omega_{pe}=0.0934\). Hence

    \[\omega/\omega_{pe}\simeq\sqrt{1+3(0.0934)^2}=1.0130.\]

    The frequency rises by 1.3%. A pressure gradient also opposes compression, adding a restoring force. The small ratio just calculated is \(k\lambda_D\), the condition for this long-wave approximation.

Numerical comparison · Measuring the wave

The periodic OneSimulation case uses a 5 mm region at \(10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), with 64 grid cells and 400 electron simulation particles per cell. It follows a sinusoidal perturbation over six periods. Compare the rate at which the phase of the electric-field mode advances with \(5.64\times10^9\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) for cold electrons and \(5.71\times10^9\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) at 1 eV. The published fits agree with the cold and warm references to within 0.3% across its 0, 1 and 3 eV runs. Inspect the particle calculation and the frequency measurement.
