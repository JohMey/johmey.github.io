Choosing an output mirror for a small optical resonator

A laboratory has two interchangeable output mirrors for the same symmetric air cavity. One transmits twice as much light as the other at each reflection. Determine which one can deliver light at the available pump, and use a beam-width measurement and a ring-down trace to test the assumptions.

Predict the geometry before inserting gain

Both mirrors have curvature radius \(R=0.50\,\mathrm m\), separation \(L=0.30\,\mathrm m\), and operating wavelength \(600\,\mathrm{nm}\). Find the Rayleigh length, the waist radius and the spot radius at the mirror. Calculate the fraction of Gaussian power beyond a circular mirror of clear radius \(0.60\,\mathrm{mm}\).

Work through the solution

Symmetry fixes \(z_R=\sqrt{(L/2)(R-L/2)}=0.2291\,\mathrm m\). Thus

\[w_0=\sqrt{\lambda z_R/\pi}=0.2092\,\mathrm{mm},\]
\[w_m=w_0\sqrt{1+(L/2z_R)^2}=0.2500\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Integrating the radial Gaussian \(r\exp(-2r^2/w_m^2)\) from \(a\) to infinity and dividing by the total integral gives

\[e^{-2a^2/w_m^2}=9.9\times10^{-6}.\]

Neglecting clipping against percent-level mirror losses is plausible for this centred mode.

Set the separation-to-radius ratio to 0.60 and verify the mirror spot

Predict the operating point for each mirror

The input mirror has intensity reflectance \(R_1=0.98\), distributed intensity loss is \(\alpha=0.10\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\), and uniform small-signal gain is \(g_0=0.50\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\). Compare output mirrors with \(R_2=0.90\) and \(R_2=0.80\). Use \(g(I)=g_0/(1+I/I_s)\) and estimate transmitted intensity relative to \(I_s\) for both.

Work through the solution

The thresholds are \(\alpha-\ln(R_1R_2)/(2L)=0.3093\) and \(0.5056\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\). The first mirror supports circulating intensity \(I/I_s=0.50/0.3093-1=0.617\), with transmitted fraction \(0.10\times0.617=0.0617\) relative to \(I_s\). The second has no positive stationary lasing solution at this pump in the ideal model. Doubling transmission per encounter has raised the required gain above that available.

Run the two reflectance settings at the same pump

Check the losses using a passive decay

For the first mirror combination a passive ring-down fit gives an intensity lifetime of \(9.0\,\mathrm{ns}\). Under the uniform model infer the effective attenuation coefficient and compare it to the stated distributed loss. Could the previously estimated clipping of the centred beam explain the discrepancy?

Work through the solution

With an air round trip,

\[1/(c\tau)=g_{\rm th,eff}=0.3706\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}.\]

Subtract the mirror term \(-\ln(0.98\times0.90)/0.60=0.2093\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) to infer \(\alpha_{\rm eff}=0.1613\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\). The excess is about \(0.0613\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\), corresponding to \(1-e^{-2L(0.0613)}=3.61\%\) extra round-trip loss. Two centred aperture losses of order \(10^{-5}\) cannot account for this observation under the assumed mode. Alignment or additional scattering could be examined directly.

Transfer the measurement to a changed geometry

Increase the separation to \(0.90\,\mathrm m\) with unchanged curvature and wavelength. Estimate the new mirror clipping for \(a=0.60\,\mathrm{mm}\). Explain why using the original measured attenuation as a material coefficient could fail.

Work through the solution

Now \(z_R=0.150\,\mathrm m\), \(w_0=0.1693\,\mathrm{mm}\), and \(w_m=0.535\,\mathrm{mm}\). The estimated per-encounter clipped fraction rises to \(e^{-2(0.60/0.535)^2}\approx0.081\). This magnitude is substantial. The field shape may itself be altered by the aperture, so a new ring-down and beam-profile measurement should accompany the estimate. Geometry-dependent clipping can be folded into an inferred attenuation at one geometry without representing distributed absorption.
