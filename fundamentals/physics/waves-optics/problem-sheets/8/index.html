Measure a double source with a two-aperture interferometer

Two distant point emitters cannot be separated in a direct image. An observer combines the light collected by two small apertures a variable distance apart. Derive how the visibility of the combined fringes can reveal the angular separation and how a faint companion differs from imperfect temporal coherence.

Add the light from one emitter

A plane wave from a small sky angle \(\theta\) arrives at apertures separated by projected baseline \(B\). One arm has an adjustable phase delay \(\phi\). Starting with the complex field amplitude from each aperture, derive the intensity as the delay is scanned. State which phase one can remove by choosing the origin of the time coordinate.

Work through the solution

The path difference for the arriving wave is \(B\sin\theta\simeq B\theta\). Its contribution to the relative phase is \(2\pi B\theta/\lambda\). Equal aperture amplitudes add as \(a[1+e^{i(\phi+2\pi B\theta/\lambda)}]\), up to an overall phase. Taking the squared modulus gives \(2|a|^2[1+\cos(\phi+2\pi B\theta/\lambda)]\). An overall phase disappears from this expression, while a relative phase remains observable when the delay is scanned. The calculation assumes equal throughput and matching polarisation in the two arms.

Combine independent sources and recover their separation

Let two mutually incoherent emitters of equal brightness have angles \(\pm\delta/2\). Show why intensities add between emitters, even though fields added between apertures for each emitter. Find the first visibility minimum and use a first null at \(B=20\,\mathrm m\), \(\lambda=600\,\mathrm{nm}\) to determine \(\delta\).

Work through the solution

Each source is coherent with its own delayed copy for sufficiently narrow bandwidth. The relative phase between the two different emitters wanders during the exposure, removing their mutual cross term. Average the two cosine fringes. Their sum is proportional to \(1+\cos(\phi)\cos(\pi B\delta/\lambda)\). The measured visibility, defined by the extremal intensities, is the absolute value of the second cosine.

\[\mathcal V=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}=|\cos(\pi B\delta/\lambda)|,\]
\[\delta=\frac{\lambda}{2B_{\rm first}}.\]

These data give \(1.5\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{rad}\), or about 3.09 milliarcseconds. A single nonzero visibility may correspond to many angular separations because the cosine repeats. Scanning outward from small baselines distinguishes the first null from subsequent ones. A baseline perpendicular to the separation measures zero projected separation, irrespective of the distance on the sky.

Infer an intensity ratio from a filled minimum

The second emitter has intensity \(r\) times that of the first with \(0<r\le1\). Derive the new visibility. A well calibrated instrument obtains minimum 0.25. Infer \(r\). Decide whether unequal emitter brightness can move the first minimum in this ideal model.

Work through the solution

The normalised complex fringe amplitude, after dropping an overall phase, is \((1+r e^{i2\pi B\delta/\lambda})/(1+r)\). Its modulus is the visibility. At odd half-fringe offsets the phasors oppose and the minimum is \((1-r)/(1+r)\). Inversion gives

\[r=(1-0.25)/(1+0.25)=0.60.\]

The phase of opposition is unchanged. Interchanging source labels leaves the measured visibility invariant, so it identifies a ratio up to that choice.

Design an observation that separates the instrument from the source

Change angular separation and intensity ratio before reading any numerical values. Compare the location of the first trough with its depth. A calibrator at effectively zero angular size yields visibility 0.80. A binary has raw minimum 0.20 in the same setup. What can be inferred if the entire contrast loss is multiplicative? Which additional observations test this assumption?

Work through the solution

Dividing by the calibrator visibility gives an intrinsic trough of 0.25 and the same inferred intensity ratio 0.60. The model assumes the instrument suppresses contrast by a common factor at that baseline, wavelength and time. Observing the calibrator as a function of baseline checks one part of that assumption. Narrowing the spectral band checks whether the fringe phase changes appreciably across the passband. Turning the baseline tests whether the measured spacing follows the projected binary direction. Each intervention singles out a different step in the derivation.
