A near-field measurement that retains the relative phases

Imagine a plane transmission mask made by repeating one profile \(t(x)\) every distance \(d\). Coherent, normally incident illumination makes the complex transverse field immediately behind the mask proportional to this transmission. In a far-field experiment one might separate diffraction orders and measure their individual intensities. To reconstruct the mask in free space, however, the orders must illuminate the same region of the screen and preserve their relative phases. Assume an effectively infinite mask and a scalar field with wavelength \(\lambda\) in the propagation medium. A wide finite illuminated region can approximate this idealisation near its centre for a limited distance.

Because \(t(x+d)=t(x)\), its Fourier series requires only transverse wave numbers \(k_{x,m}=2\pi m/d\) with integer \(m\). Write the input as \(\sum c_m e^{2\pi i m x/d}\). The homogeneous Helmholtz equation for a single plane-wave component is \(k_x^2+k_z^2=k^2\) with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). Choose \(k_z=\sqrt{k^2-k_x^2}\) with nonnegative propagation or decay, so a forward field propagates as \(e^{ik_z z}\) and an evanescent component decays. This branch choice is physical; the opposite choice could turn unresolved fine structure into unbounded growth with distance.

Subtract the common carrier \(e^{ikz}\). It has unit modulus in a lossless medium and cannot affect intensity. When \(|k_x|\ll k\), expand the square root:

\[k_z-k=-k_x^2/(2k)-k_x^4/(8k^3)+\cdots.\]

Keeping the quadratic part gives a phase proportional to the square of an integer. This arithmetic is the source of the rephasing; it does not require a caustic or refraction at an interface.

Derive the first complete field recurrence

With \(\xi=x/d\), the paraxial field envelope a distance \(z\) behind the mask is

\[U(\xi,z)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} c_m e^{2\pi i m\xi}\exp\!\left(-i\pi\lambda z m^2/d^2\right).\]

Demand that the propagation factor be one for every integer \(m\), including \(m=1\). The first positive distance that meets this condition for a generic mask is \(z_T=2d^2/\lambda\). Each factor becomes

\[e^{-2\pi i m^2}=1.\]

The complex field is thus restored up to the common carrier, a stronger result than merely recovering an intensity pattern. Some masks have extra symmetries or contain only selected orders and can give earlier intensity revivals. Define the length used in a report explicitly, particularly when shifted self-images are included.

For a 12 micrometre period and wavelength 0.60 micrometre, the full paraxial recurrence is 480 micrometres, short enough that the separation of individual far-field orders would be a misleading picture. Doubling the period at fixed wavelength produces a fourfold distance. Doubling wavelength while holding the period fixed halves the predicted length and at the same time increases the angles of the orders. The latter change makes the paraxial expansion less accurate, so variation of \(\lambda\) tests two effects at once.

Half a recurrence shifts the image; a quarter combines two images

At \(z_T/2\) the factor is

\[e^{-i\pi m^2}=(-1)^m.\]

A spatial shift by half a period contributes exactly the same phase to Fourier order \(m\), since \(e^{\pi i m}=(-1)^m\). Thus the field becomes \(t(\xi+1/2)\). A photograph at this plane can look identical if one ignores the grating origin; registration against a marked edge discloses the shift. The derivation depends on integers modulo two, not on the detailed transmission of a transparent or absorbing structure.

At \(z_T/4\) an even order has factor one and an odd order factor \(-i\). Split the field into the components even and odd under a half-period translation. Their respective projectors acting on a pattern are \([t(\xi)+t(\xi+1/2)]/2\) and \([t(\xi)-t(\xi+1/2)]/2\). Recombining with the two phases gives

\[U(\xi,z_T/4)=\frac{1-i}{2}t(\xi)+\frac{1+i}{2}t(\xi+1/2).\]

These are complex amplitudes, so they must be summed before taking an absolute square. For a real mask, the coefficients differ in phase by a right angle and the two cross terms cancel in intensity. The quarter-plane intensity is then the average of the intensities in the original and shifted masks. A complex phase mask need not have that cancellation. Replace a real transmission by one with the same modulus and a sinusoidal phase and the input photograph can remain unchanged while the propagated pattern changes markedly.

This follows by multiplying the neglected quartic term in \(k_z\) by \(2d^2/\lambda\). Which aspects of the ideal experiment survive a laboratory source?

A finite wavelength distribution produces a distribution of propagation phases. At a fixed screen location the camera records the intensity averaged over mutually incoherent wavelengths. Orders that agree in phase at the central wavelength accumulate a spread proportional to \(m^2 z\). Fine structure requiring higher orders therefore loses contrast first. Averaging wavelengths as fields instead of intensities would represent a phase-locked coherent source and a detector model with different temporal resolution. The distinction matters most when interpreting a blurred revival as evidence of a faulty grating.

A finite angular spread of incident plane waves has a different signature. Under a small tilt \(\theta\), the transverse wave number becomes \(k\theta+2\pi m/d\). Squaring produces a common phase, the usual term in \(m^2\), and a term linear in \(m\). A linear Fourier phase shifts the whole pattern laterally by approximately \(z\theta\); averaging over unknown incident angles thus convolves it with a distribution of translations. A finite lateral aperture additionally limits the distance over which all orders overlap. These mechanisms suggest separate controls: narrowing spectral width, improving collimation, and enlarging illuminated area alter different parts of the experiment.

For quantitative comparison register the predicted and observed patterns in a common coordinate, subtract camera dark level, and state whether normalisation removes total-power information. An intensity correlation near one may mean that two profiles have the same shape even when one carries much less power. A complex-field discrepancy asks an even stricter question that normally requires phase-sensitive measurements. The numerical field is available in the notebook, but the physical camera does not automatically measure that entire array.

Tutorial on recurrence, fractional images and coherence

A grating of period 20 micrometres makes a full paraxial image 1.60 mm downstream. Determine the wavelength and the location of the shifted image.

Work through the solution

Rearranging gives \(\lambda=2d^2/z_T=0.50\) micrometre. The half-period-shifted pattern appears at 0.80 mm, displaced by 10 micrometres. If the recorded plane were actually the shifted rather than full recurrence, the inferred wavelength would differ by a factor of two. A fixed lateral reference resolves that ambiguity.

Only the constant order and the pair \(m=\pm2\) are present. How soon does the exact paraxial complex envelope recur when distances are expressed using the nominal period \(d\)?

Work through the solution

The variable propagation factor is \(e^{-2\pi i\,4z/z_T}\). It reaches one first at \(z_T/4\). The actual mask constructed from these orders has fundamental period \(d/2\), whose ordinary Talbot length \(2(d/2)^2/\lambda\) gives exactly the same answer.

A screen is at 1.0 mm and incident directions vary over an rms 1 mrad. Estimate the translation blur for a grating of period 10 micrometres. Which harmonic is most sensitive?

Work through the solution

Small-angle translations have rms width about \(1.0\) micrometre, one tenth of a grating period. For a Gaussian angular distribution, averaging the intensity harmonic with lateral wave number \(2\pi n/d\) multiplies its amplitude by \(\exp[-(2\pi n z\sigma_\theta/d)^2/2]\). The fundamental retains about 82 per cent and the second harmonic about 45 per cent. Higher spatial frequencies are progressively more sensitive.
