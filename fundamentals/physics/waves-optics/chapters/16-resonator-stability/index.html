When do two curved mirrors support a confined beam?

A ray launched slightly away from the axis may stay near it for many round trips or walk off a mirror. A single round-trip matrix predicts which behaviour occurs and selects the Gaussian beam that can reproduce its wavefront.

Multiply one complete round trip

Use a paraxial ray vector \((x,\theta)\), with \(\theta=dx/dz\). Free propagation through distance \(L\) changes \(x\) by \(L\theta\), leaving \(\theta\) fixed. In unfolded coordinates reflection from a concave spherical mirror of radius \(R>0\) changes \(\theta\) by \(-2x/R\). The factor two arises from the change in direction of the local surface normal. Represent propagation and reflection by

\[P=\begin{pmatrix}1&L\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-2/R&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

For two identical mirrors a round trip is \(C=(MP)^2\) starting just after a reflection. Each constituent has determinant one. The half-trip has trace \(2-2L/R=2g\), so the identity \(\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=(\operatorname{tr}A)^2-2\det A\) gives

\[\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}C=2g^2-1.\]

Its eigenvalues are reciprocal. If their trace lies strictly between −2 and +2 they are complex conjugates of modulus one and repeated round trips remain bounded. If the absolute trace exceeds two, a component grows exponentially. This produces the usual symmetric resonator range \(0<L<2R\). The exact confocal point \(L=R\) has \(C=-I\) and bounded rays despite the repeated eigenvalue. Real alignment and apertures also influence losses.

Match the field curvature to the mirrors

For a symmetric cavity a fundamental Gaussian has its waist at the midpoint. At distance \(z\) from that waist its wavefront radius is \(R_w(z)=z+z_R^2/z\), where \(z_R=\pi w_0^2/\lambda\). At the mirror \(z=L/2\), a reproducing wave must have the spherical mirror curvature. Equating \(R_w(L/2)\) to \(R\) gives

\[z_R^2=\frac L2\left(R-\frac L2\right),\]
\[w_0=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda z_R}{\pi}},\]
\[w_m=w_0\sqrt{1+(L/2z_R)^2}.\]

The same interval emerges from requiring a positive squared Rayleigh length. The radius \(w\) is the distance at which intensity has fallen to \(e^{-2}\) of the on-axis value. For \(R=0.50\,\mathrm m\), \(L=0.50\,\mathrm m\) and \(\lambda=600\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(z_R=0.25\,\mathrm m\), \(w_0=0.219\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(w_m=0.309\,\mathrm{mm}\). These scale as the square root of the wavelength at fixed geometry.

Move towards the concentric limit

Keep the same mirror radius and increase the separation to \(0.90\,\mathrm m\). The Rayleigh length becomes \(0.150\,\mathrm m\), the waist contracts to \(0.169\,\mathrm{mm}\), and the mirror spot grows to \(0.535\,\mathrm{mm}\). A smaller focus can therefore require a larger mirror aperture. The ideal spherical paraxial solution ceases to describe an actual beam if diffraction clipping or large ray angles become important.

Change \(L/R\) and inspect the beam radius across one pass. Compare settings equidistant from the confocal ratio, such as 0.4 and 1.6. Their waist radii agree because \(L(2R-L)\) is symmetric. Their mirror spots differ because the distances from the waist differ.

Tutorial on an aperture measurement

A symmetric air cavity has \(L=0.40\,\mathrm m\) and measured \(w_m/w_0=\sqrt2\). Find \(R\) and, at \(\lambda=632.8\,\mathrm{nm}\), the waist radius.

Work through the solution

The width ratio gives \(L/(2z_R)=1\), hence \(z_R=0.20\,\mathrm m\). The curvature there is

\[R=0.20+0.20^2/0.20=0.40\,\mathrm m.\]

The cavity is confocal, and \(w_0=\sqrt{632.8\times10^{-9}\times0.20/\pi}=0.201\,\mathrm{mm}\).

At fixed curvature radius \(R\) and wavelength, which symmetric separation maximises the waist radius? Find that maximum and check it against the confocal example.

Work through the solution

Maximising \(w_0\) is equivalent to maximising \(z_R^2=L(2R-L)/4\). Differentiation sets \(L=R\), with \(z_R=R/2\). Thus \(w_{0,\max}=\sqrt{\lambda R/(2\pi)}=0.219\,\mathrm{mm}\) for the parameters in the chapter. Near either end of the ideal stability window the waist shrinks instead.

A ray has \(x=0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\theta=1\,\mathrm{mrad}\) just after a reflection in an exactly confocal cavity. Where is the ray in the same section after one and after two round trips?

Work through the solution

At \(L=R\) the round-trip matrix is \(-I\), so after one return the coordinates are \(-0.1\,\mathrm{mm},-1\,\mathrm{mrad}\). After two trips they return to their initial values. This exact symmetry of identical mirrors explains why a degenerate trace does not automatically imply secular walk-off.
