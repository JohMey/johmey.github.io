Threshold, gain saturation and the decay of stored light

After establishing a transverse cavity mode, consider its intensity over many round trips. The same mirror losses determine a threshold with pumping and a measurable decay time when gain is removed.

Record every factor in one round trip

Let \(L\) be the physical distance between mirrors. Their intensity reflectances are \(R_1,R_2\). Suppose the entire length contains a uniform medium with intensity gain coefficient \(g\) and distributed intensity loss coefficient \(\alpha\), both per metre. Traversing length \(dz\) multiplies intensity by \(1+(g-\alpha)dz\), which integrates to \(e^{(g-\alpha)L}\). During a round trip the light crosses the medium twice and reflects once at each mirror. The multiplier is \(\mathcal M=R_1R_2e^{2(g-\alpha)L}\). Small fluctuations in light intensity grow if \(\mathcal M>1\). Setting it to one and taking logarithms gives

\[g_{\rm th}=\alpha+\frac1{2L}\ln\frac1{R_1R_2}.\]

This is a balance for a resonant mode. Phase closure also selects the allowed longitudinal frequencies and only modes overlapping the gain band can meet threshold. If a shorter gain section occupies length \(\ell<L\), its exponent is \(2g\ell\) while background loss can still act over \(2L\). Using an amplitude reflectivity in a formula derived for intensity would introduce a second independent factor-of-two error.

Find the stationary intensity

As stimulated emission increases, the available population inversion typically falls. In a simple homogeneously saturated, single-mode, spatially averaged model use \(g(I)=g_0/(1+I/I_s)\) with small-signal gain \(g_0\) and saturation intensity \(I_s\). A stationary positive intensity requires \(g(I)=g_{\rm th}\), hence \(I/I_s=g_0/g_{\rm th}-1\). Below threshold the positive solution disappears and the deterministic zero-intensity state is stable. Above threshold a small increase in intensity lowers gain and pushes the system back. Spontaneous emission seeds a real laser and smooths the ideal transition. Relaxation oscillations would require an explicit time equation for the inversion.

For \(L=0.30\,\mathrm m\), \(\alpha=0.10\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(R_1=0.98\) and \(R_2=0.90\),

\[g_{\rm th}=0.3093\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}.\]

A pump giving \(g_0=0.50\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) therefore sustains \(I\simeq0.617I_s\). If the output mirror transmitted more light, it could extract a greater fraction of the circulating light per encounter. It would also raise threshold. At a fixed pump these effects compete.

Read the passive lifetime from the same losses

Switch the gain term to zero. After \(j\) round trips the intensity is \(I_j=I_0[R_1R_2e^{-2\alpha L}]^j\). The time per round trip in a uniform medium of group index \(n_g\) is \(t_{\rm rt}=2n_gL/c\). Match \(I_j/I_0=e^{-jt_{\rm rt}/\tau}\) to obtain

\[\tau=-t_{\rm rt}/\ln(R_1R_2e^{-2\alpha L}).\]

In air the preceding example gives \(\tau=10.8\,\mathrm{ns}\). This is the intensity lifetime. The electric-field amplitude would decay twice as slowly.

Tutorial on inferring a mirror reflectance

A passive air cavity of length \(0.30\,\mathrm m\) with negligible internal absorption has an intensity lifetime \(20.0\,\mathrm{ns}\). Mirror one reflects 99.0 percent. Infer mirror two's intensity reflectance and the uniform gain coefficient required for threshold.

Work through the solution

The round-trip time is \(2.00\,\mathrm{ns}\). The round-trip intensity retention is \(e^{-2.00/20.0}=0.9048\), so

\[R_2=0.9048/0.990=0.914.\]

The threshold is

\[-\ln(0.9048)/(0.60\,\mathrm m)=0.167\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}.\]

Scattering or absorption unaccounted for in the inference would be attributed to mirror two.

Compare the energy lost per round trip with the exponential time constant in the example. Why would treating the logarithm as its first-order expansion bias the lifetime?

Work through the solution

The retention is

\[0.98\times0.90e^{-0.06}=0.8306.\]

The fractional loss is 0.1694, but the exponential log decrement is \(-\ln0.8306=0.1856\). Using the fractional loss instead would give \(2.00\,\mathrm{ns}/0.1694=11.8\,\mathrm{ns}\), longer than the exact 10.8 ns. The difference vanishes only in the small-loss limit.

If an ideal gain crystal fills half the original cavity and all other losses remain, determine the gain coefficient required inside the crystal.

Work through the solution

The round-trip gain becomes \(2g\ell\) with \(\ell=L/2\). The target round-trip log gain is unchanged. Therefore the necessary crystal gain doubles from \(0.3093\) to \(0.6186\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\). This comparison assumes the gain crystal does not alter reflectance, group velocity or losses elsewhere.
