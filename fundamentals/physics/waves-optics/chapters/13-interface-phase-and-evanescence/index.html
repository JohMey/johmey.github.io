Reflection, polarisation and the field beyond a reflecting surface

A ray in glass can reflect completely at an air boundary. A second piece of glass brought close to that boundary can nevertheless change the reflection. The field outside the glass follows from the same equations that determine the reflected intensity.

Match the waves where the materials meet

Put the interface at \(z=0\), with transparent nonmagnetic media of refractive indices \(n_1,n_2\). Write the incoming wave as \(e^{i(k_xx+k_zz-\omega t)}\). To satisfy Maxwell's boundary conditions at every point on the surface, the incident, reflected and transmitted waves need the same \(k_x\) and \(\omega\). With \(k_0=\omega/c\) this gives \(n_1\sin\theta=n_2\sin\theta_t\), where angles are measured from the normal. The normal component may differ on either side.

For s polarisation the electric field points along the interface perpendicular to the incidence plane. Continuity of tangential electric and magnetic fields gives \(1+r_s=t_s\) and \(n_1\cos\theta(1-r_s)=n_2\cos\theta_t\,t_s\). The minus sign on the reflected magnetic term follows from reversing the normal wavevector. Elimination of \(t_s\) gives the first expression below. For p polarisation it is the full magnetic field that points perpendicular to the incidence plane. Resolving the electric field into tangential components supplies the interchanged index weights in the second expression. A reversal of the reflected p basis reverses the sign of \(r_p\) without changing the measurable reflectance.

\[r_s=\frac{n_1\cos\theta-n_2\cos\theta_t}{n_1\cos\theta+n_2\cos\theta_t},\]
\[r_p=\frac{n_2\cos\theta-n_1\cos\theta_t}{n_2\cos\theta+n_1\cos\theta_t},\]
\[R_{s,p}=|r_{s,p}|^2.\]

At normal incidence glass of index 1.50 against air reflects \([(1.50-1)/(1.50+1)]^2=0.040\) for either polarisation. Setting the p numerator to zero and using Snell's law gives \(\tan\theta_B=n_2/n_1\), here \(33.69^\circ\). Only the p reflection vanishes there. At higher angles both reflected intensities approach one.

Continue the transmitted wave through the critical angle

The transmitted normal wavenumber satisfies \(k_{z,2}^2=k_0^2(n_2^2-n_1^2\sin^2\theta)\). Beyond \(\theta_c=\arcsin(n_2/n_1)=41.81^\circ\) it becomes imaginary. Choose \(k_{z,2}=+i\kappa\), the root giving a field that decays into the upper half space. The tangential phase still propagates. In the s Fresnel amplitude the denominator and numerator are now complex conjugates. Its modulus is exactly one, but its phase depends on angle. The p wave also has unit reflectance with its own phase.

\[\frac{1}{\kappa}=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta-n_2^2}}.\]

For free-space wavelength \(500\,\mathrm{nm}\) at \(50^\circ\), the electric-field amplitude decays by \(e^{-1}\) over approximately 141 nm. Its squared magnitude falls by the same factor in just 70 nm. A semi-infinite, lossless second medium carries no time-averaged normal flux in this regime. A nearby second boundary changes the solution by introducing another evanescent component, enabling optical tunnelling.

Locate the two special angles

Compare s and p reflectances as the glass index changes. Read the predicted critical and Brewster angles before moving the angle slider. Near the critical angle an ideal plane wave has a very long penetration length. A finite beam has an angular spread, so its measured response mixes nearby incidence angles.

Tutorial on a liquid cladding

Replace air with a liquid of index 1.33. For glass of index 1.50 determine whether the same \(50^\circ\) beam still undergoes total internal reflection. At what angle does the p reflection vanish?

Work through the solution

The critical angle increases to \(\arcsin(1.33/1.50)=62.46^\circ\). At \(50^\circ\) a propagating wave now enters the liquid. Its p-polarised reflected amplitude is zero at \(\arctan(1.33/1.50)=41.56^\circ\). The sensitivity of the critical angle to the surrounding medium underlies a refractometer.

At \(50^\circ\) and free-space wavelength 500 nm, a dilute, weakly responding layer samples intensity 70 nm above the glass–air interface. Estimate the local intensity relative to its value immediately outside the glass.

Work through the solution

The field penetration length is \(140.6\) nm. Squaring the decay factor gives \(\exp[-2(70)/140.6]=0.369\). This ratio concerns local squared field; it does not give the net normal power crossing a lossless interface. A strongly perturbing layer would require recomputing the field.

Estimate the critical-angle shift for a small change of ambient refractive index near air. Use fixed glass index 1.50 and an increase \(\delta n_2=0.01\).

Work through the solution

Differentiating \(\arcsin(n_2/n_1)\) gives \(d\theta_c/dn_2=1/\sqrt{n_1^2-n_2^2}\) in radians. Near air the predicted shift is \(0.00894\) rad or \(0.512^\circ\). This local linear estimate becomes less accurate as the indices approach matching.
