From first principles

Project a polarised field onto an analyser

A linear polariser transmits the electric-field component along its transmission axis. Assume an ideal lossless transmitted axis, complete rejection of the perpendicular component and a monochromatic linearly polarised incident wave. For incident field amplitude \(E_0\) at angle \(\theta\) to the analyser axis \(\mathbf a\), the emerging field is the projection

\[\mathbf E_{\rm out}=(\mathbf E_{\rm in}\cdot\mathbf a)\mathbf a=E_0\cos\theta\,\mathbf a.\]

A detector averages energy flux over many optical cycles. For equal propagation impedances, intensity is proportional to squared field amplitude. This gives Malus's law and predicts extinction at crossed linear axes.

\[I_{\rm out}=I_{\rm in}\cos^2\theta .\]

A retarder takes a different action on its two orthogonal components. Both propagate through the crystal thickness \(d\) with different phase wavenumbers \(2\pi n_f/\lambda_0\) and \(2\pi n_s/\lambda_0\). Their relative phase on emerging is therefore

\[\delta=\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda_0}(n_s-n_f).\]

The analyser can turn this phase difference into a measurable intensity because it projects both coherent components onto one axis before the detector squares their sum.

Derivation and applications

Infer a plate's retardance from a rotation scan

A plate between crossed polarisers looks dark whenever an optical axis coincides with the first polariser. Away from those angles, both its orientation and its retardance control the transmitted light. Assume ideal polarisers, a uniform lossless linear retarder, normal incidence and monochromatic illumination. Let the first polariser transmit \(x\), the analyser transmit \(y\) and the fast axis of the plate make angle \(\theta\) with \(x\). Write the input field magnitude immediately after the first polariser as \(E_0\). Projection into the fast and slow axes gives

\[E_f=E_0\cos\theta,\]
\[E_s=-E_0\sin\theta .\]

The slow component emerges with relative phase \(\delta\) in the chosen complex convention. Its projection onto \(y\) has coefficient \(\cos\theta\), while the fast component projects with \(\sin\theta\). The two contributions need to be added as complex fields before computing detector intensity.

\[E_y=E_0\sin\theta\cos\theta(1-e^{i\delta}).\]

Use \(|1-e^{i\delta}|^2=4\sin^2(\delta/2)\) and normalise to the intensity \(I_0\) after the first polariser. Expressing the angular factor through a quadruple angle identifies an observable modulation at four times the rotation frequency.

\[\frac{I_\perp}{I_0}=\sin^2(2\theta)\sin^2(\delta/2)=\frac{1-\cos4\theta}{2}\sin^2(\delta/2).\]

The scan maximum provides \(\sin^2(\delta/2)\) independently of where the instrument labels zero angle, provided the full rotation is sampled. Lossless plates that differ in retardance by a full wave give identical readings, and this intensity measurement alone also loses the sign of the phase delay.

Worked example

Determine a birefringent film thickness

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

At 600 nm the measured maximum is 0.250 of \(I_0\). Independent fabrication information places \(\delta\) between zero and \(\pi\), so

\[\delta=2\arcsin(0.500)=\pi/3.\]

With index difference 0.0100 and \(\delta=2\pi\Delta n\,d/\lambda\), the thickness is \(d=10.0\) µm. At a 15.0° axis angle the predicted intensity is \(0.250\sin^2(30.0^\circ)=0.0625\) of \(I_0\).

Second application

Diagnose angular misalignment at a nominal extinction

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A calibrated half-wave plate gives 0.0100 of \(I_0\) at a setting labelled parallel to the input polariser. If angular misalignment dominates, its magnitude near the expected minimum solves \(\sin^2(2\theta)=0.0100\). Thus

\[|\theta|=\arcsin(0.100)/2=2.87^\circ.\]

A full rotation tests that explanation: the same angular offset should shift all four minima, and each corrected minimum should reach the independently measured background.

Resolve the electric field into two components

A narrow-band plane wave propagating along \(z\) in a transparent medium has a transverse electric field. Write its physical value as the real part of \((A_x\hat{\mathbf x}+A_y\hat{\mathbf y})e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\). A common phase attached to both complex amplitudes shifts the time origin of the field oscillation. Their relative phase and amplitude ratio determine how the tip of the field vector moves within a cycle.

For amplitudes in phase the field traces a line. Equal magnitudes with relative phase \(\pi/2\) produce a circle, while intermediate combinations usually give an ellipse. A two-component Jones vector describes one fully polarised coherent beam. For randomly changing amplitudes, predictions require averaging intensities or field correlations over the exposure. A single Jones vector cannot represent every partially polarised state.

Project onto a measuring axis

An ideal linear polariser with transmission direction \(\hat{\mathbf a}=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\) passes the component \(\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf A\) and emits a field along \(\hat{\mathbf a}\). Its matrix is the outer product \(\hat{\mathbf a}\hat{\mathbf a}^{\mathsf T}\). A linearly polarised input at angle \(\beta\) therefore transmits a fraction \(\cos^2(\beta-\alpha)\) of the intensity. This is Malus’s law. It applies to the projection of the field before the detector takes a squared magnitude.

Two ideal polarisers at right angles transmit zero for an input aligned with the first. Inserting a third between them at 45° can increase transmitted power. After the middle polariser the direction of the transmitted wave has changed. The first projection passes half the original power and the second passes half of that half, so one quarter reaches the detector. The inserted component changes the state incident on the last analyser.

Background · Can a rotating linear analyser determine handedness?

Take two normalised Jones vectors \((1,i)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-i)/\sqrt2\). Their electric vectors rotate in opposite senses with time. An ideal linear analyser responds to a real projection direction \((\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\). The two squared projections are identical for every angle.

\[\frac{I_\pm(\alpha)}{I_0}=\frac12|\cos\alpha\pm i\sin\alpha|^2=\frac12.\]

A quarter-wave plate that delays the second component by \(\pi/2\) first maps the two inputs to \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) in the chosen phasor convention. A linear analyser at 45° can now distinguish them. An unpolarised beam also gives a flat angular scan through a linear analyser, so constant throughput on its own cannot establish circular polarisation.

Transform a phase delay into an intensity reading

A lossless birefringent plate has two orthogonal principal axes with refractive indices \(n_f\) and \(n_s\). Through thickness \(d\), their phase difference is \(\delta=2\pi(n_s-n_f)d/\lambda_0\), where \(\lambda_0\) is vacuum wavelength. In the plate-axis basis its Jones matrix, apart from a common phase, is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\delta})\). The matrix preserves the sum of squared amplitudes. Only the relative phase changes.

Let the incident linear polarisation make an angle \(\beta\) to the fast axis. An analyser crossed with the original input has unit vector \((-\sin\beta,\cos\beta)\) in that basis. Projecting the output onto it gives amplitude proportional to \(\sin\beta\cos\beta(e^{i\delta}-1)\). Squaring yields

\[I_\perp/I_{\rm in}=\sin^2(2\beta)\sin^2(\delta/2).\]

Thus a dark crossed-analyser setting can indicate an axis-aligned input or an integer number of full-wave delays. One dark reading alone does not identify thickness or birefringence.

Distinguish a quarter-wave plate from a half-wave plate

Take \(\lambda_0=600\) nm and \(\Delta n=0.010\). The thinnest plate with \(\delta=\pi/2\) has \(d=\lambda_0/(4\Delta n)=15.0\) µm. At \(\beta=45°\), equal input amplitudes acquire a relative quarter-cycle delay, producing circular polarisation. A rotating ideal analyser then transmits half the intensity at every angle. With 4.0 mW arriving at the plate, the analyser passes 2.0 mW throughout the rotation.

Double the thickness. The delay becomes \(\pi\), so the two components have opposite signs and the output is linearly polarised along the original crossed analyser. It transmits the full 4.0 mW in that setting and zero in its orthogonal setting. Flat response under rotation does not by itself distinguish circularly polarised from unpolarised light. Adding a known quarter-wave plate before the analyser converts circular light to a linear state but leaves ideal unpolarised light unpolarised, giving an independent measurement.

Rotating the input polarization relative to a plate also checks the orientation of its principal axes. If the input is aligned with either axis, only one component is present and retardation has no effect on the polarization ellipse. The phase remains physically detectable only through interference with an independent reference. For comparable input components both axes contribute and an analyser becomes sensitive to their relative phase. This is why a small alignment error can suppress a retardance measurement even for a perfectly fabricated plate.

Recover polarization from six intensity readings

A rotating linear analyser measures orientation and some of the shape of a polarization ellipse, but opposite handedness gives the same result at every linear angle. Let the complex field components be \(E_x,E_y\) and average products over the detector integration time. Choose intensity units that absorb the common medium factor. Define \(S_0=\langle |E_x|^2+|E_y|^2\rangle\), \(S_1=\langle |E_x|^2-|E_y|^2\rangle\),

\[S_2=2\mathrm{Re}\langle E_x^*E_y\rangle,\]
\[S_3=2\mathrm{Im}\langle E_x^*E_y\rangle.\]

The sign convention for the last component specifies the handedness label used throughout the measurement.

An ideal linear analyser at angle \(\theta\) projects onto \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). Squaring the projected amplitude and using double-angle identities gives

\[I(\theta)=\tfrac12[S_0+S_1\cos(2\theta)+S_2\sin(2\theta)] .\]

Hence the difference of horizontal and vertical readings gives \(S_1\), while the difference between \(+45^\circ\) and \(-45^\circ\) gives \(S_2\). Finally pass the input through a calibrated quarter-wave analyser combination selecting the two circular basis vectors \((1,\pm i)/\sqrt2\). The difference of their outputs in this order gives \(S_3\). Their sum, like the sum of either linear pair, gives \(S_0\). Multiple estimates of total intensity check for source drift and detector gain imbalance.

Distinguish a partially polarized state from a pure ellipse

Suppose the two principal linear readings are 7.00 and 3.00 mW, the diagonal readings are 6.50 and 3.50 mW, and the circular readings are 8.00 and 2.00 mW. Each pair sums to 10.0 mW. The inferred Stokes parameters are \((10.0,4.00,3.00,6.00)\) mW. The degree of polarization is

\[\sqrt{S_1^2+S_2^2+S_3^2}/S_0=\sqrt{61}/10=0.781.\]

A single fixed Jones vector has degree one, so a pure elliptical state cannot reproduce these repeated intensity readings.

The polarized fraction has major-axis azimuth \(\psi=\tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}(S_2,S_1)=18.4^\circ\). Its signed ellipticity angle satisfies \(\sin(2\chi)=S_3/\sqrt{S_1^2+S_2^2+S_3^2}\), giving \(\chi=25.1^\circ\) and minor-to-major axis magnitude \(\tan|\chi|=0.468\). At zero polarized fraction both angles cease to describe a measurable ellipse. A fitted degree above one signals an inconsistent set of readings, since the correlation matrix must have nonnegative eigenvalues. Sequential readings should therefore include a reference power monitor when source brightness can change during the six settings.

One can predict readings at analyser settings never used in reconstruction. For a linear analyser at \(30^\circ\) relative to horizontal, the stated parameters give

\[[10+4\cos60^\circ+3\sin60^\circ]/2=7.30\,\mathrm{mW}.\]

A separate fourth linear orientation therefore validates the fitted linear components but has no sensitivity to the circular component. To test handedness, interchange the two calibrated circular analyser states. The difference should reverse while their sum remains constant. If different linearly polarized calibration standards all generate a large circular signal, an analyser retardance or alignment error is a more immediate explanation than an exotic input state.

The total intensity is itself useful for another check. An ideal lossless wave plate changes the three polarized components while preserving their length and the total intensity. Measuring before and after a known plate therefore distinguishes a true change of polarization fraction from a simple rotation of its polarization coordinates.

Read retardance between crossed polarisers

A linear retarder sits between crossed ideal polarisers. At plate angle \(\theta\) its two principal waves acquire relative phase \(\delta\); projection onto the final analyser gives

\[I/I_0=\sin^2(2\theta)\sin^2(\delta/2).\]

Here \(I_0\) is the intensity after the first polariser.
