From first principles

Sum the repeated transmissions of a cavity

Consider an ideal plane cavity with two identical lossless mirrors of power reflectance \(R<1\), normal incidence and coherent monochromatic light. The first transmitted field has magnitude \((1-R)\) times the incident field. Each later transmitted copy makes one extra round trip, acquiring an amplitude multiplier \(R\) and a phase \(\delta\) that includes any mirror reflection phases. In steady state the optical fields add as a convergent geometric series.

\[\frac{E_t}{E_i}=(1-R)e^{i\psi}\sum_{j=0}^\infty (Re^{i\delta})^j=\frac{(1-R)e^{i\psi}}{1-Re^{i\delta}}.\]

The phase \(\psi\) acquired on the first traversal cancels in intensity. Multiply the denominator by its complex conjugate and use \(1-\cos\delta=2\sin^2(\delta/2)\) to obtain

\[\mathcal T=\frac{(1-R)^2}{(1-R)^2+4R\sin^2(\delta/2)}.\]

At \(\delta=2\pi m\) all transmitted copies arrive in phase and the ideal transmission reaches unity. Increasing reflectance allows more copies to matter, so a smaller change in phase spoils their alignment and narrows the transmission peak. Loss, mirror imbalance or imperfect mode overlap can reduce its height.

Derivation and applications

Measure mirror imbalance from resonant transmission

High finesse alone does not determine the brightness of a resonant cavity. Consider two partially transmitting lossless mirrors at normal incidence, separated by a transparent gap. Their power reflectances are \(R_1\) and \(R_2\), and \(T_j=1-R_j\). Assume the incident spatial mode is matched, a single polarisation is present and reflected and transmitted fields are normalised to equal external impedances. Absorb mirror reflection phases into the round-trip phase \(\delta\). The first transmitted field has magnitude \(\sqrt{T_1T_2}\) relative to the incident field. Each extra circulation multiplies it by \(\sqrt{R_1R_2}e^{i\delta}\).

\[\frac{E_t}{E_i}=e^{i\psi}\sqrt{T_1T_2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(\sqrt{R_1R_2}e^{i\delta}\big)^n=\frac{e^{i\psi}\sqrt{T_1T_2}}{1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}e^{i\delta}}.\]

The common single-pass phase \(\psi\) disappears on taking the power ratio. At a resonance the phase gained per round trip is an integer multiple of \(2\pi\) and every contribution adds in phase. The resulting peak transmission is

\[\mathcal T_{\rm res}=\frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)}{(1-\sqrt{R_1R_2})^2}.\]

There is no internal loss, so the untransmitted fraction exits through the input port. Subtracting the numerator from the denominator and factoring the difference gives the reflected power on resonance.

\[\mathcal R_{\rm res}=1-\mathcal T_{\rm res}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_1}-\sqrt{R_2}}{1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}}\right)^2 .\]

Identical mirrors cancel the net reflected field on resonance even when each isolated mirror transmits only a small fraction. The result is symmetric under exchange of the two mirrors. A power scan by itself cannot determine which mirror is the more reflective one.

Worked example

Predict the output of unequal mirrors

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

Let the input and output mirror field-reflection magnitudes be 0.900 and 0.800. Their power transmissions are 0.190 and 0.360. The peak transmitted fraction is

\[(0.190)(0.360)/(1-0.720)^2=0.872.\]

For 5.00 mW incident in the matched mode this gives 4.36 mW transmitted and 0.638 mW reflected. The sum equals the incident power to rounding.

Second application

Identify a replacement output mirror

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A second cavity has a known input field-reflection magnitude 0.800 and an output mirror independently known to be less reflective. Its measured resonant reflected power is \(1/16\) of incident power. Taking the appropriate square root gives

\[(0.800-r_2)/(1-0.800r_2)=0.250.\]

Solving yields \(r_2=0.6875\) and \(R_2=0.473\). The predicted peak transmitted fraction is \(15/16=0.9375\), providing a separate measurement to test the lossless and mode-matching assumptions.

Many transmitted copies

A transparent gap of length \(L\) and refractive index \(n\) sits between two identical plane mirrors. Illuminate it at normal incidence with a coherent, monochromatic plane wave. Assume the mirrors are lossless, their intensity reflectivity \(R\) is constant over the band, and the outgoing beams overlap in space. Each trip between mirrors adds phase. Each completed round trip also reflects the field twice. Because one mirror reflection has amplitude magnitude \(\sqrt R\), the field magnitude after both is multiplied by \(R\).

Choose a phase convention absorbing constant reflection phases into the resonance condition. A single transmitted pass has amplitude magnitude \((1-R)\) times the incident amplitude. The pass emerging after one additional round trip acquires \(R e^{i\delta}\) with \(\delta=4\pi nL/\lambda_0\). Summing the geometric series and squaring the magnitude gives

\[\mathcal T=\frac{(1-R)^2}{|1-Re^{i\delta}|^2}=\frac{1}{1+F\sin^2(\delta/2)},\]
\[F=\frac{4R}{(1-R)^2}.\]

Resolving the peaks

Transmission reaches unity at \(\delta=2\pi m\) in this symmetric lossless model. Coherent addition of multiply reflected fields simultaneously cancels the returning field at the input. With unequal mirrors or intracavity loss, peak transmission is generally lower. The resonance frequencies are \(\nu_m=mc/(2nL)\) when dispersion is negligible, giving a free spectral range \(\mathrm{FSR}=c/(2nL)\). If \(n\) varies appreciably, differentiation of round-trip phase replaces it with the group index.

The half-power points satisfy \(|\sin(\delta/2)|=1/\sqrt F\) near resonance. Their full separation in phase is \(4\arcsin(1/\sqrt F)\) when \(F\ge1\). Dividing the \(2\pi\) phase interval by that width yields finesse,

\[\mathcal F=\pi/[2\arcsin(1/\sqrt F)].\]

For large \(F\) this reduces to \(\pi\sqrt R/(1-R)\). Finesse counts how many linewidths fit between adjacent orders. It is distinct from resolving power \(\nu/\Delta\nu\), which also depends on the order.

Inspect a scanned cavity

An air gap of 15.0 mm with \(R=0.900\) has a free spectral range of 9.99 GHz. The exact finesse is about 29.8, so adjacent half-power frequencies across one peak differ by 335 MHz. At a 600 nm carrier that width corresponds to about \(0.00040\) nm. Two lines one free spectral range apart nevertheless reach resonance together. Very narrow peaks and unambiguous identification across a broad input spectrum are different instrument requirements.

Mirror tilt makes different parts of a beam see different round-trip phases. Finite source divergence has a similar effect, since oblique rays accumulate a different axial phase. If raising the nominal mirror reflectivity no longer narrows a measured peak as predicted, compare the line shape at two illuminated spot sizes or angular apertures. Such a test separates instrumental phase averaging from a change in the source spectrum.

Measuring a frequency scan in both directions supplies a useful consistency test. Thermal expansion of the spacer or actuator hysteresis can change the calibration between scan position and optical frequency. An independently known reference line or sideband establishes that calibration without relying on the actuator displacement alone.

Derive cavity linewidth from the decay after switch-off

A cavity that transmits a narrow frequency range also releases stored energy slowly. This connection can be demonstrated without fitting another steady interference pattern. Let \(t_{\rm rt}=2nL/c\) be the round-trip time in a nondispersive empty-gap model and let \(\rho\) be the intensity survival fraction per completed round trip, including both mirrors and all distributed losses. After turning off a resonant input, the energy remaining after \(j\) round trips is \(U_j=U_0\rho^j\). For decay slow compared with one trip, replace the discrete sequence by its continuous envelope,

\[U(t)=U_0e^{-t/\tau_U},\]
\[\tau_U=-t_{\rm rt}/\ln\rho .\]

The output power leaking through a fixed mirror follows this energy envelope. A field amplitude instead decays as \(e^{-t/(2\tau_U)}\). Fourier transforming that causal amplitude gives a response proportional to \([1/(2\tau_U)-i2\pi(\nu-\nu_m)]^{-1}\) close to a resonance. Squaring the magnitude gives a Lorentzian with full width at half maximum \(\Delta\nu\simeq1/(2\pi\tau_U)\). This single-line approximation requires many round trips during a lifetime, or well-separated narrow resonances. It links a time-domain loss measurement to the spectral width obtained by scanning the cavity.

For two identical mirrors of intensity reflectivity \(R\) and negligible internal absorption, \(\rho=R^2\). In the high-reflectivity limit \(\ln\rho\simeq-2(1-R)\), reproducing the familiar finesse dependence of linewidth. Other loss channels can shorten the lifetime even if the two measured peak frequencies keep the same separation.

Determine an additional internal loss

An air cavity has length 0.300 m, giving \(t_{\rm rt}=2.00\) ns and free spectral range 500 MHz. Both mirrors have independently calibrated \(R=0.9950\). With no other losses the expected survival is \(0.990025\) and energy lifetime is

\[-2.00\,{\rm ns}/\ln(0.990025)=200\,\mathrm{ns}.\]

A fast detector instead records an intensity \(1/e\) time of 150 ns. Effective survival is then

\[\exp(-2.00/150)=0.98676.\]

Dividing by \(R^2\) gives an internal survival factor \(0.9967\) per round trip, an extra loss of about \(0.33\) percent.

The inferred full linewidth is \(1/(2\pi\times150\,{\rm ns})=1.06\) MHz, well below the free spectral range. A frequency sweep should agree if the driving laser is narrower than this line and the scan is sufficiently slow to reach steady response. If a measured line is broader while the ring-down lifetime remains unchanged, source jitter or mechanical length fluctuations can broaden the scan without absorbing additional circulating energy. At very low reflectivity the separate-resonance model fails, and individual delayed output pulses provide a more direct interpretation.

The extra loss can be turned into a material attenuation estimate if its path length is known. If all the inferred internal loss came from a sample traversed over 20.0 mm of material per round trip, its intensity attenuation coefficient would be \(-\ln(0.9967)/0.0200=0.165\) m⁻¹. This attribution assumes that inserting the sample does not add appreciable surface reflection or scattering. Measuring otherwise identical samples of two thicknesses separates a loss growing with path length from a nearly constant contribution at the surfaces, using the same ring-down protocol.
