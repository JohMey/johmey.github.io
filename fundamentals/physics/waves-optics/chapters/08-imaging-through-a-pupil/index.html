From first principles

Relate the pupil field to a point image

Treat an on-axis distant point as a uniform plane wave over a lens pupil. Assume scalar, monochromatic, paraxial propagation and an ideal thin lens of focal length \(f\). The lens cancels the quadratic propagation phase at focus. Moving a small distance \(x\) across the focal plane introduces a nearly linear phase across pupil position \(\xi\). For a one-dimensional pupil function \(P(\xi)\) the field sum is

\[U(x)\propto\int P(\xi)\exp\!\left[-i\frac{2\pi x\xi}{\lambda f}\right]d\xi .\]

The recorded point-spread function \(h\) is proportional to \(|U|^2\) and can be normalised to unit integral. For a uniform slit pupil of width \(D\), the first zero follows directly from the phase difference across its edges.

\[h(x)\propto\left[\frac{\sin(\pi D x/\lambda f)}{\pi D x/\lambda f}\right]^2,\]
\[|x_{\rm first}|=\lambda f/D .\]

An extended incoherent object consists of mutually uncorrelated point emitters, so their shifted intensity patterns add. Where the point response is independent of field position this gives \(I_{\rm image}=I_{\rm object}*h\) in common image coordinates. A wider pupil narrows the blur and passes finer object structure.

Background · Numerical aperture
\[\mathrm{NA}=n\sin\theta\]

The largest accepted angle controls which transverse spatial frequencies reach the pupil. Resolution also depends on the optical imaging and coherence assumptions.

Derivation and applications

Relate a defocus error to the on-axis point intensity

A displaced detector spreads the intensity of an unresolved point even if the lens is otherwise perfect. Consider a uniformly illuminated circular pupil of radius \(a\) and a monochromatic point on the optical axis. Use scalar paraxial diffraction in air and a clear pupil. Let \(\phi\) be the phase error at the rim relative to the centre. Defocus contributes a quadratic phase \(\phi r^2/a^2\). At the on-axis image point the in-focus contributions are in phase, so the defocused complex amplitude relative to its in-focus value is the pupil-area average

\[H(0)=\frac{1}{\pi a^2}\int_0^a e^{i\phi r^2/a^2}2\pi r\,dr=\int_0^1 e^{i\phi u}\,du,\]
\[u=r^2/a^2.\]

Equal increments of \(u\) label equal pupil areas. Integrating these phasors gives a phase factor times a real amplitude. Its squared magnitude is the axial intensity at the intended point relative to the ideal peak.

\[H(0)=e^{i\phi/2}\frac{\sin(\phi/2)}{\phi/2},\]
\[S=|H(0)|^2=\left[\frac{\sin(\phi/2)}{\phi/2}\right]^2.\]

For a detector displaced axially by magnitude \(|\Delta z|\) from a focus of focal length \(f\), the change in quadratic propagation phase has magnitude \(k|\Delta z|r^2/(2f^2)\) to first order in \(|\Delta z|/f\). With paraxial numerical aperture \(\mathrm{NA}=a/f\) this gives

\[|\phi|\simeq\frac{\pi\,\mathrm{NA}^2|\Delta z|}{\lambda},\qquad S\simeq1-\frac{\phi^2}{12}\quad (|\phi|\ll1).\]

Total transmitted power stays constant. A loss of axial intensity represents redistribution across the image plane and the same axial value can occur for displacements on either side of best focus.

Worked example

Set a permissible detector displacement

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A microscope working in air has \(\mathrm{NA}=0.100\) and \(\lambda=500\) nm. An axial displacement of 25.0 µm corresponds to \(|\phi|=\pi/2\). The exact pupil average gives

\[S=[\sin(\pi/4)/(\pi/4)]^2=0.811.\]

A detector that measures only the central intensity would therefore show a 19 percent loss even with ideal transmission.

Second application

Infer numerical aperture from a through-focus scan

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A separate lens at 640 nm shows its first axial zero 80.0 µm from best focus. The first nonzero root occurs at \(|\phi|=2\pi\), so

\[\mathrm{NA}=\sqrt{2\lambda/|\Delta z|}=0.126.\]

If the effective focal length is 50.0 mm, the inferred pupil radius is 6.32 mm. The scan thus measures the effective illuminated aperture; partial or graded illumination would change the pupil weighting in the first integral.

A point becomes an extended image

A thin lens focuses a distant point into its back focal plane. An ideal geometrical ray diagram predicts a mathematical point. The finite entrance pupil limits the transverse field that can contribute. Define the point-spread function as the image intensity from a single on-axis point. Restrict attention to an aberration-free, circular, uniformly illuminated pupil, a narrow spectral band and an image region sufficiently small that shifting the source translates the same point-spread function.

The preceding aperture transform now runs over a disk of radius \(a\). For an observation angle \(\theta\) the transverse wavenumber is \(q=k\sin\theta\). Performing the angular integral of the plane-wave phase gives \(2\pi J_0(qr)\). The radial integral then uses \(d[zJ_1(z)]/dz=zJ_0(z)\), so the field normalized at the centre is \(2J_1(qa)/(qa)\). Squaring gives the Airy intensity pattern. Its first zero is at \(qa=3.8317\), hence

\[\sin\theta_1=1.22\lambda/D,\]
\[D=2a .\]

Combine two point sources

For statistically independent emitters the fluctuating relative phase averages away. Their point-spread intensities add. The Rayleigh convention places the centre of one Airy spot on the first zero of its neighbour. It provides a useful separation scale for two comparable emitters, though achievable accuracy also depends on photon counts, background and whether the measurement estimates a known model. Two coherent emitters instead add complex fields before squaring. Their relative phase can brighten or extinguish the midpoint.

For an extended incoherent object each point contributes a shifted intensity pattern. Superposition gives convolution of object intensity with the intensity point-spread function. Taking a Fourier transform converts convolution to multiplication. The resulting optical transfer function is the autocorrelation of the pupil in spatial-frequency coordinates. Its support reaches twice the radius of the coherent field passband because displaced pupil copies overlap until their centres are separated by one diameter. In object space the incoherent cutoff in cycles per metre is \(2\,\mathrm{NA}/\lambda_0\), where \(\mathrm{NA}=n\sin\alpha\) and \(\lambda_0\) is vacuum wavelength.

Choose the object-side sampling

Use an air objective of numerical aperture 0.40 at 520 nm. The Rayleigh scale is \(0.61\lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}=0.793\) μm. The incoherent cutoff is \(1.54\) cycles/μm, corresponding to a limiting sinusoidal period of \(0.650\) μm with contrast vanishing at the cutoff. These describe different target geometries. A grating near the cutoff can have weak contrast even though its nominal period exceeds the reciprocal cutoff.

To sample the full image band without aliasing, the object-side pixel pitch must be at most \(1/(2f_{\rm cut})=0.325\) μm. A detector with 6.5 μm pixels therefore needs at least \(20\times\) lateral magnification. A still higher magnification gives more samples of the same optical image. Recovering finer physical structure would require a larger numerical aperture, a shorter wavelength, or additional measurement information.

A separate point-source image is a practical calibration target. Its recorded radial profile can be convolved with a proposed object model to predict the measured image, including overlapping spots, before attempting an inversion of noisy data.

Derive the contrast transfer from two overlapping pupils

A cutoff frequency specifies where contrast disappears, but it does not tell how much contrast survives below cutoff. For incoherent imaging with a clear circular pupil, the normalized optical transfer function is the overlap area of two pupil disks displaced in reciprocal space, divided by one disk area. Consider one spatial-frequency magnitude \(\nu\) and normalize it by the incoherent cutoff, \(q=\nu/\nu_c\). At \(q=0\) the disks coincide; at \(q=1\) their centres are one full diameter apart. For pupil radius \(a\) the centre separation is therefore \(2aq\).

The overlap lens consists of two circular segments. In either disk the half-angle between radii to the intersection points is \(\arccos q\). The sector area minus the area of its isosceles triangle is \(a^2[\arccos q-q\sqrt{1-q^2}]\). Double this result and divide by \(\pi a^2\). For ideal focus the transfer is real and nonnegative,

\[H(q)=\frac2\pi[\arccos q-q\sqrt{1-q^2}],\qquad 0\le q\le1 .\]

At zero spatial frequency \(H=1\), expressing conservation of a uniform background after suitable calibration. At the cutoff only tangential contact remains and \(H=0\). If the object intensity is \(I_0[1+m\cos(2\pi\nu x)]\), linear shift-invariant incoherent imaging leaves the average unchanged while multiplying its modulation depth by \(H(q)\). This establishes a direct experimental meaning for a frequency-dependent contrast loss.

Separate objective and detector contrast losses

An air microscope at 500 nm has NA 0.50, giving \(\nu_c=2\mathrm{NA}/\lambda=2.00\) cycles/μm in object space. A target with period 1.00 μm has \(q=0.500\). The circular overlap formula gives

\[H=(2/\pi)(\pi/3-0.5\sqrt{0.75})=0.391.\]

An object modulation \(m=0.600\) therefore appears optically with modulation \(0.235\), even under ideal focus.

Suppose each detector pixel integrates over object-side width \(p=0.250\) μm, with contiguous active pixels. Averaging the sinusoid over a width \(p\) gives another multiplier \(\sin(\pi\nu p)/(\pi\nu p)=0.900\) at this target frequency. The recorded modulation is consequently about \(0.211\). This decrease occurs before sampling values at the pixel centres and differs from aliasing. The detector Nyquist frequency is \(1/(2p)=2.00\) cycles/μm, just enough to cover the optical band. Reducing pixel pitch can recover its own averaging loss but leaves the objective's \(0.391\) transfer unchanged. Calibrating with several grating periods distinguishes an optical contrast curve from a simple constant gain error.

This test also explains why visibility estimates near the optical cutoff are noisy. Recovering an object modulation from an image would require dividing by the optical and pixel transfer factors. At the example period their product is about 0.352, already amplifying a small modulation measurement error by nearly three. Close to cutoff the corresponding inverse becomes much larger. More exposure time reduces photon-counting uncertainty but cannot recover a component at which the transfer is exactly zero. A known calibration target is preferable when testing the transfer curve, since otherwise a weak observed fringe can arise from either the specimen or the imaging system.
