From first principles

Derive the wave speed from transverse force balance

Select a short string segment of unperturbed length \(dx\) and mass \(\mu\,dx\), where \(\mu\) is mass per unit length. Assume a flexible string with uniform tension \(T\), small slopes and negligible damping. Its neighbours pull tangentially at either end. For small angles the transverse component is approximately \(T\) times the slope. Oppositely directed pulls cancel when the two ends have equal slopes, while a change in slope produces a net transverse force.

\[dF_y=T\left[\partial_xy(x+dx,t)-\partial_xy(x,t)\right]\simeq T\,\partial_x^2y\,dx .\]

Newton's second law equates this force to the mass times transverse acceleration. Divide by segment length and compare with the derivatives of a profile translated at speed \(c\).

\[\partial_t^2y=\frac T\mu\,\partial_x^2y,\]
\[y=f(x-ct)\ \Longrightarrow\ \partial_t^2y=c^2f'',\]
\[\partial_x^2y=f'' .\]

A freely travelling shape therefore moves at \(c=\sqrt{T/\mu}\). Extra tension supplies a larger force for the same curvature. More mass per unit length reduces the acceleration produced by that force.

Background · Wavenumber
\[k=2\pi/\lambda\]

The wavenumber gives phase change per unit distance in a homogeneous medium. Wavelength must be specified in the medium in which propagation is calculated.

Derivation and applications

Calculate the energy and power of a travelling pulse

A single pulse has no unique frequency, but a measurement of its slope can still determine the total energy it carries. Model a long string with constant tension \(T\), line density \(\mu\), negligible bending stiffness and small transverse slope. Choose a right-moving displacement \(y(x,t)=f(x-ct)\), with \(c=\sqrt{T/\mu}\). Stretching a segment increases its length by approximately \((\partial_xy)^2dx/2\), while its transverse velocity is \(\partial_ty=-c\partial_xy\). The kinetic and elastic energies per unit unperturbed length are consequently equal.

\[u=\tfrac12\mu(\partial_ty)^2+\tfrac12T(\partial_xy)^2=T[f'(x-ct)]^2.\]

The left portion of the string does work on the right portion through its transverse force at a cut. With positive power defined towards increasing \(x\), multiply that force by the velocity of the point at the cut. The result is nonnegative even as the two slopes of the passing pulse have opposite signs.

\[P=-T(\partial_xy)(\partial_ty)=cT[f'(x-ct)]^2=cu .\]

Consider an isolated Gaussian displacement with peak height \(A\) and spatial scale \(s\),

\[f(\xi)=A\exp[-\xi^2/(2s^2)].\]

Its slope is \(-\xi f/s^2\). Integrating over the entire string using the second moment of a Gaussian gives

\[E=T\frac{A^2}{s^4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\xi^2 e^{-\xi^2/s^2}d\xi=\frac{\sqrt\pi\,TA^2}{2s}.\]

A broader pulse of the same height has gentler slopes and stores less energy. The displacement maximum itself has zero instantaneous power because its slope vanishes. Two power peaks pass a fixed sensor on either side of that maximum.

Worked example

Budget the energy of a laboratory pulse

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A string under 40.0 N tension carries a Gaussian pulse of height 4.00 mm with \(s=50.0\) mm. Its energy is \((\sqrt\pi/2)(40.0)(0.00400)^2/0.0500=11.3\) mJ. The largest slope is \((A/s)e^{-1/2}=0.0485\), supporting the small-slope approximation. If the string absorbs the pulse completely at its far end, the absorber receives the full 11.3 mJ.

Second application

Reconstruct pulse width from two power maxima

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

On a second string the pulse speed is 25.0 m/s. Since \(P\) is proportional to \(\xi^2e^{-\xi^2/s^2}\), differentiating gives maxima at \(\xi=\pm s\). Two measured power peaks 6.00 ms apart therefore imply \(s=c\Delta t/2=75.0\) mm. For \(A=3.00\) mm and \(T=18.0\) N the predicted peak power is \(cT(A/s)^2/e=0.265\) W, an independent amplitude check.

Choose the displacement and the approximation

Strike one end of a stretched rope. A crest moves down its length, yet a marked point on the rope moves mainly up and down. Distinguishing the motion of a disturbance from the motion of the material is the starting point for predicting wave speed. Consider a uniform flexible string under tension \(F_T\) with mass per unit length \(\mu\). Its transverse displacement from a straight equilibrium line is \(y(x,t)\). Assume slopes \(|\partial_xy|\ll1\), negligible bending stiffness and negligible damping.

These assumptions have measurable consequences. To first order a segment of horizontal length \(dx\) retains that length and has mass \(\mu\,dx\). The tension magnitude can be treated as its equilibrium value, while the directions at the two ends vary with the local slope. For larger slopes the tension can change as the string stretches, and longitudinal motion need no longer decouple from transverse displacement.

Balance forces on one small segment

At the two ends of a short segment the vertical tension components have opposite signs. Expand the difference of the slopes to first order in the segment length. Newton’s law then reads

\[\begin{aligned} \mu\,dx\,\partial_t^2 y &= F_T\,[\partial_x y(x+dx,t)-\partial_x y(x,t)]\\ &= F_T\,dx\,\partial_x^2 y+O(dx^2). \end{aligned}\]

Dividing by the segment mass and taking the limit gives the wave equation with speed \(c\),

\[\partial_t^2 y=c^2\partial_x^2 y,\]
\[c=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}.\]

The equation predicts that a local curvature accelerates material towards straightening the string. Any twice-differentiable shape \(g(x-ct)\) travels right at speed \(c\) because its second time derivative is \(c^2g''\). A second independent shape \(h(x+ct)\) travels left. Their superposition solves the same linear equation, and initial displacement alone generally does not specify how the disturbance splits. An initial velocity field supplies the second piece of information.

For example, lifting a narrow region and releasing the whole string from rest produces opposite-going copies of half the initial displacement on a long uniform string. Launching the same shape exclusively to the right requires \(y_t=-cy_x\) at release. A sequence of still images can reveal the direction of motion, but a single displacement profile usually cannot.

Read a frequency from moving wavefronts

A sinusoidal displacement at a fixed point repeats every source period \(T_s\). A microphone in motion does not stay at that point between observations. To relate its measured frequency to the source clock, choose the positive axis along the sound ray, from the source towards the receiver. Let \(c\) be the sound speed in still air and \(v_s,v_r\) the signed source and receiver velocities in that same air frame. Assume steady subsonic motion along the ray and negligible reflection.

At emission of the next crest, the first crest has travelled \(cT_s\) and the emitter has moved \(v_sT_s\). The separation of the crests ahead of the emitter is consequently \(\lambda=(c-v_s)T_s\). The receiver intercepts them at relative speed \(c-v_r\). Dividing the distance swept per second by the spacing gives

\[\frac{f_r}{f_s}=\frac{c-v_r}{c-v_s}.\]

A speaker on a test trolley emits 680 Hz while travelling at 15 m/s towards a microphone moving towards it at 5 m/s. Take \(c=340\) m/s. The crest spacing is \(325/680=0.478\) m, and the microphone sweeps through the crests at 345 m/s. It registers \(721.85\) Hz. Holding the microphone still instead gives \(711.38\) Hz. The two instruments approach one another in either experiment, but moving the emitter changes wavelength while moving only the microphone leaves it fixed.

Two checks prevent sign errors. If both instruments travel at the same speed the ratio is one. For velocities small compared with \(c\), expansion to first order gives

\[\Delta f/f_s\simeq(v_s-v_r)/c.\]

Separate dependence on the two air-frame velocities reappears at second order. The distinction matters in a wind tunnel, where ground speed and air-frame speed differ. The derivation uses a material medium and cannot be used unchanged for light in vacuum.

Locate the energy being transmitted

Each segment stores kinetic energy \(\mu y_t^2dx/2\). Stretching it adds length \([\sqrt{1+y_x^2}-1]dx\simeq y_x^2dx/2\), costing potential energy \(F_Ty_x^2dx/2\) at the assumed constant tension. Defining their sum per length as \(u\), direct differentiation and substitution of the wave equation yield

\[\partial_tu=\partial_x(F_Ty_ty_x).\]

Thus the positive-direction power is \(P=-F_Ty_ty_x\) and \(\partial_tu+\partial_xP=0\).

For a right-moving wave \(y_t=-cy_x\), so the power is positive and equals \(cu\). A left-moving wave carries power in the opposite direction. The signs come from the mechanical work done across a cut. In a standing wave the two powers need not add constructively even where the displacement amplitude is large. An average amplitude alone therefore measures power only once the propagation conditions have been established.

Compare two launches

Let \(F_T=100\) N, \(\mu=0.010\) kg/m and frequency \(f=20.0\) Hz. The wave speed is 100 m/s and wavelength is 5.0 m. A sinusoidal right-moving displacement of peak amplitude \(A=3.0\) mm has peak transverse material speed \(2\pi f A=0.377\) m/s. The largest slope is \(2\pi A/\lambda=0.00377\), supporting the small-slope approximation.

The mean power is

\[\mu c(2\pi f A)^2/2=0.0711\,\mathrm{W}.\]

Doubling the amplitude at the same frequency quadruples mean power while keeping the propagation speed fixed. If the tension is doubled at fixed amplitude and frequency, the speed and wavelength both increase by \(\sqrt2\) and the mean power increases by \(\sqrt2\). These predictions separate the energy required to maintain a sinusoidal drive from the arrival time of a signal along the string.

The two field measurements needed for instantaneous power are the transverse velocity and the local slope. Measuring displacement alone at one point gives velocity after differentiation, but not the spatial slope. A second nearby displacement probe can approximate that slope if its spacing is short compared with the wavelength. Standing waves illustrate why this matters: a point may move rapidly while the time-averaged transport across it is zero because counterpropagating components carry equal powers.

Hear the initial shape in the harmonics of a string

On a finite uniform string with fixed ends the boundary values must vanish at \(x=0,L\) at every time. Separating the wave equation gives spatial eigenfunctions \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) and angular frequencies \(n\pi c/L\). If the initial transverse velocity is zero, each excited mode starts in its cosine time phase. To determine its amplitude multiply the initial displacement \(f(x)\) by the corresponding sine and integrate. Orthogonality gives

\[y(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(n\pi ct/L),\]
\[b_n=\frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx .\]

Pluck the midpoint upward by height \(h\ll L\) and release the resulting triangle from rest. Its slopes are \(2h/L\) and \(-2h/L\). Integrating the coefficient by parts makes the endpoint term vanish because \(f\) vanishes at both ends. The two remaining constant-slope integrals give

\[b_n=8h\sin(n\pi/2)/(n^2\pi^2).\]

Reflection symmetry suppresses every even mode. The negative coefficient for the third mode fixes its phase relative to the fundamental. Its energy remains positive.

Take \(L=0.600\) m, \(c=120\) m/s and \(h=3.00\) mm. The first and third frequencies are 100 and 300 Hz and the amplitudes are \(+2.43\) and \(-0.270\) mm. Energy in a mode is proportional to \(n^2b_n^2\) because its maximum spatial slope scales with \(n\). The third mode therefore has one ninth of the first mode's energy, even though its displacement amplitude magnitude is one ninth and its amplitude squared is only one eighty-first. A pickup at an individual mode node may suppress the measured line even when the mode is present in the string.

Reconstruct two outgoing pulses from initial data

A long uniform string can be started by specifying both its displacement and its velocity. Assume small slopes, constant tension and observations made before reflections return from the ends. The one-dimensional wave equation factors into left- and right-travelling pieces, so write \(y(x,t)=F(x-ct)+G(x+ct)\). At the release time the measured shape is \(f(x)=F(x)+G(x)\) and the transverse velocity is \(g(x)=-cF'(x)+cG'(x)\). Differentiating the shape equation and solving this pair gives

\[F'=(f'-g/c)/2,\]
\[G'=(f'+g/c)/2.\]

Integrate those derivatives and evaluate the two pieces at their respective moving arguments. Constants cancel when the prescribed initial shape is restored, yielding the solution

\[y(x,t)=\frac{f(x-ct)+f(x+ct)}2+\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds .\]

The interval in this formula contains every initial point whose signal can have reached the observation point by time \(t\). A localized shape released from rest splits into two half-height copies. A localized velocity impulse produces a different response because the integral accumulates the initial velocity over a growing interval. Measuring only a release photograph cannot distinguish these preparations. A right-travelling shape alone requires the specific initial velocity \(g=-cf'\), consistent with the local slope-to-velocity relation of a travelling wave.

Strike a short region of the string

A string has \(c=50.0\) m/s and line density \(\mu=0.0200\) kg/m. Initially it is straight. Impart transverse velocity \(g_0=0.400\) m/s over the interval \(-a<x<a\) with \(a=0.100\) m, and zero elsewhere. This rectangular velocity profile idealizes a short, distributed tap. At the centre, while \(ct<a\), the integration interval lies inside the struck region and \(y(0,t)=g_0 t\). At 1.00 ms the centre has moved 0.400 mm. After \(a/c=2.00\) ms the entire initial velocity region lies inside the interval, and the centre takes the constant displacement \(g_0a/c=0.800\) mm.

At that later stage the centre is at rest and locally straight while two wavefront regions move away in opposite directions. The energy that initially resided in the moving patch was \(\mu(2a)g_0^2/2=0.320\) mJ, with half propagating in each direction by symmetry. A permanent shift at a fixed point of an infinite ideal string costs no local curvature energy after the fronts pass. Finite fixed ends eventually send return waves, invalidating the infinite-string prediction at late times. An array of displacement sensors would see a linear rise followed by a local plateau, while a velocity probe would record a finite-time pulse and return to zero.

The example also predicts what a position probe placed outside the struck region records before any boundary reflection. Its signal begins when the nearer edge of the initial interval enters the backwards propagation cone. It reaches the plateau when the farther edge enters. At a point 0.500 m from centre, those events occur at 8.00 and 12.0 ms. The local rise lasts 4.00 ms, twice the centre rise time, but only one travelling front contributes there. A second sensor equally far on the opposite side should give the same record, providing an immediate symmetry check on the applied tap.

Does it matter which instrument is moving?

Compare two acoustic measurements at the same closing speed in still air. Move the source in the first experiment and move the microphone in the second. Their rates of approach agree but their crest spacings differ.
