When two copies cease to interfere

A narrow-band point source sends essentially the same waveform to two distant collectors after the proper path delay. Two extended sources can light both collectors equally while producing different fringe patterns. The measurable quantity is the complex degree of coherence \(\gamma\), normalized by the geometric mean of the two intensities. If the two collected beams have equal intensities, controlled recombination with variable phase produces fringe visibility \(|\gamma|\). Unequal intensities reduce visibility by an additional known balance factor.

Consider collectors separated by projected baseline \(B\) transverse to their line of sight. A source element at a small angular coordinate \(\theta\) along the baseline has geometric delay \(B\theta/c\) and phase \(2\pi B\theta/\lambda\). Different surface elements of a thermal source radiate independently on average, so cross terms between distinct elements disappear. Each element still interferes with itself through the two collectors. Adding their complex interference contributions, weighted by angular intensity \(S\), gives

\[\gamma(B)=\frac{\int S(\theta)e^{2\pi iB\theta/\lambda}\,d\theta}{\int S(\theta)\,d\theta}.\]

Here \(S\) is angular brightness integrated perpendicular to the baseline. Successive baselines sample different Fourier components of the projected brightness profile. The narrow-band, far-field conditions let one use a single wavelength and a planar angular phase across the entire source.

Two stars and a disk

For equally bright unresolved points at angles \(\pm s/2\), the normalized sum is \(\gamma=\cos(\pi Bs/\lambda)\). Its first zero occurs at \(B=\lambda/(2s)\). Rotating the baseline changes the projected separation and can restore visibility. If one point is brighter, the unequal phasor lengths cannot cancel completely. For a uniform circular disk of angular diameter \(\Theta\) the two-dimensional brightness integral instead gives \(2J_1(x)/x\) with \(x=\pi B\Theta/\lambda\). The first zero occurs at \(B=1.22\lambda/\Theta\). The same Bessel function appeared for a filled circular aperture, now describing coherence as a function of collector separation.

Interpret a visibility measurement

At 550 nm a uniform disk first loses its fringes at projected baseline 50.0 m. The inferred diameter is \(1.22(550\times10^{-9})/50.0=1.34\times10^{-8}\) rad or 2.77 milliarcseconds. At distance 10.0 parsecs its linear diameter would be \(0.0277\) astronomical units, about \(4.14\times10^9\) m. If independent spectroscopy suggested a binary made of two unresolved equals instead, the same null would correspond to projected separation 1.13 milliarcseconds. One zero by itself does not determine which brightness distribution produced it.

Measurements at several baseline lengths distinguish the cosine from the disk transform, and observations after rotation examine orientation. A broad optical passband averages different \(\lambda\) values and can fill a sharp null. In addition, uncontrolled fluctuations of the optical path during an exposure reduce contrast even for a point source. Observing a nearby unresolved calibrator at the same instrumental setting separates that loss from the target’s physical angular extent.

The measured baseline is a projection perpendicular to the line of sight. Earth’s rotation changes both its effective length and orientation for a fixed pair of collectors, providing additional Fourier samples over a night.

Remove telescope-dependent phase errors with three collectors

Two telescopes measure a complex visibility: its magnitude describes contrast while its argument contains the position of brightness features. Atmospheric delay at each telescope can overwhelm that phase. Use three telescopes and orient the measured baselines cyclically from 1 to 2, 2 to 3 and 3 to 1. If the instantaneous phase delay at telescope \(j\) is \(\phi_j\), measured visibility on baseline \(ij\) is \(V_{ij}^{\rm obs}=V_{ij}e^{i(\phi_i-\phi_j)}\). Multiply the three measured visibilities. Every telescope phase then appears once with each sign and cancels. The argument of the product,

\[\Phi_{\rm cl}=\arg[V_{12}V_{23}V_{31}],\]

is called the closure phase. The three oriented baseline vectors sum to zero. Translating the entire source on the sky introduces a linear phase proportional to baseline, which cancels in the same product. Closure phase thus probes relative brightness structure while losing sensitivity to an absolute position offset. A brightness distribution symmetric about a point has real visibilities after such a translation; its closure phase is zero or \(\pi\) away from zeros of visibility.

Cancellation requires phases measured over the same atmospheric realization or combined in a suitable short-time correlation. A separate instrumental phase error specific to one baseline is not expressible as a difference of telescope phases and will remain in the product. Observing a point-source calibrator can detect such a residual.

Detect asymmetry in an unequal binary

Place a star of unit brightness at the origin and a companion of relative brightness one half at angular separation \(s\) along a line of projected baselines. Adopt the Fourier sign convention yielding \(V(\phi)=(1+0.5e^{i\phi})/1.5\) with \(\phi=2\pi Bs/\lambda\). Choose collectors so that the first two oriented baselines each give phase \(\pi/2\). The closing baseline then gives \(-\pi\). Their complex visibilities are \((1+0.5i)/1.5\), \((1+0.5i)/1.5\) and \((1-0.5)/1.5=1/3\).

Only the first two terms contribute nonzero phase. Their summed argument is \(2\arctan(0.5)=53.1^\circ\). Reflect the binary about the origin and all visibility phases reverse, giving \(-53.1^\circ\) while every two-telescope visibility magnitude is unchanged. At 600 nm and projected separation 3.00 milliarcseconds, the first two projected baseline lengths are \(B=\lambda/(4s)=10.3\) m. For a vanishingly faint companion each visibility tends to one and closure phase tends to zero. Combining several independent triangles with magnitude observations can therefore distinguish asymmetric brightness distributions that reproduce an identical contrast on a single baseline.

The chosen one-dimensional triangle can be realized with three equally spaced collectors. Its closing baseline then has twice the magnitude of either short baseline and points in the opposite direction under cyclic orientation. In a general two-dimensional array the vectors still form a closed triangle, even though their lengths no longer have this simple ratio. Reversing every baseline convention conjugates the triple product and reverses the reported phase. Astrometric orientation and instrument phase conventions must therefore be calibrated before interpreting a phase sign as the direction of a companion on the sky.

Infer an angular diameter from a visibility null

For a monochromatic, uniformly bright circular disk the normalised mutual coherence is \(2J_1(s)/s\), \(s=\pi B\theta/\lambda\). A two-aperture instrument measuring contrast sees its magnitude. Seek a minimum across a range of baselines.
