Why opening a circular aperture can darken its centre

The on-axis intensity behind a round opening may decrease when the opening grows. Following the phase contributed by each annulus gives a quantitative prediction for this near-field diffraction effect.

Add the complex contributions from the pupil

Illuminate a circular hole of radius \(a\) in a thin opaque screen with a uniform monochromatic plane wave. Observe at distance \(z\) on the symmetry axis. An annulus at radius \(r\) has area \(2\pi r\,dr\). Its path to the detector is \(\sqrt{z^2+r^2}=z+r^2/(2z)-r^4/(8z^3)+\cdots\). When \(a/z\) is small, slowly varying amplitude and inclination factors can be taken at the centre. Retain the quadratic phase but demand that the discarded quartic phase \(k a^4/(8z^3)\) be small. The quadratic phase itself can span several cycles.

With the Fresnel propagator \(e^{ikz}/(i\lambda z)\), the field relative to an unobstructed plane wave at the same point is therefore an elementary integral. Use \(v=r^2\), so \(2r\,dr=dv\). The increasing annular area exactly compensates the decreasing radial width of successive equal-phase intervals.

\[\frac{U}{U_{\rm free}}=\frac{2\pi}{i\lambda z}\int_0^a r\,e^{i\pi r^2/(\lambda z)}\,dr=1-e^{i\pi N},\]
\[N=\frac{a^2}{\lambda z}.\]

Taking the squared magnitude gives \(I/I_{\rm free}=2-2\cos(\pi N)=4\sin^2(\pi N/2)\). The phase convention can reverse the exponent while leaving the intensity unchanged. Odd integer \(N\) gives intensity four times the unobstructed intensity on axis. Even integer \(N\) gives an ideal zero. A local enhancement is compatible with energy conservation because interference redistributes intensity across the observation plane.

Divide the opening into half-period zones

Boundaries \(r_j=\sqrt{j\lambda z}\) separate annuli whose outer and inner points differ by phase \(\pi\). All complete zones have area \(\pi\lambda z\) and alternating complex contributions under this approximation. Opening one complete zone gives a bright axial spot. Opening its neighbour provides the opposite amplitude. If a small detector instead averages over off-axis positions, the phases no longer have perfect azimuthal symmetry and the measured minimum fills in.

For \(\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}\) and \(z=0.50\,\mathrm m\), the first boundary has radius \(0.500\,\mathrm{mm}\). The next has radius \(0.707\,\mathrm{mm}\). At fixed opening \(a=0.500\,\mathrm{mm}\) decreasing the screen distance to \(0.25\,\mathrm m\) also changes \(N\) from one to two. Thus an axial scan with a fixed pupil tests the same interference argument as changing the aperture. The small ratio \(a/z\) at these distances makes the approximations internally consistent.

Compare amplitude addition with area scaling

At small \(N\), expand the exponential to find \(U/U_{\rm free}\simeq-i\pi N\), hence intensity grows as \(N^2\) or \(a^4\). This follows because all small-pupil contributions arrive nearly in phase. The small-opening expansion fails long before the paraxial integral does. Vary the radius and distance below. Compare the position of the first minimum with the radius at the first maximum. The display evaluates intensity as a function of physical radius so that the square-root spacing becomes visible. Real propagation includes the pupil transmission profile, spectral bandwidth and detector size.

Tutorial on a partially blocked zone

Keep only the annulus between the first and second half-period boundaries. What intensity would it produce on axis by itself? What if that annulus and the central disc both transmit, but the annulus transmits only half the electric-field amplitude?

Work through the solution

At \(N=1\) the central disc contributes \(2U_{\rm free}\). At \(N=2\) the total contribution vanishes. The annulus therefore supplies \(-2U_{\rm free}\) and by itself has normalised intensity four. With amplitude transmission one half its contribution is \(-U_{\rm free}\). Adding the disc gives \(U_{\rm free}\), or normalised intensity one.

Behind a \(0.50\) mm opening at 500 nm, find the first three on-axis maxima as the detector approaches from large positive \(z\).

Work through the solution

Maxima occur at odd \(N=1,3,5\). Since \(a^2/\lambda=0.50\,\mathrm m\), the distances are 0.50, 0.1667 and 0.100 m. The spacings shrink toward the aperture because the controlled phase is proportional to \(1/z\). Continuing the sequence requires checking the neglected quartic phase.

At the \(N=2\) null, increase the aperture radius by 1 percent with everything else fixed. Estimate the residual relative intensity from the leading nonzero term.

Work through the solution

The new Fresnel number is \(2(1.01)^2=2.0402\). Near an even integer, \(4\sin^2(\pi\delta N/2)\simeq\pi^2(\delta N)^2\), giving 0.0160. The first derivative of intensity vanishes at the ideal null but a small geometric error still creates a measurable finite signal.
