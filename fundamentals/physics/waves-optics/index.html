    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      A local force balance on a string gives a wave speed. Superpose travelling waves to satisfy interfaces and make packets. In the short-wavelength limit their phase fronts lead to rays, focal lengths and images.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Restore the information that ray tracing discards. Polarisation describes a vector field, coherence determines which fields can interfere, and an aperture maps a wavefront to a diffraction pattern that limits image detail.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Repeated optical paths select frequencies in an interferometer. Many illuminated grooves resolve nearby wavelengths. Beams and widely separated receivers offer two different ways to infer spatial structure from wave phase.

    Chapters

    1. Travelling waves and energy on a string

      Derive wave speed from the local force balance and determine how much average power a transverse travelling wave carries.

    2. Impedance, reflection and standing waves

      Derive amplitude and energy reflection at a string junction and recover the resonance conditions of a finite resonator.

    3. Wave packets and dispersion

      Derive phase and group velocity from a pair of waves, then use a dispersion relation to predict how a finite packet moves and spreads.

    4. Optical paths, lenses and images

      Use stationary optical path length and the paraxial approximation to predict refraction, focal length and the image formed on a screen.

    5. Polarisation and phase retardation

      Predict the output of a polariser–retarder arrangement and distinguish changes of polarisation from loss of beam power.

    6. Coherence and interferometry

      Use a variable path delay to connect fringe visibility to a source spectrum and estimate how far two copies of a wave can remain correlated.

    7. Diffraction as a Fourier transform

      Derive the far-field pattern from a finite aperture and identify which features control its scale and its sidelobes.

    8. Images through a finite pupil

      Separate single-point diffraction, two-point resolution and spatial-frequency contrast for a circular imaging aperture.

    9. Multiple-beam interference in a cavity

      Sum the returning fields in a plane-parallel optical cavity and turn measured line spacing and width into a mirror diagnostic.

    10. How a grating separates wavelengths

      Derive the angular dispersion and resolving power of a finite grating and diagnose an order overlap.

    11. Gaussian beams and focusing

      Relate beam waist, wavefront curvature and divergence, and estimate the focus of a collimated laser beam.

    12. Measuring a star with interference

      Infer angular diameter or binary separation from the spatial coherence of light at two separated collectors.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. Reflection, polarisation and the field beyond a reflecting surface

      A ray in glass can reflect completely at an air boundary. A second piece of glass brought close to that boundary can nevertheless change the reflection. The field outside the glass follows from the same equations that determine the reflected intensity.

    2. How a dielectric slab chooses its guided modes

      A plane interface supports a decaying field outside glass under total reflection. Put two interfaces around a thin core and a new question appears. Which transverse patterns reproduce themselves as the wave propagates along the guide?

    3. Why opening a circular aperture can darken its centre

      The on-axis intensity behind a round opening may decrease when the opening grows. Following the phase contributed by each annulus gives a quantitative prediction for this near-field diffraction effect.

    4. When do two curved mirrors support a confined beam?

      A ray launched slightly away from the axis may stay near it for many round trips or walk off a mirror. A single round-trip matrix predicts which behaviour occurs and selects the Gaussian beam that can reproduce its wavefront.

    5. Threshold, gain saturation and the decay of stored light

      After establishing a transverse cavity mode, consider its intensity over many round trips. The same mirror losses determine a threshold with pumping and a measurable decay time when gain is removed.

    6. Turning invisible phase into visible intensity in a Fourier plane

      A transparent specimen can vary the phase of a beam while transmitting nearly constant intensity. A pair of lenses gives independent access to the undiffracted beam and the waves carrying those small phase variations. Recombining them with a controlled relative phase makes the specimen visible.

    7. Building a dielectric mirror from weakly reflecting interfaces

      A single transparent dielectric interface often reflects only a few percent of the optical power. A periodic stack can reflect nearly all incident light over a finite wavelength band. Its response follows from matching both fields at every interface, including every internally reflected wave.

    8. Why a grating makes images of itself in empty space

      Illuminate a periodic mask with a monochromatic plane wave. A screen placed at certain distances records the original pattern even though there is no imaging lens. Derive the recurrence from the longitudinal phase of each diffraction order, predict the half-period displacement, and use exact propagation to test the paraxial argument.

    Applied study

    Predict the spacing and visibility of interference fringes from phase, wavelength and aperture size.

    What determines the dark fringes behind two slits?

    Light reaches a point on a screen from two open slits. Closing one slit can make that point brighter. To predict the result, start with a wave field rather than the power arriving from each slit separately. In a homogeneous lossless medium a scalar component \(u\) obeys \(\nabla^2u=v^{-2}\partial_{tt}u\). A monochromatic solution can be written \(u=\operatorname{Re}\{A(\mathbf x)e^{-i\omega t}\}\). The phase of \(A\) advances by \(kr\) along a path of length \(r\), with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\) and \(v=\omega/k\).

    The wave equation is linear, so complex amplitudes add. A detector averaging over many cycles records an intensity proportional to the squared magnitude. For two waves of the same frequency and polarisation,

    \[I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\Delta\phi.\]

    This last term records the relative phase. Random changes of relative phase over a measurement wash it out.

    Go from path difference to angular position

    Let two identical narrow slits be separated centre to centre by \(d\) and illuminated normally and in phase. In the far field the paths at angle \(\theta\) to the screen normal differ by \(d\sin\theta\). Write the two equal amplitudes as \(A_0e^{\pm ikd\sin\theta/2}\). Their sum is \(2A_0\cos(kd\sin\theta/2)\), apart from a common phase. Destructive interference therefore occurs at \(d\sin\theta=(m+1/2)\lambda\), with integer \(m\) when the angle exists.

    A real slit has width \(w\). Every point \(x\) across it contributes phase \(kx\sin\theta\). Integrating \(e^{ikx\sin\theta}\) from \(-w/2\) to \(w/2\) gives \(w\sin\beta/\beta\),

    \[\beta=\pi w\sin\theta/\lambda.\]

    Uniformly illuminated rectangular slits then give the normalised intensity

    \[\frac{I(\theta)}{I(0)}=\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2 \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right).\]

    The first factor sets the envelope. The second makes the fine fringes. The expression assumes equal coherent illumination, a common polarisation and negligible change of amplitude across each slit. For an observation distance \(z\) much larger than \((d+w)^2/\lambda\), the quadratic variation of propagation phase across this aperture can be neglected.

    Predict a fringe spacing and a missing order

    Use red light of wavelength 600 nm, \(d=0.20\) mm, \(w=0.040\) mm and a screen 2.0 m away. Here \((d+w)^2/\lambda=0.096\) m, well below the screen distance. At small angles the screen position is \(y\simeq z\sin\theta\). The first dark fringe is at \(z\lambda/(2d)=3.0\) mm. Adjacent nominal bright orders are 6.0 mm apart.

    The first single-slit zero lies at \(z\lambda/w=30\) mm. It coincides with the fifth positive interference order, so that order is missing even with perfect coherence. Changing slit width can remove an order without changing the underlying spacing of the interference fringes.

    What sets the resolving power of a lens?

    Apply the aperture integral to a different observation. Two distant point sources viewed through a lens each produce a diffraction pattern in the focal plane. For a rectangular pupil of width \(W\), a single source produces a first zero at angle \(\lambda/W\) in the direction across that width. Translating a second source through an angle \(\Delta\theta\) shifts its entire pattern by \(f\Delta\theta\) for focal length \(f\).

    When a second maximum falls at the first zero of the first source, the angular separation is about \(\lambda/W\) (the Rayleigh criterion for this pupil). With \(W=5.0\) mm, \(\lambda=600\) nm and \(f=100\) mm, this is \(1.2\times10^{-4}\) rad or 12 µm between image peaks. For mutually incoherent sources the intensities of the two patterns add. In the slit experiment the coherent amplitudes added. This distinction determines whether overlapping light shows interference fringes. At an interface the relative strength of each optical polarisation can change. Polarisation and reflection at glass.

    Exercises on visibility and wavelength

    1. Distinguish imbalance from lost coherence

    At the observation point one slit alone gives intensity \(4I_0\), the other \(I_0\). The phase can be varied. What are the extremes and fringe visibility for perfectly coherent, parallel-polarised illumination? What would completely random relative phase do?

    Calculate the extremes

    The individual amplitudes have ratio 2:1. In phase,

    \[I_{\max}=(2+1)^2I_0=9I_0.\]

    Out of phase,

    \[I_{\min}=(2-1)^2I_0=I_0.\]

    The visibility \((I_{\max}-I_{\min})/(I_{\max}+I_{\min})=0.80\). A nonzero minimum can therefore coexist with perfect coherence. With a uniformly random relative phase the time-averaged intensity is \(5I_0\) throughout the phase scan and visibility vanishes.

    2. Change the light source

    Replace 600 nm light by 450 nm light in the worked arrangement. Find the first dark position, the first envelope zero and the index of the first missing bright order.

    Compare the two length scales

    Both screen distances scale with wavelength at small angle, giving \(3.0(450/600)=2.25\) mm and \(30(450/600)=22.5\) mm. The condition for coincidence of a bright order and the first envelope zero gives \(m=d/w=5\), independent of wavelength.

    The numerical example drives two acoustic slits in phase. Sound pressure obeys the scalar Helmholtz equation, so the path-difference and aperture calculations transfer directly for the same \(d/\lambda\) and \(w/\lambda\). This model uses rigid acoustic walls and has no optical polarisation. Its receivers lie at a finite distance of 0.90 m. Two-slit pressure field and angular probes.

    Run the notebook with its supplied ratios = [2, 3, 4] sweep, \(d=0.30\) m, \(w=0.050\) m and sound speed 343 m/s. Each frequency is a separate solve on the same mesh. Read the first minimum on the positive-angle side of the pressure-amplitude profile. Squaring the pressure magnitude gives a mean-square pressure pattern with the same minima. In a locally travelling plane wave, mean-square pressure divided by acoustic impedance gives the mean intensity.

    1. Baseline. At 3430 Hz, \(d=3\lambda\). Predict \(\arcsin(1/6)=9.59°\) and compare with the computed minimum.
    2. Lower the frequency. At \(d=2\lambda\), predict the first minimum at 14.48°. Check that the separation between the two dark fringes nearest the centre increases.
    3. Raise the frequency. At \(d=4\lambda\), expect 7.18°. Check all three first minima within 0.5° of the far-field estimate. Then compare the outer minima and report where the finite observation distance and the assumed uniform slit illumination begin to matter.
