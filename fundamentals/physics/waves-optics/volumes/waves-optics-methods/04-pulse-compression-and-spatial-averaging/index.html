Dispersive pulse compression and a detector's view of a tilted pulse

An ultrashort pulse can broaden because its spectral components acquire different group delays or because different parts of its beam arrive at different times. Derive the two mechanisms separately, predict when added glass can shorten an already chirped pulse, and decide which measurements distinguish them.

Derive the propagation of a spectral envelope

Write the positive-frequency electric field as the real carrier with complex envelope \(A(z,t)\), using the Fourier convention \(A(t)\propto\int\tilde A(\Omega)e^{-i\Omega t}d\Omega\),

\[\Omega=\omega-\omega_0.\]

Propagation through a transparent homogeneous length \(L\) multiplies the spectrum by \(e^{ik(\omega)L}\). Expand \(k=k_0+k_1\Omega+k_2\Omega^2/2+\cdots\). The constant is an overall phase, the linear term shifts the pulse by group delay \(k_1L\), and the quadratic term gives group-delay dispersion \(D=k_2L\) in seconds squared. The group-delay difference between spectral components separated by \(\Delta\Omega\) is \(D\Delta\Omega\).

Take a Gaussian spectral amplitude \(\tilde A(\Omega)=A_0\exp[-\tau_0^2\Omega^2/2]\), and give it an initial quadratic spectral phase \(\phi_2\Omega^2/2\). The effective quadratic coefficient after propagation is \(\Phi=\phi_2+D\). Fourier transforming the Gaussian at the retarded time \(T=t-k_1L\) gives an envelope proportional to \((\tau_0^2-i\Phi)^{-1/2}\exp[-T^2/(2(\tau_0^2-i\Phi))]\). Its intensity is Gaussian with temporal scale \(\tau=\tau_0\sqrt{1+(\Phi/\tau_0^2)^2}\), where intensity is proportional to \(\exp[-T^2/\tau^2]\). The intensity full width at half maximum is \(2\sqrt{\ln2}\,\tau\); keeping this convention explicit prevents confusing amplitude widths, intensity widths and rms durations.

The intensity spectrum is unchanged by quadratic phase in a lossless element. Temporal broadening reduces the peak at fixed integrated pulse energy by \(\tau_0/\tau\). Its duration depends on \(\Phi^2\), so one measured width at a single setting cannot determine the sign of chirp. Measuring the width after a known added dispersion does break that degeneracy. The full temporal phase contains a quadratic term giving instantaneous frequency that changes approximately linearly through the pulse. A transform-limited Gaussian has \(\Delta t_{\rm FWHM}\Delta\nu_{\rm FWHM}=2\ln2/\pi\simeq0.441\) when frequency is in cycles per second.

Calculate an imperfect compressor setting

Start with a transform-limited duration \(30.0\,{\rm fs}\), so \(\tau_0=18.0\,{\rm fs}\) and \(\tau_0^2=325\,{\rm fs^2}\). A spectral phase of \(+400\,{\rm fs^2}\) broadens this pulse to about \(47.6\,{\rm fs}\), with peak intensity about \(0.630\) of its transform-limited value. Passing it through a component that contributes \(-300\,{\rm fs^2}\) leaves \(\Phi=100\,{\rm fs^2}\) and duration \(31.4\,{\rm fs}\). The same component applied to a pulse initially at \(-400\,{\rm fs^2}\) instead gives \(-700\,{\rm fs^2}\) and substantially more broadening. A quoted dispersion accuracy without the initial sign does not establish compression.

Experimental scans are simplest in squared width. Vary known \(D\) and fit \(\Delta t^2=\Delta t_0^2[1+((D+\phi_2)/\tau_0^2)^2]\). The parabola has a minimum at \(D=-\phi_2\). A non-Gaussian spectrum or a cubic spectral phase can produce pedestals, asymmetric profiles or a minimum width larger than the transform limit inferred from spectrum. In an intensity autocorrelation a Gaussian pulse has a correlation width \(\sqrt2\) times its intensity width. Apply the correct deconvolution for the assumed shape, or measure a spectrally resolved temporal diagnostic when the shape itself is unknown.

Derive pulse-front tilt and the spatially averaged width

Suppose the centre arrival time varies across one transverse coordinate as \(T_c(x)=s x\). The slope \(s\) has units of time per length. A grating or misaligned dispersive optic can correlate frequency, propagation angle and position. For a local pulse of intensity \(\exp[-(t-sx)^2/\tau^2]\) and beam weighting \(\exp[-x^2/w^2]\), a detector integrating the whole beam sees the temporal convolution of the local profile with the distribution of arrival centres. Independent Gaussian variances add, giving measured temporal scale \(\tau_{\rm d}^2=\tau^2+s^2 w^2\). A single-pixel trace at fixed \(x\) remains narrow and shifts in time. A whole-beam trace broadens even when no additional spectral phase changes along any one local ray.

With \(w=0.50\,{\rm mm}\) in this intensity convention and \(s=40\,{\rm fs/mm}\), the extra scale is \(20\,{\rm fs}\). A locally transform-limited \(30.0\,{\rm fs}\) pulse has \(\tau_0=18.0\,{\rm fs}\), so the detector width becomes

\[2\sqrt{\ln2}\sqrt{18.0^2+20^2}=44.8\,{\rm fs}.\]

Changing the aperture alters the broadened trace by changing the sampled transverse weighting. A hard slit does not produce a Gaussian weighting, so the variance addition remains applicable for independent position and local pulse shape but the full width at half maximum need not follow the same Gaussian conversion.

Angular dispersion can create such a delay gradient. Near the reference propagation axis a spectral plane wave carries transverse phase \(k_x(\omega)x\). In the inverse transform the term \((dk_x/d\omega)\Omega x\) shifts the local envelope time by \((dk_x/d\omega)x\), giving \(s=dk_x/d\omega\) under the adopted conventions. Keeping only \(k_x(\omega_0)\) accounts for a tilted carrier phase but misses the tilted pulse front. Mapping cross-correlation delay versus transverse position separates this linear slope from uniform material dispersion.

Predict a compression scan and perturb its detection area

The live temporal traces compare a local pulse and the second trace integrated across a known Gaussian beam, with the same normalised pulse energy. Predict the added dispersion that gives the narrowest local pulse, then turn up the transverse delay slope without moving the compressor. Which curve keeps its width? A larger minimum in an area-integrated scan is compatible with spatial averaging even when a local sample reaches its transform limit. Use a second spatial sampling condition before fitting that extra duration entirely to a higher-order material phase.

Check the group delay with two narrow spectral slices

A pulse-width scan determines the magnitude of residual quadratic phase but can hide its sign at a single setting. Split off two narrow spectral slices centred at offsets \(+\Delta\Omega/2\) and \(-\Delta\Omega/2\), and compare their envelope delays against a common reference. To first order the arrival-time difference is \(\Phi\Delta\Omega\). At \(\Phi=+400\,{\rm fs^2}\) and an ordinary-frequency separation of \(5.0\,{\rm THz}\), \(\Delta\Omega=0.0314\,{\rm rad/fs}\), giving a \(12.6\,{\rm fs}\) delay difference. Under the adopted propagation convention the higher-frequency slice arrives later for positive dispersion.

The spectral filters themselves can contribute unequal group delays and broaden their selected pulses. Calibrate their differential delay using a reference path or a reference spectrum with independently characterised phase. Narrowing each slice improves its frequency selectivity while increasing its temporal duration, so a cross-correlation delay fit must retain adequate signal and temporal stability. Measuring several frequency centres permits a slope fit; visible curvature of delay against angular frequency indicates the need for a cubic or higher spectral phase. A camera position offset correlated with selected wavelength can add an apparent slope through pulse-front tilt, so compare at the same physical beam position as well as after spatial integration.

Tutorial on interpreting pulse widths

A \(50\,{\rm fs}\) transform-limited Gaussian has a measured optical bandwidth of \(5.0\,{\rm THz}\). Are the two measurements consistent if both are intensity full widths at half maximum?

Work through the solution

Their product is \(0.250\), below the Gaussian transform-limited value \(0.441\). A \(50\,{\rm fs}\) Gaussian requires about \(8.82\,{\rm THz}\) of ordinary-frequency width. Check whether the reported bandwidth was angular frequency, an amplitude rather than intensity width, or clipped by the spectrometer. Pure spectral dispersion cannot reduce the time-bandwidth product below the transform limit.

The minimum duration occurs at \(D=-250\,{\rm fs^2}\). What is the input quadratic phase, and what additional observation would show significant residual cubic phase?

Work through the solution

The simple model gives

\[\phi_2=+250\,{\rm fs^2}.\]

A minimum much longer than the transform limit for the measured spectrum, or asymmetric temporal satellites that persist near the minimum, motivates a higher-order spectral-phase measurement. Spatial averaging must be controlled separately before attributing every residual feature to cubic dispersion.

Two local cross-correlation peaks differ by \(24\,{\rm fs}\) between transverse positions separated by \(0.60\,{\rm mm}\). What extra rms time spread is expected across the \(w=0.50\,{\rm mm}\) Gaussian beam?

Work through the solution

The slope is \(40\,{\rm fs/mm}\). Since the transverse rms width is \(w/\sqrt2\) for the stated intensity convention, the rms arrival spread is

\[40(0.50)/\sqrt2=14.1\,{\rm fs}.\]

The corresponding Gaussian scale parameter \(s w=20\,{\rm fs}\) is larger by \(\sqrt2\). Match the width convention to the formula used by the detector fit.
