Infer a superconducting gap from a tunnelling spectrum

A tunnel junction reports differential conductance versus voltage. Extracting a superconducting energy gap requires connecting a condensate's quasiparticle dispersion to a density of states, then accounting for temperature and finite spectral resolution before comparing peaks with the data.

Diagonalise the paired-state Hamiltonian

In a simple isotropic weak-coupling description, pair a state of normal-state energy \(\xi_k=\epsilon_k-\mu\) with the time-reversed hole amplitude. Take a real gap \(\Delta>0\) by a phase choice. The two-component mean-field matrix has rows \((\xi_k,\Delta)\) and \((\Delta,-\xi_k)\), giving eigenvalues \(\pm E_k\) with \(E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+\Delta^2}\). The positive-energy quasiparticle mixes particle-like and hole-like amplitudes with squared coefficients \((1+\xi_k/E_k)/2\) and \((1-\xi_k/E_k)/2\). Their sum is one. Near the normal-state Fermi surface they are equal, while far above it the excitation becomes predominantly particle like.

The minimum positive excitation energy is \(\Delta\). Creating two independent quasiparticles requires at least \(2\Delta\) in the ideal isotropic spectrum. These two thresholds refer to different probes. In an ideal normal-metal–insulator–superconductor tunnel junction, a bias can transfer one electron into an allowed quasiparticle state; the zero-temperature threshold is \(|eV|=\Delta\). In a junction between two identical gapped electrodes, lowest-order quasiparticle tunnelling at zero temperature instead begins near \(2\Delta\), aside from pair tunnelling and other processes. Confusing the physical junction type can double the reported gap.

Transform the normal-state density of levels

Assume approximately constant normal electronic density near the Fermi level over an energy range much larger than \(\Delta\). The quasiparticle dispersion flattens near \(\xi=0\). Mapping the two normal-state branches \(\xi=\pm\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}\) onto a positive energy and accounting for their electron spectral weights gives the normalised superconducting tunnelling density

\[\frac{N_s(E)}{N_n(0)}=\begin{cases}0,&|E|<\Delta,\\ |E|/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2},&|E|>\Delta.\end{cases}\]

The divergence at the gap edge is integrable. Indeed integrating from \(\Delta\) to \(A>\Delta\) gives \(\sqrt{A^2-\Delta^2}\), tending toward the corresponding normal integral at large cutoff. The edge feature represents redistribution of states from the gap, rather than creation of an infinite number of states. In a finite normal band the constant-density approximation is only local, so statements about total spectral weight should use a consistent large-energy model.

For \(\Delta=1.50\,{\rm meV}\), the ideal density at \(E=2.00\,{\rm meV}\) is \(2/\sqrt{4-2.25}=1.51\) times the normal level. At \(5.0\,{\rm meV}\) it is approximately \(1.048\), illustrating the slow approach to the normal baseline. A normalising window too close to the edge can bias the whole inferred curve downward. A comparison with a normal-state spectrum taken with the same junction can control a varying normal density or voltage-dependent transmission better than a single distant conductance point.

Derive the thermal convolution measured by a normal probe

In lowest-order tunnelling with a featureless normal tip and approximately constant matrix elements, the current is proportional to \(\int N_s(E)[f(E-eV)-f(E)]\,dE\). The two Fermi factors count occupied initial and available final states. Differentiating in bias gives normalised conductance \(G(V)/G_n=\int[N_s(E)/N_n][-f'(E-eV)]\,dE\). Here \(-f'(x)=\operatorname{sech}^2[x/(2k_BT)]/(4k_BT)\) has unit integral. At zero temperature it becomes a delta function and the conductance reproduces the density. At finite temperature it samples a window: conductance can be nonzero inside the underlying hard gap without any actual subgap states.

The full width at half height of this derivative is \(4k_BT\,\operatorname{arcosh}\sqrt2\approx3.53k_BT\). At \(T=2.0\,{\rm K}\) this is \(0.608\,{\rm meV}\), appreciable compared with a \(1.50\,{\rm meV}\) gap. The electron temperature at the tunnel contact can exceed the temperature reading of a nearby bath. Voltage noise and a finite lock-in excitation add other convolution kernels. A fit that varies gap, thermal width and lifetime broadening all freely against a single narrow window can have strongly correlated parameters. Measure an independent thermal reference or repeat with deliberately varied excitation to separate instrumental from material broadening.

Compute the ideal convolution with an edge-safe quadrature

Numerically sampling the divergent density at one grid point exactly on the edge gives an artificial infinity. A variable transformation removes it. For \(E>\Delta\) set \(\xi=\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}\). Then \((E/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2})\,dE=d\xi\). Integrate over positive \(\xi\) and include the negative-energy mirror:

\[G/G_n=\int_0^\infty[K_T(\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}-eV)+K_T(-\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}-eV)]\,d\xi.\]

Both terms are smooth at zero for positive temperature. This numerical reformulation follows directly from state counting.

The live spectrum uses this transformed integral with energies in meV and voltages in mV for a single electron. First choose a low but finite temperature and locate the two coherence features. Increase temperature at fixed \(\Delta\) to isolate the effect of the measurement kernel. A physically heated sample would generally also have a temperature-dependent gap; this controlled calculation keeps the intrinsic spectrum explicit. Compare the zero-bias conductance, peak height and high-bias baseline. A change in one feature alone seldom establishes the source of broadening.

Identify a spectral asymmetry that the symmetric model cannot fit

For a featureless normal tip and a particle–hole symmetric superconducting spectrum, the convolution with the even thermal kernel gives

\[G(V)=G(-V).\]

The average of opposite bias measurements estimates the even component, while half their difference isolates an odd component. A small bias-offset calibration error \(V_0\) produces an apparent odd part approximately \(-V_0\,dG_{\rm even}/dV\) for small shifts. It is therefore largest on steep gap-edge flanks and changes sign when the bias offset reverses. An energy-dependent normal background can generate a smoother intrinsic asymmetry; a temperature sweep through the transition and an independent voltage-zero calibration help distinguish these mechanisms.

Suppose the spectral flanks have slope \(1.8G_n/{\rm mV}\) and the measured odd part peaks near \(0.036G_n\). A voltage offset of only \(0.020\,{\rm mV}\) could generate that size, much smaller than the illustrative gap. Comparing the sign pattern on both flanks tests the derivative shape rather than fitting one data point. A single shifted peak position can reflect offset, thermal broadening or changing gap amplitude. Record both bias directions, both coherence peaks and their separation as well as their midpoint. A common shift of both peaks changes the midpoint but ideally leaves their separation unchanged.

The model also assumes a normal electrode with roughly flat density. If the tip itself is superconducting, the current is a convolution of both electrode densities and their occupation difference. In that case symmetric features can shift even when the sample gap is unchanged, requiring an independent tip calibration or a different junction model.

Tutorial on tunnelling data

An ideal normal-tip spectrum at very low temperature has gap edges separated by \(3.20\,{\rm mV}\). Estimate \(\Delta\), and compare with a weak-coupling transition temperature using \(2\Delta/(k_BT_c)\approx3.53\).

Work through the solution

The symmetric single-particle edges lie near \(\pm\Delta/e\), hence \(\Delta\approx1.60\,{\rm meV}\). Inserting \(k_B=0.08617\,{\rm meV/K}\) gives \(T_c\approx10.5\,{\rm K}\). This ratio is a weak-coupling benchmark for the isotropic model, not an exact conversion for every material. Verify the actual transition and junction type independently.

An experiment at \(4.2\,{\rm K}\) tries to distinguish two gaps differing by \(0.10\,{\rm meV}\) using peak positions alone. Which scale should be compared first?

Work through the solution

The thermal derivative width is \(3.53(0.08617)(4.2)\approx1.28\,{\rm meV}\), much larger than that gap difference. High signal quality and a constrained model can sometimes infer parameters finer than a raw linewidth, but separate visible peaks are unlikely from thermal resolution alone. Lower electron temperature and calibrated voltage modulation add stronger discriminating evidence.

What check distinguishes a quadrature error from true thermal subgap conductance?

Work through the solution

Refine the transformed integration grid, extend its energy cutoff and confirm the conductance is even in voltage for this symmetric model. A normal reference with \(\Delta=0\) should yield unity at every bias when the kernel is fully integrated. The computed zero-bias value for positive gap should fall as temperature decreases. A subgap signal sensitive to whether an energy grid happens to land on an edge is a numerical artifact.
