    Contents

    1. Which reciprocal-lattice points produce a diffraction spot?

      A bright spot depends on the wavelength and orientation of an incident beam, on the periodic repeat of a crystal, and on the atoms inside one repeat. Separate these three conditions so that a missing reflection becomes an interpretable observation. Then use the relative strength of two spots to estimate chemical order.

    2. Read atomic motion from Bragg attenuation and diffuse scattering

      A crystal can retain its periodic mean positions while its diffraction pattern changes markedly with temperature. Start with the thermal average of a scattering amplitude, derive the displacement factor and learn which component of atomic motion a given reflection measures.

    3. Construct allowed bands from a cell transfer matrix

      In a finite well a bound level follows from matching its wavefunction at the two walls. A periodic lattice replaces those two external walls by a matching condition on every cell. Derive a band criterion that works without guessing a wave inside the forbidden gap and use it to predict transmission through a finite sequence of cells.

    4. Why a large joint density of states can leave a dark optical edge

      A band diagram gives the energy an electron needs to move between bands. It does not by itself tell whether light couples those states. Construct a two-orbital model with an explicit velocity operator, calculate its allowed and suppressed transitions, and distinguish electronic band curvature from optical selection rules.

    5. Count vacancies and infer an atomic migration barrier

      Tracer atoms spread through a crystal even when the average lattice positions remain fixed. The rate can depend both on the number of vacant sites and on how readily an atom jumps into one. Derive the two factors separately, then compare an equilibrated specimen with a rapidly quenched one.

    6. Measure a phonon mean free path without confusing it with a sample length

      A cold insulating bar can carry heat primarily through lattice vibrations. Heat capacity tells how much energy the vibrations can carry; conductivity also depends on how far and in which direction they carry it. Derive the connection, test its limiting regimes, and identify when a measured thermal resistance belongs to a boundary.

    7. A field profile that distinguishes a superconductor from a perfect conductor

      Zero dc resistance tells how a current can persist. Field expulsion tells how a specimen responds when it enters a superconducting equilibrium state. Derive the London length, calculate what a finite slab and a thin film actually screen, and connect magnetic and electrical measurements to the same superfluid density.

    8. Infer a superconducting gap from a tunnelling spectrum

      A tunnel junction reports differential conductance versus voltage. Extracting a superconducting energy gap requires connecting a condensate's quasiparticle dispersion to a density of states, then accounting for temperature and finite spectral resolution before comparing peaks with the data.
