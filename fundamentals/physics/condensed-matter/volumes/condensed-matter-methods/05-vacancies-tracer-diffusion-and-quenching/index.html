Count vacancies and infer an atomic migration barrier

Tracer atoms spread through a crystal even when the average lattice positions remain fixed. The rate can depend both on the number of vacant sites and on how readily an atom jumps into one. Derive the two factors separately, then compare an equilibrated specimen with a rapidly quenched one.

Minimise the free energy of a dilute vacancy population

Take \(N\) lattice sites and \(n\) equivalent vacancies, ignoring their interactions. The microscopic formation Gibbs free energy \(G_f=H_f-TS_f\) includes local vibrational and other nonconfigurational changes associated with creating one defect and transferring the removed atom to an appropriate reservoir. The configurational multiplicity is \(\binom{N}{n}\). Thus \(G-G_0=nG_f-k_BT\ln\binom{N}{n}\). Applying Stirling's approximation and differentiating with respect to \(n\) gives \(G_f+k_BT\ln[n/(N-n)]=0\), or \(c_v/(1-c_v)=e^{-G_f/k_BT}\), where \(c_v=n/N\). In the dilute limit \(c_v\simeq e^{S_f/k_B}e^{-H_f/k_BT}\).

A positive formation enthalpy does not imply zero vacancies in thermal equilibrium. The number of ways to place a few defects contributes entropy. The dilute independent-site argument ceases to apply if vacancies cluster, interact strongly, or if charge neutrality ties their population to other charged defects. Specifying the atomic reservoir is especially important in compounds: removing a metal atom and removing a nonmetal atom are different chemical operations with potentially different energies and charge states. The present simple model describes one kind of neutral vacancy under fixed thermodynamic conditions.

For \(H_f=1.00\,{\rm eV}\), \(S_f=k_B\) and \(T=800\,{\rm K}\), \(k_BT=0.06894\,{\rm eV}\) and \(c_v\simeq1.36\times10^{-6}\). A cubic micrometre containing an illustrative \(5\times10^{10}\) sites then contains about \(6.8\times10^4\) vacancies on average. The fraction is tiny while the count in a region accessible to a diffusion experiment can be large. In a nanocrystal with far fewer sites the mean can be much less than one; a single defect entering or leaving may then change the observed motion intermittently.

Derive a tracer diffusivity from correlated jumps

Let the coordination number be \(z\), jump length \(d\), and the conditional rate of one atom jumping into one specified neighbouring vacancy be \(\Gamma=\nu_0e^{-H_m/k_BT}\). For dilute, randomly located vacancies a tracer has on average \(zc_v\) vacant nearest neighbours and jump rate \(z c_v\Gamma\). For an uncorrelated isotropic three-dimensional random walk \(\langle|\Delta\mathbf r|^2\rangle=z c_v\Gamma t\,d^2=6Dt\). Successive tracer jumps can be correlated because the vacancy just exchanged with it is now directly behind it. Introduce the cumulative correlation factor \(f\) to obtain

\[D=f z d^2\Gamma c_v/6.\]

The definition of \(\Gamma\) per specified neighbour matters: a tabulated total jump frequency already summed over neighbours must not acquire a second factor \(z\).

Under the stated equilibrium assumptions the apparent Arrhenius activation energy of the tracer is \(H_f+H_m\). The first term controls whether an opportunity to jump exists, the second controls the rate at which an available opportunity is taken. At fixed excess vacancy concentration only the migration contribution enters to leading order if \(f\) and the prefactor stay nearly constant. This offers a way to separate the two barriers, provided the excess concentration persists during the measurement. A fitted straight line through data from specimens with different thermal histories is not automatically the property of one microscopic jump.

For \(H_m=0.50\,{\rm eV}\), \(\nu_0=10^{13}\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), \(z=12\), \(d=0.25\,{\rm nm}\), \(f=0.78\) and the equilibrium vacancy fraction at \(800\,{\rm K}\), one obtains \(\Gamma\simeq7.1\times10^9\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(D\simeq9.4\times10^{-16}\,{\rm m^2/s}\). The apparent discrepancy between a gigahertz conditional jump rate and the slow macroscopic spreading comes largely from the small vacancy fraction. After one hour the rms spread in one Cartesian direction is \(\sqrt{2Dt}\simeq2.6\,\mu{\rm m}\); the three-dimensional rms displacement is larger by \(\sqrt3\).

Read a diffusion measurement and identify a changing population

For a thin tracer layer deposited at the origin of a long homogeneous specimen the one-dimensional conservative diffusion equation gives

\[c(x,t)=M(4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)},\]

where \(M\) is the deposited amount per area. Its variance is \(2Dt\). An initial Gaussian width or an independent Gaussian instrument resolution adds its own variance. Measure the growth of variance with time to estimate \(D\), instead of attributing the entire final width to diffusion. An asymmetric tail can signal surface or grain-boundary transport, which may not be described by a uniform bulk \(D\).

Now quench a specimen rapidly from a higher temperature. Vacancies may initially remain near their high-temperature population while their mobility changes immediately. If their excess decays to sinks as \(c_v(t)=c_{\rm eq}+c_{\rm exc}e^{-t/\tau_s}\), tracer spreading at fixed temperature follows \(\sigma_x^2(t)-\sigma_x^2(0)=2\int_0^t D(t')dt'\). If \(D\) is proportional to \(c_v\), this consists of a linear equilibrium contribution and a saturating excess term proportional to \(\tau_s(1-e^{-t/\tau_s})\). A single long-time apparent diffusivity conceals early rapid motion. Measuring several dwell times and different specimen sizes can reveal the role of sinks.

Use unequal dwell times to test a quenched vacancy excess

Take a quenched specimen at fixed measuring temperature and suppose the excess initially raises the tracer diffusivity from its equilibrium value \(D_{\rm eq}\) to \(D_{\rm eq}+3D_{\rm eq}\). Let excess vacancies decay with time constant \(600\,{\rm s}\). A variance measurement over the first \(300\,{\rm s}\) infers a time-averaged coefficient \(D_{\rm app}/D_{\rm eq}=1+3(600/300)[1-e^{-300/600}]=3.36\). The same calculation over the first \(3600\,{\rm s}\) gives about \(1.50\). Neither averaged coefficient equals the initial fourfold value or the long-time equilibrium value. A collection of samples analysed after unequal dwell times can therefore seem to have different activation energies even at equal measurement temperatures.

Independent reheating or quenching measurements can help identify this bias. First anneal for several estimated sink times, then perform a fresh short tracer measurement at the same temperature. Agreement of late-time slopes for different initial quench histories supports a common equilibrium diffusion coefficient. Changes in specimen thickness that shorten the distance to a vacancy sink should affect the excess decay stronger than the elementary jump barrier. Verify that changing the thickness has not simultaneously changed grain size or surface diffusion paths. Combining the full time-dependent variance with an independent vacancy-sensitive observable is more informative than treating a series of single final widths as separate equilibria.

Tutorial on activation energies

The slope of \(\ln D\) versus \(1/T\) is \(-1.74\times10^4\,{\rm K}\) in an equilibrated specimen. A short quenched measurement with a fixed vacancy population gives \(-5.80\times10^3\,{\rm K}\). Estimate both enthalpies.

Work through the solution

Multiplying slopes in magnitude by \(k_B=8.6173\times10^{-5}\,{\rm eV/K}\) gives approximately \(1.50\,{\rm eV}\) total and \(0.50\,{\rm eV}\) migration. Their difference gives \(1.00\,{\rm eV}\) formation. This inference assumes the same dominant vacancy mechanism and no significant changes of the prefactor or defect population over the quenched measurement.

A tracer profile grows in rms width from \(1.5\) to \(3.0\,\mu{\rm m}\) in \(3600\,{\rm s}\). Estimate the one-dimensional diffusion coefficient.

Work through the solution

Subtract the initial variance rather than the initial width:

\[D=[(3.0)^2-(1.5)^2]10^{-12}/7200=9.38\times10^{-16}\,{\rm m^2/s}.\]

If the instrument resolution changes between runs its variance change must also be estimated. A constant independent resolution cancels in the subtraction.

What would distinguish fast grain-boundary transport from an unexpectedly low bulk migration barrier?

Work through the solution

Compare single crystals with specimens of varying grain size at the same thermal history and fit the full profile rather than only its second moment. Enhanced fast tails that grow with boundary area support an additional path. An independent defect-population measurement helps separate a low jump barrier from an enhanced concentration. A single Arrhenius slope represents the dominant observed transport, not a unique proof of its mechanism.
