Why a polar crystal has two optical phonon frequencies

A uniform relative displacement of positive and negative ions creates an electric polarisation. A longitudinal vibration induces a macroscopic restoring field while a transverse displacement has a different electrical boundary condition. An infrared spectrum can reveal both frequencies.

Separate the ionic and electronic contributions

For one infrared-active optical mode let \(u\) be relative displacement per primitive cell, \(M\) the effective reduced mass, \(Z^*\) the effective charge in coulombs, and \(N\) the number of cells per volume. The oscillator equation driven by the macroscopic field is

\[M(\ddot u+\gamma\dot u+\omega_{\rm TO}^2u)=Z^*E.\]

The zero-macroscopic-field stiffness defines the transverse optical frequency in this oscillator model. Bound electronic response at these frequencies supplies a real relative background permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\). The ionic polarisation is \(P_{\rm ion}=NZ^*u\), so \(D=\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty E+P_{\rm ion}\).

With time convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\), solving for \(u\) gives

\[\epsilon_r(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{S}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\]
\[S=\frac{N(Z^*)^2}{\epsilon_0 M}.\]

The signed effective charge appears twice, once as drive and once in the dipole density. Its square therefore sets oscillator strength. At zero frequency the ions can follow the field and \(\epsilon_s=\epsilon_\infty+S/\omega_{\rm TO}^2\). Far above the phonon resonance but below electronic absorption the ionic term is small, making \(\epsilon_\infty\) the observed plateau.

Apply Gauss's law to a longitudinal vibration

Consider a long-wavelength, nonzero wavevector \(\boldsymbol k\) with no external free charge. Gauss's law gives

\[\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol D=0.\]

For a longitudinal field \(D_L=0\), so the ions create \(E_L=-NZ^*u/(\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty)\). Substituting this opposing field into the undamped oscillator equation adds a restoring term. Thus

\[\omega_{\rm LO}^2=\omega_{\rm TO}^2+S/\epsilon_\infty,\]
\[\frac{\omega_{\rm LO}^2}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2}=\frac{\epsilon_s}{\epsilon_\infty}.\]

Equivalently the longitudinal collective mode is the zero of the lossless dielectric function. The transverse optical frequency is its pole. For a single isotropic mode this is the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation. With several infrared modes, anisotropy, damping or mobile free carriers, assigning one measured peak and one dielectric ratio to a single oscillator needs further justification.

Connect a reflectance band with the two modes

In the lossless limit rewrite \(\epsilon_r=\epsilon_\infty(\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega^2)/(\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2)\). Between the two frequencies this is negative. A transverse electromagnetic wave satisfies \(k^2=\epsilon_r\omega^2/c^2\), so its wavenumber becomes imaginary there in a nonmagnetic lossless crystal. At a planar vacuum boundary a semi-infinite crystal therefore has unit ideal reflectance over this interval. Finite damping makes the refractive index complex and allows absorption, smoothing the edges. The two mode frequencies have been deduced from mechanical response and electric boundary conditions; an electromagnetic wave interacting with the lattice is a coupled phonon–polariton excitation.

For \(f_{\rm TO}=6.0\,\mathrm{THz}\), \(\epsilon_\infty=4\) and \(\epsilon_s=9\), \(f_{\rm LO}=9.0\,\mathrm{THz}\). At 7.5 THz in the lossless limit \(\epsilon_r=4(81-56.25)/(36-56.25)=-4.89\). Below the mode, normal-incidence static-index reflectance is

\[[(\sqrt9-1)/(\sqrt9+1)]^2=0.25.\]

Above the phonon plateau the corresponding background reflectance approaches \(1/9\). The interactive plot evaluates the passive complex refractive index and the exact single-interface reflectance. Tutorial on recovering mode parameters

Optical data give \(f_{\rm TO}=8.0\) THz and \(f_{\rm LO}=10.0\) THz for a single weakly damped mode. With \(\epsilon_\infty=5.0\), estimate the static permittivity and dielectric increment.

Work through the solution
\[\epsilon_s=5(10/8)^2=7.8125.\]

The increment attributed to the optical mode is

\[7.8125-5=2.8125.\]

Angular and cyclic frequency ratios are equal, although an absolute oscillator strength \(S\) requires angular frequencies in the equation of motion.

At a fixed wavevector a probe sees displacement perpendicular to the wavevector. Why is the longitudinal restoring term absent in the electrostatic argument, and what additional coupling can still occur?

Work through the solution

Its ionic polarisation has \(\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol P_{\rm ion}=0\), hence it creates no longitudinal bulk bound charge \(-i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol P_{\rm ion}\). There is no obligatory depolarising longitudinal field. A transverse propagating electromagnetic field can still drive the infrared-active vibration, as encoded in \(\epsilon_r(\omega)\).
