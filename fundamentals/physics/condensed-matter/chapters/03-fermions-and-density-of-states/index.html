From first principles

Count the states in a filled Fermi sphere

Take independent spin-one-half electrons in a large cube of volume \(V=L^3\) with periodic boundary conditions. The condition \(e^{ik_xL}=1\) permits \(k_x=2\pi n_x/L\), with the same spacing in the other directions. Thus each allowed wave vector occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For the free-particle energy \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\), all states below energy \(E\) lie in a sphere. Counting its volume and two spin states gives

\[N(E)=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.\]

At zero temperature the exclusion principle fills these single-particle states up to \(E_F\). Writing \(n=N/V\), solve the counting formula at that upper limit:

\[k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\]
\[E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}.\]

The number of available states in a thin energy shell is the derivative \(D(E)dE\). In three dimensions \(D(E)\propto\sqrt E\) because the shell area grows with \(k^2\) while \(dk/dE\) falls as \(1/k\). This counting assumes an isotropic parabolic band. Additional valleys or a different effective dimension change the result.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the pressure of a filled Fermi sea by compressing its states

Even at zero temperature a box of mobile electrons exerts a pressure on its boundaries. Consider \(N\) noninteracting spin-one-half fermions in a three-dimensional volume \(V\), with fixed isotropic mass \(m\) and quadratic energy. Take the bottom of the band as a volume-independent energy reference. Every state below \(E_F\) is occupied once per allowed spin state. Since the density of states including spin has the form \(D(E)=C\sqrt E\), dividing two elementary integrals eliminates the normalization constant,

\[\frac{U}{N}=\frac{\int_0^{E_F} E\,C\sqrt E\,dE}{\int_0^{E_F} C\sqrt E\,dE}=\frac35 E_F .\]

State counting gives \(E_F=(\hbar^2/2m)(3\pi^2 N/V)^{2/3}\). During a uniform compression at fixed \(N\) the upper occupied energy rises. At zero entropy the energy derivative with respect to box volume is the negative pressure, so

\[P=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac{2U}{3V}=\frac25 nE_F,\]
\[n=\frac NV .\]

Density appears both explicitly and through \(E_F\). A second volume derivative gives the positive resistance to a small fractional compression,

\[B=-V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac53P=\frac23 nE_F .\]

Both \(P\) and the bulk modulus \(B\) have units of energy per volume. The factor \(5/3\) distinguishes the change of pressure from pressure itself. An actual solid also has electrostatic and lattice contributions. Extracting the electronic contribution from a measured solid modulus requires information about these other stresses and the volume dependence of the band parameters.

Worked example

Estimate the electronic stiffness of a simple metal

Take \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(E_F=5.00\,\mathrm{eV}\). With \(1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J\), the product \(nE_F\) is \(4.01\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The pressure contribution is \(16.0\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and the modulus is \(26.7\,\mathrm{GPa}\). A one percent volume decrease would raise this pressure by approximately \(0.267\,\mathrm{GPa}\) in the linear response regime.

Second application

Predict a finite compression without linearizing

Suppose a separate trapped gas starts at pressure \(12.0\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and is compressed slowly to \(0.800\) of its original volume at fixed particle number and negligible entropy. Its pressure becomes \(12.0(1/0.800)^{5/3}=17.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Its energy per particle rises by only \((1/0.800)^{2/3}=1.16\) times. The larger pressure ratio also contains the increase in particles per unit volume.

Count occupied states at zero temperature

Consider a cubic box of volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries and free electrons of effective model mass \(m\). Allowed wavevectors occupy a volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) per state for each spin. At zero temperature each state below the chemical potential is filled. For \(\epsilon=\hbar^2 k^2/2m\) the filled region is a sphere and each \(k\) has two spin states. Hence

\[N=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3},\qquad k_F=(3\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2 k_F^2}{2m}.\]

With \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ and the free electron mass, \(k_F\) is about \(1.36\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\) about 7.0 eV. The corresponding temperature \(E_F/k_B\) is roughly 81,000 K. Ordinary room temperature therefore changes occupation only near the outer surface of this sea in a simple metal. This is a model estimate; anisotropic bands and multiple pockets change its interpretation.

The density of states is a derivative

Replacing \(k_F\) by \(k(E)=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) gives the integrated state count below energy \(E\). Its derivative is

\[D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E,\qquad D(E_F)=\frac{3N}{2E_F}.\]

Check dimensions by integrating \(D(E)\,dE\) to recover a count. In two dimensions the occupied area grows as \(k^2\) and the density per energy is constant for a parabolic band. In one dimension the count grows as \(k\) and the density has an integrable \(E^{-1/2}\) singularity at its bottom. A large density of states means many states fit in a narrow energy window. It does not imply a large group velocity: a flat dispersion may create the large density.

Why only a shell contributes to heat capacity

At finite temperature the occupation is \(f(E)=[1+e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT}]^{-1}\). Heating can promote an electron only into a vacant state. Deep in the filled sea all nearby destinations are occupied. The active shell has width of order \(k_BT\), involving about \(D(E_F)k_BT\) states; the typical energy change is another factor \(k_BT\). Thus the thermal energy increases quadratically and the heat capacity rises linearly with \(T\).

A coefficient follows by expanding the smooth density of states across that shell. At fixed particle number, the relevant excess energy to leading order is \(\int (E-E_F)D(E_F)[f(E)-\Theta(E_F-E)]\,dE\). The filled and empty sides contribute equally to this order. Substituting \(x=|E-E_F|/k_BT\) and using \(\int_0^\infty x/(e^x+1)\,dx=\pi^2/12\) gives \(C_{\rm el}=(\pi^2/3)D(E_F)k_B^2T\). This expansion requires the density to be smooth on the thermal scale; a band edge at the chemical potential needs separate treatment.

Separate two low-temperature contributions

In a three-dimensional simple metal combine \(C_\mathrm{el}=\gamma T\) with the phonon result \(C_\mathrm{ph}=\beta T^3\). Plot \(C/T\) against \(T^2\); the intercept estimates \(\gamma\), and the slope estimates \(\beta\). If two points are \((4,3)\) and \((16,6)\) in K² and mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻², respectively, \(\beta=0.25\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴ and \(\gamma=2.0\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻². Curvature across the fitted interval would be evidence against this two-term description. To go beyond a free sphere we next retain the periodic potential of the ions.

Count charges and electronic compressibility in two dimensions

A two-dimensional electron layer illustrates how changing available spatial dimensions alters the density of states and an electrical measurement. Assume one parabolic subband \(E=E_c+\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)/(2m^*)\), spin degeneracy two and no valley degeneracy. A square sample of area \(A\) with periodic boundary conditions supplies one state per spin per reciprocal area \((2\pi)^2/A\). Counting the disk up to \(k_F\) gives areal density \(n_s=k_F^2/(2\pi)\), hence

\[E_F-E_c=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m^*},\qquad D_A(E)=\frac{dn_s}{dE_F}=\frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}\]

above the subband edge at zero temperature. Unlike the square-root density in three dimensions, each occupied parabolic two-dimensional subband contributes a constant step. When an additional subband begins to fill it supplies another step, possibly with different degeneracy or mass. Temperature and disorder broaden the step in a real measurement.

An electrostatic gate must both create a field across the insulating spacer and raise the layer’s chemical potential. For areal capacitance \(C_g'\) of the spacer and increment of sheet charge magnitude \(dQ'=e\,dn_s\), the required voltage is \(dV=dQ'/C_g'+dE_F/e\). The second term defines the quantum capacitance \(C_q'=e^2D_A\). Their inverse capacitances add in series. For a small density of states the electronic contribution measurably lowers the total capacitance even when the geometric dimensions are known precisely.

Measure which capacitance limits a device

Take \(m^*=0.067m_e\) and \(n_s=2.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻². Then \(k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_s}=1.12\times10^8\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F-E_c=7.15\) meV. At 4 K the thermal scale \(k_BT=0.345\) meV is smaller than this separation, supporting the degenerate estimate. The electronic capacitance per area is \(0.0448\) F/m². For a spacer with \(C_g'=0.0100\) F/m², the measured series value is \((1/0.0100+1/0.0448)^{-1}=0.00818\) F/m².

Over a gate area of \(100\) μm², the predicted capacitance is \(0.818\) pF rather than the purely geometric \(1.00\) pF. Treating that measured value as a change of dielectric thickness would misidentify an electronic effect. In the limit of a very high density of states, the quantum capacitance becomes large and the geometric value is recovered. Near depletion the assumptions of a sharp occupied subband and uniform layer require scrutiny. Comparing capacitance with an independent sheet-density measurement can test whether a low reading reflects depleted electronic states or simply a different insulating spacer.

Using the series result, a small gate swing can be split into two measurable energy changes. A 10.0 mV swing at the stated operating point changes sheet charge magnitude by \(8.18\times10^{-5}\) C/m², or sheet density by \(5.10\times10^{14}\) m⁻². The geometric voltage increment is \(8.18\) mV and the chemical-potential contribution is \(1.82\) mV, corresponding to a Fermi-level shift of 1.82 meV. Their sum recovers the imposed swing. This decomposition assumes the layer remains in the same subband and equilibrates through its electrical contact on the capacitance measurement timescale.

A slow measurement with significant leakage instead mixes capacitance with conductance and can corrupt both estimated voltage contributions.

Count states added by raising the Fermi level

For a parabolic band, states within a radius \(k\) fill a length, area or volume in reciprocal space. Since \(k\) scales as \(\sqrt E\), the integrated count varies as \(E^{d/2}\). Normalise at one energy to compare exponents independently of the sample dimensions.
