From first principles

See how pairing opens a spectroscopic gap

Begin with a single normal-state band and let \(\xi\) be its energy measured from the chemical potential. A minimal mean-field model for a uniform paired state mixes an electron amplitude with a hole amplitude through a complex pairing energy \(\Delta\). The two diagonal energies have opposite signs. To find its stationary excitations write the eigenvalue condition:

\[H=\begin{pmatrix}\xi&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det(H-EI)=(\xi-E)(-\xi-E)-|\Delta|^2=0 .\]

It follows that the two branches are \(E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi^2+|\Delta|^2}\). At the former Fermi surface \(\xi=0\), even the cheapest positive excitation costs \(|\Delta|\). To predict a tunnelling spectrum for constant isotropic gap and nearly constant normal density of states, count the normal states mapping into an energy interval \(dE\). The Jacobian from \(\xi=\pm\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) gives the normalized density, with the electron and hole weights combined:

\[\frac{D_s(E)}{D_n(0)}=\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}}\quad (|E|>|\Delta|).\]

There are no states within the ideal gap at this level of description. Finite temperature, lifetime broadening and instrumental resolution change the measured conductance near its edges. The model assumes a nonzero \(\Delta\); it predicts consequences of pairing without deriving the attractive interaction that supports it.

Derivation and applications

Predict the weights of a particle peak and its partner

A pairing gap determines excitation energies, but a momentum-resolved spectrum also measures how strongly an electron couples to each peak. Consider a clean, uniform superconductor with a real positive gap \(\Delta\), at temperature low enough to neglect thermal broadening. Measure the normal-state energy \(\xi\) at one wavevector from the chemical potential. In the electron and hole basis the positive-energy eigenvector has components \(u,v\). The first row of its eigenvalue equation requires

\[(\xi-E)u+\Delta v=0,\]
\[\frac{v}{u}=\frac{E-\xi}{\Delta},\]
\[E=\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}.\]

Normalize the vector to unit probability. Since \((E-\xi)(E+\xi)=\Delta^2\), the squared amplitude ratio can be written without subtracting two nearly equal energies. The resulting coherence factors are

\[|u|^2=\frac{1}{1+(E-\xi)/(E+\xi)}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{\xi}{E}\right),\]
\[|v|^2=\frac12\left(1-\frac{\xi}{E}\right).\]

The negative-energy eigenvector is orthogonal to the positive one and consequently has electron weight \(|v|^2\). In the ideal single-particle spectral function at this momentum the two sharp peaks have unit total weight,

\[A(\epsilon)=|u|^2\delta(\epsilon-E)+|v|^2\delta(\epsilon+E),\]
\[\int A(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon=1 .\]

Thus the weights exchange as the wavevector passes through the normal Fermi surface. At \(\xi=0\) the two weights are equal. Well above the surface the positive-energy peak carries almost all the weight. To compare both sides quantitatively an experiment must account for occupation and matrix elements; ordinary photoemission alone samples the occupied side most directly.

Worked example

Resolve a weak partner peak

At a selected momentum let \(\xi=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=4.00\,\mathrm{meV}\). The spectral energies are \(+5.00\) and \(-5.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), with respective electron weights \(0.800\) and \(0.200\). At a momentum with the opposite \(\xi\) and the same gap the two peak energies stay fixed while their weights reverse. Such a weight exchange tests particle–hole mixing more specifically than the peak separation alone.

Second application

Reconstruct a gap using corrected peak areas

In a different sample the peaks are separated by \(6.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), and corrected electron spectral areas have positive-to-negative ratio \(3:2\). Their normalized weights are \(0.600\) and \(0.400\), so

\[\xi/E=0.200.\]

With \(E=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) this gives \(\xi=0.600\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=\sqrt{3.00^2-0.600^2}=2.94\,\mathrm{meV}\). Half the separation overestimates the gap here because the probed momentum lies away from the normal Fermi surface.

Build excitations on a paired state

For a conventional fully gapped superconductor, a mean-field description mixes particle and hole amplitudes near the Fermi surface. With \(\xi_k=\epsilon_k-\mu\) and gap parameter \(\Delta\), a simplified matrix for a time-reversed pair of states is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\xi_k&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi_k\end{pmatrix}\binom{u_k}{v_k}=E\binom{u_k}{v_k},\qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta|^2}.\]

A positive-energy quasiparticle has minimum energy \(|\Delta|\) where the normal band crossed the chemical potential. Breaking a pair into two excitations can cost at least \(2|\Delta|\). These are different thresholds, so the probe and transition being discussed must be specified. The matrix displays level repulsion, reminiscent of the nearly free electron calculation, but its basis states and the physical origin of the coupling are different.

Transform a density of states

Suppose the normal-state density is approximately constant over the gap scale and the gap is isotropic. For \(E>|\Delta|\),

\[|\xi|=\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2},\]
\[d|\xi|/dE=E/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}.\]

Counting the paired branches relative to the normal-state convention yields \(D_s(E)/D_n=|E|/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) outside the gap and zero inside in the ideal zero-broadening model. The edge singularity is integrable. States removed from the gap reappear near its edges. Thermal broadening, disorder, nodes or an anisotropic gap can change the measured shape.

A low-temperature tunnel junction with a normal counterelectrode of nearly constant density can use differential conductance as a probe of this spectrum, subject to matrix-element and broadening effects. Peaks at biases \(\pm1.5\) mV suggest \(|\Delta|\simeq1.5\) meV in this geometry. Peak separation on the voltage axis is 3.0 mV. Using the weak-coupling BCS ratio \(2|\Delta(0)|/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.53\) gives \(T_c\simeq9.86\) K. Measure \(T_c\) independently; agreement with this estimate supports the simple model, while deviation calls for closer investigation.

Select a second observation that distinguishes the mechanism

A nearly free electron band gap separates eigenvalues near a zone boundary. For conduction it also matters where the chemical potential sits. A superconducting gap opens at the Fermi surface on entering an ordered state, and has magnetic screening and a characteristic temperature dependence. A photonic stop band instead excludes real propagating wavevectors in a frequency range for the specified periodic optical medium. All three can produce missing spectral weight or reduced transmission. Their energies, frequency units, occupation rules and control parameters differ.

Return to the numerical optical stack with a prediction: making both layers identical closes its first stop band even though the artificial zone boundary remains. For a proposed superconducting gap, repeat the tunnelling and magnetisation measurements as temperature crosses the transition. For an electronic band edge, relate spectroscopy to filling, dispersion and transport. This combination of an analytic limit, a controlled change and an independent observable is what makes an interpretation of a spectrum persuasive.

For continued study, see the MIT lectures on Theory of Solids, including quasiparticles and superconducting electrodynamics.

Read the phase difference across a superconducting weak link

A single-particle tunnelling spectrum can reveal an excitation gap. A weak junction between two superconductors can also carry a coherent pair current that responds directly to their relative condensate phase. Use the gauge-invariant phase difference \(\varphi\), including the vector-potential line integral across the junction. For a simple tunnel junction in the low-transparency regime, its leading coupling energy is

\[U_J=-E_J\cos\varphi.\]

Its \(2\pi\) periodicity reflects invariance under an integer phase winding and the energy minimum at aligned phase for a conventional junction.

A potential difference \(V\) between the condensates causes their relative phase to advance at \(\dot\varphi=2eV/\hbar\), choosing a consistent orientation of current and voltage. Combining electrical power \(IV\) with the rate of change of junction energy gives \(IV=E_J\sin\varphi\,\dot\varphi\), hence

\[I=I_c\sin\varphi,\]
\[I_c=\frac{2eE_J}{\hbar}.\]

Under a steady subcritical current the phase can remain constant while \(V=0\). At constant nonzero voltage the phase winds and the ideal coherent current oscillates with frequency \(f_J=2e|V|/h\). These two statements distinguish a DC pair current with no terminal voltage from an AC pair response under bias. A real junction includes quasiparticle conduction and capacitance, so its observed total current can contain additional components.

Convert a microwave frequency into a voltage

A junction biased at 5.00 μV has Josephson oscillation frequency \(f_J=(2e/h)V=2.42\) GHz. Illuminate the junction with a stable microwave reference of frequency 10.0 GHz. If its mean phase advance locks to one full winding per reference cycle, then \(\langle\dot\varphi\rangle=2\pi f_{\rm ref}\), requiring mean voltage \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)=20.7\) μV. Locking to two windings per cycle gives 41.4 μV. Such voltage steps probe phase dynamics and the charge associated with coherent transfer.

Suppose the same device has measured critical current \(I_c=30.0\) nA at the operating temperature. Its coupling energy is \(\hbar I_c/(2e)=9.87\times10^{-24}\) J, corresponding to \(E_J/k_B=0.715\) K. At a bath temperature of 50 mK thermal energy is appreciably smaller, although phase fluctuations can still depend on capacitance and the surrounding circuit. If either electrode is driven normal, the equilibrium phase-link energy disappears and the ideal supercurrent relation ceases to apply. Observing quantized phase locking and an excitation gap therefore accesses two different signatures of the superconducting state; their characteristic frequencies need not be numerically identical.

The two Josephson relations yield a further calibration test that does not require measuring energy directly. At fixed microwave frequency, the voltage separation of successive locking steps is \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)\) independently of junction area in the ideal phase model. Increasing junction area can change total critical current and the visibility or width of current intervals over which locking persists, yet should leave the quantized voltage spacing fixed. Looking at spacing and current width separately distinguishes uncertainty in the reference frequency from a change in the junction coupling or its electromagnetic environment.
