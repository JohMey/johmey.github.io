Why a honeycomb lattice has Dirac cones

In an alternating chain the two paths from A to B can cancel at a special wavevector. The honeycomb lattice adds a third path. Their cancellation now occurs at isolated points in a two-dimensional Brillouin zone and produces a linear low-energy dispersion.

Add the three Bloch phases

Each honeycomb A site has three nearest B neighbours at displacements \(d(0,1)\), \(d(\sqrt3/2,-1/2)\), and \(d(-\sqrt3/2,-1/2)\), where \(d\) is the bond length. For equal real hopping magnitude \(t\), sum the translation phase for each displacement. The common off-diagonal factor is

\[f(\boldsymbol k)=e^{ik_yd}+2e^{-ik_yd/2}\cos(\sqrt3 k_xd/2),\]
\[E_\pm=\pm t|f|.\]

This uses a basis phase convention that includes the orbitals' intra-cell positions. A rephasing can change the phase of \(f\) but leaves its magnitude invariant. Along \(k_y=0\), cancellation occurs where \(1+2\cos(\sqrt3 k_xd/2)=0\). Choose \(K=(4\pi/(3\sqrt3d),0)\). The counterpart at \(-K\) is inequivalent up to primitive reciprocal vectors. Near either valley many wavevectors represent distinct states even when their energies almost coincide.

Linearise at a zero, then test the approximation

Write \(\boldsymbol k=K+\boldsymbol q\) with \(qd\ll1\). At \(K\) the cosine equals \(-1/2\) and the sine \(\sqrt3/2\). Differentiating the cosine term with respect to \(k_x\) consequently gives \(-3d/2\). Differentiating the two exponential factors with respect to \(k_y\) gives

\[id+id/2=3id/2.\]

The constant terms cancel. Therefore

\[f(K+\boldsymbol q)\simeq \tfrac{3d}{2}(-q_x+iq_y),\]
\[E_\pm\simeq\pm\hbar v_F|\boldsymbol q|,\]
\[v_F=\frac{3td}{2\hbar}.\]

With \(t=2.7\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(d=0.142\,\mathrm{nm}\) and \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{eV\,fs}\), the speed is

\[0.874\,\mathrm{nm/fs}=8.74\times10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

At \(qd=0.05\) the leading energy magnitude is \(0.2025\,\mathrm{eV}\). The energy varies linearly with momentum displacement even though the microscopic Hamiltonian contains only hopping between neighbours. The two components describe sublattice amplitude, distinct from the real electron spin.

Break the equivalence of the sublattices

Assign on-site energies \(+\Delta\) to A and \(-\Delta\) to B. The characteristic equation of the two-by-two Hamiltonian becomes

\[E^2=\Delta^2+t^2|f|^2.\]

A gap \(2|\Delta|\) opens at K. For \(\Delta=0.10\,\mathrm{eV}\), the edge sits at \(0.10\,\mathrm{eV}\) and the predicted conduction energy at \(qd=0.05\) becomes about \(0.226\,\mathrm{eV}\). Far enough from the origin the slopes recover their nearly linear values. Close to it, expansion of the square root gives

\[E_+\simeq|\Delta|+\hbar^2v_F^2q^2/(2|\Delta|).\]

A band-edge mass \(|\Delta|/v_F^2\) has emerged. The expansion requires \(\hbar v_Fq\ll|\Delta|\).

Compare the exact positive-energy cut along \(q_x\) with the local cone in the graph. Unequal deviations to the right and left of K are allowed by the lattice and cannot be captured by the lowest-order isotropic cone. The reflected cut at the other valley restores the time-reversal relation.

Tutorial on a measured slope

A near-valley spectrum has slope \(0.55\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}\). Infer \(v_F\) and the nearest-neighbour hopping for a bond length \(d=0.142\,\mathrm{nm}\). A gap of \(80\,\mathrm{meV}\) then appears with the same asymptotic slope. What is \(|\Delta|\)?

Work through the solution

The slope equals \(\hbar v_F\). Dividing by \(0.6582\,\mathrm{eV\,fs}\) gives \(0.836\,\mathrm{nm/fs}\). Since the slope also equals \(3td/2\),

\[t=2(0.55)/(3\times0.142)=2.58\,\mathrm{eV}.\]

The two edges shift symmetrically by half the full gap, giving \(|\Delta|=40\,\mathrm{meV}\).

Consider a gapless honeycomb cone exactly at the valley centre. What does the tight-binding Hamiltonian predict for the energy and eigenvectors, and what information comes from approaching along a direction?

Work through the solution

At \(f=0\) the matrix vanishes, so every two-component vector in the degenerate subspace has energy zero. For a nearby nonzero \(f\), a chosen eigenbranch fixes a relative phase between its A and B components according to the argument of \(f\). The limiting eigenvector therefore depends on the direction of approach even though the limiting energy is always zero.

At \(q=0\), repeat the eigenvector argument with \(\Delta>0\). Predict a consequence for sublattice-resolved spectroscopy at the two band edges.

Work through the solution

At the valley centre the Hamiltonian is diagonal with entries \(+\Delta,-\Delta\). The upper state lives on A and the lower on B in this basis. A probe selective to A should see more weight at the conduction edge than at the valence edge. Farther from the valley centre the hopping mixes the sublattices again.
