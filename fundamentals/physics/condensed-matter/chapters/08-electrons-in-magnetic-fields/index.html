From first principles

Find the orbit and energy spacing imposed by a magnetic field

Consider an electron in an isotropic parabolic band with effective mass \(m^*\), no electric field and a uniform magnetic induction \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\). The Lorentz force is perpendicular to velocity, so it changes the direction of the transverse velocity without changing its magnitude. Taking absolute values in the radial force balance gives

\[\frac{m^* v_\perp^2}{r_c}=e v_\perp B,\]
\[r_c=\frac{m^*v_\perp}{eB},\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m^*}.\]

To obtain energy levels one also needs quantum mechanics. Choose \(\mathbf A=(0,Bx,0)\) and electron charge \(-e\) with \(e>0\). The Hamiltonian \((\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2/(2m^*)\) is independent of \(y,z\), so their canonical momenta can be treated as fixed in a plane-wave state. Complete the square:

\[H=\frac{p_x^2}{2m^*}+\frac{m^*\omega_c^2}{2}\left(x+\frac{p_y}{eB}\right)^2+\frac{p_z^2}{2m^*}.\]

This is a harmonic oscillator about a centre set by \(p_y\). Its transverse levels have energies \(\hbar\omega_c(n+1/2)\); longitudinal translation remains free. Spin splitting, anisotropic band curvature and collision broadening require further information. The same frequency occurs in the classical orbit and the quantum level spacing for distinct reasons made explicit by these two constructions.

Derivation and applications

Determine which mass a cyclotron resonance measures

An orbit on an anisotropic band need not be circular. Consider a weak uniform magnetic induction \(B\) along \(z\), and motion in a single band with elliptical constant-energy contours in the \(xy\) plane. Neglect scattering over an orbit, electric fields, and spin splitting. Write the local dispersion as \(E=\hbar^2 k_x^2/(2m_x)+\hbar^2 k_y^2/(2m_y)\), measured from its minimum. For charge \(-e\), the semiclassical force law and the velocity components give

\[\dot k_x=-\frac{eB}{m_y}k_y,\]
\[\dot k_y=\frac{eB}{m_x}k_x .\]

Taking a second derivative cancels the other component. Both coordinates oscillate at the same fundamental angular frequency even though their amplitudes differ,

\[\ddot k_x=-\frac{e^2B^2}{m_xm_y}k_x,\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m_c},\]
\[m_c=\sqrt{m_xm_y}.\]

A geometric measurement leads to the same mass. The two semiaxes of the contour at energy \(E\) are \(\sqrt{2m_iE}/\hbar\). Its reciprocal-space area \(A(E)\) and energy derivative are

\[A(E)=\frac{2\pi E}{\hbar^2}\sqrt{m_xm_y},\]
\[\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\frac{dA}{dE}=m_c .\]

The derivative explains how a measurement of orbit area over a small energy range tests the resonance result. Two bands with the same area at one energy can have different derivatives there. For a circle, both principal masses and the cyclotron mass coincide. A single resonance frequency for this orientation measures their geometric mean and leaves their ratio undetermined.

Worked example

Predict the resonance of an elliptical pocket

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A layer has \(m_x=0.100m_e\), \(m_y=0.900m_e\) and is placed in \(B=1.50\,\mathrm T\). The cyclotron mass is \(0.300m_e\), so the ordinary frequency is

\[f_c=eB/(2\pi m_c)=140\,\mathrm{GHz}.\]

The period is \(7.14\,\mathrm{ps}\). The long reciprocal-space semiaxis lies along \(y\) and is three times the short one, even though both components complete their cycle in exactly the same time.

Second application

Use two nearby contour areas as a mass measurement

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

In a different sample the orbit area rises by \(0.200\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}\) when energy rises by \(20.0\,\mathrm{meV}\). Treat the measured slope as locally constant. In SI units \(dA/dE=2.00\times10^{17}/(3.204\times10^{-21})\,\mathrm{m^{-2}J^{-1}}\). The area formula gives

\[m_c=1.10\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}=0.121m_e.\]

An independent determination of \(m_x=0.0600m_e\) would then imply \(m_y=m_c^2/m_x=0.245m_e\) at the displayed precision.

Follow a wave packet in a band

A narrow wave packet in a well-isolated band moves with \(\mathbf v=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}E\). In a static uniform magnetic field the semiclassical force law is

\[\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=-e\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B.\]

Thus \(\nabla E\cdot\dot{\mathbf k}=0\): the energy is constant because a magnetic field does no work. The wavevector component along \(\mathbf B\) is also constant. The orbit in reciprocal space lies along the intersection of a constant-energy surface and a plane perpendicular to the field. Tilting the field samples different sections of a nonspherical Fermi surface.

For an isotropic parabolic band, motion transverse to the field is circular at \(\omega_c=eB/m^*\). The real-space orbit radius is \(v_\perp/\omega_c\). One can measure an effective mass from the resonant angular frequency without separately knowing the elastic scattering time. Whether the motion survives long enough to be resolved depends on \(\omega_c\tau\). A very short scattering time smears the resonance and suppresses oscillatory magnetotransport.

Derive the quantised spacing

For a two-dimensional electron choose \(A_y=Bx\) and \(A_x=0\). Since the Hamiltonian for charge \(-e\) is \(H=[p_x^2+(p_y+eBx)^2]/(2m^*)\), a state of definite \(p_y=\hbar k_y\) leaves a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator in \(x\), centred at \(-\hbar k_y/(eB)\). Its levels are \(E_j=(j+1/2)\hbar\omega_c\), neglecting spin. At fixed level, changing \(k_y\) moves the orbit centre without changing its energy. The centres allowed inside a rectangle of area \(A\) count to \(eBA/h\) per spin, up to edge corrections. In three dimensions motion along the field adds a dispersing kinetic-energy term.

Take \(B=5\) T and \(m^*=0.10m_e\). The orbital spacing is about 5.79 meV, equivalent to \(k_B T\) at 67 K. At 4 K the thermal scale is about 0.345 meV, smaller than the spacing. An observation still requires sufficiently weak disorder broadening and appropriate carrier filling. As the field changes, both the level spacing and the number of states carried by each level change.

Extract a reciprocal-space area from a measurement

For a closed semiclassical orbit of area \(A_k(E)\), quantisation gives

\[A_k=2\pi eB(j+\gamma)/\hbar,\]

where the phase offset \(\gamma\) depends on the orbit. Successive levels crossing the Fermi energy give oscillations periodic in reciprocal field with frequency \(F=\hbar A_k(E_F)/(2\pi e)\), expressed in tesla. For \(F=100\) T, \(A_k\simeq9.55\times10^{17}\) m⁻². A circular section of that area has radius \(5.51\times10^8\) m⁻¹. In a three-dimensional metal dominant oscillations come from extremal sections. Inferring a full Fermi volume from one frequency requires additional assumptions about shape and symmetry; measurements at several orientations can test them.

Count orbit centres and Hall filling

Landau level spacing determines the energy cost of changing orbital index. A second question is how many independent states fit into each level. Take a rectangular two-dimensional layer of area \(L_xL_y\), magnetic field \(B\) normal to it and Landau gauge \(A_y=Bx\). Translation symmetry along \(y\) gives wavevectors \(k_y=2\pi j/L_y\). In the transverse Hamiltonian the term \((\hbar k_y+eBx)^2/(2m^*)\) describes a harmonic oscillator with centre \(x_0=-\hbar k_y/(eB)\).

To place an orbit centre inside the sample, \(k_y\) must range over an interval of length \(eBL_x/\hbar\). Dividing by the allowed spacing \(2\pi/L_y\) gives \(N_\phi=eBL_xL_y/h\) bulk states per spin-resolved orbital level, neglecting edge corrections. Each magnetic flux quantum \(h/e\) adds one state per level. For sheet density \(n_s\) define filling factor \(\nu=n_sh/(eB)\). This counts occupied spin-resolved levels when it is an integer; if a residual spin degeneracy is unresolved, levels arrive in pairs.

In an ideal low-temperature integer quantum Hall state, with the chemical potential in a mobility gap, longitudinal dissipation can vanish and Hall conductance magnitude is \(\nu e^2/h\). The classical expression \(n_se/B\) has the same value at exact integer filling, but alone cannot explain persistence of plateaus over a finite interval of field. Localization and edge transport are essential for that observation.

Locate an integer filling in a measurement

A two-dimensional electron system has \(n_s=3.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻² and effective mass \(0.067m_e\). The field for total filling \(\nu=2\) is

\[B=n_sh/(2e)=6.20\,\mathrm{T}.\]

The number of states per area per spin-resolved level is \(eB/h=1.50\times10^{15}\) m⁻², half the measured sheet density. The orbital spacing at this field is

\[\hbar eB/m^*=10.7\,\mathrm{meV}.\]

At 4.2 K the thermal energy is 0.362 meV, so thermal smearing is smaller than the orbital gap; disorder and spin structure still matter.

The ideal Hall resistance magnitude on the \(\nu=2\) plateau is \(h/(2e^2)=12.9\) kΩ. A weak current of \(0.500\) μA would produce a transverse voltage magnitude of \(6.45\) mV. Increasing the field at fixed density decreases filling and can move the Fermi level into another broadened level. A simultaneous longitudinal voltage measurement is indispensable: a Hall voltage of the right numerical size at a single field can also occur in a classical conductor. A broad flat Hall trace together with a longitudinal-resistance minimum gives stronger evidence for quantized transport.

Changing the sample area at constant sheet density and magnetic field increases the number of occupied particles and the number of orbit centres in the same proportion. Filling factor and the ideal Hall resistance therefore remain unchanged, even though total particle count can be very different. In contrast, a gate that varies sheet density at fixed field moves the layer through successive fillings. The expected density spacing per spin-resolved level is \(eB/h\). A gate-calibrated scan can thus test Landau degeneracy independently of the cyclotron energy spacing inferred from optical or thermal observations.

When can a Landau-level spacing clear thermal broadening?

For a two-dimensional band with isotropic mass \(0.12m_e\) and negligible spin splitting, calculate \(\hbar e B/m^*\). Compare the result with the thermal energy for two temperatures. This gives a scale for designing a sweep, rather than a sharp visibility criterion.
