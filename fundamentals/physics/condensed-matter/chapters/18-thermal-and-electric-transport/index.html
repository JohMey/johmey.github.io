How the same electrons carry heat and charge

A metal with high electrical conductivity often also conducts heat well. The quantitative relation involves an open-circuit measurement, the thermal width of the Fermi surface, and the energy dependence of the states able to carry current.

Weigh the states that can carry a current

Use an isotropic band of quasiparticles with energy \(E\), velocity magnitude \(v(E)\), density of states per volume \(D(E)\) and elastic relaxation time \(\tau(E)\). In a weak electric field or temperature gradient the departure from local equilibrium is proportional to the relaxation time times the applied driving term. For a given transport direction the angular average gives \(\langle v_x^2\rangle=v^2/3\) in three dimensions. Define \(\Sigma(E)=D(E)v(E)^2\tau(E)/3\). Electrical current weights a particle by its charge \(q=-e\), while heat current weights it by \(E-\mu\). Three moments consequently suffice for the two linear responses:

\[K_j=\int\Sigma(E)(E-\mu)^j[-\partial_E f(E)]\,dE,\]
\[j=0,1,2.\]

Let \(\mathcal E\) denote the electric/electrochemical field with any chemical-potential gradient properly included. The resulting currents along one direction are \(j_e=q^2K_0\mathcal E-qK_1\nabla T/T\) and \(j_Q=qK_1\mathcal E-K_2\nabla T/T\). The factor \(E-\mu\) in the thermal drive follows from differentiating the Fermi function with respect to temperature. The repeated first moment is a manifestation of reciprocal coupling of electrical and thermal transport in this zero-field model.

Impose the actual electrical boundary condition

A voltage probe draws negligible charge current. Set \(j_e=0\) to obtain \(\mathcal E=S\nabla T\) with \(S=K_1/(qTK_0)\). Substitute this field into the heat current to get

\[\kappa_e=(K_2-K_1^2/K_0)/T.\]

Under the positive measure \(\Sigma(-f')dE/K_0\), the bracket divided by \(K_0\) is the variance of \(E-\mu\). Hence the open-circuit electronic thermal conductivity is nonnegative. Measuring with zero imposed electric field would retain a thermally driven charge current and a different heat-flow coefficient.

For a degenerate metal with smooth \(\Sigma\), the weight \(-f'\) is a narrow even peak. Replacing \(\Sigma\) by \(\Sigma(\mu)\) gives \(K_1=0\), \(K_0=\Sigma(\mu)\), and \(K_2=(\pi^2/3)(k_BT)^2\Sigma(\mu)\). The dimensionless second moment follows by integrating \(x^2/[4\cosh^2(x/2)]\) over the real axis. With \(\sigma=q^2K_0\),

\[\frac{\kappa_e}{\sigma T}=L_0=\frac{\pi^2k_B^2}{3e^2}=2.44\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,\Omega/K^2}.\]

At \(T=100\,\mathrm K\), a metal with \(\sigma=2.0\times10^7\,\mathrm{S/m}\) has estimated electronic \(\kappa_e=48.8\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\). A total measured conductivity of 60 would leave about \(11\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\) for other carriers if the assumptions hold. Phonons, inelastic scattering or strong energy dependence may change that inference.

Select a narrow range of transport energies

A model energy filter \(\Sigma\propto\exp[-(x-c)^2/(2w^2)]\), with \(x=(E-\mu)/(k_BT)\), \(c,w\) dimensionless, introduces a tunable departure from this metallic limit. The Seebeck coefficient is \((k_B/q)\langle x\rangle\) and the normalised Lorenz ratio is \(\operatorname{var}(x)/(\pi^2/3)\). A very narrow filter centred above the chemical potential produces strong signed thermopower but little heat transport at zero charge current, because current-carrying particles have almost identical excess energies. Those statements refer to the ratio and thermal moments; the absolute conductivity also depends on the overall transmission.

The experiment integrates these moments numerically and plots their normalised weight against the reference Fermi derivative. Vary \(c\) from positive to negative at fixed width. Predict which observable reverses sign and which is invariant before reading the numerical results.

Tutorial on open-circuit transport

For a fitted transport weight with \(\langle x\rangle=0.50,\ \langle x^2\rangle=2.25\), determine the Seebeck coefficient and \(L/L_0\). Use \(k_B/e=86.17\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}\).

Work through the solution

For electrons \(S=-43.1\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}\). The variance is

\[2.25-0.50^2=2.00.\]

Hence

\[L/L_0=2.00/(\pi^2/3)=0.608.\]

Using the second moment without subtracting the square of the first would represent the wrong electrical boundary condition.

Expand a smooth transport function to first order about \(\mu\). What controls the low-temperature sign of \(S\)?

Work through the solution

The constant term contributes nothing to \(K_1\) by symmetry. The slope gives

\[K_1\simeq\Sigma'(\mu)(\pi^2/3)(k_BT)^2.\]

Dividing by \(qTK_0\) yields

\[S\simeq[\pi^2k_B^2T/(3q)]\partial_E\ln\Sigma|_\mu.\]

For electrons a rising transport function gives negative \(S\) in the stated field convention.
