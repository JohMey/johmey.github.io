From microscopic moments to measured magnetisation

Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

A magnetised sample can make a compass turn without a power supply attached. The macroscopic theory assigns it bound currents in its bulk and on its surface. These are calculated sources for the magnetic field. We will reconstruct them from magnetic moments, predict signals from finite samples, and ask what an exterior field measurement can identify.

Define a material field at an intermediate scale

Choose a volume large enough to average over microscopic moments and small enough to resolve the sample's macroscopic variation. Let \(\boldsymbol M(\boldsymbol r)\) be the vector sum of moments within it divided by its volume. Its units are \(\mathrm{A/m}\). A permanent aligned collection may maintain \(\boldsymbol M\) without an applied field. An induced collection may point with or against the applied field. Electric charges need not traverse the entire sample in a closed macroscopic wire for either mechanism. Electron orbital moments and spin contribute to the measured moment; for a classical exterior field calculation any small magnetic dipole has the same leading field once its moment is specified.

A bar of volume \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) with uniform \(M=2.0\times10^4\,\mathrm{A/m}\) has total moment \(m=0.160\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\). A torsion balance in a uniform \(10\,\mathrm{mT}\) transverse field would measure a maximum torque \(1.60\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N\,m}\) if its moment stayed fixed. Dividing a measured torque by field and sample volume infers the component normal to the external field. Alignment of a previously soft material could instead alter the very moment being measured. Distinguishing fixed-source and induced-response experiments will matter when we calculate mechanical energy.

Derive the bound sources by integration by parts

Sum the dipole vector potentials over a material region \(V\). With observation point outside the matter, a volume element contributes \((\mu_0/4\pi)\boldsymbol M(\boldsymbol r')\times(\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol r')dV'/R^3\), where \(R=|\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol r'|\). Note that \(\nabla'(1/R)=(\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol r')/R^3\). Apply the identity \(\nabla'\times(\boldsymbol M/R)=(\nabla'1/R)\times\boldsymbol M+(\nabla'\times\boldsymbol M)/R\). The desired first cross product is minus the first term on the right. A volume integral of a curl is the surface integral of outward normal crossed with the vector. This yields

\[\boldsymbol A=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left[\int_V\frac{\nabla'\times\boldsymbol M}{R}\,dV'+\oint_{\partial V}\frac{\boldsymbol M\times\boldsymbol n'}{R}\,dA'\right].\]

Comparing with the ordinary current potential identifies \(\boldsymbol J_b=\nabla\times\boldsymbol M\), in A/m², and \(\boldsymbol K_b=\boldsymbol M\times\boldsymbol n\), in A/m. The first can vanish throughout a uniformly magnetised object, while the second closes around its exterior. A literal set of microscopic loops illustrates the sign. Neighbouring loop-edge currents cancel between equal adjacent moments, whereas their edges on the outer boundary remain uncompensated. When the moments vary slowly, interior cancellation is incomplete and yields the curl.

An alternative treatment extends \(\boldsymbol M\) by zero outside. Its distributional curl already includes the surface sheet at the discontinuity. Use either this full derivative or the separate bulk and surface integrals; including both descriptions simultaneously doubles the boundary source. At an internal interface with chosen normal from material 1 to 2 the two surface contributions combine to \((\boldsymbol M_1-\boldsymbol M_2)\times\boldsymbol n\). This expression is unchanged by interchange of the two labels accompanied by reversal of the normal.

Work through a uniformly magnetised cylinder

Let a finite circular cylinder of radius \(a\) and length \(2l\) have \(\boldsymbol M=M\hat{\boldsymbol z}\). On the curved side \(\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\hat{\boldsymbol s}=\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}\), hence \(K_\varphi=M\); on its end faces the cross product vanishes. The cylinder is equivalent, for its magnetic field, to a finite solenoid having turn-current per unit length \(M\). A strip of sidewall of height \(dz'\) carries ring current \(M\,dz'\). Insert this into the axial finite-loop field from the first chapter and integrate from \(-l\) to \(l\).

\[B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0 M}{2}\left[\frac{z+l}{\sqrt{a^2+(z+l)^2}}-\frac{z-l}{\sqrt{a^2+(z-l)^2}}\right] .\]

At the centre \(B_z=\mu_0M l/\sqrt{a^2+l^2}\). For \(a=10\,\mathrm{mm}, l=20\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(M=1.0\times10^4\,\mathrm{A/m}\), the value is \(11.24\,\mathrm{mT}\), below the long-cylinder limit \(12.57\,\mathrm{mT}\). At the end centre \(z=l\), only the first term contributes, giving \(6.10\,\mathrm{mT}\). A probe just outside the end and one just inside see the same normal \(\boldsymbol B\) in the limit, even though the auxiliary normal \(\boldsymbol H\) can jump because \(\boldsymbol M\) ends abruptly. The field near an edge is not uniform and can vary strongly with the sensor's position. Stating the axis and position is therefore essential when inferring magnetisation from a reading.

Use a nonuniform sample to separate bulk and surface effects

For an infinitely long cylinder set \(\boldsymbol M=M_0(s/a)\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}\) for \(s<a\), zero outside. The cylindrical curl gives \(J_{b,z}=s^{-1}\partial_s(sM_\varphi)=2M_0/a\) in the bulk. The side normal is \(\hat{\boldsymbol s}\), so \(K_{b,z}=-M_0\). Integrated over a cross section the bulk current is \(2\pi aM_0\); the axial sheet supplies \(-2\pi aM_0\). Their cancellation means an exterior Amperian circle sees zero enclosed current. Its zero field follows from symmetry and the absence of externally imposed fields.

Inside, a circle at \(s\) encloses \(2M_0\pi s^2/a\). Dividing its circulation by \(2\pi s\) gives \(B_\varphi=\mu_0M_0s/a\). Thus \(\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol B/\mu_0-\boldsymbol M=0\) both inside and outside here, despite nonzero interior \(\boldsymbol B\). For \(a=20\,\mathrm{mm},M_0=500\,\mathrm{A/m}\), the bulk current density is \(5.0\times10^4\,\mathrm{A/m^2}\), total bulk current \(62.8\,\mathrm A\), and field just inside the rim \(0.628\,\mathrm{mT}\). A magnetometer outside reports zero within this ideal geometry. Such a null measurement establishes neither zero \(\boldsymbol M\) nor absence of all microscopic moments.

Compare a field map and a pickup-coil measurement

A stationary inductive pickup near a stationary permanent magnet has no emf from that static flux alone. An experiment can move the sample, rotate it or modulate a known magnetising field to produce \(-N\,d\Phi/dt\). A Hall sensor or calibrated magnetometer can instead respond to a static field. To compare methods, place a small pickup near the axis of the preceding finite cylinder and oscillate its axial position as \(z=z_0+\zeta\cos\omega t\). For \(\zeta\) small, the flux per turn is approximately \(A_p[B_z(z_0)+\zeta B'_z(z_0)\cos\omega t]\). The voltage amplitude is \(N A_p\zeta\omega |B'_z(z_0)|\). It measures a gradient multiplied by displacement amplitude, whereas a static Hall value measures the field itself.

At the symmetric cylinder centre \(B'_z(0)=0\), so the first harmonic vanishes even though the field is strong. Expanding one more order gives a constant shift and a second harmonic proportional to \(B''_z(0)\zeta^2\), since \(\cos^2\omega t=(1+\cos2\omega t)/2\). Finding a very small first harmonic at the centre is therefore a check on positioning and symmetry. It is not a calibration of zero magnetisation. Far from the ends, a measurement of the whole sample's dipole field instead yields \(\int\boldsymbol M\,dV\). Different internal distributions can share that integral while producing different nearer fields. Report the geometry, the actual observable and the assumed uniformity whenever quoting a single recovered value of \(M\).

Examine bound currents and field measurements in a magnetised sample

Use fictitious poles to compute a measurable field

There is a second useful representation of a given magnetisation. In a simply connected region without free current, \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol H=0\), so choose \(\boldsymbol H=-\nabla\psi\). Substitution into \(\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol H+\boldsymbol M)=0\) gives \(\nabla^2\psi=\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol M\). The potential therefore behaves mathematically as if it were produced by volume density \(\rho_m=-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol M\) and surface density \(\sigma_m=\boldsymbol M\cdot\boldsymbol n\). The surface term follows by extending the magnetisation by zero across the boundary, or by requiring continuity of \(B_n=\mu_0(H_n+M_n)\). Integrate these densities with kernel \(1/(4\pi R)\) to obtain \(\psi\); there is no factor of \(\epsilon_0^{-1}\) because these conventions assign \(\psi\) units of amperes. The resulting \(\boldsymbol H\) must be converted back to \(\boldsymbol B\), adding \(\mu_0\boldsymbol M\) inside matter.

For the uniformly magnetised finite cylinder the pole representation puts densities \(+M\) and \(-M\) on the two end faces, none on the sides. Earlier the bound-current representation placed current on the curved side and none on the ends. They predict the same physical field. A single uniformly loaded disk of pole density \(\sigma_m\) at \(z=0\) has on-axis scalar potential \(\psi=\sigma_m[\sqrt{z^2+a^2}-|z|]/2\), obtained by integrating \(2\pi s\,ds/[4\pi\sqrt{s^2+z^2}]\). Its derivative gives \(H_z=(\sigma_m/2)[\operatorname{sgn}(z)-z/\sqrt{z^2+a^2}]\). At the disk plane the two one-sided fields jump by \(\sigma_m\), precisely the normal-\(H\) jump demanded by the magnetisation.

At the midpoint of the cylinder add the two oppositely charged disks. Their axial fields both point opposite the magnetisation and total \(H_z=-M[1-l/\sqrt{a^2+l^2}]\). Adding \(M\) yields the same \(B_z\) as the side-current integral. At \(l=2a\), this internal demagnetising field is \(-0.1056M\); for a very short cylinder \(l\ll a\) it approaches \(-M\). A uniformly specified magnetisation in a thin disc therefore produces only a small normal \(B\) at its centre despite the large \(M\) and \(H\) terms that nearly cancel. In practice, a soft magnetic material in such a shape may choose a nonuniform magnetisation or domains. The calculation describes the field of the imposed distribution; it does not establish that the distribution is a stable material state.

The 'poles' are convenient mathematical source terms, not isolated magnetic charges: adding the effective volume and surface poles over a finite bounded sample gives \(-\int\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol M\,dV+\oint\boldsymbol M\cdot\boldsymbol n\,dA=0\). North and south effective poles may be far apart, but a surrounding closed Gaussian surface still has zero net magnetic flux. If a model predicts a \(1/r^2\) radial field of one sign through an entire distant sphere around a fully included bar magnet, test whether the opposite face or volume contribution has been accidentally dropped.

Infer the field of a spherical void by subtracting a known sample

Superposition can calculate a local cavity field before introducing a detailed microscopic model. Imagine a large body with uniform imposed magnetisation \(\boldsymbol M\) near an interior point, and remove a small spherical piece. The magnetic field after removal is the original macroscopic field minus the contribution of the excised sphere, provided the surrounding magnetisation stays unchanged. A uniformly magnetised sphere in otherwise empty space alone has uniform internal \(H=-M/3\), as can be derived from its pole distribution \(\sigma_m=M\cos\theta\). At the sphere centre \(\boldsymbol H\) follows by integrating the field from surface elements \(-\sigma_m\hat{\boldsymbol r}\,dA/(4\pi a^2)\). The \(z\)-component involves the spherical average \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\); transverse contributions vanish by symmetry. The unique dipolar harmonic extends that central value uniformly throughout the sphere.

The sphere's own internal \(B\) is therefore \(2\mu_0M/3\). Immediately after its removal the field in the empty cavity is \(B_{\rm cav}=B_{\rm bulk}-2\mu_0M/3=\mu_0(H_{\rm bulk}+M/3)\). This assumes the sphere is small relative to the variation scale of the pre-existing magnetisation and far from the sample's external boundary on its own size scale. A different cavity shape changes the subtraction factor, and a physical bore can rearrange the surrounding magnetisation or admit demagnetising fields from its ends. Local-field interpretations depend on probe geometry as well as material properties.

As a numerical illustration, suppose a permanent or pinned surrounding material has local \(M=1500\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and \(H_{\rm bulk}=400\,\mathrm{A/m}\), aligned. The macroscopic bulk field is \(\mu_0(1900)=2.39\,\mathrm{mT}\). A small spherical empty cavity under the stated frozen-distribution approximation has \(B_{\rm cav}=\mu_0(900)=1.13\,\mathrm{mT}\). Both readings can be correct for the same initial material. A Hall element inserted into a drilled hole measures the field in that hole; labelling it 'the internal material field' without modelling the probe cavity can cause a substantial discrepancy. Small hole volume does not by itself make the local cavity field correction small, since area and inverse-square field factors cancel in the spherical surface integral.

For a final comparison, cut a very thin flat slit perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol M\) instead of a sphere, idealising its lateral dimensions as large relative to its thickness. The facing effective pole sheets produce a strong additional field within the slit. Normal \(B\) stays continuous across its faces, so the vacuum reading tends towards the original normal \(B_{\rm bulk}\) when edge effects and magnetisation changes are negligible. A long narrow cavity parallel to the magnetisation instead tends towards continuity of tangential \(H\) on its long sides. Comparing the sphere and slit is an operational way to understand why \(B\) and \(H\) are different macroscopic variables rather than two symbols for one ambiguous 'magnetic intensity'.

Reconstruct an invisible current distribution in a magnetised film

An extended magnetic film fills the slab \(-a<z<a\). A fabrication process leaves a tangential magnetisation \(M_x(z)\hat x\). Let the magnetisation vanish outside. There is no free current in this calculation, and there is no applied field at infinity. A field measurement above the centre of a very wide film can then give nearly zero even when the magnetisation inside varies substantially. Work out both the bound currents and the local magnetic field before deciding what an external zero reading means.

Inside the material the bound volume current is \(J_{{\rm b},y}=dM_x/dz\). The upper face has outward normal \(+\hat z\), so its bound sheet current is \(-M_x(a^-)\hat y\). On the lower face the outward normal reverses and the sheet current is \(+M_x(-a^+)\hat y\). Integrating the volume current through the thickness gives \(M_x(a^-)-M_x(-a^+)\). Thus the combined sheet and volume currents sum to zero per unit length across the entire film. This cancellation is independent of how much structure the magnetisation has inside the slab. An alternative derivation extends \(M_x\) by zero beyond the faces and differentiates that piecewise function as a distribution. The jumps generate both surface terms with their proper signs.

For an explicit depth profile take \(M_x=M_0(1+z/a)\). There is a uniform bulk current \(M_0/a\), no lower surface current, and an upper surface current \(-2M_0\). Planar symmetry allows only \(B_x(z)\). Ampère's law gives \(dB_x/dz=\mu_0 J_y\). Integrating from below the film, where the field vanishes, gives \(B_x=\mu_0 M_0(1+z/a)\) inside. At the upper face the field jumps back to zero. The result also follows directly from \(H_x=B_x/\mu_0-M_x\): its tangential component is continuous wherever there is no free surface current, and there is no volume source for its variation. In this geometry \(H_x=0\) throughout.

Take \(a=0.20\,{\rm mm}\) and \(M_0=2.0\times10^4\,{\rm A/m}\). The interior current density is \(1.0\times10^8\,{\rm A/m^2}\). That large number represents microscopic circulating moments after coarse graining. It does not imply a transport current passing through external electrical contacts. The peak interior field is approximately \(50.3\,{\rm mT}\) just below the upper face, and the average is about \(25.1\,{\rm mT}\). An exterior sensor over the central region of the infinite idealisation reads zero at any height. With a finite lateral extent, currents must close around the film edges. The resulting fringe signal contains shape information and becomes measurable as the probe approaches the rim.

Rotate the magnetisation through ninety degrees so that it now points in the \(z\) direction while its magnitude keeps the same depth profile. Each local curl vanishes. Each face has parallel magnetisation and normal, giving zero bound sheet current. Consequently the solution has zero \(B\) everywhere in the ideal infinite geometry. Within the film \(H_z=-M_z\). In the magnetic-pole construction the two surface pole densities and the bulk density \(-dM_z/dz\) provide precisely that auxiliary field. These two slab examples illustrate a real inverse problem: very different internal magnetic states can produce the same exterior measurement. Knowing the material orientation or imposing a changed external field supplies additional information.

Can two depth profiles with the same average magnetisation be distinguished by an ideal measurement of the tangential magnetic flux through the film thickness?

Work through the solution

Choose a rectangular sensing surface with its normal along \(x\), its height equal to the film thickness, and its length \(w\) along \(y\). The ideal internal flux is \(w\mu_0\int_{-a}^{a}M_x(z)\,dz\). This measurement determines one weighted integral. A constant \(M_0\) and the linear example above give identical results even though the constant profile has only surface currents. A depth-selective measurement would weight the profiles differently. For example, integrating over just the upper half gives \(\mu_0waM_0\) in the constant case and \(3\mu_0waM_0/2\) in the linear case. Actual pickup loops must close outside the sample, so include their full geometry when inferring an internal flux.

Suppose the linear profile relaxes uniformly in amplitude after an aligning field is removed. What information would the integrated voltage of a calibrated pickup provide?

Work through the solution

If the pickup links \(N\) turns with the preceding full-thickness flux and \(M_0\) decays from its initial value to zero, the time-integrated emf has magnitude \(2N\mu_0waM_0(0)\). Faraday's law supplies this integral independently of whether the decay is exponential or has several relaxation times. A finite integration window that ends before zero magnetisation underestimates the initial amplitude unless the residual flux is measured. Leakage from the changing applied alignment field can also drive the loop directly. A reference winding of the same area, measured without the specimen, allows that part of the transient to be estimated.

Interfaces, demagnetising fields and the work of magnetisation

Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

Two samples made of the same material can respond very differently to an externally applied field if one is a long rod and the other is a thin disc. The induced moment depends on the internal field, which the magnetised sample helps create. The same distinction reappears when one tries to infer a force from stored magnetic energy.

Introduce the auxiliary field by removing a known current

With magnetisation present the total steady current is \(\boldsymbol J_f+\nabla\times\boldsymbol M\), where the curl includes any sheet contribution if \(\boldsymbol M\) is extended distributionally. Define \(\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol B/\mu_0-\boldsymbol M\), giving \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol J_f\). A small ribbon crossing an interface with normal \(\boldsymbol n\) directed from 1 to 2 gives \(\boldsymbol n\times(\boldsymbol H_2-\boldsymbol H_1)=\boldsymbol K_f\), and a thin pillbox gives \(\boldsymbol n\cdot(\boldsymbol B_2-\boldsymbol B_1)=0\). The boundary conditions distinguish the field whose circulation counts free current from the field whose flux has no sources.

If both media are linear, isotropic and local, \(\boldsymbol B_i=\mu_i\boldsymbol H_i\). In the absence of free surface current \(H_t\) and \(B_n\) are continuous. For angles measured from the normal, \(\tan\theta=B_t/B_n\) therefore gives \(\tan\theta_2/\tan\theta_1=\mu_2/\mu_1\) when the nonzero components permit the ratio. For instance, going from vacuum into a medium with \(\mu_r=5\), let \(B_{1n}=2\,\mathrm{mT}\) and \(B_{1t}=1\,\mathrm{mT}\). The new components are \(2\) and \(5\,\mathrm{mT}\), tilting the local field from \(26.6^\circ\) to \(68.2^\circ\). A mounted probe sensitive only to the normal component would register no jump. This local argument says which components can jump; it does not solve the complete field of a finite piece of material.

Solve a sphere to find its internal field

Put a uniform isotropic sphere of radius \(a\) and permeability \(\mu_r\mu_0\) in an originally uniform external \(\boldsymbol H_0=H_0\hat{\boldsymbol z}\). There is no free current in or near it, so locally \(\boldsymbol H=-\nabla\psi\). In each uniform domain \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0\) implies Laplace's equation for \(\psi\). Spherical symmetry of the geometry and the linear far-field potential select the angular harmonic \(\cos\theta\). The finite interior solution is \(-C r\cos\theta\); the exterior is \(-H_0r\cos\theta+D\cos\theta/r^2\). Tangential \(H\) agrees at the surface when \(\psi\) is continuous up to a chosen common constant. Normal \(B\) enforces the second equation:

\[C=H_0-D/a^3,\]
\[\mu_r C=H_0+2D/a^3,\]
\[C=\frac{3H_0}{\mu_r+2}.\]

Inside, \(H_{\rm int}=C\) is uniform. Multiplying by susceptibility \(\chi=\mu_r-1\) gives \(M=\chi C\). Rearranging yields the memorable self-consistency relation \(H_{\rm int}=H_0-M/3\). The factor \(1/3\) came from solving both boundary conditions for a sphere and is not a universal material property. The dipolar exterior carries moment \(m=4\pi D=(4\pi a^3/3)M\), as expected from summing the uniform magnetisation. An ellipsoid with a principal axis parallel to the drive can likewise have uniform magnetisation and \(H_{\rm int}=H_0-N_dM\) with a geometric demagnetising factor; an arbitrary block usually has spatial variations near edges.

Take \(\mu_r=10\) and \(H_0=200\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The sphere has \(H_{\rm int}=50\,\mathrm{A/m}\), \(M=450\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and \(B_{\rm int}=0.628\,\mathrm{mT}\). A naive use of \(M=9H_0\) would give four times the actual moment. A long narrow specimen parallel to the drive, with very small \(N_d\), comes much closer to that latter value if the material remains linear. For the sphere \(\mu_r\to\infty\) gives \(B_{\rm int}\to3\mu_0H_0\) rather than infinity; meanwhile \(H_{\rm int}\to0\). These two trends are compatible because \(B/H\) is growing. Calling either simply a 'field enhancement' without specifying the measured field obscures the limiting result.

Derive the incremental energy for a reversible medium

Imagine increasing the current in a winding slowly while holding all material boundaries fixed. Faraday's law makes the terminal voltage, apart from resistance, \(d\lambda/dt\), where \(\lambda\) is the flux linkage. The source delivers incremental energy \(I\,d\lambda\). In a uniform toroidal core of mean path length \(\ell\), area \(A\), and \(N\) turns, \(H=NI/\ell,\ \lambda=NBA\). Therefore \(I\,d\lambda=(A\ell)H\,dB\). In a lossless quasistatic medium the magnetic energy density accumulated along a reversible constitutive path is \(w(B)=\int_0^B H(B')\,dB'\). When \(B=\mu H\) with constant \(\mu\), this reduces to \(BH/2\). The corresponding coenergy \(w'(H)=\int_0^H B(H')\,dH'\) satisfies \(w+w'=BH\) for a single-valued curve through the origin.

As a nonlinear example, use \(H=\alpha B+\beta B^3\) with \(\alpha=10^3\,\mathrm{A/(m\,T)}\) and \(\beta=4.0\times10^3\,\mathrm{A/(m\,T^3)}\) over the measured range. At \(B=0.50\) T, \(H=1000\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The integrated energy is \(w=\alpha B^2/2+\beta B^4/4=187.5\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\); half the endpoint product gives \(250\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\) and misses the curvature. Coenergy is \(312.5\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\), compensating the difference. The differential permeability is \(dB/dH=(\alpha+3\beta B^2)^{-1}=2.50\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{H/m}\), whereas the ratio \(B/H=5.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{H/m}\). A small alternating signal about a DC operating point samples the differential value. These numbers are properties of the model curve and should not be mistaken for a universal ferromagnetic law.

Add an air gap and localise the energy

Give a toroidal magnetic circuit a thin gap of length \(g\), neglecting fringing, leakage and flux crowding. For equal cross sections the same normal \(B\) passes through core and gap. Ampère's law gives \(NI=H_c\ell_c+B g/\mu_0\). For linear \(\mu_r\), this yields \(B=\mu_0NI/(\ell_c/\mu_r+g)\). With \(\ell_c=0.20\,\mathrm m,\ g=1.0\,\mathrm{mm},\ \mu_r=1000,\ NI=100\) A-turns the result is \(0.1047\) T. The gap consumes \(83.3\) A-turns; the much longer core consumes \(16.7\). Vary the two lengths separately to see why high permeability cannot remove the gap contribution.

Their energy ratio follows from \(B^2/(2\mu)\) times volume, giving \(U_g/U_c=g\mu_r/\ell_c=5.0\). For area \(A=1.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), the gap stores \(0.436\,\mathrm{mJ}\) and the core \(0.0873\,\mathrm{mJ}\). The simple force scale at one nearly uniform normal gap face is \(B^2A/(2\mu_0)=0.436\,\mathrm N\). Decreasing \(g\) at fixed current changes the total flux, so differentiating only the instantaneous gap energy while holding everything else implicitly fixed need not reproduce the mechanical force. One may use a vacuum stress surface or a coenergy derivative at prescribed current. Both methods require a clear statement about what is held fixed during the imagined displacement.

Vary the gap and the permeability independently

Distinguish reversible storage from a measured loop

Real ferromagnetic samples can have history-dependent magnetisation. If \(B\) and \(H\) trace a closed hysteresis loop, the integral \(\oint H\,dB\) measures energy per volume delivered in one quasistatic cycle, with a positive sign for a dissipative cycle under passive conventions. A single-valued energy density of \(B\) alone cannot reproduce this nonzero path integral. At finite frequency conductive eddy losses and relaxation can broaden a measured loop, so a loop measured at one rate alone cannot isolate a purely quasistatic hysteresis loss.

For an approximately parallelogram-shaped cycle spanning switching fields \(\pm120\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and switching induction swing \(0.80\,\mathrm T\) from lower to upper branch, its approximate enclosed area is \(240\times0.80=192\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\) per cycle. At 50 Hz a \(12\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) sample dissipates approximately \(0.115\,\mathrm W\). The stated swing here is the full separation, eliminating a common factor-of-two ambiguity. A power meter on the terminals also sees the winding's \(I^2R\) and any energy leaving the apparatus; a separate resistance measurement and a frequency sweep help isolate the material contribution.

Reconstruct an anisotropic interface from boundary data

At an interface with a single crystal the relation between \(\boldsymbol H\) and \(\boldsymbol B\) may be tensorial. The formulas for angle refraction derived earlier assume scalar permeability; the underlying boundary conditions do not. Take a planar interface with normal \(+\hat{\boldsymbol z}\), vacuum below and a linear reciprocal crystal above. Choose units such that its relative permeability in the \(x,z\) plane is the symmetric matrix with entries \(\mu_{xx}=4,\mu_{xz}=\mu_{zx}=1,\mu_{zz}=2\). Its positive determinant and positive diagonal make the associated magnetic energy positive. Suppose the vacuum drive near the boundary has \(H_{1x}=100\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and \(B_{1z}/\mu_0=200\,\mathrm{A/m}\), with no \(y\) component and no free sheet current. These are local interface data, not a complete boundary condition for a finite crystal.

Continuity of tangential \(H\) gives \(H_{2x}=100\). Continuity of normal \(B\) gives \(100+2H_{2z}=200\), hence \(H_{2z}=50\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The crystal's tangential flux density then follows from \(B_{2x}/\mu_0=4(100)+50=450\,\mathrm{A/m}\); \(B_{2z}/\mu_0=200\) by construction. In the crystal \(\boldsymbol H\) and \(\boldsymbol B\) point at different angles to the normal, with tangent ratios 2 and 2.25 respectively. The local reversible magnetic energy density is \(\boldsymbol H\cdot\boldsymbol B/2=\mu_0[100(450)+50(200)]/2=0.0346\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\). Replacing the tensor by a scalar fitted to the normal component would give the wrong tangent field and energy even though that fitted scalar reproduces one measurement by design.

For a general symmetric matrix, the cross energy in \(w(\boldsymbol H)=\boldsymbol H\cdot\boldsymbol\mu\boldsymbol H/2\) involves both off-diagonal elements. Its derivative with respect to one component yields the corresponding component of \(\boldsymbol B\). One can test reciprocity by preparing two small field increments about the same reversible operating point and interchanging their directions. The mixed incremental energy should agree. Large hysteresis loops, saturation along an easy axis or externally biased nonreciprocal media require more information before equating the two cross responses. The scalar material tables often used for a gapped iron core therefore encode a particular direction and operating regime.

Determine a demagnetising factor or an intrinsic susceptibility

For an ellipsoidal sample along a principal axis, combine \(H_{\rm int}=H_0-N_dM\) with \(M=\chi H_{\rm int}\). Define apparent susceptibility \(\chi_{\rm app}=M/H_0\). Rearrangement gives \(1/\chi_{\rm app}=1/\chi+N_d\). This linear relation suggests a comparative measurement that can separate material and shape without having a probe inside either sample. Prepare geometrically distinct ellipsoids from one linear isotropic material, measure their moments along specified axes in the same calibrated uniform field, and divide by their respective volumes. If their demagnetising factors are independently known and the material history has not changed, the quantities \(1/\chi_{\rm app}-N_d\) should agree.

Take intrinsic \(\chi=20\). A sphere with \(N_d=1/3\) gives \(\chi_{\rm app}=2.609\); a slender ellipsoid with \(N_d=0.050\) gives \(\chi_{\rm app}=10.0\). One might attribute their nearly fourfold moment ratio to different compositions if the shapes were omitted. Suppose the measured slender-sample value is \(9.0\) instead. Its inferred intrinsic susceptibility would be \(1/(1/9-0.05)=16.36\). Comparing the sphere's inferred value of 20 tests whether the discrepancy exceeds shape, volume and magnetometer uncertainties. The inversion is especially ill-conditioned near the large-\(\chi\) apparent saturation \(1/N_d\); small errors in a heavily demagnetised sample then produce large errors in the recovered intrinsic response.

The sensitivity can be quantified without introducing a fitting convention. Differentiate \(\chi=\chi_{\rm app}/(1-N_d\chi_{\rm app})\), giving \(d\chi/d\chi_{\rm app}=1/(1-N_d\chi_{\rm app})^2\). At the preceding spherical point this derivative is nearly 59, so an absolute error 0.02 in the apparent value shifts the recovered intrinsic result by about 1.18. For the slender sample the derivative is only four at the stated point. The large spherical amplification follows from subtraction of nearly equal quantities, not poor electrical calibration. An elongated specimen may therefore yield a more informative intrinsic measurement even if a sphere is easier to machine and align.

Check a material explanation against mechanical and electrical work

Imagine measuring a single coil fitted with an adjustable magnetic insert. At a particular position the current ramp up from zero to 0.50 A produces linkage \(\lambda(I)=aI+bI^3\) with \(a=0.040\,\mathrm H\), \(b=-0.020\,\mathrm{H/A^2}\) over this restricted range. Differential inductance is \(a+3bI^2\) and stays positive here. Stored energy is \(\int I\,d\lambda=aI^2/2+3bI^4/4\), whereas coenergy is \(\int\lambda dI=aI^2/2+bI^4/4\). At the stated end current they are \(4.06\) and \(4.69\) mJ. Their sum \(8.75\) mJ equals \(I\lambda\). A measured terminal waveform can integrate the first expression directly by accumulating \(I(v-RI)dt\); independently plotting \(\lambda\) versus \(I\) allows the second by planimetric integration. These two areas have different shapes on a nonlinear constitutive curve.

If the insert moves, coenergy at each position can be obtained by repeating the reversible \(\lambda(I)\) scan. Its spatial difference divided by travel distance estimates the average force for fixed end current. Simply using half the product of the end current and linkage would predict neither energy correctly in this example. A hysteretic insert follows different curves on raising and lowering the current, so a measured mean force or cycle loss must state the prepared branch. A force predicted by one reversible branch can be checked with a load cell at the same position and current; an electrical scan at another bias is not enough to test the mechanical boundary condition.

Derive the directional permeability of a stack of thin layers

A material assembled from alternating sheets can respond differently to fields along and across the sheets even when each constituent is isotropic. Consider many parallel layers of two linear, reversible magnetic materials with absolute permeabilities \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\). Their volume fractions in a representative period are \(f\) and \(1-f\). Start far from the outer edges. Suppose the imposed field varies only on lengths much longer than a period, and take the frequency low enough that eddy currents do not change the field distribution. These conditions make a comparison with homogeneous bulk measurements meaningful.

First apply a field tangent to the layers. The tangential field \(H_t\) is continuous at each interface because no free sheet current flows there. Each material has a different magnetic induction \(B_{it}=\mu_i H_t\). Averaging the induction over one period and dividing by the shared field gives \(\mu_\parallel=f\mu_1+(1-f)\mu_2\). This is an arithmetic mean. A heavily permeable layer can carry a large fraction of the tangential flux through only part of the period. No condition requires equal tangential induction on the two sides of the internal interface.

Next drive the magnetic flux through the stack in the normal direction. Normal induction is continuous because magnetic flux lines cannot end at a layer. The normal field in each layer is \(B_n/\mu_i\). Averaging the field at fixed common induction now gives a harmonic mean,

\[\frac{1}{\mu_\perp}=\frac{f}{\mu_1}+\frac{1-f}{\mu_2}.\]

A small fraction of a weakly permeable spacer can account for most of the magnetomotive drop across a stack. One can derive the same result by adding the magnetic reluctances of a column of common area. That circuit calculation works here because the interface conditions guarantee a common induction. Tilting the applied field creates both normal and tangential components and requires both averages at once. In axes attached to the layers, \(\langle B_t\rangle=\mu_\parallel\langle H_t\rangle\) and \(\langle B_n\rangle=\mu_\perp\langle H_n\rangle\). The averaged field vectors are generally not parallel.

For a stack containing \(90\%\) by volume of a material with relative permeability \(1000\) and \(10\%\) of a spacer with relative permeability one, the two effective relative permeabilities are \(900.1\) and approximately \(9.91\). Suppose a fixture produces equal average components \(H_t=H_n=10\,{\rm A/m}\). The induction components are then about \(11.31\,{\rm mT}\) and \(0.125\,{\rm mT}\). Their ratio corresponds to an induction angle only \(0.63^\circ\) above the layers while the average field points at \(45^\circ\). A probe sensitive to a single component could therefore return an apparently inconsistent permeability when the orientation of an anisotropic specimen is ignored.

Verify the energy before using the homogenised material law. For a purely normal induction the microscopic average energy density is \(fB_n^2/(2\mu_1)+(1-f)B_n^2/(2\mu_2)=B_n^2/(2\mu_\perp)\). For tangential field the microscopic average is \(H_t^2[f\mu_1+(1-f)\mu_2]/2=\mu_\parallel H_t^2/2\). Adding both contributions gives \(\langle\mathbf B\rangle\cdot\langle\mathbf H\rangle/2\) for this linear model. Directly replacing each constituent field by the spatially averaged field before evaluating its energy would give an incorrect normal contribution. The field concentration in the spacer is a physical effect that survives passage to an effective continuum.

Estimate how precisely the spacer fraction must be known if a low-frequency normal-induction calibration is to be reproducible.

Work through the solution

Write \(s=1-f\) and \(\mu_{\perp,r}=[(1-s)/1000+s]^{-1}\). Differentiating its logarithm gives \(d\ln\mu_{\perp,r}/ds=-0.999/[0.001+0.999s]\), about \(-9.90\) at \(s=0.10\). Increasing the spacer volume fraction by one absolute percentage point therefore reduces normal permeability by roughly ten percent to first order. Direct substitution at \(s=0.11\) gives \(9.02\), or a reduction of about nine percent, showing the size of the linearisation error for that relatively large change. Derive the time and frequency responses of a relaxing polarisation, locate the loss maximum and decide whether a measured sample contains more than one relaxation process.

From a delayed polarisation to a constitutive equation

A slab of insulating material is placed between parallel electrodes. At first the measured charge increases very quickly when a small voltage is switched on. It then continues to rise slowly even though the applied voltage is fixed. A constant real relative permittivity can describe the final charge but cannot predict this record. The experiment reveals at least two relevant time scales. For a measurement window that resolves only the slower process, treat the fast electronic response as instantaneous and describe the slower polarisation \(P\) by its approach to an equilibrium value.

At fixed weak field the equilibrium slow polarisation is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E\), with \(\Delta\epsilon>0\). Assume the rate of change is proportional to the distance from that equilibrium, with one positive relaxation time \(\tau\). Then \(\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E(t)\). This is a phenomenological material law. Its assumptions include linear response about the prepared state, a time-independent \(\tau\), and a sample whose constituents can be described by one dominant relaxation. Testing those assumptions is part of the experiment.

For \(E(t)=E_s\) switched on at zero from an unpolarised state, solve using the integrating factor \(e^{t/\tau}\). The result is \(P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E_s(1-e^{-t/\tau})\). After one \(\tau\), 63.21% of the slow response has developed; after \(3\tau\), 95.02%. Measuring an absolute fraction requires subtracting the fast initial jump and knowing the eventual plateau. A long baseline drift can look like a second relaxation if those references are not controlled.

Derive the complex dielectric response

Now use peak phasors with time dependence \(e^{-i\omega t}\). Differentiation contributes \(-i\omega\), so \(P/E=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon/(1-i\omega\tau)\). Include the high-frequency relative permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\), where “high” means above the slow process but below additional electronic resonances. The real and imaginary parts of relative permittivity are

\[\epsilon'=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{\Delta\epsilon}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\]
\[\epsilon''=\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.\]

At fixed field amplitude the polarisation current is \(\dot P=-i\omega P\). The mean electrical work per unit time and volume is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E^*\dot P)=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon''|E|^2\). Loss therefore has positive \(\epsilon''\) in the chosen time convention. Using \(e^{+i\omega t}\) reverses the sign of the imaginary part of permittivity but leaves mean absorbed energy positive when the current convention is changed consistently.

Set \(u=\omega\tau\). Differentiating \(u/(1+u^2)\) gives \((1-u^2)/(1+u^2)^2\). Hence \(\epsilon''\) peaks at \(u=1\), with height \(\Delta\epsilon/2\), and the real part lies halfway between its low- and high-frequency plateaux. The peak identified here is in the imaginary *permittivity*. A plot of dissipated power at fixed field includes an additional factor of frequency and need not have a peak at the same location.

Tie a frequency sweep to a step test

Suppose a material has \(\epsilon_\infty=2\), \(\Delta\epsilon=6\), and a measured loss peak at 1 kHz. Then \(\tau=1/(2\pi\,1000)\simeq159\ \mu\mathrm s\). At the peak the relative permittivity is \(5+3i\) in our convention. A time-domain voltage step should show a slow charge transient reaching 63.2% of its added charge in roughly 159 μs. This comparison connects two experiments that weight electrode offsets and parasitic impedances differently.

An independent shape check follows by eliminating \(u\). Let \(X=\epsilon'-\epsilon_\infty\) and \(Y=\epsilon''\). From the formulas \(X^2+Y^2=\Delta\epsilon X\). Thus \((X-\Delta\epsilon/2)^2+Y^2=(\Delta\epsilon/2)^2\): a single Debye relaxation traces a semicircle in the complex plane. Departures from a semicircle can reflect several relaxation times, spatially inhomogeneous samples, leakage or imperfect fixture correction. They do not uniquely identify the microscopic mechanism.

Identify mobile charge with a transverse field

Derive a Hall-voltage measurement from transverse force balance, reverse current and field to reject offsets and explore what fails when two carrier types conduct.

Choose signs from a drawing

A rectangular conducting sample has its long axis along \(x\), width \(w\) along \(y\), and thickness \(t\) along \(z\). A uniform magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\) is applied. An external circuit maintains conventional current \(I>0\) in \(+x\). High-impedance transverse probes draw negligible current. Define the measured signal as \(V_H=V(y=0)-V(y=w)\). For uniform transverse field this convention gives \(V_H=E_yw\). Reversing the voltmeter leads reverses the reported sign; writing a carrier sign without a lead convention is incomplete.

With one charge carrier species of number density \(n\) and signed charge \(q\), the drift velocity satisfies \(j_x=nqv_x=I/(wt)\). If the transverse velocity vanishes, its magnetic force component is \(-qv_xB\), opposed by \(qE_y\). Thus \(E_y=v_x B=j_xB/(nq)\). The resulting port measurement is

\[V_H=\frac{IB}{nq t}.\]

The width cancels for a uniform bar; changing the contact geometry or using a current source with a nonuniform injection profile can spoil that simplification near the terminals. The cancellation also exposes the sign. For electrons \(q<0\), positive conventional current means that the mobile charge drifts in \(-x\). The inferred transverse field is negative for positive \(B\) in the chosen convention.

Reconstruct density and mobility from separate observations

Take \(t=0.50\) mm, \(I=5.0\) mA, \(B=0.20\) T and a transverse signal of \(-1.25\) mV. The inferred signed Hall coefficient is \(R_H=tV_H/(IB)=-6.25\times10^{-4}\ {\rm m^3/C}\). Using singly charged electrons gives \(n=1/(e|R_H|)\simeq1.00\times10^{22}\ {\rm m^{-3}}\). A longitudinal voltage measurement, after correcting for probe separation, can yield the material conductivity. If \(\sigma=80\) S/m, the single-carrier mobility is \(\mu=\sigma |R_H|=0.050\ {\rm m^2/(V\,s)}\).

These operations use different pieces of information. The longitudinal measurement alone gives only the product \(n|q|\mu\). The transverse measurement supplies a sign and, in the one-carrier model, the density independently of mobility. This is an inverse experiment whose two observables have different sensitivities, not a more precise measurement of the same scalar.

One can derive the same result from a stationary Drude equation \(0=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)-m\mathbf v/\tau\). The longitudinal drag gives \(E_x=mv_x/(q\tau)\); force balance in \(y\) gives the transverse field just obtained. In a homogeneous single-carrier sample, \(E_y/E_x=q\tau B/m\), the signed Hall angle tangent. At fixed sample and field, doubling \(I\) doubles both voltages in this linear regime and leaves their ratio unchanged. Significant current-dependent apparent density can indicate heating or nonlinear contacts.

When a Hall density stops being a particle count

With electrons and holes, longitudinal conductivities add. In a weak magnetic field their transverse contributions also depend on squared mobilities. For positive mobilities \(\mu_e,\mu_h\) and electron and hole densities \(n,p\), the two-carrier weak-field coefficient in the sign convention above is \(R_H=(p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2)/[e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2]\). To see where the square appears, note that one factor of mobility converts the longitudinal field into drift; a second converts the resulting transverse magnetic force into a drift contribution. Summing species currents and imposing zero total transverse current gives the denominator management through the summed longitudinal conductivity.

Equal electron and hole densities with equal mobilities give zero Hall signal while current still flows readily. A single-species inversion would suggest unbounded density. At equal densities but unequal mobilities, the faster species can set the sign even though neither species is more numerous. Additional field dependence, temperature variation and independent structural information are needed before treating a Hall number as a carrier census.

When does a polar crystal change its preferred state?

Fields and the response of matter

Derive stable and unstable dielectric responses from a free-energy landscape. Separate a reversible small-signal capacitance from a switching experiment.

Why one voltage can support several measured polarisations

Some insulating crystals develop a spontaneous electric polarisation as temperature is lowered. The high-temperature phase may be symmetric under inversion so that states with polarisation \(+P\) and \(-P\) are equivalent in zero field. Near a continuous transition, treat one component of polarisation as an order parameter. A local free-energy density compatible with the symmetry is \(f(P)=a(T-T_c)P^2/2+bP^4/4\) with \(a,b>0\). The omitted terms limit how far in temperature or field this truncated expansion is reliable. The positive quartic term keeps the energy bounded when the quadratic coefficient is negative.

For a device whose applied electric field \(E\) is controlled, the appropriate potential in this simple model is \(g(P;E)=f(P)-EP\). The linear term records external work; changing what is held fixed changes the energy to minimise. Differentiation gives the constitutive equation \(E=a(T-T_c)P+bP^3\). An algebraic root is an equilibrium candidate. A stable local response also requires positive second derivative \(a(T-T_c)+3bP^2\). A very accurate rootfinder can return an unstable stationary point if this second test is omitted.

Find the equilibrium branches and their response

Introduce scaled polarisation \(p\), field \(e\) and temperature parameter \(s\) such that \(g=p^4/4+sp^2/2-ep\). Then \(e=p^3+sp\). For \(s>0\), the slope \(3p^2+s\) stays positive. Each field has one stable solution. At \(e=0\), \(p=0\) and the small-signal susceptibility \(dp/de=1/s\) increases when approaching the ideal critical point from above.

For \(s=-1,\ e=0\), stationary values are \(-1,0,+1\). The middle root has curvature −1 and is a local maximum. Each outer root has curvature 2 and is a minimum. The susceptibility about a selected outer state is \(1/2\) in the scaled units. The fact that the two domains can carry opposite zero-field polarisation does not make the harmonic response negative. The mere existence of a negative-slope segment in the cubic constitutive curve is insufficient to infer a stable isolated small-signal device with that differential response.

Derive the limit of a metastable homogeneous state

Imagine preparing the negative-polarisation state at \(s=-1\), then increasing the applied field slowly. A positive field lowers the energy of the positive state. The negative state can persist locally behind a barrier even after it ceases to be the global minimum. A model of perfectly homogeneous evolution without early nucleation follows this metastable branch until the curvature vanishes. Solve \(3p^2-1=0\), choose \(p=-1/\sqrt3\), and insert it into \(e=p^3-p\). The positive limiting field is \(e=2/(3\sqrt3)\simeq0.3849\). At the opposite spinodal the signs reverse.

This is a limit of the particular energy model. Real switching can occur earlier through domain walls, nucleation, defects or thermally activated barrier crossing. Observing a smaller coercive field in a specimen is not evidence that the cubic equation was solved inaccurately. To compare an actual hysteresis loop with a calculation, state the sweep rate, boundary circuit, sample preparation and a dynamical or nucleation model in addition to equilibrium free energy.

Turn a capacitance measurement into a consistency test

For a small harmonic voltage imposed about a prepared local minimum, the differential response is \(\delta P/\delta E=1/[a(T-T_c)+3bP^2]\), provided the driving frequency is slow enough for this local variable to respond and the amplitude is small enough to stay in the same well. Add the vacuum and fast polarisation terms when converting to total displacement \(D\). In a parallel-plate geometry capacitance per area then follows from \(\delta D/\delta E\) divided by thickness.

A large-signal charge–voltage measurement crosses different branches and may dissipate work around a loop. The energy per volume over a closed polarisation cycle is \(\oint E\,dP\) with the sign convention for electrical work. A single equilibrium graph of \(f(P)\) returns to its initial state with no prescribed loop area. Measured hysteresis thus carries dynamical information that a polynomial fit to one reversible branch cannot uniquely reconstruct.

Distinguish magnetic screening from electrical diffusion

Fields and the response of matter

Compare the frequency dependence, phase and thickness dependence of field penetration in a normal metal and a superconducting slab.

Perfect conductivity and magnetic expulsion are different statements

A normal conductor has current density \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E\). Combining Faraday's and low-frequency Ampère laws in a homogeneous medium gives \(\partial_t\mathbf B=(\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf B\). A step in field diffuses inwards over a length of order \(\sqrt{t/(\mu\sigma)}\). Under sustained sinusoidal drive the skin depth is \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\). As \(\omega\) tends to zero, this skin depth tends to infinity. An ordinary nonmagnetic conductor in its final static equilibrium generally admits a uniform applied magnetic field.

The London description of an isotropic superconductor in a Meissner state makes a distinct, equilibrium constitutive assumption: \(\nabla\times\mathbf J_s=-\mathbf B/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2)\), with penetration length \(\lambda_L\). In a homogeneous specimen carrying supercurrent and at frequencies where displacement current and normal losses may be neglected, \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J_s\). Curl once more and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). The resulting field satisfies \(\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2\). Unlike the diffusive skin depth, the equilibrium length remains finite for a static applied field.

Taking the formal limit \(\sigma\to\infty\) in a normal conductor suppresses the electric field for a finite current and can freeze a pre-existing flux during changes. That argument does not determine which static field a specimen adopts when cooled through its transition. Magnetic expulsion is extra thermodynamic physics. The comparison below assumes a weak enough field and a suitable state, omitting flux vortices, trapped flux and nonlinear or nonlocal effects.

Solve a slab with equal applied fields on both faces

Let a broad slab fill \(-d/2<x<d/2\), and impose identical tangential magnetic fields \(B_a\hat{\mathbf z}\) on its two faces. Symmetry requires \(B'(0)=0\). Applying both boundaries to the London equation gives

\[B(x)=B_a\frac{\cosh(x/\lambda_L)}{\cosh[d/(2\lambda_L)]},\qquad\]

\frac{\overline B}{B_a}=\frac{2\lambda_L}{d}\tanh\frac{d}{2\lambda_L}.

Differentiating gives the screening current \(J_y=-B'_z/\mu_0\). Currents flow in opposite directions at opposite faces; their net transport current across the thickness is zero. For a thick slab each face approaches a half-space with exponential decay. For \(d\ll\lambda_L\), the two surface layers overlap. Expanding the average gives \(1-d^2/(12\lambda_L^2)+\cdots\), so even a superconductor can have a substantial mean internal field in a sufficiently thin specimen under this parallel-field geometry.

Take \(d=100\) nm and \(\lambda_L=50\) nm. The centre field is \(B_a/\cosh 1\simeq0.6481 B_a\); the mean is \(\tanh1\simeq0.7616 B_a\). The distinction between centre value and volume average is already larger than many instrumental error bars. A measurement of magnetic moment in a finite non-slab geometry additionally requires its demagnetising field. Do not directly substitute the infinite-slab mean into an arbitrary sample-shape calibration.

Obtain the equilibrium length as an energy minimum

The same static profile follows from minimising \(\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}[B^2+\lambda_L^2(B')^2]\,dx/(2\mu_0)\) with boundary values imposed. The first term is magnetic field energy; the second is the energy associated with the screening current in this effective description. Vary \(B\) by a function \(\eta\) vanishing at the faces. Integrating \(B'\eta'\) by parts leaves an interior factor \(B-\lambda_L^2B''\). Its vanishing returns the London equation. This route makes the physical tradeoff clear: complete expulsion would require extremely sharp gradients and costly currents, while uniform penetration pays magnetic energy throughout the bulk.

The reduction assumes a fixed superconducting condensate amplitude. Near a critical temperature or critical field, allowing that amplitude to change introduces an additional coherence length and may alter both the field state and the validity of London theory. A numerical fit giving a stable value of \(\lambda_L\) at one temperature does not prove this approximation over an entire phase diagram.

Why rotating a layered dielectric changes a capacitance measurement

Fields and the response of matter

Derive the two principal permittivities of a fine laminate, rotate the constitutive tensor and distinguish the applied electrode condition from an assumed field direction.

Compare the same material in two orientations

Build a specimen by repeating layers with scalar permittivities \(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2>0\), occupying fractions \(f\) and \(1-f\). The period is much smaller than both the specimen scale and the spatial variation of the applied field. Suppose both materials are linear, free of mobile charge and effectively lossless in the measurement band. An observer using a large capacitor wants one law relating averaged displacement and electric field. A scalar average cannot describe both arrangements of the same layers relative to the plates.

Place the field tangential to all internal interfaces first. A narrow rectangular electrostatic loop straddling an interface gives continuity of tangential \(E\). Both layers see the same tangential field, but their displacements differ in proportion to permittivity. Averaging gives \(\overline D_t=[f\epsilon_1+(1-f)\epsilon_2]\overline E_t\). The tangent-direction effective permittivity is an arithmetic mean. This is a parallel connection of capacitances at shared voltage in the special capacitor geometry.

Apply a purely normal displacement instead. No free charge lies at the internal interfaces, so their normal displacement is continuous. Normal fields differ as \(E_i=D_n/\epsilon_i\). Averaging the voltage drops gives \(\overline E_n=[f/\epsilon_1+(1-f)/\epsilon_2]\overline D_n\), with a harmonic-mean effective permittivity. The local continuity condition changes which field is shared between constituents and thereby changes the relevant averaging operation.

Check the amount of anisotropy from field energy

For \(\epsilon_1=2\epsilon_0,\epsilon_2=10\epsilon_0\) at equal fractions, \(\epsilon_t=6\epsilon_0\) and \(\epsilon_n=(10/3)\epsilon_0\). The arithmetic-to-harmonic ratio is 1.8. A constituent does not need to be crystalline or anisotropic for the composite to show distinct principal responses. The geometry of the internal boundaries is enough.

Average the energy density for the tangential experiment, \(u=f\epsilon_1E_t^2/2+(1-f)\epsilon_2E_t^2/2=\epsilon_t E_t^2/2\). For the normal experiment, \(u=D_n^2[f/\epsilon_1+(1-f)/\epsilon_2]/2=D_n^2/(2\epsilon_n)\). These expressions supply a check independent of the capacitor-circuit analogy and identify which energy should be used when field or free charge is controlled. Energy positivity follows directly from the positive constituent permittivities. It does not require guessing an effective law from the largest measured component.

Rotate the law, then specify what the electrodes control

Use principal axes \(\hat n,\hat t\) and suppose a macro-field makes angle \(\theta\) to the layer normal. If \(\overline{\mathbf E}=E(\cos\theta\,\hat n+\sin\theta\,\hat t)\), then \(\overline{\mathbf D}=E(\epsilon_n\cos\theta\,\hat n+\epsilon_t\sin\theta\,\hat t)\). Project along the imposed field and divide by \(E\):

\[\epsilon_E(\theta)=\epsilon_n\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_t\sin^2\theta .\]

The perpendicular displacement is \(E(\epsilon_t-\epsilon_n)\sin\theta\cos\theta\). It is generally nonzero. A device that instead fixes the direction of averaged displacement while allowing a transverse field measures \(\epsilon_D(\theta)=[\cos^2\theta/\epsilon_n+\sin^2\theta/\epsilon_t]^{-1}\). These two directional responses correspond to different constraints. They agree at principal axes and for isotropic material. A pair of unspecified “angled capacitor” measurements cannot be interpreted uniquely without describing the contacts and edge conditions.

At 45° in the example, the field-controlled projection gives about \(4.667\epsilon_0\), while the displacement-controlled projection gives \(4.286\epsilon_0\). These are both legitimate scalar ratios from one symmetric positive-definite tensor. Neither supplies a universal scalar permittivity for the rotated material. The complete vector response also predicts displacement tilt and field energy for any orientation.

A grounded box on every side is a different boundary problem and can suppress the tangential response. Electrostatic homogenisation assumes the cell fields respond quasistatically. In a wave experiment near the Bragg gap, phase changes substantially across one period and a local constant tensor is inadequate. A finely layered capacitor and a dielectric mirror can be made from the same constituents while requiring different models at different frequencies. Test the Bloch spectrum to identify the regime where layering begins selecting propagation bands. A low-frequency effective index alone cannot predict that gap.

Large spatial contrast also places a practical demand on mesh and interface handling. A normal gradient can jump strongly even when potential and displacement are continuous. A smooth-looking interpolated voltage surface is weak evidence for an accurate energy density. How a magnetic field rotates the polarisation of transmitted light

Fields and the response of matter

Resolve a linearly polarised wave into circular eigenmodes, infer a refractive-index difference from an analyser and distinguish nonreciprocal rotation from a simple change of reference axes.

Derive rotation from propagation rather than drawing an arrow

Let monochromatic light propagate along \(+z\) through a transparent magneto-optic sample, with an externally prepared axial magnetic field. Define fixed laboratory vectors \(\mathbf e_\pm=(\hat x\pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2\) and use phasors \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\). These definitions specify the handedness algebraically. Calling a vector simply “right circular” without stating the viewing and time conventions can reverse a reported rotation sign.

An input linearly polarised in \(x\) is \((\mathbf e_++\mathbf e_-)/\sqrt2\). Suppose the medium supports wave numbers \(k_\pm=2\pi n_\pm/\lambda_0\) for the two bases, with negligible absorption. After a distance \(L\), factor out the common phase \(e^{i(k_++k_-)L/2}\). The remaining Cartesian phasor is \(\hat x\cos\Delta-\hat y\sin\Delta\), where \(\Delta=(k_+-k_-)L/2\). Thus the polarisation angle in the fixed \(x,y\) plane is

\[\theta=\frac{\pi L}{\lambda_0}(n_- - n_+).\]

Equal transmissions of the two components preserve a linearly polarised output. Unequal absorption makes the circular amplitudes unequal and generally produces an ellipse. Rotating a linear analyser then finds a finite minimum, even for a perfectly coherent field. Residual intensity at the nominal extinction angle can also arise from stray light or imperfect polariser extinction, which must be measured independently.

Derive a magneto-optic eigenbasis

For axial magnetisation, a simple lossless transverse relative-permittivity block is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\epsilon&i g\\-i g&\epsilon\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) with real \(g\) at the frequency of observation. Acting on \((1,i)\) gives \((\epsilon-g)(1,i)\); acting on \((1,-i)\) gives \((\epsilon+g)(1,-i)\). For relative permeability near one, \(n_+^2=\epsilon-g\), \(n_-^2=\epsilon+g\). At small \(g\) their difference \(n_- - n_+\simeq g/\sqrt\epsilon\). Reversing magnetisation reverses the sign of the off-diagonal coefficient and interchanges the two eigenvalues. Both the existence of circular modes and the sign-reversal test now follow from a constitutive model.

If \(n_--n_+\) is proportional to magnetic field over a specified range, write \(\theta=VBL\), with Verdet constant \(V\) in rad/(T m). This is a measurable material and wavelength dependent coefficient. It is not determined by the mean refractive index alone. A strong nearby absorption line can give substantial frequency dependence and dichroism, invalidating a lossless narrow-band extrapolation.

Work through an analyser calibration

For \(\lambda_0=600\) nm, \(L=0.20\) mm and measured index difference \(5.0\times10^{-4}\), the rotation is \(\pi(2.0\times10^{-4})(5.0\times10^{-4})/(6.0\times10^{-7})=\pi/6\), or 30°. An ideal analyser at angle \(\alpha\) transmits \(I/I_\mathrm{in}=\cos^2(\alpha-\theta)\). Its maxima occur at \(30^\circ\) and \(210^\circ\); an intensity measurement determines a linear polarisation angle modulo 180°. For a sample thick enough to rotate several half turns, an intensity maximum alone cannot give an unwrapped \(\theta\). Vary field or path length continuously from a known reference.

If the applied field is independently 0.40 T in that example, \(V=\theta/(BL)\simeq6.54\times10^3\) rad/(T m). The arithmetic is dimensionally correct, but the large value warrants a physical check of thickness, field calibration, units and material spectrum before reporting it as an ordinary glass coefficient. Degrees substituted for radians would make the inferred material coefficient too large by \(180/\pi\).

For the ideal Faraday medium at fixed magnetisation, the circular eigenmodes relative to a fixed laboratory basis are determined by its transverse material tensor. On propagation back through the sample, the accumulated positive propagation path again distinguishes the same laboratory circular basis after accounting for the reflection boundary. With a suitable simple mirror at normal incidence, the resulting material rotation doubles on a round trip in fixed transverse coordinates. In a reciprocal optically active material, the eigenvalue assignment with respect to the laboratory basis reverses on reversing propagation, and the single-pass rotation cancels on the ideal return. Write both transformations in one fixed basis before comparing signs.

A real double-pass setup includes mirror phase shifts, stress birefringence of windows and possible ellipticity. Reversing magnetic field, removing the sample and recording both analyser quadratures provide separate checks. A nonzero rotation in one transmitted beam proves only that its polarisation changed; establishing nonreciprocity requires comparing a reversed propagation experiment with a stated polarisation convention.

Indistinguishable particles and quantum gases

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Use single-state occupancy to derive Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions and estimate when classical gas statistics cease to apply.

From first principles

Derive the mean population of one quantum state

Consider a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\) exchanging particles and energy with a large reservoir at temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\). In a noninteracting gas, occupation \(n\) of this state changes \(E-\mu N\) by \(n(\epsilon-\mu)\). Its statistical weight is \(q^n\), where \(q=e^{-(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}\). For one specified state including its spin label, fermions allow occupations 0 and 1. Bosons allow every nonnegative integer,

\[Z_F=1+q,\]
\[Z_B=1+q+q^2+\cdots=\frac{1}{1-q}.\]

The Bose sum requires \(q<1\). Differentiating a weight with respect to \(q\) brings down its occupancy, so the normalized average is \(q\,\partial_q\ln Z\). Applying this to the two sums gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1},\]
\[\bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

For \(q\ll1\), both means approach \(q\); visits by additional particles to the same state are then rare. The difference between the distributions follows entirely from which occupation numbers the quantum statistics allow.

Derivation and applications

Derive the first exchange correction to ideal-gas pressure

Before quantum degeneracy becomes strong, exchange statistics already affect the relation between pressure and density. Consider noninteracting particles of mass \(m\) in a large three-dimensional box. All \(g\) internal states have the same energy and chemical potential. Introduce fugacity \(z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\), and a sign \(s=+1\) for bosons or \(-1\) for fermions. At small fugacity the occupation of a one-particle energy \(\epsilon\) is a geometric expansion,

\[\bar n(\epsilon)=z e^{-\beta\epsilon}+s z^2e^{-2\beta\epsilon}+O(z^3),\]
\[\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}.\]

Integrating the first term over momentum gives \(g/\lambda_T^3\) times \(z\). In the second term, doubling the inverse temperature narrows each Cartesian Gaussian by \(\sqrt2\). The density integral therefore gains a factor \(2^{-3/2}\). At fixed temperature the Gibbs–Duhem relation \(dp=n\,d\mu\) gives \(d[p/(k_BT)]=n\,dz/z\). Integration from the empty-gas limit gives the pressure series,

\[\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{3/2}}+O(z^3),\]
\[\frac{p\lambda_T^3}{g k_BT}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(z^3).\]

Experiments often fix density. Put \(y=n\lambda_T^3/g\) and invert the density series once to get \(z=y-s y^2/2^{3/2}+O(y^3)\). Substituting into the pressure eliminates the unmeasured chemical potential,

\[\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1-\frac{s}{2^{5/2}}\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}+O(y^2).\]

At fixed density exchange raises the pressure of fermions and lowers that of bosons. The magnitude grows on cooling because the thermal wavelength increases. Real interactions can also alter the pressure, and the truncated expansion requires small \(y\).

Worked example

Estimate a pressure excess for a dilute Fermi gas

A spin-balanced ideal Fermi gas has \(g=2\), \(n=2.00\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(\lambda_T=400\,\mathrm{nm}\). It has \(y=0.0640\) and \(p/(nk_BT)\simeq1.0113\). A measured classical pressure scale \(nk_BT=20.0\,\mathrm{pPa}\) would therefore acquire an exchange correction of about \(0.226\,\mathrm{pPa}\).

Second application

Recover density from a bosonic pressure deficit

A single-state Bose gas at known \(\lambda_T=500\,\mathrm{nm}\) gives \(p/(nk_BT)=0.9950\). To leading order the deficit fixes \(y=0.0050\times2^{5/2}=0.0283\). The inferred density is \(y/\lambda_T^3=2.26\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). At unchanged temperature halving this density halves the fractional pressure deficit, whereas the absolute pressure correction falls by a factor of four.

Decide which exchanges leave the state unchanged

An ideal classical gas can often be treated with Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. Cooling the gas or increasing its density eventually makes wave packets overlap appreciably. For indistinguishable quantum particles, merely assigning names to exchanged particles must not create extra states. In an ideal gas one can instead specify the occupation of each single-particle state. Identical fermions occupy a given quantum state at most once, including its spin label. Identical bosons can accumulate in the same state.

Use a reservoir that exchanges both energy and particles with the system. Its temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\) are fixed. A microstate with energy \(E\) and particle number \(N\) has grand-canonical weight proportional to \(\exp[-\beta(E-\mu N)]\). This follows by applying the thermodynamic entropy differential to a reservoir that loses both \(E\) and \(N\). The chemical potential sets the mean particle number through these statistical weights.

Sum the allowed occupancies of one state

For a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\), define \(z_\epsilon=\exp[-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)]\). An occupancy of \(n\) contributes weight \(z_\epsilon^n\). For fermions the single-state grand partition sum is \(1+z_\epsilon\), so the mean occupancy is \(z_\epsilon/(1+z_\epsilon)\). For bosons the sum runs over all nonnegative integers and equals \(1/(1-z_\epsilon)\), provided \(z_\epsilon<1\). Differentiating the logarithm of each sum with respect to \(\ln z_\epsilon\) gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad \bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.\]

These expressions describe one state, not the total population within an energy interval. To get the latter, multiply by the density of states and integrate. If the exponential is large then both occupancies reduce to \(e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\). The upper sign suppresses double occupancy while the lower sign enhances occupation near the lowest available energy.

Count the occupied sphere of a cold electron gas

In a large cubic volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries, allowed wave vectors fill reciprocal space with one state per volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) for each spin. At zero temperature the noninteracting electron gas fills all states inside a sphere of radius \(k_F\) and leaves higher ones empty. Including two spin states per wave vector gives \(N=2V(4\pi k_F^3/3)/(2\pi)^3\), so \(k_F=(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}\). For electrons of mass \(m_e\), the Fermi energy is \(\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m_e)\).

With density \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(k_F=1.33\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\simeq6.77\) eV. The associated temperature \(T_F=E_F/k_B\) is approximately \(7.9\times10^4\) K. At room temperature \(k_BT\) is small compared with this energy, so only a narrow region around the Fermi surface changes occupation noticeably. A classical equipartition estimate of the electron contribution to heat capacity would ignore this restriction.

Test the dilute limit with a cold atomic gas

A useful scale for translational wave packets is the thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\). For a single internal state the dimensionless product \(n\lambda_T^3\) compares the volume per particle with the thermal wave volume. Small values support the dilute classical approximation. Near unity the occupancy of low-energy states can become appreciable and the statistics of indistinguishability matter.

For particles of mass \(6.6\times10^{-27}\) kg at 1.0 K the thermal wavelength is approximately 0.88 nm. At density \(10^{25}\) m⁻³, \(n\lambda_T^3\simeq0.0068\). Reducing temperature to 0.040 K at unchanged density multiplies that product by \(25^{3/2}=125\), giving about 0.85. This is a degeneracy estimate, not a prediction of the phase of a real interacting gas. Once quantum statistics matter, particle interactions, dimensionality and confinement can also affect which phase the experiment actually finds.

Internal degeneracy enters these comparisons through the density per available internal state. Suppose the same total particle density is spread equally across two spin states with identical spatial dispersion. Each can fill the same momentum levels independently. In a cold ideal Fermi gas the Fermi wavevector at fixed total density is then smaller than in a fully spin-polarized gas. A measured pressure change after altering spin populations can therefore contain a statistical contribution even if the mass, volume and total particle count remain fixed.

Determine the capacity of excited Bose states

Ideal bosons can accumulate macroscopically in one spatial state. Consider a uniform three-dimensional noninteracting gas of identical spinless particles with fixed particle number in a large box. Set the lowest energy to zero. The excited-state occupation at energy \(\epsilon\) is \(1/[\exp((\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT))-1]\) and requires \(\mu\le0\). In the thermodynamic limit, counting momentum states in a sphere gives an excited-state density per volume proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\). Consequently, as the density rises, taking \(\mu\) toward zero from below can increase the excited population only up to a finite value.

Explicitly integrate \(d^3p/(2\pi\hbar)^3\) over positive energies \(\epsilon=p^2/(2m)\). At \(\mu=0\), setting \(x=\epsilon/(k_BT)\) reduces the integral to \(\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}/(e^x-1)dx=\Gamma(3/2)\zeta(3/2)\). The result is

\[n_{\rm ex,max}=\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\lambda_T^3},\qquad \lambda_T=\frac h{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}},\qquad \zeta(3/2)=2.6124\ldots .\]

Below the temperature where this capacity equals the total density, additional particles occupy the lowest state. Solving gives \(T_c=(2\pi\hbar^2/mk_B)[n/\zeta(3/2)]^{2/3}\) and \(N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^{3/2}\). The state count explains why a high phase-space density matters. Reaching this regime does not require individual particles to lose their identity at a sharply defined minimum separation.

Estimate the onset temperature in a box

Take \(n=1.00\times10^{20}\) m⁻³ and particle mass \(1.44\times10^{-25}\) kg. At the ideal transition the thermal wavelength is \((2.6124/n)^{1/3}=297\) nm. Inverting its definition gives \(T_c=h^2/(2\pi m k_B\lambda_T^2)=0.398\) μK. At \(T=0.250\) μK, the excited fraction is \((0.250/0.398)^{3/2}=0.498\) and the condensate fraction is approximately 0.502. Doubling the density raises \(T_c\) by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\) at fixed particle mass.

The predicted fraction tends to zero continuously as \(T\) approaches \(T_c\) from below, and tends to one as temperature approaches zero for the ideal gas. A harmonic trapping potential has a different density of states and hence a different temperature exponent from a uniform box. Finite size smooths the mathematical onset; interactions and inaccurate thermometry can shift its inferred location. Comparing the number in the narrow lowest-momentum component with the thermal-cloud population requires that the analysis resolve these two contributions rather than simply count all particles near the centre of a spatial image.

The dimensionality of the gas is as essential as Bose exchange itself. In a uniform two-dimensional ideal box the single-particle density of states tends to a constant at low energy. At chemical potential approaching zero, Bose occupation varies as \(k_BT/\epsilon\) there. Its energy integral consequently diverges logarithmically rather than approaching a finite excited-state capacity. The three-dimensional derivation of a nonzero transition temperature cannot then be carried across unchanged. A finite box, a confining trap or interparticle interactions can all alter the observed low-energy behaviour and must be identified when comparing different experimental geometries.

Repeat the counting in a harmonic trap

A harmonic trap changes the number of available excited states even for particles with the same mass and statistics. For three oscillator frequencies \(\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z\), measure energy above the lowest level and suppose \(k_BT\) greatly exceeds each \(\hbar\omega_i\). The quantum labels \(n_i\) are nonnegative integers with energy \(\sum_i\hbar\omega_i n_i\). The count below energy \(E\) is approximately the volume of the positive-coordinate tetrahedron bounded by this plane, namely \(E^3/(6\hbar^3\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)\). Differentiating gives an energy density of states proportional to \(E^2\).

At the onset of condensation integrate this density against \(1/(e^{E/k_BT}-1)\). The identity \(\int_0^\infty x^2/(e^x-1)\,dx=2\zeta(3)\) then gives

\[N_{\rm ex,max}=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar\omega}\right)^3,\qquad \bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}.\]

For \(N=1.00\times10^5\) particles in an isotropic 100 Hz trap, \(\hbar\bar\omega/k_B=4.80\) nK and \([N/\zeta(3)]^{1/3}=43.7\), so the predicted transition temperature is \(0.210\) μK. At \(0.150\) μK the condensate fraction is \(1-(0.150/0.210)^3=0.636\). An analysis using the uniform-box exponent at the same temperature ratio would instead give 0.396. The difference comes entirely from the confinement-dependent state count.

Reducing all three trap frequencies at fixed particle number lowers the onset temperature in direct proportion. This differs from enlarging a uniform box at fixed mean density, which leaves its bulk critical temperature unchanged. Specifying how particle number, volume and external confinement are controlled is therefore essential when interpreting a measured condensate fraction.

Vibrations and the Debye heat capacity

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Count vibrational modes in a solid and explain why a crystal’s low-temperature heat capacity follows a cube law.

From first principles

Derive the low-temperature heat capacity from acoustic modes

In a large three-dimensional crystal, long-wavelength acoustic vibrations have approximately linear dispersion \(\omega=vk\). Count allowed wavevectors inside a sphere of radius \(k\). Its volume grows as \(k^3\), so the number of modes below \(\omega\) grows as \(\omega^3\). The frequency density is its derivative. In a single-speed model normalized to the \(3N\) displacements of \(N\) atoms,

\[g(\omega)=\frac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3},\]
\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N .\]

Each mode stores mean thermal energy \(\hbar\omega/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]\). Multiply by the mode count and substitute \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) to expose the temperature dependence,

\[U_{\rm th}=\frac{9N(k_BT)^4}{\hbar^3\omega_D^3}\int_0^{\hbar\omega_D/(k_BT)}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx .\]

When \(T\ll\hbar\omega_D/k_B\), the omitted tail above the upper limit is exponentially small. The integral approaches a constant, giving \(U_{\rm th}\propto T^4\) and \(C_V=(\partial U/\partial T)_V\propto T^3\). The cube law relies on both three-dimensional mode counting and linear low-frequency dispersion.

Derivation and applications

Recover the Debye velocity from distinct sound branches

A three-dimensional solid usually carries one longitudinal and two transverse acoustic polarizations. Their speeds can differ substantially. Consider a large elastically isotropic crystal of volume \(V\), with linear dispersion \(\omega=v_s k\) for each polarization \(s\) in the thermally populated range. Impose periodic boundaries; one wavevector state occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For each polarization count all points in a sphere of radius \(\omega/v_s\), which includes opposite propagation directions. Differentiating gives the total density of modes,

\[N_s(<\omega)=\frac{V\omega^3}{6\pi^2v_s^3},\]
\[g(\omega)=\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi^2}\left(v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}\right).\]

A slower sound branch has more low-frequency states because the same frequency reaches a larger wavevector sphere. Define one effective velocity \(v_D\) to preserve this acoustic density of states. Requiring an isotropic monatomic Debye model to contain \(3N\) vibrations, with number density \(n=N/V\), fixes its frequency cutoff and temperature scale,

\[\frac{3}{v_D^3}=\frac{1}{v_L^3}+\frac{2}{v_T^3},\]
\[\omega_D=v_D(6\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\]
\[\Theta_D=\frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B}.\]

At low temperature the cutoff can be moved to infinity in the thermal energy integral. Changing variables to \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) uses \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\). A temperature derivative yields the directly measurable coefficient

\[\frac{C_V}{VT^3}=\frac{2\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3}\left(v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}\right).\]

The inverse cubes explain why replacing the three velocities by their arithmetic mean can noticeably underestimate the heat capacity. Optical or electronic contributions must be identified separately in specimens where they are thermally active.

Worked example

Predict the acoustic temperature scale

For \(v_L=6000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(v_T=3000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), the longitudinal branch contributes only \(1/17\) of the low-temperature coefficient. The effective velocity is \(3000(24/17)^{1/3}=3365\,\mathrm{m/s}\). With \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), the cutoff wavevector is \(1.44\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), giving \(\Theta_D\simeq369\,\mathrm K\). The long-wave estimates apply well below that scale.

Second application

Extract the transverse speed from calorimetry

Suppose a separate specimen gives \(v_D=2500\,\mathrm{m/s}\) from its low-temperature heat capacity and \(v_L=5000\,\mathrm{m/s}\) from a longitudinal pulse. Solving for the missing speed gives \(v_T=[2/(3/v_D^3-1/v_L^3)]^{1/3}=2.22\times10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}\). A shear-wave transit across \(10.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) should consequently take \(4.51\,\mathrm{\mu s}\). This timing test uses a distinct mechanical measurement of the same states counted by calorimetry.

What a solid can store

Consider an electrically insulating three-dimensional crystal of \(N\) atoms, volume \(V\), sufficiently cold that its structure remains fixed and normal-mode frequencies vary weakly with temperature. In a harmonic approximation displacements about equilibrium separate into \(3N\) independent oscillators. Their excitations are called phonons. Phonon number is not conserved when the solid exchanges heat, so each mode has a Bose mean occupation with zero chemical potential. The modes exist because the crystal can deform, including at very long wavelengths.

A model assigning the same frequency to every oscillator predicts an exponentially small low-temperature heat capacity, since exciting any mode would require a fixed nonzero energy. Acoustic vibrations instead approach zero frequency as their wavenumber approaches zero. Even in a cold macroscopic crystal, long-wavelength modes can remain thermally accessible. The key quantity is how many have energies of order \(k_BT\) or less.

Test the single-frequency model before introducing a spectrum

The premise of the Einstein solid is deliberately restrictive. All \(3N\) vibrational coordinates of a crystal are independent harmonic oscillators with one frequency \(\omega_E\). For one coordinate the levels are \(E_n=\hbar\omega_E(n+1/2)\). Write \(x=\hbar\omega_E/(k_BT)=\Theta_E/T\). Summing the geometric series gives \(Z_1=e^{-x/2}/(1-e^{-x})\). The partition function of the crystal is \(Z_1^{3N}\), because each normal coordinate can be occupied independently and phonon number is free to change.

Apply \(-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\), to obtain \(U=3N\hbar\omega_E[1/2+1/(e^x-1)]\). The zero-point energy contributes no heat capacity at fixed volume. Differentiating the occupation term and using \(dx/dT=-x/T\) gives

\[C_{V,E}=3Nk_B\,\frac{x^2e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}.\]

At high temperature \(1-e^{-x}\simeq x\), so the heat capacity per mole of atoms approaches \(3R\). At low temperature the denominator tends to one, and the surviving factor \(x^2e^{-x}\) measures the cost of the lowest available excitation. For \(\Theta_E=240\) K the same mole would have \(C_V=18.06\) J/(mol K) at 120 K, \(7.585\) at 60 K and \(0.536\) at 30 K. Halving temperature does not give a fixed ratio. The curve crosses from a classical plateau to thermally rare excitations.

A real three-dimensional macroscopic crystal supports collective vibrations whose frequencies approach zero with wavelength increasing. Replacing the single frequency by a distribution puts modes below any fixed nonzero thermal scale. The next calculation finds the density of those modes, and therefore a low-temperature power law. High-temperature agreement alone cannot distinguish the two models since both count the same total number of coordinates.

Count to a cutoff

Use a continuum approximation with three acoustic polarizations and a common sound speed \(v\). The modes in a shell of wavevectors grow as \(k^2dk\), as for photons. Real crystals generally have distinct longitudinal and transverse speeds. An effective Debye speed averages their inverse cubes. Converting via \(\omega=vk\) gives density \(g(\omega)=3V\omega^2/(2\pi^2v^3)\). The continuum would supply infinitely many modes, whereas a crystal with \(N\) atoms has exactly \(3N\). Choose an upper cutoff \(\omega_D\) to match that count,

\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N,\qquad g(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3 .\]

This cutoff is an approximate representation of the lattice at high wavenumber. The low-temperature prediction depends on the long-wave acoustic spectrum and is insensitive to precise details near the cutoff. In a multi-atom cell additional optical branches change the intermediate-temperature behaviour, which cannot generally be reconstructed with a single effective speed.

Two limits and a measurement

The thermal energy is the integral of \(g(\omega)\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) over the band. Define \(\Theta_D=\hbar\omega_D/k_B\). When \(T\ll\Theta_D\), extend the upper limit of the dimensionless integral to infinity. The resulting energy scales as \(T^4\) and differentiation yields

\[C_V=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3.\]

At high temperature each mode instead approaches \(k_BT\) of thermal energy and the heat capacity tends to \(3Nk_B\). The acoustic model has the correct count by construction. The constant zero-point energy never enters either heat capacity because it disappears upon differentiation.

For one mole of atoms with \(\Theta_D=300\) K, the low-temperature prediction at 10.0 K is \(0.0720\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below the classical \(3R=24.9\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 20.0 K the cubic asymptote predicts \(0.576\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Measuring several temperatures and plotting \(C/T^3\) tests the constant coefficient directly. In a conducting solid an electronic term proportional to \(T\) can dominate at low temperatures. Separating that term through a plot of \(C/T\) against \(T^2\) allows the intercept and slope to reveal the two physically distinct energy stores.

Finite size eventually supplies a lower frequency cutoff related to the crystal dimensions, so the continuum counting argument presupposes a specimen large enough to have many wavelengths populated throughout the temperature interval.

Connect vibrations to thermal expansion

A purely harmonic crystal with force constants independent of volume can store vibrational energy yet has no vibrational mechanism for thermal expansion. Real vibrational frequencies usually vary as the lattice spacing changes. The quasiharmonic approximation treats each mode as harmonic at each volume while allowing its angular frequency \(\omega_j(V)\) to change smoothly. Define the mode Grüneisen parameter \(\gamma_j=-\partial\ln\omega_j/\partial\ln V\). A positive value means expansion softens that mode.

For one oscillator the Helmholtz free energy is \(F_j=\hbar\omega_j/2+k_BT\ln[1-e^{-\hbar\omega_j/(k_BT)}]\). Differentiation at fixed \(T\) gives \(\partial F_j/\partial\omega_j=U_j/\omega_j\), where \(U_j\) includes the zero-point energy and the mean thermal excitation. Since vibrational pressure is \(-\partial F/\partial V\), the chain rule gives \(p_{\rm vib}=V^{-1}\sum_j\gamma_j U_j\). A temperature derivative at fixed volume suppresses the constant zero-point contribution. The mechanical relation \((\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha_V B_T\) finally yields

\[\alpha_V=\frac{\sum_j\gamma_j C_{V,j}}{B_TV} .\]

Here \(B_T\) is the isothermal bulk modulus and \(\alpha_V\) the volume expansion coefficient. The mode sum weights each Grüneisen parameter by its thermally active heat capacity. A low-frequency mode that softens under compression can contribute negatively and can dominate in a cold crystal while high-frequency modes remain frozen. A single positive average parameter is therefore a material model rather than a thermodynamic necessity.

Infer a mode average from dilatometry

For a cubic crystal take measured molar heat capacity \(C_{V,m}=12.0\) J/(mol K), molar volume \(V_m=1.20\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol and bulk modulus \(B_T=100\) GPa. A measured linear expansion coefficient \(6.00\times10^{-6}\) K⁻¹ corresponds to \(\alpha_V\simeq3\alpha_L=1.80\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹. The effective heat-capacity-weighted parameter is then \(\bar\gamma=\alpha_VB_TV_m/C_{V,m}=1.80\). The factor of three comes from differentiating the cube of a length, not from counting acoustic polarizations again.

If the same positive average and bulk modulus remain approximately constant down to a regime where phonons give \(C_V\propto T^3\), the volume expansion coefficient should also fall as \(T^3\). Doubling temperature there increases the coefficient by about eight, although the accumulated length change requires integrating it over temperature. A comparison of \(\alpha_V/C_V\) at several temperatures can test whether the active modes really share a common volume sensitivity. Molar heat capacity and molar volume must be taken on the same chemical counting basis before inferring any dimensionless Grüneisen parameter.

Hydrostatic pressure spectroscopy gives an independent way to measure a mode parameter. By the chain rule, the definition yields \(\gamma_j=B_T(\partial\ln\omega_j/\partial p)_T\) within the quasiharmonic description. For the numerical bulk modulus, a mode with parameter 1.80 would increase its frequency by approximately 1.8 percent under a 1.00 GPa hydrostatic pressure increment. Agreement between this slope and the value inferred from expansion requires that the probed mode be representative of the thermally populated spectrum. A strong optical mode visible to a spectrometer need not dominate low-temperature heat capacity or expansion.

Quoted pressure derivatives must therefore specify the mode, the temperature and whether hydrostatic or directional stress was applied in the measurement.

Extracting a length from thermal spin fluctuations

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

A one-dimensional row of magnetic moments favours local alignment. At low temperature a snapshot can contain a long row of identical spins. An exact calculation distinguishes a large correlated domain from a thermodynamic phase with permanent order.

Sum all configurations by multiplying a small matrix

Consider \(N\) classical spins \(s_j=\pm1\) on a ring with \(s_{N+1}=s_1\). At zero applied field the energy is \(-J\sum_j s_js_{j+1}\) with \(J>0\). The Boltzmann weight factors into nearest-neighbour terms \(e^{Ks_js_{j+1}}\), with \(K=J/(k_BT)\). Construct the matrix \(T_{ss'}=e^{Kss'}\), whose diagonal elements are \(e^K\) and off-diagonal elements \(e^{-K}\). Multiplying \(T\)'s automatically sums each intermediate spin over its two choices. Closing the first and last indices gives \(Z_N=\operatorname{tr}(T^N)\).

The symmetric and antisymmetric vectors \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) have eigenvalues \(\lambda_+=2\cosh K\) and \(\lambda_-=2\sinh K\). Thus \(Z_N=\lambda_+^N+\lambda_-^N\). At every finite positive temperature the ratio of eigenvalues is strictly below one. In the large-system limit the free energy per spin is \(-k_BT\ln(2\cosh K)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\beta\) yields the energy per spin \(-J\tanh K\).

Insert two local measurements

Multiplying an individual configuration by a measured spin corresponds to inserting \(S=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) in the matrix trace. To measure two sites separated by \(r\) bonds use \(\operatorname{tr}(ST^rST^{N-r})/Z_N\). In the eigenbasis of \(T\), \(S\) exchanges the symmetric and antisymmetric vectors. The numerator therefore contains one factor \(\lambda_-^r\lambda_+^{N-r}\) and one with the eigenvalues exchanged. With \(q=\lambda_-/\lambda_+=\tanh K\),

\[\langle s_0s_r\rangle_N=\frac{q^r+q^{N-r}}{1+q^N},\]
\[\langle s_0s_r\rangle_\infty=q^r=e^{-ra/\xi},\]
\[\xi=-\frac{a}{\ln q}.\]

Here \(a\) is the lattice spacing and the infinite-chain limit holds for fixed \(r\). The second finite-ring term comes from the other way around the circle. At equal sites the full ratio equals one. In zero field spin reversal makes the mean spin zero in the thermal distribution. Thus this correlation already equals the connected correlation.

Compare a chain at two temperatures

At \(k_BT=J\), \(q=0.7616\) and \(\xi=3.67a\). Spins ten bonds apart have correlation only \(0.0657\). At \(k_BT=0.5J\), \(\xi=27.30a\) and the corresponding correlation is \(0.693\). Lower temperature suppresses individual domain walls. Since \(s_js_{j+1}\) is +1 for alignment and −1 for a wall, its average equals \(1-2P_{\rm wall}=q\). At low temperature \(P_{\rm wall}\simeq e^{-2K}\) and \(\xi/a\simeq e^{2K}/2\). Even rare walls eventually destroy alignment in a sufficiently long chain.

Compare the finite ring with the infinite-chain result. Change the ring length while leaving the temperature fixed. When the correlation length becomes comparable to the circumference, the long route contributes appreciably. Returning to the starting site must restore correlation one. A straight-line fit to the back half of a semilog finite-ring plot would consequently give a misleading decay length.

Why only a narrow shell of a Fermi gas carries heat

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Heating a filled Fermi sea does not give every particle a classical thermal energy. Deep below the chemical potential, nearby states are already occupied. Quantifying the accessible states predicts both the low-temperature heat capacity and the shift of the chemical potential at fixed particle number.

Convert a sharp Fermi surface into a narrow weight

For independent fermions with occupancy \(f(E)=1/[1+\exp((E-\mu)/(k_BT))]\), consider integrals \(I=\int_0^\infty H(E)f(E)\,dE\). Choose \(F(E)=\int_0^E H(s)ds\). Integration by parts gives \(I=\int_0^\infty F(E)[-\partial_E f(E)]dE\), since the boundary terms vanish. The derivative is a peak of width \(k_BT\) around \(\mu\). With \(x=(E-\mu)/(k_BT)\) the weight becomes \(w(x)=1/[4\cosh^2(x/2)]\).

For \(\mu\) many thermal units above the bottom and \(H\) smooth over the thermal shell, extend the lower limit to \(-\infty\) when expanding locally around \(\mu\). The weight has unit integral and is even, so the linear term in \(F(\mu+k_BTx)\) integrates to zero. Its second moment is \(\pi^2/3\), obtained by expanding \(w(x)=\sum_{\ell\ge1}(-1)^{\ell+1}\ell e^{-\ell x}\) on \(x>0\) and integrating \(x^2\) termwise. Since \(F''=H'\),

\[I=\int_0^\mu H(E)dE+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2H'(\mu)+O(T^4).\]

The first correction samples the slope of the smooth weighting function. If a band edge or singularity lies within the shell, replacing the lower limit and Taylor expanding are poor approximations.

Conserve the number of particles

For a three-dimensional parabolic dispersion the density of states is \(D(E)=A\sqrt E\). At \(T=0\), \(N=(2A/3)E_F^{3/2}\). At small \(T\), use the expansion with \(H=D\) and write the chemical potential as \(E_F+\delta\mu\), where \(\delta\mu=O(T^2)\). Subtract the zero-temperature count. To quadratic order \(0=D(E_F)\delta\mu+(\pi^2/6)(k_BT)^2D'(E_F)\). Because \(D'/D=1/(2E_F)\),

\[\mu/E_F=1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}(T/T_F)^2+O[(T/T_F)^4],\]
\[T_F=E_F/k_B.\]

The increasing density of states offers more available levels just above the Fermi energy than just below it. The chemical potential shifts down to preserve the count. At \(T/T_F=0.10\) the predicted shift is \(-0.00822E_F\). For an ideal gas with \(E_F=5.0\,\mathrm{eV}\), this particular reduced temperature corresponds to about 5800 K, so reduced temperature and ordinary laboratory temperature should be distinguished.

Differentiate the energy at fixed number

Use \(H=ED\) in the same integral, including the just-derived shift of \(\mu\). The boundary shift contributes \(E_FD(E_F)\delta\mu\). In the Sommerfeld term the derivative is \(D(E_F)+E_FD'(E_F)\). The terms involving the slope of \(D\) cancel after fixing number. Thus \(U-U_0=(\pi^2/6)D(E_F)(k_BT)^2\), and \(C_V=(\pi^2/3)D(E_F)k_B^2T\). In three dimensions \(D(E_F)=3N/(2E_F)\), giving \(C_V/(Nk_B)=(\pi^2/2)T/T_F\). This is an electronic contribution for independent particles. Other degrees of freedom such as phonons can dominate a measured material heat capacity.

The experiment reconstructs \(\mu\) by numerical integration and bisection at fixed number. It overlays the asymptotic prediction, allowing the fractional error to be checked before using it at larger \(T/T_F\). The quadrature uses the momentum-like substitution \(E/E_F=y^2\) to remove the square-root endpoint singularity.

Mean-field ordering, response and the critical point

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Interactions can make a magnet favour a nonzero moment even when the applied field vanishes. Averaging the local exchange field gives a solvable model whose critical predictions can be compared with the exact one-dimensional chain.

Build the free energy from the count of spin states

Take \(N\) spins \(s_i=\pm1\), nearest-neighbour energy \(-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\) and Zeeman energy \(-h\sum_i s_i\), where \(h\) has energy units. Denote mean spin by \(m\). The fractions of positive and negative spins are \(p_\pm=(1\pm m)/2\). The binomial count and Stirling's approximation give entropy per spin \(-k_B(p_+\ln p_++p_-\ln p_-)\). Independent-neighbour mean field replaces each pair average by \(m^2\). With \(z\) neighbours and each bond counted once, interaction energy per spin becomes \(-zJm^2/2\).

\[f(m)=-\tfrac12zJm^2-hm+k_BT[p_+\ln p_++p_-\ln p_-].\]

Differentiate at fixed \(T,h\). The entropy contribution has derivative \(k_BT\operatorname{atanh}m\), giving \(m=\tanh[(zJm+h)/(k_BT)]\). This equation can have three real roots, so an iterative solver returning a root alone is insufficient. The equilibrium phase minimises \(f\); the second derivative \(k_BT/(1-m^2)-zJ\) distinguishes local minima and maxima.

Expand about the loss of stability

Near zero magnetisation, \(\operatorname{atanh}m=m+m^3/3+\cdots\). Set \(T_c=zJ/k_B\) and integrate the expanded derivative to obtain

\[f(m)-f(0)\simeq\tfrac{k_B}{2}(T-T_c)m^2+\tfrac{k_BT}{12}m^4-hm .\]

At zero field and \(T>T_c\), \(m=0\) is stable. Below \(T_c\), symmetry gives two minima whose leading magnitudes satisfy \(m^2\simeq3(T_c-T)/T\). Thus the order parameter vanishes like \((T_c-T)^{1/2}\) close to \(T_c\). At \(T=T_c\) a small nonzero field instead gives \(m\simeq[3h/(k_BT_c)]^{1/3}\). These powers describe this model's limiting behaviour. For a finite sample symmetry makes the unbiased ensemble average zero if it samples both minima. Spontaneous magnetisation refers to the thermodynamic limit with an infinitesimal field selecting a sign.

Measure equilibrium field fluctuations with a capacitor

Fields and the response of matter

Derive an electrical noise measurement in two ways, determine what fraction reaches a finite-bandwidth detector and use agreement to calibrate an experiment.

A zero-average voltage can store measurable energy

Connect a capacitor \(C\) across a resistor \(R\) at a uniform absolute temperature \(T\), with no external drive. Ideal circuit equations predict that any deliberately deposited charge decays on time scale \(RC\). At thermal equilibrium, microscopic fluctuations continually exchange charge and energy with the capacitor. Symmetry gives zero mean voltage, but the stored energy involves \(V^2\). In the classical limit a quadratic degree of freedom has mean energy \(k_BT/2\), giving \(\langle V^2\rangle=k_BT/C\). The resistor sets the time scale of equilibration; equilibrium mean-square voltage does not depend on resistance in this ideal model.

Check the dimensions: \(k_BT\) is an energy in joules and capacitance has units coulombs per volt. Their ratio is volts squared. At \(T=300\) K and \(C=100\) pF the RMS voltage is \(\sqrt{k_BT/C}\simeq6.44\ \mu{\rm V}\). A voltmeter that averages the signed voltage for a long time reports a value near zero. Measuring the noise requires an RMS, variance or appropriately calibrated spectral measurement.

Derive the same variance from a spectral measurement

Use a one-sided voltage power spectral density per hertz at positive frequencies. The classical Johnson–Nyquist open-circuit result for a resistor is \(S_R(f)=4k_BTR\) V²/Hz. Its square root is an amplitude spectral density in V/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\), not a voltage variance. Use an equivalent series voltage noise source and the RC transfer function \(H(f)=1/[1+2\pi i fRC]\). A stationary linear system multiplies the voltage power spectrum by \(|H|^2\), so

\[S_C(f)=\frac{4k_BTR}{1+(2\pi fRC)^2},\qquad\]

\int_0^\infty S_C(f)\,df=\frac{k_BT}{C}.

The integral follows by \(u=2\pi RCf\) and \(\int_0^\infty du/(1+u^2)=\pi/2\). It furnishes a detailed check on the factor four, the use of one-sided density, the conversion from hertz to angular frequency and the amplitude-versus-power distinction. If your result disagrees by a constant factor of two or \(2\pi\), audit these conventions before assigning a new noise temperature.

For \(R=10\) kΩ, \(T=300\) K, the low-frequency amplitude spectral density is approximately 12.9 nV/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). The RC corner at \(C=100\) pF is \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\simeq159\) kHz. The open-circuit low-frequency density rises with \(R\) while the bandwidth shrinks in inverse proportion. Their integrated product gives the earlier resistance-independent variance.

Include a real observation bandwidth

Suppose a first measurement adds an ideal low-pass cutoff \(B\). The recorded fraction of equilibrium variance is \(F=(2/\pi)\arctan(B/f_c)\). Half of the noise variance lies below the RC corner. That may be surprising if the corner was introduced as the frequency at which the spectral density reaches half its zero-frequency value. These two properties coincide for this single-pole spectrum for different mathematical reasons.

At \(B=0.1f_c\), \(F\simeq0.06345\), so the recorded RMS is about 25.2% of the infinite-band RMS. At \(B=10f_c\), \(F\simeq0.93655\), giving about 96.8% of the RMS. In an experiment, taking a square root one step too early changes this comparison. A real detector transfer function replaces the sharp cutoff by an integral of \(S_C(f)|H_\mathrm{det}(f)|^2\).

In the classical regime spectral density and integrated variance are proportional to \(T\), while RMS voltage grows only as \(\sqrt T\). A plot labelled “noise” without units cannot distinguish these two dependencies.

A physical check and a calibration check

An RC time-domain experiment provides a second observable beyond integrated power. Under the same single-pole equilibrium model the voltage autocovariance is \(\langle V(t)V(t+\tau)\rangle=(k_BT/C)e^{-|\tau|/(RC)}\). Its variance at zero lag agrees with equipartition, and its decay time is obtained from the homogeneous charge-relaxation equation. Repeating the measurement with a known extra parallel capacitance lowers the RMS and lengthens the correlation time. Seeing only one of those changes suggests that the observed noise may include substantial amplifier input voltage or an independent background.

The classical approximation assumes \(hf\ll k_BT\) over the relevant band and a resistor that can be treated as frequency independent. A MHz room-temperature experiment is far inside this regime. At sufficiently high frequencies or cryogenic temperatures, a quantum noise model, an explicit definition of the measured ordering of the spectrum and the detector's energy exchange are needed. Extending an ideal classical white source to infinite frequency is only permissible as an approximation inside the convergent low-frequency RC calculation; it is not a microscopic model at arbitrarily high frequency.

From mobile ions to screening lengths and differential double-layer capacitance

Electrostatic field theory and measurement

A charged planar wall in an electrolyte can be studied without assuming a capacitance in advance. Diffusion and electrostatic drift first establish an ion cloud. The charge–voltage slope of that cloud is an experimental output of the model.

An electric field competes with entropy of mixing

Take a bulk reservoir containing mobile ions with charges \(+e,-e\), each at number density \(n_b\). The solvent is a uniform continuum of permittivity \(\epsilon\), the bath holds absolute temperature \(T\), and no reactions or Faradaic currents occur at the wall. At thermal equilibrium the electrochemical potential of each species is spatially constant. For an ideal dilute gas of particles it contains \(k_BT\ln(n_\pm/n_b)\) from mixing plus electrostatic energy \(\pm eV\). Set the bulk potential to zero. Equating the local and bulk electrochemical potentials yields \(n_\pm=n_b e^{\mp eV/k_BT}\).

At positive \(V\) the negative counterions concentrate and the positive coions are depleted. Total mobile charge is \(\rho=e(n_+-n_-)=-2en_b\sinh(eV/k_BT)\). Substituting into Poisson's equation and defining dimensionless \(\psi=eV/(k_BT)\) gives

\[\nabla^2\psi=\kappa^2\sinh\psi,\]
\[\kappa^2=\frac{2e^2 n_b}{\epsilon k_BT}.\]

The parameter \(n_b\) is a density of one species, not both together. For a 1:1 salt specified in mol/litre convert to formula units per cubic metre using \(1000N_A\) before substitution. For a mixture the linear screening coefficient generalises to \(e^2\sum_i z_i^2n_i/(\epsilon k_BT)\), whereas the full nonlinear right-hand side remains a sum of exponentials. An externally maintained reservoir fixes chemical potentials; a sealed very small cell can instead require separate number conservation for every species.

What the small-potential limit predicts and what it neglects

For \(|\psi|\ll1\), replace \(\sinh\psi\) by \(\psi\). Above a planar wall at \(z=0\) with prescribed potential \(V_s\) and \(V\to0\) far away, the bounded solution is \(V(z)=V_s e^{-\kappa z}\). With the normal into liquid \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\), the wall displacement is \(D_z(0^+)=-\epsilon V'(0)=\epsilon\kappa V_s\). For a conducting wall with no interior field this equals its free surface charge. Therefore \(C_{\rm diff}/A=d\sigma/dV_s=\epsilon\kappa\), dimensionally a dielectric layer of thickness \(\kappa^{-1}\). The ionic concentration actually varies continuously; it is not a plate of opposite charge located at that distance.

Integrating charge density \(-\epsilon\kappa^2V(z)\) over \(z>0\) gives \(-\epsilon\kappa V_s=-\sigma\). The screening cloud and wall together are neutral per area. The fraction of compensating charge within distance \(h\) is \(1-e^{-\kappa h}\). At one screening length the potential has fallen to \(1/e\) and \(63.2\%\) of the countercharge has accumulated. For a spherical bead curvature changes this simple relationship between potential ratio and enclosed charge. The ionic constitutive relation is the same, but the spatial Laplacian and collecting area differ.

With \(\epsilon_r=78.5,\ T=298\) K and \(1.00\) mM of a fully dissociated symmetric monovalent salt, \(n_b\simeq6.02\times10^{23}\) m⁻³ and \(\kappa^{-1}\simeq9.62\) nm. The linear diffuse capacitance is about \(0.072\) F/m². Increasing concentration fourfold halves the screening length and doubles this capacitance at fixed solvent response. Close to molecular length scales the continuum assumptions and concentration-independent permittivity become progressively questionable, so the square-root law should be tested in the intended concentration range.

Solve the nonlinear planar boundary problem

With planar symmetry \(\psi''=\kappa^2\sinh\psi\). Multiply both sides by \(\psi'\) and integrate from the remote bulk where \(\psi,\psi'\to0\). This gives \(\tfrac12(\psi')^2=\kappa^2(\cosh\psi-1)=2\kappa^2\sinh^2(\psi/2)\). Choose the branch \(\psi'=-2\kappa\sinh(\psi/2)\), which decays to zero for either sign of surface potential. The identity \(d\ln|\tanh(\psi/4)|/d\psi=[2\sinh(\psi/2)]^{-1}\) turns the separated equation into

\[\tanh\!\frac{\psi(z)}4=\tanh\!\frac{\psi_s}4\,e^{-\kappa z}.\]

The physical potential itself is not a simple exponential at finite bias. Its distant tail has the same decay length but extrapolates back to a different amplitude \(4\tanh(\psi_s/4)\) in dimensionless units. A far-field slope can therefore recover \(\kappa\), while a linear extrapolation of its intercept underestimates large surface potentials. The nonlinear solution reduces continuously to the linear one because \(\tanh x\sim x\).

At \(z=0\), using \(D_z=-\epsilon k_BT\psi'/e\) gives

\[\sigma(V_s)=\frac{2\epsilon\kappa k_BT}{e}\sinh\!\left(\frac{eV_s}{2k_BT}\right),\]
\[\frac{C_{\rm diff}}{A}=\epsilon\kappa\cosh\!\left(\frac{eV_s}{2k_BT}\right).\]

The first relation is odd in surface voltage and the differential capacitance is even for this symmetric salt model. Increasing voltage concentrates counterions and allows more charge per further voltage increment. For \(V_s=50\) mV at 298 K, \(V_s/(2k_BT/e)\simeq0.973\). The differential capacitance is about \(1.51\) times its zero-bias value, while the secant ratio \(\sigma/V_s\) is only about \(1.17\) times that value. A small AC perturbation superposed slowly about a DC bias measures the differential slope if ionic transport remains equilibrated.

An independent mechanical check of the nonlinear field

The ideal mobile ions have an osmotic pressure excess relative to the reservoir \(\Delta p=k_BT(n_++n_--2n_b)=2n_bk_BT(\cosh\psi-1)\). From the first integral the electrical normal stress magnitude \(\epsilon E^2/2\) equals this excess. Thus \(\Delta p-\epsilon E^2/2\) is spatially constant and vanishes in the bulk of the isolated-wall problem. One can derive the same result by differentiating the pressure: \(d\Delta p/dz=\rho E\) at equilibrium. The cancellation provides a sensitive joint test of the ion distribution and the potential gradient.

Confine electrolyte between two equal positively biased parallel walls instead. By symmetry \(E=0\) at the midplane but \(\psi_m\) generally remains positive. The constant excess normal pressure is then \(2n_bk_BT(\cosh\psi_m-1)>0\). Overlap of the diffuse layers produces a repulsive osmotic force per area in this mean-field model. With large separation the midplane potential tends to zero and so does that force. A solution built by naively superposing two high-voltage nonlinear wall profiles will generally violate the constant-pressure test.

Separate a near-wall compact layer from the diffuse response

A finite exclusion distance for ion centres near a real electrode can be modelled at first by a compact dielectric layer of capacitance per area \(c_s\). Its voltage drop is \(\sigma/c_s\); the diffuse layer starts at potential \(V_d\). For applied bias measured relative to the bulk and a chosen neutral reference, \(V_{\rm app}=V_d+\sigma(V_d)/c_s\). Differentiate with respect to surface charge:

\[\frac1{c_{\rm meas}}=\frac1{c_s}+\frac1{c_d(V_d)} .\]

The diffuse constitutive relation must use \(V_d\), not the entire applied voltage. For \(c_s=0.20\) F/m² and low-bias \(c_d=0.072\) F/m², the measured capacitance is about 0.053 F/m². Even as \(c_d\) grows, this simple series model cannot exceed \(c_s\). It offers a physically distinct explanation for a measured saturation, alongside finite ion size, adsorption, dielectric variation and other model extensions. A static capacitance measurement alone cannot uniquely distinguish all of them.

Use layer overlap to infer a mechanical force independently of capacitance

Consider identical parallel electrodes at \(z=\pm H\), held at the same small positive potential \(V_s\) relative to a salt reservoir in chemical equilibrium with the gap. Neither electrode is an ordinary reference for the other: a voltage meter connected only between them reads zero while each diffuse layer experiences a nonzero potential relative to the reservoir. Under the linear screening approximation the potential obeys \(V''=\kappa^2V\). Reflection symmetry enforces \(V'(0)=0\). Matching both wall potentials gives \(V(z)=V_s\cosh(\kappa z)/\cosh(\kappa H)\), so the midplane voltage is \(V_m=V_s/\cosh(\kappa H)\).

The magnitude of free charge per area on each inner electrode face is \(\epsilon\kappa V_s\tanh(\kappa H)\). At large \(H\) each plate recovers its isolated-wall charge. At small \(H\) the whole gap is nearly at \(V_s\), so very little normal field is required to maintain it and each facing surface carries less charge. This behaviour differs from two oppositely biased plates, whose nearly uniform internal field grows as their separation decreases. The reservoir reference and voltage symmetry decide which experiment is being performed.

To leading nonzero order in \(V_s\), the osmotic excess force per area between these identical walls can be found at the field-free midplane. There it is \(2n_bk_BT(\cosh\psi_m-1)\simeq n_b k_BT\psi_m^2=\epsilon\kappa^2V_s^2/[2\cosh^2(\kappa H)]\). Why use a term quadratic in \(V_s\) when the potential was found linearly? The pressure's zeroth-order excess vanishes and the first-order ionic density changes cancel between opposite species. Its first measurable correction is quadratic. The cubic error in the linear potential changes the computed pressure only at fourth order in \(V_s\), making the mixed expansion consistent.

For \(\kappa^{-1}=10\) nm, \(\epsilon=78.5\epsilon_0\) and \(V_s=5.0\) mV, at half-gap \(H=10\) nm the predicted repulsive pressure is about 36.5 Pa. At \(H=20\) nm it drops to roughly 6.14 Pa. The potential rose by only a few millivolts yet its ionic osmotic consequence extends across the entire overlap region. As \(H\) becomes large, \(\cosh^{-2}(\kappa H)\simeq4e^{-2\kappa H}\), giving exponential decay with the full separation \(2H\). This is a force measurement of the same screening scale measured independently by a voltage probe. Surface roughness, salt exclusion and adsorption can influence a real nanometre-scale force curve and should be distinguished from deviations in the bulk screening length.

If the electrode charges rather than their potentials are fixed, the wall derivative is prescribed. The even solution then has \(V_m=\sigma/[\epsilon\kappa\sinh(\kappa H)]\) in the linear regime, and pressure \(\sigma^2/[2\epsilon\sinh^2(\kappa H)]\). As \(H\) shrinks the linear approximation will eventually fail for fixed nonzero \(\sigma\), because \(V_m\) grows. Comparing the constant-potential and constant-charge formulas without adjusting their boundary conditions could suggest contradictory predictions where each applies to a different experiment.

Estimate how quickly the ion cloud can follow a capacitance measurement

Static Poisson–Boltzmann theory assumes ions are in equilibrium at each measured voltage. A time-dependent measurement can test that assumption. For dilute symmetric monovalent ions with equal diffusivity \(D_i\), small density perturbations obey the linearised number fluxes \(\mathbf j_\pm=-D_i[\nabla\delta n_\pm\pm e n_b\nabla V/(k_BT)]\), combining diffusion with electric drift. Assume no bulk reactions. Subtract the two continuity equations and multiply by \(e\), yielding a charge-current density \(\mathbf J=-D_i[\nabla\rho+2 e^2 n_b\nabla V/(k_BT)]\). Use \(\nabla^2V=-\rho/\epsilon\) in the bulk to obtain

\[\partial_t\rho=D_i(\nabla^2\rho-\kappa^2\rho).\]

A spatial harmonic of wavenumber \(k\) decays at rate \(D_i(k^2+\kappa^2)\) in the unbounded linear bulk model. The long-wavelength Debye charge-relaxation time is \(\tau_D=1/(D_i\kappa^2)\), also recovered by dividing permittivity by the dilute ionic conductivity \(\sigma_{\rm ion}=2 e^2n_bD_i/(k_BT)\). The total salt-density perturbation instead obeys ordinary diffusion because the leading positive and negative drift terms cancel. Fast local charge relaxation and slower redistribution of neutral salt are different observables.

For \(D_i=1.0\times10^{-9}\) m²/s and \(\kappa^{-1}=10\) nm, this local time is 100 ns, corresponding to an angular rollover scale \(1/\tau_D=10^7\) s⁻¹ or ordinary frequency about 1.6 MHz. Blocking electrodes in a cell of thickness \(L\gg\kappa^{-1}\) can display a slower charging time. Estimate its series resistance \(R=L/(\sigma_{\rm ion}A)\) and diffuse capacitance \(C\sim\epsilon\kappa A\) for one interface. Their product is of order \(L/(D_i\kappa)\). For \(L=100\) μm with the preceding parameters this is about 1 ms, orders of magnitude above the local bulk time. Two identical interfaces in series supply an extra numerical factor; finite reservoirs and unequal mobilities can introduce further scales.

A low-frequency impedance experiment interpreted as the equilibrium \(d\sigma/dV\) must therefore allow the actual circuit and cell to charge, not merely wait several local Debye times. Varying cell spacing helps distinguish a geometry-dependent charging time from an intrinsic screening-length change. This diffusion calculation and the static pressure calculation share the same \(\kappa\), giving independent checks on a physical fit to voltage, force and time-resolved measurements.

Check reservoir and continuum assumptions using actual particle numbers

The grand-canonical ion profiles implicitly allow the diffuse layers to draw particles from a large reservoir without changing the reservoir's chemical potentials. Whether this is reasonable can be checked with a dimensioned inventory. For a 1 mM monovalent salt, each species has about \(6.02\times10^{23}\) particles per cubic metre. A cell of area \(1\) mm² and depth \(100\) μm therefore starts with \(6.02\times10^{13}\) ions of each species. A surface charge density of \(1.0\) mC/m² on one plate amounts to \(6.24\times10^9\) elementary charges on that area. Even a conservative estimate in which each net countercharge requires one ion from the bulk uses only about \(10^{-4}\) of the available counterions.

Keep the same area and salt concentration but reduce the gap to 20 nm. Now only \(1.20\times10^{10}\) ions of each species occupy the cell initially. The same surface charge is comparable with half the inventory of one species. The remote-neutral-reservoir approximation is no longer automatically credible, especially if both walls attract counterions of the same sign. One must specify how ions and compensating charge can enter or leave, what the electrodes' chemical or electrical preparations enforce, and how much fluid actually belongs to the reservoir. An applied electrostatic boundary voltage fixes no ionic chemical potential by itself.

For a sealed cell write the equilibrium densities instead as \(n_\pm(z)=A_\pm \exp[\mp eV(z)/(k_BT)]\). The constants \(A_\pm\) must be chosen so that integrals of \(n_\pm\) over the physical volume equal the known numbers of each species. Substitution into Poisson's equation gives a coupled boundary problem with two additional global constraints. Adding a constant to \(V\) and appropriately rescaling \(A_\pm\) leaves the densities unchanged; electrical reference and chemical normalisation must be chosen consistently. A calculation holding both fixed bulk densities and exact sealed inventories can be overconstrained if diffuse layers hold appreciable salt.

The continuum approximation also makes a statement about scale. A cube of side one screening length near 10 nm at 1 mM holds roughly \(0.60\) ions of either species on average. This does not by itself invalidate a mean density for a large planar electrode, which averages over many lateral regions and thermal configurations. It does warn against interpreting the smooth one-dimensional density as a snapshot of a single nanometre-sized volume with a deterministic fractional particle. Force or voltage measurements with different lateral averaging areas can show quite different fluctuations even when their mean profiles follow the same equation.

As another self-check compare the predicted contact concentration with available molecular packing. The ideal theory gives \(n_{\rm counter}(0)=n_b e^{|\psi_s|}\). Starting from 1 mM, a surface potential near \(0.25\) V at 298 K would multiply concentration by roughly \(1.7\times10^4\), corresponding to tens of molar even before allowing room for solvent. A molecular model or a measured finite-size constitutive relation is then more appropriate for quantitative contact density. The failure is in extrapolating the dilute ideal entropy, not in Gauss's law or the electrostatic boundary condition. Source, solvent, reservoir and detector scales determine where the otherwise elegant analytic solution becomes an approximation to test.

Count the salt adsorbed in a planar diffuse layer

The total countercharge per area equals the negative surface charge. Separately calculate the excess number of ions of both signs over the neutral bulk value for a symmetric electrolyte at prescribed surface potential. Which quantity changes sign if the wall voltage reverses?

Derive and check the result

Define \(\Gamma=\int_0^\infty[n_+(z)+n_-(z)-2n_b]\,dz=2n_b\int_0^\infty(\cosh\psi-1)dz\). The integrand is nonnegative for either sign of wall voltage. For \(\psi_s>0\), substitute \(dz=-d\psi/[2\kappa\sinh(\psi/2)]\) from the first integral. Then \(\cosh\psi-1=2\sinh^2(\psi/2)\) makes the resulting integral elementary. The result, also valid at negative bias by symmetry, is \(\Gamma=(4n_b/\kappa)[\cosh(\psi_s/2)-1]\). It has units particles per area. The signed excess charge divided by elementary charge, in contrast, is \(-\sigma/e=-(4n_b/\kappa)\sinh(\psi_s/2)\).

For positive surface charge the negative counterion excess and positive coion depletion follow by taking half the sum and difference of these two measurements. At small \(\psi_s\), the net charge excess is linear in voltage while \(\Gamma\) is quadratic: the two ion species redistribute in opposite directions before appreciable neutral salt adsorption appears. Reversing the bias swaps which species is enriched but leaves the total ion-number excess unchanged. Thus a mass-sensitive and a charge-sensitive measurement give complementary responses even in the same equilibrium model.

At \(\psi_s=2\), \(\Gamma/[|\sigma|/e]=[\cosh1-1]/\sinh1=\tanh(1/2)=0.462\). The total excess count can be smaller than the magnitude of countercharge measured in elementary charges because some compensation occurs through removing coions rather than adding counterions. An optical concentration measurement that tracks only total dissolved ion count cannot be converted into net countercharge by multiplying by \(e\). Sampling both quantities, or calibrating the signal against the appropriate species, is needed to test the adsorption prediction. The derivation assumes the same dilute reservoir as the planar Poisson–Boltzmann solution and should not be applied unmodified to a cell with fixed small ion inventories.

Quantum mechanics

Match stationary waves across a potential discontinuity and use probability current to distinguish reflection, propagation and an evanescent field.

From first principles

Obtain reflected and transmitted probabilities at a potential step

Suppose a constant-mass particle reaches a flat potential step from the left with energy above both sides. In a region of constant potential the stationary Schrödinger equation has waves \(e^{\pm ikx}\) with \(\hbar^2k^2/2m=E-V\). Set the incoming amplitude to one and use asymptotic waves \(e^{ik_1x}+r e^{-ik_1x}\) at left and \(t e^{ik_2x}\) at right. Integration of the differential equation across the finite step makes the derivative continuous; the wave function itself is continuous as well:

\[1+r=t,\]
\[k_1(1-r)=k_2 t .\]

Solving the two linear conditions gives \(r=(k_1-k_2)/(k_1+k_2)\) and \(t=2k_1/(k_1+k_2)\). The current from the previous chapter for one travelling component is \(\hbar k|A|^2/m\) in its propagation direction. Ratios to incoming current therefore yield

\[R=\frac{(k_1-k_2)^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2},\]
\[T=\frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2},\]
\[R+T=1 .\]

Equal potentials give unity transmission. Near a right-hand threshold \(k_2\to0\), a finite boundary amplitude can coexist with vanishing transmitted flux because the outgoing velocity also tends to zero.

Derivation and applications

Match both faces of a finite barrier

A decaying wave inside a classically inaccessible interval does not alone determine its transmission. Boundary matching at the exit also contributes. Let \(V(x)=V_0\) for \(0<x<L\) and zero outside, with a particle of constant mass and \(0<E<V_0\) incident from the left. Define \(k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) and \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\). Set the phase reference at the exit so the outgoing wave is \(t e^{ik(x-L)}\). At \(x=L\), continuity gives both the exit value \(t\) and its derivative \(ikt\).

Inside, \(\psi''=\kappa^2\psi\). Propagate these exit data back to the entrance using the even and odd solutions:

\[\psi(0)=t[\cosh(\kappa L)-i(k/\kappa)\sinh(\kappa L)],\]
\[\psi'(0)=t[-\kappa\sinh(\kappa L)+ik\cosh(\kappa L)].\]

On the left write \(e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}\). Adding its value to its derivative divided by \(ik\) removes the reflected amplitude. Inserting the propagated data yields

\[2=t\left[2\cosh(\kappa L)+i(\kappa/k-k/\kappa)\sinh(\kappa L)\right].\]

Both asymptotic waves propagate in the same material, so their velocity factors cancel in the current ratio. Take the squared magnitude and use \(\cosh^2u=1+\sinh^2u\):

\[T=|t|^2=\left[1+\frac{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2(\kappa L)\right]^{-1}.\]

A barrier of vanishing width transmits fully. An exponential estimate at large \(\kappa L\) neglects the interface-dependent prefactor. Close to the top, the \(\sinh\) factor and its apparently singular coefficient must instead be taken to the limit together.

Worked example

Infer a width from a tunnelling measurement

At \(E=V_0/2\), \(k=\kappa\) and the result simplifies to \(T=\operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa L)\). A device with \(\kappa=2.00\) nm⁻¹ has \(T=0.250\). Thus \(\cosh(\kappa L)=2\) and \(L=\operatorname{arcosh}(2)/2.00=0.658\) nm. Doubling that width gives \(\cosh(2\kappa L)=2(2)^2-1=7\), hence \(T=1/49=0.0204\).

Second application

Approach the top from below

Let \(E\to V_0\) at fixed \(L\). With \(\sinh(\kappa L)\simeq\kappa L\) the transmission becomes \(T=[1+(kL)^2/4]^{-1}\). For \(k=3.00\) nm⁻¹ and \(L=0.500\) nm this is \(0.640\). A vanishing inverse decay length therefore does not guarantee complete transmission. Exactly at the barrier top the internal solution becomes linear and still has to match the slopes on both sides.

Build a scattering state

Send a narrow band of particles from the left towards a region where the potential changes. Far from the obstacle a stationary energy component has incoming and outgoing travelling waves. Such plane waves are not normalisable on the whole line, but their flux ratios describe what happens to a sufficiently long incident packet whose momentum distribution is narrowly centred. Assume a particle of constant mass and a real, finite, time-independent potential.

The stationary Schrödinger equation is \(-\hbar^2\phi''/(2m)+V\phi=E\phi\). Integrating it over a shrinking interval around a finite step gives continuity of \(\phi'\); a finite jump in \(\phi\) itself would introduce a more singular derivative, so \(\phi\) is continuous as well. These matching laws change for an ideal delta potential, an infinite wall or an effective mass discontinuity. Stating the model at an interface matters as much as solving the constant-potential regions.

Partly reflect above a potential step

Take \(V=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(V=V_0\) on the right. For \(E>V_0\), write \(\phi_L=e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}\) and \(\phi_R=t e^{iqx}\), with \(k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) and \(q=\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}/\hbar\). Matching gives \(1+r=t\) and \(k(1-r)=qt\). Thus \(r=(k-q)/(k+q)\) and \(t=2k/(k+q)\). A wave can reflect from a change of wavelength even if a classical particle has enough energy to proceed.

A right-moving component \(Ae^{ikx}\) carries current \(\hbar k|A|^2/m\). Consequently \(R=|r|^2\) while \(T=(q/k)|t|^2\), and direct substitution gives \(R+T=1\). For \(E=4V_0/3\), \(q=k/2\), so \(r=1/3\), \(t=4/3\), \(R=1/9\) and \(T=8/9\). Although the transmitted wavefunction amplitude is larger than the incident one, its reduced group velocity keeps the transmitted probability flux below the incident flux.

Follow an evanescent field

If \(0<E<V_0\), the right-hand wave number becomes \(i\kappa\) with \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\). The physical solution for a semi-infinite step decays as \(e^{-\kappa x}\). This single exponential times a constant has zero current, since its spatial derivative has the same complex phase. It nevertheless has finite density a short distance into the forbidden region. Matching at the interface now gives a reflected wave of unit magnitude with an energy-dependent phase.

A finite barrier differs because an allowed region begins again at \(x=L\). Both decaying and growing exponentials must be retained within the barrier, and their relative complex coefficient allows nonzero current. Match the wavefunction and derivative at both ends. For equal zero potentials on the outside the transmission probability reduces to

\[T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa L)}{4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1}.\]

The large-width dependence comes from \(\sinh(\kappa L)\sim e^{\kappa L}/2\). At fixed barrier height it is the exponential sensitivity to width that makes tunnelling useful as a distance probe. A single semi-infinite exponential fitted to density cannot by itself supply the finite-barrier prefactor.

Estimate the effect of a subnanometre change

For an electron with \(E=1.0\) eV and \(V_0=2.0\) eV, \(\kappa\simeq5.12\) nm⁻¹ and the prefactor of \(\sinh^2\) is exactly one. At width 0.50 nm, \(T=1/\cosh^2(2.56)\simeq0.0236\). At 1.00 nm, \(T\simeq1.43\times10^{-4}\). Doubling this subnanometre gap suppresses transmitted current by roughly a factor of 165 for equal incident current.

The calculation assumes a sharp rectangular barrier, one occupied incident energy and elastic coherent scattering. A measured electric tunnel current generally combines many available occupied and empty states across an energy window. A current ratio is therefore most directly compared with the single-energy exponential when barrier shape and occupation stay sufficiently similar across the distance change.

A more detailed test uses scattering from opposite sides of a real, static barrier at the same energy. Time-reversal symmetry implies equal transmission probabilities for a single channel, even when the spatial barrier profile is asymmetric. The two reflection phases can differ. If the exterior potentials differ, the transmitted amplitude ratios from the two sides need not coincide because their travelling states carry different current per unit amplitude. Normalizing the prepared and detected states to unit flux removes that ambiguity. Checking incident current against the sum of outgoing currents separately tests whether the model conserves probability.

Solve for scattering and binding at one narrow defect

A point interaction illustrates how the same Hamiltonian predicts both scattering and bound states. On an infinite line take \(V(x)=g\delta(x)\), with \(g\) having units of energy times length. This limiting model replaces a narrow feature when the wavelength of interest greatly exceeds its microscopic width. The wavefunction remains continuous, but integrating the stationary Schrödinger equation from \(-\eta\) to \(+\eta\) before taking \(\eta\to0\) gives the derivative jump

\[\psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-)=\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}\psi(0).\]

For a particle incident from the left, write \(\psi=e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx}\) on the left and \(te^{ikx}\) on the right. Continuity gives \(t=1+r\). Inserting both derivatives into the jump condition and defining \(\xi=mg/(\hbar^2 k)\) gives \(t=1/(1+i\xi)\) and \(r=-i\xi/(1+i\xi)\). The external speeds are equal on both sides, so \(T=1/(1+\xi^2)\) and \(R=\xi^2/(1+\xi^2)\). The sum is one for real \(g\). Transmission probability depends only on the magnitude of the interaction, but the complex phase of the transmitted wave distinguishes its sign.

For negative energies the exterior solution must decay as \(e^{-\kappa|x|}\). Its derivatives jump by \(-2\kappa\psi(0)\), giving \(\kappa=-mg/\hbar^2\). A normalizable state requires \(\kappa>0\), hence only an attractive defect binds. The bound energy is \(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)\). A one-dimensional narrow attractive defect can therefore support binding even when a scattering-probability measurement alone cannot distinguish its sign.

Compare two defects with the same transmission

Use electron mass and take \(|g|=0.0762\) eV nm. Since \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV nm², \(m_e|g|/\hbar^2=1.00\) nm⁻¹. An incoming wave with \(k=2.00\) nm⁻¹ has energy \(0.152\) eV and \(|\xi|=0.500\). Both signs of \(g\) transmit 0.800 and reflect 0.200 of the incident probability current. The transmission phases, however, are \(\mp\arctan(0.500)=\mp26.6^\circ\), a difference observable against a reference path.

For the attractive sign the decay length of the bound state is \(1/\kappa=1.00\) nm and its energy is \(-0.0381\) eV. The repulsive sign has no such state. At high incident \(k\) both become nearly transparent; at small \(k\) both produce nearly complete reflection for fixed nonzero strength. The order of these limits matters: taking \(g\) to zero first gives perfect transmission at any finite incident wavenumber.

An attractive defect with the inferred decay constant has normalized bound state \(\psi=\sqrt{\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa|x|}\). The probability outside distance \(b\) from the defect is \(e^{-2\kappa b}\) after integrating both tails. With \(b=1.00\) nm in the example this gives 0.135, despite each wave amplitude at that distance being \(1/e\) of its central value. A numerical calculation in a box can use this estimate to choose outer boundaries. Placing artificial zero-value boundaries only one decay length away would remove substantial tail probability and shift the apparent bound energy upward.

Increasing the box symmetrically and tracking the bound energy is more informative than merely refining the spatial mesh in the original box. Mesh convergence at fixed outer boundaries can certify a well-resolved solution of the wrong finite-domain physical problem.

Transitions under a timed drive

Quantum mechanics

Derive the line shape of a weak finite-duration drive and distinguish Fourier width from a system’s intrinsic lifetime.

From first principles

How the time profile of a drive becomes a transition amplitude

Assume an initially occupied eigenstate \(|i\rangle\) of a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H_0\), perturbed by a weak time-dependent operator \(V(t)\). Expand the state in its unperturbed energy eigenvectors and factor out their known phases. Inserting the expansion into the time-dependent Schrödinger equation cancels the unperturbed energy terms:

\[|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle,\]
\[i\hbar\dot c_f=\sum_n V_{fn}(t)e^{i(E_f-E_n)t/\hbar}c_n .\]

At first order the right side uses the initial amplitudes \(c_i=1,c_{n\ne i}=0\). Integrating from zero through pulse duration \(T\) gives

\[c_f^{(1)}(T)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^T V_{fi}(t)e^{i\omega_{fi}t}dt,\]
\[\omega_{fi}=(E_f-E_i)/\hbar .\]

A square nearly resonant sinusoidal drive has a slowly varying integrand proportional to \(e^{i\Delta t}\) plus a rapidly oscillating component. Keeping the slow term produces amplitude proportional to \(T\,\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta T/2)\) and probability proportional to its square. The approximation requires small population transfer; a longer resonant drive eventually requires coupled state amplitudes.

Derivation and applications

Why a strong resonant pulse stops increasing the transition probability

Perturbation theory predicts a probability growing as pulse duration squared on resonance. To follow the subsequent return of population, keep both amplitudes coupled. Consider an isolated pair of levels driven by a monochromatic field with a nearly resonant frequency. Use the rotating-wave approximation, justified when the detuning and coupling are both small compared with the carrier frequency. Let \(\Delta\) be the angular detuning and \(\Omega\) the resonant angular Rabi frequency. During a square pulse the rotating-frame Hamiltonian, up to an identity term, is

\[H_R=\tfrac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega\sigma_x+\Delta\sigma_z),\]
\[H_R^2=\tfrac{\hbar^2}{4}(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)I .\]

The square follows because the two Pauli matrices anticommute. In the exponential propagator the even powers of \(H_R\) therefore multiply the identity and the odd powers multiply \(H_R\). Sum the two Taylor series with \(\Omega_e=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\):

\[U(t)=I\cos(\Omega_e t/2)-i\,\frac{\Omega\sigma_x+\Delta\sigma_z}{\Omega_e}\sin(\Omega_e t/2).\]

Starting in the lower bare state, only \(\sigma_x\) contributes to the upper-state amplitude. Its squared magnitude is

\[P_e(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\,t}{2}\right).\]

On resonance a pulse area \(\Omega t=\pi\) transfers the entire population; twice that duration returns it to the lower state. At short times the leading term is \(\Omega^2t^2/4\), recovering perturbation theory. Detuning both speeds up the oscillation and reduces its attainable maximum. These two signatures help distinguish mistuning from a change in drive strength.

Worked example

Design a population inversion pulse

If \(\Omega/(2\pi)=2.00\) MHz, the resonant inversion time is \(\pi/\Omega=0.250\) microseconds. A \(0.125\) microsecond pulse gives \(P_e=0.500\). Holding the drive on for \(0.500\) microseconds instead gives zero in the ideal model. The source supplies and subsequently receives energy during a reversible oscillation.

Second application

Infer the mistuning from an incomplete oscillation

Suppose the same drive produces maxima of \(0.640\) and full population oscillations repeat every \(0.400\) microseconds. The period gives \(\Omega_e/(2\pi)=2.50\) MHz. The maximum gives \(\Omega/\Omega_e=\sqrt{0.640}=0.800\), recovering the calibrated \(2.00\) MHz drive. The detuning magnitude is \(\sqrt{2.50^2-2.00^2}=1.50\) MHz in ordinary-frequency units. A population trace alone leaves its sign undetermined.

How a time-dependent perturbation transfers population

For a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H_0\) the energy components of a state accumulate phases without changing populations. Let a weak applied perturbation \(V(t)\) act for a known interval. Prepare the unperturbed eigenstate \(|i\rangle\) at \(t=0\) and write \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle\). The interaction-picture amplitudes isolate changes beyond the free phases. Substitution into Schrödinger’s equation and projection onto \(|f\rangle\) give

\[i\hbar\dot c_f=\sum_n V_{fn}(t)e^{i\omega_{fn}t}c_n,\qquad \omega_{fn}=(E_f-E_n)/\hbar .\]

At first order retain \(c_i=1\) on the right-hand side. For \(f\ne i\) the resulting amplitude is the Fourier component of the perturbation at the transition frequency, \(c_f(T)=-(i/\hbar)\int_0^T V_{fi}(t)e^{i\omega_{fi}t}\,dt\). An energetically resonant drive still fails to populate a state if the coupling matrix element vanishes by symmetry. The operator describing the drive is as important as its frequency.

A rectangular pulse

Let \(V_{fi}(t)=V_{fi}\cos(\omega t)\) for \(0<t<T\), with an upward transition so \(\omega_{fi}>0\). Splitting the cosine into exponentials gives frequencies \(\omega_{fi}-\omega\) and \(\omega_{fi}+\omega\). Near resonance, for a pulse lasting many carrier cycles, the rapidly oscillating sum-frequency term contributes little. With detuning \(\Delta=\omega_{fi}-\omega\) the remaining integral gives

\[P_{i\to f}\simeq \frac{|V_{fi}|^2T^2}{4\hbar^2}\left[\frac{\sin(\Delta T/2)}{\Delta T/2}\right]^2 .\]

At exact resonance all contributions build with the same phase, so for a weak short pulse probability grows quadratically in time. The first zeros occur at \(|\Delta|=2\pi/T\), or ordinary-frequency detuning \(1/T\). The line shape measures coherent addition over the finite observation time. It can be broad even when the isolated energy levels have negligible natural width.

Calibrate a pulse

Take a 2.00 ms rectangular pulse with \(|V_{fi}|/\hbar=200\) rad/s. The resonant transition probability is \((200\times0.002/2)^2=0.0400\). At 250 Hz detuning the sinc argument is \(\pi/2\), leaving probability \(0.0162\). The first zeros lie 500 Hz on either side. Doubling pulse duration at the same coupling predicts four times the peak probability and half the first-zero spacing within the weak-drive approximation. A measurement can test both the area dependence and frequency scaling.

Once the predicted population is no longer small, depletion of the initial level removes the first-order approximation. For an isolated resonantly driven two-state system the rotating-wave solution is \(\sin^2(|V_{fi}|T/(2\hbar))\), describing coherent population transfer and return. Lifetime decay or field fluctuations introduce still other envelopes. Varying pulse duration at fixed drive strength distinguishes finite-time broadening from linewidths that persist as the pulse is lengthened.

The pulse envelope provides an additional independent control. Replacing the sudden rectangular turn-on and turn-off with smooth edges reduces spectral sidelobes because the Fourier transform of the smoother envelope falls faster away from resonance. Peak transfer at resonance, in the weak-drive regime, depends on the time integral of the drive envelope. Two pulses of different shapes can therefore have the same low-power resonant transition probability yet excite a nearby unwanted level with different probabilities. Specifying duration alone would be insufficient to reproduce such a measurement.

From a finite pulse to Fermi’s golden rule

An isolated resonant final state initially collects amplitude proportional to time. A dense set of final states can instead produce a nearly constant total transition rate. To see the change, let a weak drive connect one initial state to levels around energy \(E_\star=E_i+\hbar\omega\). Denote the matrix element of the resonant Fourier component by \(M\). For a cosine drive it contains the factor of one half. First-order time integration over a square pulse gives a phase times \((Mt/\hbar)\,\mathrm{sinc}[(E-E_\star)t/(2\hbar)]\), where \(\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\sin(u)/u\).

The different final states are orthogonal, so their probabilities add. If the density \(\rho(E)\) counts accessible final states per unit energy, and \(\rho\) and \(M\) vary slowly over the line width,

\[P(t)\simeq \frac{|M|^2t^2}{\hbar^2}\int \rho(E)\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{(E-E_\star)t}{2\hbar}\right)\,dE.\]

Set \(u=(E-E_\star)t/(2\hbar)\). Away from a band edge one can extend the integral across the line and use \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{sinc}^2u\,du=\pi\). One power of time cancels against the narrowing energy interval, leaving \(P(t)\simeq\Gamma t\) with \(\Gamma=2\pi |M|^2\rho(E_\star)/\hbar\). This intermediate-time result assumes many final levels within the line and little depletion of the initial state. For a small isolated system the long-time dynamics can resolve individual levels again.

As a numerical example, use \(M=0.500\,\mathrm{\mu eV}=0.000500\,\mathrm{meV}\), \(\rho=1000\,\mathrm{meV^{-1}}\) and \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{meV\,ps}\). The rate is \(0.00239\,\mathrm{ps^{-1}}\), or a transition probability of \(0.0239\) after \(10.0\,\mathrm{ps}\). The first zero lies \(2\pi\hbar/t=0.414\,\mathrm{meV}\) from resonance, spanning about 414 level spacings on each side for a locally uniform density. The small transition probability and dense spectrum are distinct checks on the approximation.

Separate phase accumulation from the drive pulses

A single spectroscopy pulse ties spectral resolution to its own duration. The Ramsey method separates two short coherent pulses by an interval of free evolution. Work with two isolated states \(|g\rangle,|e\rangle\) in the rotating frame of a stable reference. Approximate each pulse as short compared with the free interval and strong enough to rotate the Bloch vector by \(\pi/2\) about the same transverse axis. Ignore relaxation and pulse detuning during the rotations. Starting in \(|g\rangle\), the first pulse prepares \((|g\rangle-i|e\rangle)/\sqrt2\) for the chosen pulse phase.

Over free interval \(T\) the excited component gains relative phase \(e^{-i\Delta T}\), with \(\Delta=\omega_{eg}-\omega_{\rm ref}\). The second pulse mixes both paths to \(|e\rangle\). Using the rotation matrix \(R_x(\pi/2)=(I-i\sigma_x)/\sqrt2\) gives

\[c_e=-\frac i2(1+e^{-i\Delta T}),\qquad P_e=\frac{1+\cos(\Delta T)}2 .\]

A controllable phase on the second pulse shifts these fringes; on exact resonance two aligned pulses complete a \(\pi\) rotation and transfer all population. For a statistical mixture with the same half populations after pulse one, the two contributions to the final probability have no mutual phase and give one half regardless of delay. Thus the separated-pulse signal measures phase retained between pulses, even when no energy transfer takes place during most of the sequence.

Measure a small reference-frequency error

Take a free interval of 2.00 ms. Adjacent maxima occur at ordinary detunings separated by \(1/T=500\) Hz. The first minimum on either side occurs at 250 Hz. The separation of the two half-height points immediately surrounding resonance is \(1/(2T)=250\) Hz. These three figures refer to different linewidth or spacing conventions and should be identified before comparing experimental reports.

Set the second pulse phase so that the calibrated signal near zero detuning is \(P_e=[1+\sin(2\pi\delta\nu T)]/2\). A measured \(P_e=0.600\) gives \(\delta\nu=\arcsin(0.200)/(2\pi T)=16.0\) Hz on the branch nearest the reference. This setting is more sensitive to small signed frequency errors than the top of a fringe, where the first derivative vanishes. A first scan or a shorter free interval is needed to identify which repeated fringe is being tracked.

If the transverse coherence decays during free evolution, observed fringe amplitude is multiplied by the coherence factor, often approximated by \(e^{-T/T_2}\). Extending the delay narrows fringe spacing but eventually reduces contrast. Measuring two phase settings in quadrature can separate an actual phase shift from a loss of visibility due to decoherence or pulse-area errors.

The frequency sensitivity per detected particle can also be estimated. At half population an ensemble of \(N\) independent measurements has standard error \(1/(2\sqrt N)\) in the estimated excited fraction. On the central quadrature slope \(dP_e/d\delta\nu=\pi T\), giving frequency uncertainty approximately \(1/(2\pi T\sqrt N)\) for ideal contrast. With the 2.00 ms delay and ten thousand independent detections this is about 0.80 Hz for a single ensemble. Imperfect contrast reduces the slope, and an unknown phase offset adds a systematic error that collecting more particles alone cannot remove.

Separate detuning from shortened observation time

Start a two-level system in its ground state, apply a monochromatic drive of resonant Rabi frequency \(\Omega\), and neglect relaxation. In the rotating-wave approximation detuning \(\delta\) changes the oscillation frequency to \(\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}\) and reduces its maximum transfer.

Boundary conditions that carry a current around a ring

Quantum mechanics

An electron on an ideal narrow ring can sustain current in a stationary state. Work out how the topology of its configuration space and a magnetic flux through the hole select the allowed currents.

Recover the allowed state space from a boundary term

Wrap a coordinate \(s\) of length \(L\) around the ring. The points \(s=0\) and \(s=L\) label the same physical place. For a uniform one-dimensional kinetic operator, integration by parts shows that the difference between \(\langle\phi,H\psi\rangle\) and \(\langle H\phi,\psi\rangle\) is proportional to

\[[\,\phi^*\partial_s\psi-(\partial_s\phi)^*\psi\,]_{0}^{L}.\]

Periodic values and periodic first derivatives make this expression vanish for any two admissible states. They also make the probability current continuous at the join. A plane wave \(e^{iks}/\sqrt L\) then requires \(kL=2\pi n\) with integer \(n\). The labels \(n\) and \(-n\) have equal energy at zero flux but circulate oppositely. A real combination of the pair has zero net current. An energy measurement on its own does not tell us which combination was prepared.

Put flux through the hole

For a particle of charge \(q\), the kinetic momentum along the ring is \(-i\hbar\partial_s-qA_s\). Choose a constant tangential vector potential \(A_s=\Phi/L\) giving the required circulation \(\oint A_s ds=\Phi\). Define signed flux \(\varphi=q\Phi/h\). Periodic plane waves remain eigenfunctions, while their kinetic momenta and energies now change:

\[p_{\rm kin}=\frac{h}{L}(n-\varphi),\]
\[E_n=E_L(n-\varphi)^2,\]
\[E_L=\frac{h^2}{2mL^2}.\]

A gauge transformation can eliminate the constant \(A_s\) locally. It multiplies a wavefunction by a phase whose value after one circuit differs from its initial value by \(e^{-2\pi i\varphi}\) in this gauge. The transformed wavefunction must carry that twist at the join. Imposing both zero vector potential and unchanged periodicity would discard the physical flux dependence. Increasing \(\varphi\) by one reorders the spectrum by a shift of the integer labels.

Work out what a current measurement sees

One particle of velocity \(v=p_{\rm kin}/m\) goes around the ring in a time \(L/|v|\). The signed charge passing a fixed section per unit time is \(I=qv/L\). Differentiating \(E_n\) at fixed \(n\) gives the same result:

\[I_n=\frac{qh}{mL^2}(n-\varphi)=-\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial \Phi}.\]

Take \(\varphi=0.30\). In units of \(E_L\), the \(n=0\) energy is 0.09 and the \(n=1\) energy is 0.49. The currents in units of \(qh/(mL^2)\) are −0.30 and +0.70 respectively. A probe that selects the lower-energy branch here sees a circulating state even though its probability density is uniform around the ring.

At \(\varphi=0.50\) the two energies agree at \(0.25 E_L\). Following a given eigenstate through this crossing and allowing a system to relax into its lowest energy at every flux describe different experiments. An ideal isolated perfectly uniform ring conserves its angular momentum label. Weak imperfections or a reservoir can change the populations and modify the behaviour near degeneracy.

Inspect the neighbouring branches

Change the signed flux continuously. Compare the slope of the branch containing \(n=0\) with its current. Predict where the lowest pair changes ordering and check that the complete set repeats with unit flux.

Work through the solution

The canonical and kinetic wave numbers each halve because the integer label and signed flux are fixed. \(E_L\) scales as \(L^{-2}\), and so does the current unit \(qh/(mL^2)\). Energy and current both fall to one quarter. The flux period \(h/|q|\) stays unchanged.

A ring with fixed flux is enlarged to twice its original circumference without changing the particle mass. How do energy gaps and branch currents change? Does the location of a crossing in flux move?

Work through the solution

\(E_L=h^2/(2mL^2)\) falls by a factor of four. The current scale \(qh/(mL^2)\) falls by the same factor. The signed flux variable contains no circumference when total flux is held fixed, so crossings stay at half-integer flux. Holding the magnetic field fixed as ring area grows would instead change the enclosed flux.

At \(\varphi=0.50\), prepare an incoherent mixture with equal populations on \(n=0\) and \(n=1\). Calculate its current and energy. Can observing zero average current distinguish this mixture from zero flux?

Work through the solution

The currents are opposite, each of magnitude half the current unit, and the mean vanishes. Both energies are \(0.25E_L\). A zero-flux ground state also has zero current, but its energy is zero in the same calibration. Spectroscopy or a change of population separates the two cases.

Adiabatic following and a finite sweep through an avoided crossing

Quantum mechanics

Two unperturbed energies cross as a control rises. A nonzero coupling opens a gap. Whether a prepared particle follows the lower energy through the crossing depends on how quickly the eigenvectors rotate compared with the dynamical phase they accumulate.

Find the gap and the rotation rate

Let \(\hbar=1\) temporarily, with Hamiltonian \(H(t)=\begin{pmatrix}vt/2&g\\g&-vt/2\end{pmatrix}\), \(v>0\), \(g>0\). The fixed basis is called diabatic because each basis vector keeps its identity as the detuning \(vt\) changes sign. Squaring the Hamiltonian gives \([(vt/2)^2+g^2]I\); its energies are \(\pm h(t)\) with \(h=\sqrt{(vt/2)^2+g^2}\). The minimum gap is \(2g\). The corresponding Bloch axis points along \((g,0,vt/2)\); write its polar angle as \(\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(2g,vt)\).

Differentiating the angle gives \(\dot\theta=-2gv/[(vt)^2+4g^2]\). A spinor aligned with this axis rotates through half its geometric angle in Hilbert space. The magnitude of the off-diagonal derivative coupling is \(|\dot\theta|/2\), largest at the crossing where it equals \(v/(4g)\). Comparison with the minimum gap frequency \(2g/\hbar\) gives a local adiabatic ratio \(\hbar v/(8g^2)\). A slow sweep or large coupling suppresses transitions between instantaneous energy states. Following the lower adiabatic state exchanges the two fixed basis labels across the crossing.

Derive the fast-sweep scale independently

In the interaction picture of the diagonal part, the energy difference \(vt\) gives an accumulated relative phase \(vt^2/(2\hbar)\). First-order perturbation theory gives a transition amplitude proportional to \((g/\hbar)\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{ivt^2/(2\hbar)}dt\). The magnitude of this Fresnel integral is \(\sqrt{2\pi\hbar/v}\), obtained by continuing the convergent Gaussian integral from a positive real damping parameter. The leading probability to change fixed basis states is therefore \(2\pi g^2/(\hbar v)\), valid only when small. The exact infinite linear-sweep Landau–Zener solution resums this dependence: the probability of retaining the initial diabatic label is \(\exp[-2\pi g^2/(\hbar v)]\). It refers to endpoints asymptotically far from the avoided crossing with constant coupling and no decoherence.

For \(g=0.5\) and \(v=1\) in units where \(\hbar=1\), the infinite-sweep retention probability is \(e^{-\pi/2}=0.2079\). Halving \(v\) squares it to 0.0432. The slow sweep therefore gives greater transfer between fixed basis states while preserving the lower-energy identity more faithfully. This distinction prevents reversing the interpretation of a measured probability.

Evolve a finite preparation numerically

Prepare the instantaneous ground state at detuning \(vt=-10\) and finish at \(+10\). Over each short step freeze \(H\) at its midpoint. Since \(H^2=h^2I\), the constant-step propagator is exactly \(\cos(h\Delta t)I-i\sin(h\Delta t)H/h\) in the chosen units. The product is unitary up to roundoff; finite time resolution still changes accumulated phases. Compare the population of the original fixed basis state with its population in the instantaneous ground state. Their separation through the crossing directly shows where ideal adiabatic following fails. The calculated final population can differ from the infinite-sweep expression because of endpoint mixing and phases.

When a periodic dielectric has no propagating Bloch wave

Fields and the response of matter

Derive a band gap using two lossless transparent materials and distinguish evanescent Bloch propagation from absorption of optical power.

Construct a unit cell whose waves interfere

Stack two transparent, lossless, nonmagnetic dielectrics periodically in one dimension. Their refractive indices are \(n_1,n_2\), their physical thicknesses \(d_1,d_2\), and the period \(a=d_1+d_2\). Work at normal incidence, so polarisation directions are equivalent for isotropic layers. Each separate material admits a propagating wave at the chosen optical frequency, yet the periodic stack may not. The effect comes from coherent interface reflections, not from a negative refractive index or an absorptive constituent.

Describe the local field by its tangential electric and magnetic components. Propagation through one homogeneous layer connects them by a \(2\times2\) transfer matrix with diagonal \(\cos\phi_j\), off-diagonal terms proportional to \(iZ_j\sin\phi_j\) and \(i\sin\phi_j/Z_j\), where \(\phi_j=\omega n_jd_j/c\) and \(Z_j=Z_0/n_j\). Changing the chosen propagation direction or time convention may reverse the two imaginary signs together. The real trace and unit determinant of the cell matrix below are unaffected.

Read the dispersion relation from the trace

At an interface both tangential components are continuous in the absence of imposed sheet currents. Multiply the two propagation matrices to form one cell. Its determinant is one because each layer's determinant is \(\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi=1\). Thus the two eigenvalues are reciprocal. For a Bloch mode they are \(e^{\pm i k_Ba}\) on a pass band. Taking half the trace gives

\[\cos(k_Ba)=\cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2-\frac12\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}+\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)\sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2 .\]

When the right side lies between −1 and 1 a real Bloch phase exists. If its magnitude exceeds one, a decaying and a growing Bloch factor replace a pair of unit-modulus factors. For a bounded excitation of a semi-infinite stack choose the decaying direction. The attenuation per cell is \(\operatorname{arcosh}|\operatorname{trace}(M)/2|\). A lossless semi-infinite crystal in a stop band reflects incident power. Spatial attenuation of a Bloch amplitude in this region is compatible with zero volumetric heat production.

Obtain a quantitative stop-band width

Choose \(n_1d_1=n_2d_2=\lambda_0/4\). Writing \(s=\omega/\omega_0\), both layer phases are \(\phi=\pi s/2\). At \(s=1\) the half trace is \(-\tfrac12(n_2/n_1+n_1/n_2)\). Recognising \(\cosh(\log r)=(r+r^{-1})/2\) gives centre attenuation \(|\log(n_2/n_1)|\) per cell. For \(n_1=1.5,n_2=2.0\) this is 0.2877, so a decaying eigencomponent has amplitude factor 0.75 per cell. This statement about an eigencomponent should not be confused with the exact transmittance of a specific finite stack, which also depends on entrance and exit impedances.

The first gap edges obey half trace −1. With \(\cos^2\phi=1-\sin^2\phi\), solve for the edge sine or cosine. The two edge phases are symmetric about \(\pi/2\) and satisfy \(|\cos\phi|=|n_2-n_1|/(n_1+n_2)\). Hence the fractional angular-frequency width is \((4/\pi)\arcsin(|n_2-n_1|/(n_1+n_2))\), about 0.183 for the example. The lowest gap width tends continuously to zero as index contrast vanishes. A gap in a plotted numerical spectrum at \(n_1=n_2\) would indicate an implementation or plotting problem.

Measuring magnetic core loss by a hysteresis loop and by calorimetry

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A closed loop on a graph of magnetic induction against field encloses an area with units of energy per unit volume. A thermometer measures kelvins. Bring these two observations onto the same scale, account for the winding heat, and determine when the two experiments actually test the same material loss.

Infer the field and the induction from independent windings

Wind a toroidal specimen with a primary winding of \(N_p\) turns and a lightly loaded pickup of \(N_s\) turns. Let \(A\) be the representative iron cross-sectional area, \(\ell\) the mean magnetic path length and \(V_c=A\ell\) the magnetic volume. Neglect a gap and leakage initially. Ampère's law around a path through the core gives \(H\simeq N_pi_p/\ell\). This relation is accurate when the toroid is slender enough that the spread of path radii is small and the pickup current is negligible. In a thick toroid \(H(r)=N_pi_p/(2\pi r)\), so one point on a nominal loop can actually represent a range of local field values.

Faraday's law supplies the second observable. Choose winding polarity so the reported voltage is \(v_s=N_sA\,dB/dt\). Integrating the voltage gives the change in cross-section-averaged magnetic induction. The pickup cannot determine a constant induction offset, because static flux induces no voltage. A closed loop area is insensitive to adding a constant to \(B\) or \(H\), but a dc offset in the voltage integrates into a time-dependent ramp and prevents the reconstructed trajectory from closing. In steady periodic operation one can subtract the voltage mean over a complete period before integration. Applying that correction to a genuinely evolving magnetisation process would remove part of a real flux change.

Multiply the two measured field quantities in the appropriate order. The field work supplied per unit material volume on a small induction change is \(H\,dB\). The source term associated with the magnetising flux is \(i_p v_{p,{\rm induced}}\,dt\). Using \(v_{p,{\rm induced}}\simeq(N_p/N_s)v_s\), its value is \(i_pN_pA\,dB=V_cH\,dB\). Integrating around a settled cycle gives the magnetic loss

\[W_c=V_c\oint H\,dB,\]
\[P_c=fV_c\oint H\,dB.\]

The field can absorb positive work and return some of it later in the same cycle. The area measures the algebraic remainder. Reversing the polarity of a single signal flips the loop orientation. A passively heated specimen must receive positive net energy when signs are defined for power entering the core. Simply taking the absolute value of all instantaneous products would count reversible storage twice and can grossly overestimate the heat.

Work an elliptical loop from time-domain signals

Suppose a nearly sinusoidal field is \(H=H_p\cos\omega t\) and the induction lags it by a positive angle \(\delta\), \(B=B_p\cos(\omega t-\delta)\). Differentiate the latter and multiply by \(H\). Over one cycle \(\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\) averages to zero, and \(\cos^2\omega t\) averages to one half. The resulting mean power density is \(\omega H_pB_p\sin\delta/2\), giving \(\oint H\,dB=\pi H_pB_p\sin\delta\). With zero lag the two signals trace a line and the area vanishes, even if the peak values are large. A saturation-distorted loop need not be elliptical; the original time integral remains valid with synchronised data.

Take \(H_p=120\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_p=0.200\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta=12^\circ\). The energy per cycle and volume is \(15.68\,{\rm J/m^3}\). A toroid with \(\ell=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(A=2.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\) and \(V_c=40\,{\rm cm^3}\) dissipates \(0.627\,{\rm mJ}\) per cycle. At \(f=200\,{\rm Hz}\) its power is \(0.125\,{\rm W}\). With \(N_p=100\), the necessary current peak is \(0.240\,{\rm A}\). With \(N_s=50\), the pickup voltage has peak \(N_sA(2\pi f)B_p=2.51\,{\rm V}\). These last two predictions allow the raw oscilloscope traces to be checked before a calculated loss in watts is trusted.

For sampled periodic data use \(w=\sum_n(H_n+H_{n+1})(B_{n+1}-B_n)/2\), including the segment from the last point back to the first. This trapezoidal line integral computes an oriented polygon area. Plot the starting and ending points before adding the closing segment. Connecting widely separated endpoints by hand hides drift and can generate a fictitious wedge whose area depends on acquisition timing. Repeat at higher sampling rates to see whether corners or switching features carry appreciable extra area. When a periodic excitation has harmonics the time increment must resolve the highest significant harmonic in both measured signals.

Compare a thermal measurement with the magnetic area

Consider first a calorimeter that receives heat only from the core. Let the heat capacity of specimen, holder and sensor be \(C_{\rm eff}\). Let the leakage conductance to an ambient bath be \(G\), and let \(\Delta T=T-T_a\). The thermal balance \(C_{\rm eff}\dot{\Delta T}=P_c-G\Delta T\) predicts a steady offset \(P_c/G\) and an initial heating slope \(P_c/C_{\rm eff}\). A small thermometer lag or poor thermal contact can make the earliest measured slope misleading even though environmental losses are then small. A separate electrical heater placed where the core deposits its heat can calibrate the effective thermal response.

For \(C_{\rm eff}=20\,{\rm J/K}\), \(G=0.015\,{\rm W/K}\), and a \(2.0\,{\rm K}\) rise above ambient, the magnetic loss \(0.125\,{\rm W}\) would give a slope of about \(0.00477\,{\rm K/s}\). The heat already leaking to the bath is \(0.030\,{\rm W}\), too large to discard in a ten-percent comparison. If the winding is inside the same calorimeter, its Ohmic heat must enter the balance as well. With primary resistance \(1.50\,\Omega\) and the computed sinusoidal current, winding heating is \((0.240/\sqrt2)^2(1.50)=0.0432\,{\rm W}\). The expected slope of the combined package at the same temperature is now \(0.00693\,{\rm K/s}\). Subtraction of winding heat recovers the core result only when the estimated resistance represents the winding at its operating temperature and frequency.

A thermal estimate drawn from a whole interval is often more robust than a derivative from two adjacent temperature samples. Integrating gives \(Q_{\rm deposited}=C_{\rm eff}[\Delta T(t_2)-\Delta T(t_1)]+G\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Delta T\,dt\). Divide by the duration for mean power, then remove other measured heat sources. A drifting ambient must be recorded explicitly because the calorimeter stores energy relative to its own absolute temperature. If the core and coil equilibrate slowly with each other, a single fitted heat capacity can depend on the fitting time window. A two-temperature model or a longer dwell then supplies an independent diagnostic.

Separate reversible exchange from hysteretic and induced currents

A single measured loop at finite frequency gives the total energy dissipated by the core at that prescribed waveform and temperature. The loop can include irreversible domain processes, eddy currents and other rate-dependent effects. It does not by itself split these contributions. To probe their different scaling hold the peak induction and waveform shape fixed, then change frequency. Under a restricted low-frequency model the quasi-static hysteresis energy per cycle is approximately constant, while classical thin-lamination eddy-current power scales as \(f^2B_p^2\). Its energy per cycle therefore scales roughly as \(f\). Plotting the measured loss per cycle and volume against \(f\) tests whether such an intercept-plus-slope description fits the chosen range.

Suppose the area is \(12\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(100\,{\rm Hz}\) and \(20\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(300\,{\rm Hz}\), at the same peak induction. A straight line gives intercept \(8\,{\rm J/m^3}\) and slope \(0.040\,{\rm J\,s/m^3}\). It predicts \(16\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(200\,{\rm Hz}\), near the elliptical-loop calculation. Two points always determine a line. A third and fourth frequency, together with uncertainties, are required to test the law. Different losses can share similar frequency scaling over a short interval, and temperature-dependent conductivity can bend a nominally simple curve.

Use the pickup to reject the reversible part of terminal power

Measuring primary terminal voltage and current with a power analyser provides a separate electrical route to loss, but the wanted number can be a small difference between terms. The applied voltage contains winding resistance, induced magnetising voltage, leakage-inductance voltage and possible parasitic contributions. In steady periodic operation the energy stored in a fixed linear leakage inductance returns after each cycle, while winding resistance dissipates energy permanently. The secondary pickup preferentially measures the core-linked flux. Forming \(p_c(t)=(N_p/N_s)i_p(t)v_s(t)\) and averaging over a full cycle estimates core power without directly including the primary's copper voltage drop. It relies on both windings linking the same relevant flux and on negligible power drawn by the pickup.

For the elliptical example the primary magnetising voltage has peak \(5.03\,{\rm V}\) and leads the induction by ninety degrees. Its phase relative to the current is \(90^\circ-\delta=78^\circ\). The core branch's apparent power is \(V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\approx0.603\,{\rm VA}\); its mean dissipative part is about \(0.125\,{\rm W}\), and the reactive part is about \(0.590\,{\rm var}\). Only the mean part contributes net heating of the magnetic volume. Including the winding voltage adds about \(0.043\,{\rm W}\) of real power to the terminal reading. A simple product of peak current and peak secondary voltage, without the turns ratio, factor one half and phase factor, has no reason to agree with either measured loss.

Consider a nearly ideal core with the same peak signals but a lag of only one degree. Its loss is about \(10.5\,{\rm mW}\), while the much larger reversible power exchange is almost unchanged. A time offset of \(10\,\mu{\rm s}\) at \(200\,{\rm Hz}\) adds \(0.72^\circ\), causing a relative loss error of approximately \(72\%\) for that small lag. Calibrate the relative channel delays using a reference signal through the actual current transducer and voltage chain, including their filters. Adjusting traces on screen until a core loop appears narrow assumes the very loss one is trying to measure. A synchronous measurement over many periods suppresses random noise but does not remove a persistent delay bias.

Test reconstruction on a waveform with a third harmonic

Let \(H(t)=H_1\cos\omega t+H_3\cos3\omega t\) while \(B(t)=B_1\cos(\omega t-\delta_1)+B_3\cos(3\omega t-\delta_3)\). With these definitions \(\delta_n\) is the phase lag of the \(n\)th harmonic in its own sinusoid. Multiplying \(H\) by \(\dot B\) and integrating over an integer number of fundamental periods eliminates products of different harmonics. Each surviving harmonic contributes the number of its own cycles in one fundamental period. The area per fundamental period is therefore \(\pi[H_1B_1\sin\delta_1+3H_3B_3\sin\delta_3]\). This identity tests whether a numerical area calculation tracks the full waveform. It does not assume that a real material is linear; here the two signals are supplied as measured periodic functions.

Use the earlier fundamental values and add \(H_3=20\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_3=0.030\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta_3=30^\circ\). The third harmonic contributes \(2.83\,{\rm J/m^3}\) in each fundamental period. This represents an additional \(0.0226\,{\rm W}\) for the chosen specimen and frequency. A fundamental-only sine fit would miss nearly fifteen percent of the resulting total area. An apparent jagged loop can also arise from asynchronous sampling or an offset. Distinguish genuine harmonics by checking that they are phase locked to the drive and persist under a change in sampling rate and analogue bandwidth.
