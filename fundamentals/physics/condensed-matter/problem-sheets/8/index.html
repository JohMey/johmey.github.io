Why a crystal with three sound speeds has one low-temperature heat coefficient

Calorimetry measures a slope in a plot of heat capacity divided by temperature against temperature squared. Determine what it says about the elastic modes of a three-dimensional crystal and how an unnoticed electronic term changes an estimate of the sound speed.

Count states of one acoustic branch

Take an isotropic, periodic cube of volume \(V\). Each allowed wavevector occupies reciprocal-space volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). Derive the number of acoustic modes per unit volume and per unit angular frequency for a branch of speed \(v_s\). Decide whether an extra factor of two for waves travelling in opposite directions is appropriate.

Work through the solution

The number of vectors in a shell equals \(4\pi k^2\,dk\) divided by the reciprocal-space volume per state. The shell already includes opposite wavevectors. With \(\omega=v_s k\) the result for a single polarisation is

\[g_s(\omega)/V=\omega^2/(2\pi^2 v_s^3).\]

There are two transverse polarisations and one longitudinal polarisation in the isotropic solid. The weight of a slow transverse branch is large because many wavevectors can carry the same small energy.

Obtain the calorimetric coefficient without a fitted cutoff

At temperatures far below the acoustic cutoff, change variables to \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) and replace the upper limit with infinity. Derive \(c_\mathrm{lat}/T^3\), where lower-case \(c\) is heat capacity per volume. Why can the zero-point energy be omitted here?

Work through the solution

Thermal energy per mode is \(\hbar\omega/(e^x-1)\); its zero-point contribution is independent of temperature at fixed spectrum. Summing over the three branches and using \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) gives energy density proportional to \(T^4\). Differentiation produces

\[\frac{c_\mathrm{lat}}{T^3}=\beta=\frac{2\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3}\left(\frac{1}{v_L^3}+\frac{2}{v_T^3}\right).\]

The reciprocal cubes must be summed before taking a mean. The effective speed defined by \(3/v_D^3=1/v_L^3+2/v_T^3\) has units of velocity. With \(v_L=6000\) and \(v_T=3000\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) it is about \(3365\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). A direct arithmetic average would weight spectral phase space incorrectly.

Extract the lattice and electronic parts from two measurements

An isotropic metal has \(c/T=8.0\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}K^{-2}}\) at 2 K and \(20.0\) in the same units at 4 K. Assuming \(c=\gamma T+\beta T^3\), determine \(\gamma,\beta\). Predict \(c\) at 3 K and give a clear experimental sign of a missing contribution.

Work through the solution

Fit the straight line \(c/T=\gamma+\beta T^2\). The slope is \((20-8)/(16-4)=1.0\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}K^{-4}}\) and its intercept is \(4.0\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}K^{-2}}\). At 3 K predict

\[3(4+9)=39\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}K^{-1}}.\]

A reproducibly curved plot, or a slope depending systematically on the selected temperature window, invalidates at least one assumption. Magnetic excitations, a low optical branch or a thermal background can each introduce curvature. Two measurements alone cannot discriminate those possibilities.

Decide which sound measurement is worth improving

Change one sound speed at a time. Inspect the separate longitudinal and transverse contributions before inspecting their sum. If the transverse speed has a small relative bias, how does that bias propagate into the total lattice coefficient? How could an independent longitudinal measurement help?

Work through the solution

Differentiating \(v^{-3}\) gives a fractional contribution change of \(-3\,dv/v\). At the example speeds the transverse fraction of \(\beta\) is \(16/17\). A positive one-percent bias in \(v_T\) biases the total coefficient low by about \(2.82\%\) at first order. A positive one-percent bias in \(v_L\) changes it by only \(0.18\%\). One calorimetric slope can identify a weighted inverse cube, not two independent velocities. A measured \(v_L\) makes it possible to infer \(v_T\), provided that isotropy, the two-branch degeneracy and the temperature window are justified.
