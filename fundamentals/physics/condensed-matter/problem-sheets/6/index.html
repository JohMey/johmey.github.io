1. A magnetic profile below a transition

A thick superconducting half-space has \(B(120\ \mathrm{nm})=0.20 B(0)\) in a low parallel field. (a) Infer the penetration depth and predict the ratio at 240 nm. (b) If superfluid density falls by a factor four, how does the profile change at 120 nm, assuming constant mass? (c) Why could this estimate fail in a mixed state?

Use a length inferred from an amplitude

For an exponential \(\lambda=120/\ln 5=74.6\) nm. At double depth the ratio is

\[0.20^2=0.040.\]

A factor-four decrease in density doubles \(\lambda\); at the original depth the ratio becomes

\[\sqrt{0.20}=0.447.\]

Vortex penetration creates spatially varying fields and the measured signal may no longer describe screening from one flat surface.

2. Flux and a tunnelling spectrum

A thick ring encloses \(6.20\times10^{-15}\) Wb on a path with negligible current. A normal-metal–superconductor junction on the same material shows peaks at \(\pm0.90\) mV. (a) Estimate the flux integer for charge \(2e\). (b) Infer the gap and weak-coupling estimate of \(T_c\). (c) Identify two independent quantities a measurement could check.

Keep flux quantisation independent of the spectroscopic estimate

Dividing by \(2.068\times10^{-15}\) Wb gives approximately three flux quanta. The peak magnitude suggests \(\Delta=0.90\) meV. Using \(k_B=0.08617\) meV/K yields

\[T_c=1.80/(3.53\times0.08617)=5.92\,\mathrm{K}.\]

A magnetisation or heat-capacity measurement locates the transition independently; a scan over ring flux states tests the period without using the tunnelling interpretation.

3. Two missing signals with different mechanisms

A dielectric multilayer has a narrow optical transmission minimum. A separate metal film develops a low-temperature gap in tunnelling conductance. (a) Give a controlled change and a quantitative limit for the optical structure. (b) Give two independent tests of the superconducting interpretation. (c) Explain why small transmission alone cannot establish dissipation.

Test the mechanism that produced the missing signal

For the lossless two-layer stack, take both indices to \(n\) at fixed cell size. The first folded branches then meet at \(fa/c=1/(2n)\), with no forbidden interval. For the film, follow the tunnelling feature with temperature and check for low-field magnetic screening at the same transition. A lossless stack can reject transmission by reflection; testing absorption requires an energy balance including reflected power and other output channels.

Infer a Fermi surface from a transport measurement

A three-dimensional metal at low temperature has Hall coefficient −6.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m³/C and resistivity 2.00 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. Assume a single isotropic electron band with free-electron mass and spin degeneracy two. Estimate the carrier density, Fermi wavevector, scattering time and mean free path. Explain what a second mobile carrier species would change.

Worked solution

In the one-band Drude limit R_H = −1/(ne), giving n ≈ 1.00 × 10²⁹ m⁻³. Counting occupied states in a sphere with two spin states gives n = k_F³/(3π²), so k_F ≈ 1.44 × 10¹⁰ m⁻¹. The associated Fermi speed ℏk_F/mₑ is 1.66 × 10⁶ m/s.

The conductivity is 1/ρ = ne²τ/mₑ. Therefore τ ≈ 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁴ s and ℓ = v_F τ ≈ 29.5 nm. Multiplying k_F by ℓ gives about 423, comfortably larger than unity. The lifetime only becomes a distance after choosing the velocity of carriers at the Fermi surface.

For simultaneous electron and hole conduction, the weak-field Hall signal weights the densities with squared mobilities and with opposite signs. The longitudinal conductivity instead adds their positive mobility-weighted contributions. A small Hall coefficient can then arise from compensation. Inserting it into the single-band density formula would give a misleadingly large density.
