Chapter 1 · Translational symmetry and diffraction

Work backwards from a scattering pattern to the repeating translations and the atoms within one cell. Learn which structural information an intensity measurement loses.

From first principles

Recover a lattice spacing from phase addition

Illuminate a row of \(N\) equally spaced atoms with a coherent plane wave and observe weak elastic scattering far away. Each atom sees an incident phase and contributes an outgoing phase. Their difference depends on \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}\). With the origin at the first atom, let \(\phi=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a\) be the phase difference between neighbours displaced by \(\mathbf a\). Up to an overall sign convention the total amplitude is a geometric sum:

\[A=f\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{ij\phi}=f\,\frac{1-e^{iN\phi}}{1-e^{i\phi}} .\]

Here \(f\) is the amplitude from one atom. Use \(|1-e^{ix}|=2|\sin(x/2)|\) and take the squared magnitude. When \(\phi=2\pi m\), evaluate the original sum directly:

\[I\propto |f|^2\frac{\sin^2(N\phi/2)}{\sin^2(\phi/2)},\]
\[I_{\rm peak}\propto N^2|f|^2 .\]

For a three-dimensional lattice every primitive translation must meet this phase condition. The resulting peak positions identify reciprocal-lattice vectors. For several atoms in each cell the single-cell factor becomes \(\sum_s f_s e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r_s}\). Its squared magnitude can vanish even at a permitted reciprocal vector. An absent peak can therefore reveal interference inside the cell.

Derivation and applications

Extract atomic displacement from the loss of Bragg intensity

A lattice can keep the same mean spacing while its Bragg peaks lose intensity on heating. Consider a crystal of identical atoms whose displacements about their mean positions fluctuate during the exposure. Treat scattering in the single-scattering approximation. At a reciprocal vector of magnitude \(Q\), every mean lattice position supplies the same phase. The instantaneous displacement projected along the scattering vector, \(u\), adds a phase \(Qu\). If this component is Gaussian with mean zero and variance \(s^2\), averaging the complex amplitude gives

\[\left\langle e^{iQu}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s^2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2/(2s^2)}e^{iQu}\,du=e^{-Q^2s^2/2}.\]

The last equality follows by Fourier transformation of the normalized Gaussian. The coherent peak measures the square of this average amplitude. Squaring the instantaneous phase factor first would give unity and erase the effect of phase differences between sites. With \(I_0(Q)\) the coherent intensity of the perfectly stationary array, the Debye–Waller attenuation is

\[I_{\rm Bragg}(Q)=I_0(Q)e^{-Q^2s^2}.\]

The formula describes the sharp coherent contribution. The difference between the total averaged intensity and the coherent intensity appears in diffuse scattering. Stronger attenuation at large \(Q\) follows because an identical displacement spans a larger fraction of a scattering phase cycle. For corrected integrated intensities of the same reflection at two temperatures, taken at essentially the same \(Q\), the unknown stationary intensity cancels,

\[s_2^2-s_1^2=-\frac{\ln[I_2(Q)/I_1(Q)]}{Q^2}.\]

This comparison assumes unchanged occupancies, instrument normalization and negligible changes in the mean structure. For isotropic displacement \(s^2\) is one third of the mean squared three-dimensional displacement.

Worked example

Recover a change in mean-square displacement

A reflection at \(Q=50.0\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) falls from 10,000 to 8,000 corrected counts. Its projected variance increases by \(-\ln(0.800)/2500=8.93\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{nm^2}\). If its original projected root-mean-square displacement was \(0.00600\,\mathrm{nm}\), the new value is \(\sqrt{0.00600^2+0.0000893}=0.0112\,\mathrm{nm}\). Adding the displacements directly would use the wrong measured quantity.

Second application

Test the angular dependence with another reflection

A second specimen retains 90.0 percent of its low-temperature peak intensity at \(Q=30.0\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\). For the same displacement direction at twice that wavevector, the attenuation exponent quadruples. The predicted retention is \(0.900^4=0.656\). A corrected retention near 0.90 for both reflections would instead suggest a common change in illuminated volume or flux that this displacement model cannot explain.

What repeats in a crystal?

A one-dimensional chain with identical atoms every \(a\) has translations \(R=na\). Replacing every other atom by a different species doubles the primitive translation, even if the nearest-neighbour separation stays fixed. The set of equivalent cell origins is the Bravais lattice; the atoms placed relative to each origin form the basis. Choosing a larger unit cell is allowed but folds distinct waves onto extra branches. Count independent degrees of freedom per unit length as a check on any cell choice.

In three dimensions write \(\mathbf R=\sum_i n_i\mathbf a_i\). Define reciprocal vectors by \(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}\). Then \(\mathbf G=\sum_i h_i\mathbf b_i\) has \(e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1\) at every equivalent origin. These vectors have units of inverse length. For a rectangular lattice doubling a real-space period halves the corresponding reciprocal spacing. The first Brillouin zone consists of wavevectors closer to the reciprocal origin than to any other reciprocal point. Later we will label collective vibrations and electronic states inside this region.

Construct reciprocal space from the primitive cell

A diffraction experiment measures momentum transfer. To predict its possible values, ask which plane waves acquire the same phase at every translated lattice point. For a Bravais lattice with translations \(\mathbf R=\sum_i n_i\mathbf a_i\), the condition is \(\exp(i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R)=1\) for every integer choice of \(n_i\). It suffices to require \(\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_i=2\pi h_i\). A reciprocal basis therefore satisfies \(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}\) and generates all such vectors by integer combinations. In matrix form, if \(A\) holds the primitive vectors as columns, then \(B=2\pi(A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\). The transpose matters whenever the original axes are oblique.

For a triangular layer take \(\mathbf a_1=(a,0)\) and \(\mathbf a_2=(a/2,\sqrt3a/2)\). The constraints on \(\mathbf b_1\) first give \(b_{1x}=2\pi/a\) and then \(b_{1y}=-2\pi/(\sqrt3a)\). Similarly \(\mathbf b_2=(0,4\pi/(\sqrt3a))\). Verify that translating along \(\mathbf a_2\) leaves the phase of \(\mathbf b_1\) unchanged. Reciprocal vectors have units of inverse length.

How far can a wave vector move from the origin before an equivalent representative is nearer? The boundary shared with \(\mathbf G\) solves \(|\mathbf k|=|\mathbf k-\mathbf G|\), or \(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=|\mathbf G|^2/2\). The half-planes around the origin define the first Brillouin zone. Here the six shortest reciprocal vectors give a hexagon. With \(a=0.300\,\mathrm{nm}\) the shortest \(|\mathbf G|\) is \(4\pi/(\sqrt3 a)=24.18\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\), putting a face midpoint \(12.09\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) from the centre. The zone area is \((2\pi)^2/(\sqrt3a^2/2)=506.5\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}\), equal to one reciprocal primitive-cell area. Doubling all lattice lengths halves the distances in the diffraction pattern and quarters its zone area. These reciprocal positions follow from translation symmetry. The measured intensities also depend on what occupies each cell.

Add amplitudes before taking intensities

An elastic plane wave scattered from positions \(na+d_j\) accumulates a phase equal to the momentum-transfer wavevector \(Q\) times position. For \(N\) identical cells the amplitude factorises:

\[A(Q)=\underbrace{\sum_j f_j(Q)e^{iQd_j}}_{F(Q)}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{iQna},\qquad I(Q)\propto |A(Q)|^2 .\]

The last sum is geometric. Its magnitude is \(|\sin(NQa/2)/\sin(Qa/2)|\), with limiting value \(N\) at \(Qa=2\pi h\). Large finite crystals have narrow peaks of height proportional to \(N^2\). The separation to the adjacent first zero is \(2\pi/(Na)\); doubling coherent length halves this width. The basis factor \(F\) sets the relative integrated strengths for a given lattice. A finite instrument resolution also broadens measured peaks and should be characterised before inferring a sample size.

An extinction is evidence about the basis

Place two sites at \(d=0,a/2\), with real scattering amplitudes \(f_A=3\) and \(f_B=1\) held constant over the angular range. At \(G_h=2\pi h/a\), \(F_h=3+(-1)^h\). Even and odd orders have intensities proportional to 16 and 4 respectively, before angular or instrumental corrections. If both amplitudes become equal, the odd peaks vanish. If the two sites have identical chemistry as well as identical positions up to translation, a cell of length \(a/2\) would describe the structure more economically. Equal scattering amplitudes in a particular probe alone cannot establish chemical identity.

For incident wavelength \(\lambda\), with the total scattering angle \(2\theta\), \(|Q|=(4\pi/\lambda)\sin\theta\). At \(\lambda=0.154\) nm and spacing \(d=0.200\) nm the first Bragg peak therefore occurs at \(\theta=22.6^\circ\), or detector angle \(45.3^\circ\). A factor-of-two error in reading the detector would corrupt any inferred lattice constant.

What survives a change of origin?

Translating the entire basis by \(d_0\) multiplies \(A(Q)\) by \(e^{iQd_0}\). Intensity is unchanged, so diffraction cannot locate the absolute origin. More generally the phase of the amplitude is missing from a measurement of \(|A|^2\). Peak positions suggest candidate lattices, corrected intensities test a proposed basis and peak widths probe finite coherence or strain. These are different questions even when addressed with the same scan.

Derive the selection rules of a cubic powder pattern

A powdered crystal supplies many crystallite orientations, so a fixed detector angle can intercept every family of reciprocal planes with the appropriate spacing. For a cubic lattice with conventional cell parameter \(a\), reciprocal vectors have magnitude \(2\pi\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}/a\). Bragg's condition then gives \(\sin^2\theta=\lambda^2(h^2+k^2+l^2)/(4a^2)\). The sequence of squared sines can be used to index the pattern, but only reciprocal points with nonzero structure amplitude produce peaks.

For a monatomic face-centred cubic crystal, describe the lattice with conventional cubic coordinates and basis positions \((0,0,0)\), \((0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), \((\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)\) and \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)\). For identical atomic amplitude \(f\) their phase sum is

\[F_{hkl}=f[1+(-1)^{k+l}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{h+k}] .\]

If all indices are even or all are odd, every phase factor is positive and \(F=4f\). Mixed parity makes the sum zero. Thus the first allowed squared-index sums are 3 for (111), 4 for (200), 8 for (220), 11 for (311) and 12 for (222). These extinctions encode the centring translations; adding an internal basis to each lattice site can introduce additional missing peaks. Powder intensity also depends on multiplicity and on angle-dependent scattering factors, so the strongest peak need not have the smallest indices.

Infer a lattice parameter and a strain

Suppose powder peaks under wavelength 0.1540 nm occur at detector angles \(2\theta=38.48^\circ\), \(44.74^\circ\) and \(65.10^\circ\). Their squared half-angle sines are about 0.1086, 0.1448 and 0.2896. After division by the first they give \(1:1.33:2.67\), consistent with \(3:4:8\). Indexing the middle peak as (200) yields \(a=\lambda/\sin22.37^\circ=0.405\) nm. The other two peaks independently give the same value to the displayed precision, unlike an arbitrary assignment to successive integers.

Now let the (200) detector angle fall by \(0.100^\circ\) under load. Differentiating \(2d\sin\theta=\lambda\) gives \(\Delta d/d\simeq-\cot\theta\,\Delta\theta\). Here \(\Delta\theta=-0.0500^\circ=-8.73\times10^{-4}\) rad, hence \(\Delta d/d=+2.12\times10^{-3}\). That is tensile strain along the scattering vector. A single peak shift cannot establish an isotropic volume strain without further orientations. Multiple peaks can also separate a uniform scale error in wavelength from a genuine directional strain. In the unstrained limit the lattice parameter extracted independently from every correctly indexed family should agree within instrumental uncertainty.

Peak position and width respond differently to imperfections. If all sampled crystallites undergo the same fractional change of their plane spacing, their peaks shift together by the differential Bragg relation. A distribution of tensile and compressive strains can instead broaden the peak around an almost unchanged mean angle. A finite crystallite size can also broaden it, but its angular dependence across several reflection families differs from the leading strain broadening. Comparing several indexed peaks therefore gives more information than treating a wide single line as direct evidence for a particular mean lattice parameter.

Instrumental angle calibration should be checked with a reference powder so that a detector zero shift does not masquerade as a specimen strain.

Tell a finite-size minimum from a basis extinction

Eight identical cells contain sites at 0 and half a lattice period. Sum the eight cell amplitudes coherently, then multiply by the basis amplitude \(f_A+f_B e^{iQa/2}\). Here the form factors are real, positive and independent of angle.

Chapter 2 · Vibrations of monoatomic and diatomic chains

Derive normal modes from a local force law and identify the physical motion behind the acoustic and optical branches.

From first principles

Turn a local spring law into a phonon dispersion relation

Consider identical atoms of mass \(m\) spaced by \(a\) along a chain. Write \(u_j\) for the displacement from the equilibrium position of atom \(j\). For small changes of bond length, expand the bond energy to second order about its minimum. Its first derivative vanishes and its positive second derivative \(K\) is the measured bond stiffness. The bond on the right pulls with force \(K(u_{j+1}-u_j)\); the left bond contributes \(K(u_{j-1}-u_j)\). Newton’s law gives

\[m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}) .\]

Translation of a normal mode by one site can change its phase but not its shape. Insert \(u_j=Ue^{i(kja-\omega t)}\), divide by the common amplitude, and simplify:

\[-m\omega^2=K(e^{ika}+e^{-ika}-2)=-4K\sin^2(ka/2),\]
\[\omega=2\sqrt{K/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.\]

For wavelengths much larger than \(a\), \(\sin(ka/2)\simeq ka/2\). The slope gives the sound speed \(a\sqrt{K/m}\). At \(k=0\) all atoms move together and no bond stretches, which explains the zero frequency. More than one atom per primitive cell permits internal relative motion even at \(k=0\), the ingredient needed for an optical branch.

Derivation and applications

Count the modes that crowd against a chain’s band edge

Why can a narrow frequency bin near the top of a vibration band contain a substantial share of all modes? Take a monoatomic chain of \(N\) equal masses \(m\) with one longitudinal displacement each. Impose periodic ends and use identical harmonic nearest-neighbour springs of stiffness \(K\) with spacing \(a\). The frequency on the positive half of the first zone rises monotonically to \(\omega_m=2\sqrt{K/m}\). Inverting that branch gives the wavevector bounding the states below a trial frequency,

\[k(\omega)=\frac{2}{a}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_m}\right),\]
\[0\leq\omega\leq\omega_m .\]

Allowed wavevectors are spaced by \(2\pi/(Na)\). Counting the interval of width \(2k(\omega)\) includes both propagation directions. In a large chain the fractional count and its derivative per atom are therefore

\[F(\omega)=\frac{2k(\omega)}{2\pi/(Na)}\frac{1}{N}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_m}\right),\]
\[g(\omega)=\frac{dF}{d\omega}=\frac{2}{\pi\omega_m\sqrt{1-(\omega/\omega_m)^2}}.\]

Here \(g\,d\omega\) is a fraction of the modes. The normalization follows from \(F(\omega_m)=1\), as required by the count of independent displacements. Near the edge the slope of the dispersion tends to zero, so evenly spaced wavevectors map into progressively smaller frequency intervals. This causes an integrable singularity in the continuum density. A finite spectrometer bin always contains a finite count. A measured spectrum can also weight modes differently through a probe’s coupling to atomic motion.

Worked example

Count modes within the upper tenth of the band

For \(N=10{,}000\), the interval between \(0.9\omega_m\) and the upper edge contains \(N[1-(2/\pi)\arcsin(0.9)]\simeq2871\) modes, allowing a mode or two for endpoints. A uniform distribution in frequency would give 1000. A nearly stationary wave packet near the upper edge and a large number of modes per unit frequency have the same origin in the small group velocity.

Second application

Use the spectral median to infer a spring stiffness

An ideal chain with atomic mass \(5.00\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}\) has half of its longitudinal modes below an ordinary frequency of \(4.00\,\mathrm{THz}\). Setting \(F=1/2\) gives \(\omega_{1/2}/\omega_m=\sin(\pi/4)\). The upper frequency is \(5.66\,\mathrm{THz}\), and \(K=m(2\pi f_m)^2/4=15.8\,\mathrm{N/m}\). The frequency ratio is unchanged by conversion to angular frequency, but the final stiffness requires that conversion.

From bond extension to a travelling displacement

Let atoms of mass \(m\) on a line be coupled by springs of stiffness \(K\), spaced \(a\) apart. The spring to the right pulls site \(n\) by \(K(u_{n+1}-u_n)\); the left contributes \(K(u_{n-1}-u_n)\). A uniform shift produces no force. Translation symmetry suggests \(u_n=u e^{i(kna-\omega t)}\). Substitution gives

\[\omega^2(k)=\frac{2K}{m}(1-\cos ka)=\frac{4K}{m}\sin^2(ka/2).\]

The sound speed at long wavelength is \(a\sqrt{K/m}\), consistent with a continuum rod whose one-dimensional stiffness is \(Ka\) and whose mass per length is \(m/a\). At \(k=\pi/a\) neighbouring atoms move in opposition and the slope of the dispersion vanishes. A wave packet made of nearby \(k\)'s moves with the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\), which can differ greatly from the phase velocity \(\omega/k\) near the boundary.

Introduce an internal coordinate

Place masses \(m,M\) alternately, with spacing \(a/2\) between neighbours and cell period \(a\). Let their displacements in cell \(n\) be \(Ue^{ikna}\) and \(Ve^{ikna}\) times the same time factor. The two force balances contain sums of neighbouring amplitudes. For example the A mass sees B in cell \(n\) and in cell \(n-1\), giving \(m\omega^2 U=2KU-K(1+e^{-ika})V\). Exchanging the masses and conjugating the phase gives the second row. Requiring a nonzero solution yields

\[mM\omega^4-2K(m+M)\omega^2+4K^2\sin^2(ka/2)=0 .\]

There are two nonnegative solutions for \(\omega^2\) per \(k\). With \(N\) cells under periodic boundary conditions this gives \(2N\) longitudinal modes, exactly matching the number of independent displacements. At \(k=0\) the lower branch has \(U=V\) and zero frequency. For the upper branch the centre of mass stays fixed, \(mU+MV=0\), with \(\omega^2=2K(1/m+1/M)\). An electric field can excite some optical modes in polar crystals; the branch name alone does not guarantee optical activity.

A gap and a sound speed answer different questions

At the zone boundary \(1+e^{ika}=0\), so the sublattices decouple. If \(M=4m\), the two edge frequencies are \(\sqrt{K/(2m)}\) and \(\sqrt{2K/m}\), a factor of two apart. When the masses become identical the two folded branches meet, recovering the original monoatomic chain in a doubled cell. An apparent branch boundary created by plotting in a smaller zone need not be a physical frequency gap.

Expand the determinant for small \(k\) on the lower branch. Since \(\omega^2\) scales as \(k^2\), discard the term of order \(k^4\). This gives \(v_s=a\sqrt{K/[2(m+M)]}\). The two springs in a cell act in series for long waves, while both masses contribute to inertia. The sound speed alone determines a stiffness-to-mass ratio; measuring the upper branch supplies independent information about the internal motion.

Convert a mode count into thermal predictions

Quantisation assigns a harmonic mode levels \(\hbar\omega(j+1/2)\). The thermal part of its mean energy is \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\). Low-temperature excitations thus select long-wavelength acoustic modes while high-frequency optical modes remain scarcely populated. Counting states in a three-dimensional sphere gives a low-frequency density proportional to \(\omega^2\) for linear branches. Rescaling the energy integral by \(\hbar\omega/k_BT\) gives thermal energy proportional to \(T^4\) and heat capacity proportional to \(T^3\). Dimensionality and linear dispersion are essential assumptions behind that power.

Count thermally generated vacancies

A mode calculation for a perfect crystal starts with one atom on every site. Heating a real sample can leave some atoms at a surface or reservoir and some lattice sites empty. How large should that defect population be even at thermal equilibrium? Consider \(N\) equivalent sites and \(n_v\) independent vacancies. At fixed temperature and pressure let \(g_f\) be the Gibbs cost of making one vacancy and transferring its atom to the reservoir. It may contain local vibrational contributions. We count the separate entropy of choosing the missing sites explicitly.

There are \(W=N!/[n_v!(N-n_v)!]\) such arrangements. With \(x=n_v/N\) and macroscopic occupations, Stirling’s formula gives

\[\frac{G-G_0}{N}=xg_f+k_BT\left[x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right].\]

Minimizing with respect to \(x\) gives \(g_f+k_BT\ln[x/(1-x)]=0\). The second derivative is positive. Thus \(x=1/(1+e^{g_f/(k_BT)})\). For positive \(g_f\gg k_BT\) there are few vacancies and \(x\simeq e^{-g_f/(k_BT)}\). Each vacancy costs energy, but at tiny \(x\) the number of possible positions grows fast enough to favour a nonzero population. A finite microscopic specimen can still contain zero vacancies at a given instant.

Take \(g_f=0.800\,\mathrm{eV}\) as constant over the chosen range and \(k_B=8.6173\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV/K}\). At \(900\,\mathrm K\), \(k_BT=0.07756\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(x=3.31\times10^{-5}\). A region with \(10^{15}\) sites contains an expected \(3.31\times10^{10}\) empty sites. At \(600\,\mathrm K\) the equilibrium fraction drops to \(1.91\times10^{-7}\). Rapid cooling may leave an excess because vacancies must migrate to sinks before the concentration relaxes. The formation free energy in this calculation sets an equilibrium population. A migration barrier would enter the time to attain it.

A light substitution can localize a vibration

An infinite monatomic chain has a continuous vibration band, while a single altered atom can support a vibration spatially concentrated near one site. Let identical springs of stiffness \(K\) connect masses \(m\) on a chain of spacing \(a\), except the central mass at site zero is \(M\). Seek a symmetric localized solution with alternating neighbouring amplitudes \(u_n=A(-q)^{|n|}e^{-i\omega t}\) for \(0<q<1\). Alternation is motivated by the top of the perfect-chain band where adjacent atoms oscillate out of phase. Exponential decay supplies the localization.

At any ordinary site away from the impurity, the equation \(-m\omega^2u_n=K(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_n)\) gives \(m\omega^2=K(2+q+q^{-1})\). Since \(q+q^{-1}>2\), this frequency lies above the perfect-chain band edge \(\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{K/m}\). At site zero both neighbours have displacement \(-q u_0\), giving \(M\omega^2=2K(1+q)\). Eliminating \(\omega\) and cancelling \(1+q\) yields

\[q=\frac{M}{2m-M},\qquad \omega^2=\frac Km(2+q+q^{-1}).\]

The physical condition \(0<q<1\) requires a lighter central mass \(0<M<m\). A heavy isotope can still scatter travelling waves or produce resonant response within the band. This ansatz predicts no exponentially localized symmetric mode above the band for the heavier substitution. Its absence follows from the admissible decay factor rather than an assumption that a heavy impurity cannot move.

Predict a localized spectral peak

Take host atomic mass \(m=4.00\times10^{-26}\) kg, spring stiffness \(K=40.0\) N/m, lattice spacing \(a=0.300\) nm and impurity \(M=m/2\). The host upper angular frequency is \(2\sqrt{40.0/(4.00\times10^{-26})}=6.32\times10^{13}\) rad/s. For the impurity \(q=1/3\), so \(\omega^2=(16/3)K/m\), giving \(\omega=7.30\times10^{13}\) rad/s or \(f=11.6\) THz. The ordinary chain ends near \(10.1\) THz.

The neighbour amplitude is minus one third of the central amplitude, and the second neighbour has plus one ninth. Expressing the absolute envelope as \(e^{-|n|a/\xi}\) gives localization length \(\xi=a/\ln3=0.273\) nm. As \(M\) approaches \(m\) from below, \(q\) approaches one, localization length diverges and the extra frequency merges with the band edge. A finite-chain calculation should therefore vary chain length before claiming that a very weak impurity mode is spatially localized: a box shorter than its decay length cannot separate that broad tail from a standing host mode.

Spatially resolved displacement data can provide two independent estimates of impurity mass. Invert the decay-factor relation to obtain \(M/m=2q/(1+q)\) from neighbouring displacement amplitudes. Separately insert the measured localized frequency and the same host force constant in the central equation of motion. Agreement tests the assumption that the impurity changes only inertia. A substituted atom may also change the two neighbouring springs: in that case the observed frequency and decay factor can no longer be fitted by altering mass alone. This inversion distinguishes an isotope-like substitution from a stronger local bonding perturbation.

A weak second impurity far away perturbs the localized mode only through its exponentially small tail. Bringing two such impurities within a few decay lengths allows the modes to mix and split in frequency, offering another test of their spatial localization.

Problem sheet 1 · Identifying a lattice and its mechanical modes

Start from reciprocal-space or frequency observations and infer which properties of the crystal they determine.

1. Two peaks in a one-dimensional specimen

Successive reciprocal-lattice orders occur at 12.57 and 25.13 nm⁻¹. The corrected integrated intensity of the second order is nine times that of the first. (a) Find the cell length. (b) For sites at \(0,a/2\) with real positive amplitudes \(f_A>f_B\), determine their ratio. (c) What changes if the crystal is translated by one quarter of a cell?

Separate positions, amplitudes and phases

The spacing gives \(a=2\pi/12.56\simeq0.500\) nm. Even orders have amplitude \(f_A+f_B\) and odd ones \(f_A-f_B\), so \((f_A+f_B)/(f_A-f_B)=3\), hence \(f_A/f_B=2\). Translation adds the phase \(e^{iG_h a/4}\) to every amplitude for order \(h\). Peaks and intensities stay fixed. The ratio calculation assumes that instrumental and form-factor differences between orders have already been corrected.

Chapter 3 · Filling electronic states and measuring the Fermi surface

Use exclusion and state counting to predict carrier energies, thermal response and the effect of dimensionality.

From first principles

Count the states in a filled Fermi sphere

Take independent spin-one-half electrons in a large cube of volume \(V=L^3\) with periodic boundary conditions. The condition \(e^{ik_xL}=1\) permits \(k_x=2\pi n_x/L\), with the same spacing in the other directions. Thus each allowed wave vector occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For the free-particle energy \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\), all states below energy \(E\) lie in a sphere. Counting its volume and two spin states gives

\[N(E)=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.\]

At zero temperature the exclusion principle fills these single-particle states up to \(E_F\). Writing \(n=N/V\), solve the counting formula at that upper limit:

\[k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\]
\[E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}.\]

The number of available states in a thin energy shell is the derivative \(D(E)dE\). In three dimensions \(D(E)\propto\sqrt E\) because the shell area grows with \(k^2\) while \(dk/dE\) falls as \(1/k\). This counting assumes an isotropic parabolic band. Additional valleys or a different effective dimension change the result.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the pressure of a filled Fermi sea by compressing its states

Even at zero temperature a box of mobile electrons exerts a pressure on its boundaries. Consider \(N\) noninteracting spin-one-half fermions in a three-dimensional volume \(V\), with fixed isotropic mass \(m\) and quadratic energy. Take the bottom of the band as a volume-independent energy reference. Every state below \(E_F\) is occupied once per allowed spin state. Since the density of states including spin has the form \(D(E)=C\sqrt E\), dividing two elementary integrals eliminates the normalization constant,

\[\frac{U}{N}=\frac{\int_0^{E_F} E\,C\sqrt E\,dE}{\int_0^{E_F} C\sqrt E\,dE}=\frac35 E_F .\]

State counting gives \(E_F=(\hbar^2/2m)(3\pi^2 N/V)^{2/3}\). During a uniform compression at fixed \(N\) the upper occupied energy rises. At zero entropy the energy derivative with respect to box volume is the negative pressure, so

\[P=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac{2U}{3V}=\frac25 nE_F,\]
\[n=\frac NV .\]

Density appears both explicitly and through \(E_F\). A second volume derivative gives the positive resistance to a small fractional compression,

\[B=-V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac53P=\frac23 nE_F .\]

Both \(P\) and the bulk modulus \(B\) have units of energy per volume. The factor \(5/3\) distinguishes the change of pressure from pressure itself. An actual solid also has electrostatic and lattice contributions. Extracting the electronic contribution from a measured solid modulus requires information about these other stresses and the volume dependence of the band parameters.

Worked example

Estimate the electronic stiffness of a simple metal

Take \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(E_F=5.00\,\mathrm{eV}\). With \(1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J\), the product \(nE_F\) is \(4.01\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The pressure contribution is \(16.0\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and the modulus is \(26.7\,\mathrm{GPa}\). A one percent volume decrease would raise this pressure by approximately \(0.267\,\mathrm{GPa}\) in the linear response regime.

Second application

Predict a finite compression without linearizing

Suppose a separate trapped gas starts at pressure \(12.0\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and is compressed slowly to \(0.800\) of its original volume at fixed particle number and negligible entropy. Its pressure becomes \(12.0(1/0.800)^{5/3}=17.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Its energy per particle rises by only \((1/0.800)^{2/3}=1.16\) times. The larger pressure ratio also contains the increase in particles per unit volume.

Count occupied states at zero temperature

Consider a cubic box of volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries and free electrons of effective model mass \(m\). Allowed wavevectors occupy a volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) per state for each spin. At zero temperature each state below the chemical potential is filled. For \(\epsilon=\hbar^2 k^2/2m\) the filled region is a sphere and each \(k\) has two spin states. Hence

\[N=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3},\qquad k_F=(3\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2 k_F^2}{2m}.\]

With \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ and the free electron mass, \(k_F\) is about \(1.36\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\) about 7.0 eV. The corresponding temperature \(E_F/k_B\) is roughly 81,000 K. Ordinary room temperature therefore changes occupation only near the outer surface of this sea in a simple metal. This is a model estimate; anisotropic bands and multiple pockets change its interpretation.

The density of states is a derivative

Replacing \(k_F\) by \(k(E)=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) gives the integrated state count below energy \(E\). Its derivative is

\[D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E,\qquad D(E_F)=\frac{3N}{2E_F}.\]

Check dimensions by integrating \(D(E)\,dE\) to recover a count. In two dimensions the occupied area grows as \(k^2\) and the density per energy is constant for a parabolic band. In one dimension the count grows as \(k\) and the density has an integrable \(E^{-1/2}\) singularity at its bottom. A large density of states means many states fit in a narrow energy window. It does not imply a large group velocity: a flat dispersion may create the large density.

Why only a shell contributes to heat capacity

At finite temperature the occupation is \(f(E)=[1+e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT}]^{-1}\). Heating can promote an electron only into a vacant state. Deep in the filled sea all nearby destinations are occupied. The active shell has width of order \(k_BT\), involving about \(D(E_F)k_BT\) states; the typical energy change is another factor \(k_BT\). Thus the thermal energy increases quadratically and the heat capacity rises linearly with \(T\).

A coefficient follows by expanding the smooth density of states across that shell. At fixed particle number, the relevant excess energy to leading order is \(\int (E-E_F)D(E_F)[f(E)-\Theta(E_F-E)]\,dE\). The filled and empty sides contribute equally to this order. Substituting \(x=|E-E_F|/k_BT\) and using \(\int_0^\infty x/(e^x+1)\,dx=\pi^2/12\) gives \(C_{\rm el}=(\pi^2/3)D(E_F)k_B^2T\). This expansion requires the density to be smooth on the thermal scale; a band edge at the chemical potential needs separate treatment.

Separate two low-temperature contributions

In a three-dimensional simple metal combine \(C_\mathrm{el}=\gamma T\) with the phonon result \(C_\mathrm{ph}=\beta T^3\). Plot \(C/T\) against \(T^2\); the intercept estimates \(\gamma\), and the slope estimates \(\beta\). If two points are \((4,3)\) and \((16,6)\) in K² and mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻², respectively, \(\beta=0.25\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴ and \(\gamma=2.0\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻². Curvature across the fitted interval would be evidence against this two-term description. To go beyond a free sphere we next retain the periodic potential of the ions.

Count charges and electronic compressibility in two dimensions

A two-dimensional electron layer illustrates how changing available spatial dimensions alters the density of states and an electrical measurement. Assume one parabolic subband \(E=E_c+\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)/(2m^*)\), spin degeneracy two and no valley degeneracy. A square sample of area \(A\) with periodic boundary conditions supplies one state per spin per reciprocal area \((2\pi)^2/A\). Counting the disk up to \(k_F\) gives areal density \(n_s=k_F^2/(2\pi)\), hence

\[E_F-E_c=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m^*},\qquad D_A(E)=\frac{dn_s}{dE_F}=\frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}\]

above the subband edge at zero temperature. Unlike the square-root density in three dimensions, each occupied parabolic two-dimensional subband contributes a constant step. When an additional subband begins to fill it supplies another step, possibly with different degeneracy or mass. Temperature and disorder broaden the step in a real measurement.

An electrostatic gate must both create a field across the insulating spacer and raise the layer’s chemical potential. For areal capacitance \(C_g'\) of the spacer and increment of sheet charge magnitude \(dQ'=e\,dn_s\), the required voltage is \(dV=dQ'/C_g'+dE_F/e\). The second term defines the quantum capacitance \(C_q'=e^2D_A\). Their inverse capacitances add in series. For a small density of states the electronic contribution measurably lowers the total capacitance even when the geometric dimensions are known precisely.

Measure which capacitance limits a device

Take \(m^*=0.067m_e\) and \(n_s=2.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻². Then \(k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_s}=1.12\times10^8\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F-E_c=7.15\) meV. At 4 K the thermal scale \(k_BT=0.345\) meV is smaller than this separation, supporting the degenerate estimate. The electronic capacitance per area is \(0.0448\) F/m². For a spacer with \(C_g'=0.0100\) F/m², the measured series value is \((1/0.0100+1/0.0448)^{-1}=0.00818\) F/m².

Over a gate area of \(100\) μm², the predicted capacitance is \(0.818\) pF rather than the purely geometric \(1.00\) pF. Treating that measured value as a change of dielectric thickness would misidentify an electronic effect. In the limit of a very high density of states, the quantum capacitance becomes large and the geometric value is recovered. Near depletion the assumptions of a sharp occupied subband and uniform layer require scrutiny. Comparing capacitance with an independent sheet-density measurement can test whether a low reading reflects depleted electronic states or simply a different insulating spacer.

Using the series result, a small gate swing can be split into two measurable energy changes. A 10.0 mV swing at the stated operating point changes sheet charge magnitude by \(8.18\times10^{-5}\) C/m², or sheet density by \(5.10\times10^{14}\) m⁻². The geometric voltage increment is \(8.18\) mV and the chemical-potential contribution is \(1.82\) mV, corresponding to a Fermi-level shift of 1.82 meV. Their sum recovers the imposed swing. This decomposition assumes the layer remains in the same subband and equilibrates through its electrical contact on the capacitance measurement timescale.

A slow measurement with significant leakage instead mixes capacitance with conductance and can corrupt both estimated voltage contributions.

For a parabolic band, states within a radius \(k\) fill a length, area or volume in reciprocal space. Since \(k\) scales as \(\sqrt E\), the integrated count varies as \(E^{d/2}\). Normalise at one energy to compare exponents independently of the sample dimensions.

Chapter 4 · Bloch waves, band edges and effective mass

Derive an electronic band from both weak and strong binding, then use its slope and curvature to predict motion.

From first principles

Why translating a lattice state produces an energy band

In a perfect periodic chain the Hamiltonian is unchanged when the site labels are shifted by one. A wave may therefore have site amplitudes of the form \(c_j=C e^{ikja}\), with \(k\) defined up to \(2\pi/a\). To give that phase a physical energy, choose one localized orbital per cell of spacing \(a\). Assume an orthonormal basis, on-site energy \(\epsilon_0\), and a real hopping matrix element \(-t\) to either nearest neighbour. Projecting the stationary Schrödinger equation on orbital \(j\) gives

\[E c_j=\epsilon_0 c_j-t(c_{j-1}+c_{j+1}).\]

Successive amplitudes differ only by \(e^{\pm ika}\). Cancel \(c_j\), then combine these phases into a cosine:

\[E(k)=\epsilon_0-t(e^{ika}+e^{-ika})=\epsilon_0-2t\cos(ka).\]

For \(t>0\) the in-phase combination has the lowest energy. Comparing \(k=0\) and \(k=\pi/a\) gives bandwidth \(4t\). Form a narrow wave packet from neighbouring \(k\)'s. Its envelope speed is \(d(E/\hbar)/dk=(2ta/\hbar)\sin(ka)\). Translation symmetry determines the allowed phase labels, while the coupling strength determines the numerical dispersion. A real crystal can require several orbitals and longer-range couplings.

Derivation and applications

Use band curvature to predict acceleration off the force axis

Near a smooth, isolated band minimum the first derivative of energy vanishes. In a low-symmetry crystal its second derivatives can depend on direction. Choose principal axes \(x,y\) in the plane near the minimum \(\mathbf k_0\), and write \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k-\mathbf k_0\). Taylor expansion defines two positive effective masses through the curvatures,

\[E(\mathbf k_0+\mathbf q)\simeq E_0+\frac{\hbar^2 q_x^2}{2m_x}+\frac{\hbar^2 q_y^2}{2m_y}.\]

Treat a narrow wave packet semiclassically in a uniform force \(\mathbf F\), over a time short enough for scattering to be negligible. Neglect interband transitions and any anomalous velocity. Differentiating energy component by component gives group velocity. Combining this with the crystal-momentum equation gives its acceleration,

\[v_i=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E}{\partial q_i}=\frac{\hbar q_i}{m_i},\]
\[\hbar\dot q_i=F_i,\]
\[a_i=\frac{F_i}{m_i}.\]

Thus a force rotated through the principal axes traces an ellipse of accelerations. To describe a longitudinal measurement, project the full vector onto the applied force. If the force makes angle \(\theta\) to the \(x\) axis and \(\phi\) is the direction of the resulting acceleration in the same quadrant, then

\[\frac{a_\parallel}{F}=\frac{\cos^2\theta}{m_x}+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{m_y},\]
\[\tan\phi=\frac{m_x}{m_y}\tan\theta .\]

The longitudinal inverse mass is a weighted average of inverse principal masses. The difference \(\phi-\theta\) vanishes along principal axes and for an isotropic band. The expansion requires the whole accelerated packet to stay inside the region of roughly constant curvature.

Worked example

Follow an oblique force pulse

Let \(m_x=0.20m_e\), \(m_y=0.80m_e\), where \(m_e\) is the free electron mass. A force pulse of magnitude \(1.00\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm N\) acts at \(45^\circ\) for \(50.0\,\mathrm{fs}\) on a packet initially at rest. Its velocity gains are \(F\Delta t/(\sqrt2m_i)\), or \(1.94\,\mathrm{m/s}\) along \(x\) and \(0.485\,\mathrm{m/s}\) along \(y\). The velocity points only \(14.0^\circ\) above the light-mass axis.

Second application

Recover an unseen principal curvature

A second band has longitudinal mass \(0.300m_e\) for a force at \(45^\circ\), and a measurement along its \(x\) axis gives \(m_x=0.200m_e\). In units of \(1/m_e\), inversion yields \(m_e/m_y=2/0.300-1/0.200=1.667\), so \(m_y=0.600m_e\). A pulse along \(y\) should produce one third of the acceleration measured along \(x\) for the same force, providing an independent check of the principal-axis assignment.

OneSimulation laboratory · Open a band gap in a periodic dielectric

Why label a state by crystal momentum?

A lattice translation by \(a\) commutes with the Hamiltonian when \(V(x+a)=V(x)\). Translation eigenvalues have modulus one because translation preserves norm. Write them \(e^{ika}\); then \(\psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}u_k(x)\) with \(u_k(x+a)=u_k(x)\). Since \(e^{i(k+G)a}=e^{ika}\) for \(G=2\pi h/a\), a single Brillouin zone suffices. The index distinguishing states at the same \(k\) is the band index. Crystal momentum labels translation symmetry and can change by a reciprocal vector in scattering.

A weak potential couples a degenerate pair

Near \(k=G/2\), free states \(|k\rangle\) and \(|k-G\rangle\) have nearly equal kinetic energies. The periodic potential has Fourier coefficients \(V_G\) that couple these waves. Absorbing the mean potential in both energies, the two-state characteristic equation is \((E-E_k)(E-E_{k-G})=|V_G|^2\), with solutions

\[E_\pm=\tfrac12(E_k+E_{k-G})\pm\sqrt{\tfrac14(E_k-E_{k-G})^2+|V_G|^2}.\]

At equality the splitting is \(2|V_G|\). For a real coefficient the states are standing waves whose densities peak at different locations relative to the potential. Far from the degeneracy, the square root is dominated by the kinetic splitting and one obtains two nearly free waves again. A Fourier component that vanishes by symmetry cannot open a first-order gap at that crossing. Check other bands before interpreting a local splitting as a gap for the entire solid.

Build a band from a localised orbital

Start instead with one localised orbital \(|n\rangle\) per cell, all of energy \(\epsilon_0\), with overlap neglected in the basis and nearest-neighbour hopping matrix element \(-t\). A translation eigenstate has site amplitudes \(c_n=e^{ikna}/\sqrt N\). Acting with \(H\) on one site amplitude gives \(E c_n=\epsilon_0 c_n-t(c_{n-1}+c_{n+1})\), and hence \(E(k)=\epsilon_0-2t\cos ka\). The bandwidth \(4|t|\) measures the energy spread produced by hopping. In the limit of vanishing hopping the local energy repeats for every \(k\). More than one orbital per cell generates a matrix eigenproblem even in this simple construction.

The group velocity is \(v=\hbar^{-1}dE/dk\). Under a weak force with \(\hbar\dot k=F\), acceleration is \(F E''(k)/\hbar^2\). Define \(m^*=\hbar^2/E''(k)\), a local measure of curvature. Near the bottom of the cosine band with \(t>0\), \(E\simeq\epsilon_0-2t+t(ka)^2\) and \(m^*=\hbar^2/(2ta^2)\). Near the top the curvature changes sign. Describing the missing electrons in a nearly full band as positively charged holes with positive mass simplifies transport calculations.

Use filling to decide whether current can flow

For a complete time-reversal-symmetric band, the contributions of \(k\) and \(-k\) to current cancel. More generally the integral of the derivative of a periodic band over a whole zone is zero. A weak field can redistribute carriers near a partially filled band edge, whereas a fully filled isolated band has no vacant neighbouring states. With one spin-degenerate orbital per primitive cell there are two states per cell. One electron per cell half fills this noninteracting band; two fill it. Symmetry breaking, interactions and additional bands can change the conclusion. A single measured zone-edge splitting therefore gives incomplete information about conduction.

For further work on three-dimensional bands, Fermi surfaces and electron motion, see David Tong’s Solid State Physics lectures at Cambridge.

Open a gap by doubling a chain’s period

A uniform one-dimensional chain with one spin-degenerate orbital and one electron per site has a half-filled band. A distortion can change both the spectrum and the periodicity used to count states. Let the sites be separated by \(a\) before the distortion. Group successive sites into \(A,B\) pairs and allow alternating hopping amplitudes \(t_1,t_2>0\). The distorted unit cell has length \(2a\). For equal on-site energies, chosen as zero, the amplitude on \(A\) couples to \(B\) inside its own cell and to \(B\) one cell away. Bloch translation supplies a phase \(e^{-2ika}\) for the latter coupling. The sublattice equations give a two-by-two matrix with zero diagonal and off-diagonal elements \(-(t_1+t_2 e^{\mp 2ika})\). Its characteristic equation yields

\[E_\pm(k)=\pm\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2+2t_1t_2\cos(2ka)},\qquad |k|\leq\pi/(2a).\]

At the zone centre the hoppings add. At the new zone edge they subtract, opening a gap \(2|t_1-t_2|\). In the uniform limit the folded branches simply touch. Merely drawing a smaller zone did not change the crystal. It takes a physical alternation to split the states. With two electrons per doubled cell, the lower band is exactly filled when spin is counted. Its electronic energy falls on dimerization at fixed mean hopping: writing \(t_{1,2}=t\pm\delta\) gives \(E_-=-2\sqrt{t^2\cos^2(ka)+\delta^2\sin^2(ka)}\), which is at most the undistorted lower energy. The lattice also pays an elastic cost. Quantitative distortion amplitudes require that competing energy.

For \(t_1=1.20\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(t_2=0.80\,\mathrm{eV}\) the zone-centre energies remain \(\pm2.00\,\mathrm{eV}\), while the zone-edge energies become \(\pm0.40\,\mathrm{eV}\). The minimum direct excitation costs \(0.80\,\mathrm{eV}\), corresponding to a vacuum wavelength of about \(1.55\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) if the optical transition is allowed. The cause of this insulating filling is the pairing of bonds. Away from the commensurate filling the chemical potential can lie inside a band even if the same structural splitting remains visible.

Follow a wave packet through the whole Brillouin zone

In a weak static field a band electron's crystal momentum changes even though its group velocity need not keep rising. Take one isolated tight-binding band \(E(k)=E_0-2t\cos(ka)\), positive hopping energy \(t\), lattice spacing \(a\) and electron charge \(-e\). Let the wave packet be narrow in \(k\) compared with the zone and neglect scattering and transitions to other bands. The semiclassical force equation is \(\hbar\dot k=-e\mathcal E\), while velocity follows from the band gradient: \(v=(2ta/\hbar)\sin(ka)\).

Starting at \(k=0\) yields \(ka=-\omega_B t_{\rm lab}\) with Bloch angular frequency \(\omega_B=e\mathcal E a/\hbar\). Integrating velocity over laboratory time gives

\[x(t_{\rm lab})-x(0)=\frac{2t}{e\mathcal E}[\cos(\omega_Bt_{\rm lab})-1],\qquad T_B=\frac{h}{e\mathcal E a}.\]

Although the field never changes sign, velocity reverses when the packet samples regions of opposite band slope. Over a complete zone traversal there is no net displacement because the band energy is periodic. Energy conservation offers another derivation: the electrostatic potential-energy change of a negatively charged electron in a uniform field is \(+e\mathcal E\Delta x\), offsetting the change in band energy. The maximal excursion equals the band width \(4t\) divided by the force magnitude.

Estimate an observable oscillation in a superlattice

Use a one-dimensional superlattice with \(a=8.00\) nm, hopping \(t=5.00\) meV and field \(\mathcal E=2.00\times10^5\) V/m. The potential-energy change per lattice period is \(e\mathcal E a=1.60\) meV. Thus \(T_B=h/(1.60\,{\rm meV})=2.58\) ps and the peak-to-peak excursion is \(20.0\,{\rm meV}/(e\mathcal E)=100\) nm, about 12.5 lattice periods. The maximum speed from the band derivative is \(2ta/\hbar=1.22\times10^5\) m/s. At one quarter cycle velocity reaches its largest negative magnitude, while at half a cycle the electron reaches its furthest position and momentarily stops.

A momentum-relaxation time of 0.20 ps would interrupt most trajectories before a cycle completes, obscuring a Bloch oscillation despite the periodic dispersion. Conversely a scattering time several times longer than 2.58 ps would make coherent oscillatory transport plausible if the field does not tunnel carriers into other bands. In the weak-field limit the period and excursion both grow, so the mathematical limit of arbitrarily large excursions cannot be taken in a finite imperfect specimen. Recording the oscillation frequency as field is varied tests the predicted linear scaling separately from the much more sample-dependent amplitude.

The motion also clarifies the role of negative effective mass near a band top. The electron's crystal momentum continues to change in the direction of the applied force throughout the period. Its acceleration is proportional to the local band curvature, however, and can point in the opposite direction once that curvature changes sign. This reverses packet acceleration without a sudden elastic collision with a particular lattice plane. A spectrum measured along the direction of motion can predict the velocity through its local slope, giving a direct cross-check of the acceleration inferred from a time-domain trace.

The probe must resolve the oscillations before relaxation for that comparison to be meaningful.

Problem sheet 2 · Occupied states, heat capacity and band curvature

Keep spin degeneracy, state counting and energy units consistent while comparing three electronic measurements.

1. Counting a two-dimensional electron gas

Electrons with spin degeneracy two occupy an isotropic parabolic band in an area \(A\). (a) Derive \(k_F\) and \(D(E)/A\). (b) Estimate \(E_F\) at \(n=10^{16}\) m⁻² and \(m^*=0.10m_e\). Use \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV nm². (c) State whether a zero-temperature step gives a close picture at 300 K.

Count the disk with two spin states

The density is \(n=2\pi k_F^2/(2\pi)^2=k_F^2/(2\pi)\), hence \(k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n}\). Substituting \(k^2=2m^*E/\hbar^2\) and differentiating gives \(D(E)/A=m^*/(\pi\hbar^2)\). Convert the density to 0.010 nm⁻²; the Fermi energy measured from the band minimum is \(0.381(2\pi)(0.010)=0.0239\) eV. At 300 K, \(k_BT\) is about 0.0259 eV; the sharp-shell approximation is poor.

2. Calorimetry on a metal

At 2 K a metal has \(C/T=4.8\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻²; at 4 K it has \(C/T=7.2\) in the same units. (a) Fit the low-temperature electronic and phonon coefficients. (b) At what temperature do the two contributions become equal according to the fit? (c) What further measurement is needed to assess that extrapolation?

Fit the straight line in squared temperature

The slope is \(\beta=(7.2-4.8)/(16-4)=0.20\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴. Substituting either point gives \(\gamma=4.0\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻². Equality requires \(T=\sqrt{\gamma/\beta}=\sqrt{20}=4.47\) K. Two observations always determine a straight line. Measure several points across and beyond this temperature and inspect \(C/T\) versus \(T^2\) for curvature or an anomaly.

3. A cosine band from spectroscopy

For a one-orbital chain the lower band edge at \(k=0\) is −1.2 eV and the upper edge at \(k=\pi/a\) is 0.8 eV. Take \(a=0.30\) nm. (a) Find \(t,\epsilon_0\). (b) Find the bottom effective mass in units of \(m_e\) and the wavevector of maximum positive group velocity in the first zone. (c) How many electrons fit in this band for 100 cells?

Separate the centre and width of the band

The centre is \(-0.2\) eV and the width is 2.0 eV, giving \(t=0.50\) eV. Then \(m^*/m_e=0.0381/(0.50\times0.30^2)=0.847\). The derivative of \(-2t\cos ka\) is largest and positive at \(ka=\pi/2\). There are 100 allowed \(k\) values and two spin states per \(k\), so 200 electrons fill the band.

Recover alternating hoppings from spectroscopy

Two bands in a dimerized chain are separated by \(3.20\,\mathrm{eV}\) at the zone centre and by \(0.240\,\mathrm{eV}\) at the edge. Infer both hopping magnitudes in the equal-on-site-energy model. Can this energy spectrum identify which bond starts the chosen cell? Would the same gap make a chain with one electron per doubled cell insulating?

Chapter 5 · Carriers, neutrality and transport in semiconductors

Determine the chemical potential from particle balance and connect the same equilibrium to conductivity and a Hall measurement.

From first principles

Determine electron and hole densities from one chemical potential

In thermal equilibrium fermions occupy a level \(E\) with probability \(1/[e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1]\). Take a nondegenerate semiconductor with chemical potential several \(k_BT\) inside the gap and parabolic edges \(E_c,E_v\). For electrons in the conduction band the exponential dominates the denominator. Write energy as \(E=E_c+\varepsilon\) and integrate the density of states against this occupation:

\[n=e^{-(E_c-\mu)/(k_BT)}\int_0^\infty D_c(\varepsilon)e^{-\varepsilon/(k_BT)}\,d\varepsilon=N_c e^{-(E_c-\mu)/(k_BT)}.\]

For holes count missing valence electrons. Taking \(E=E_v-\varepsilon\) instead gives \(p=N_v e^{-(\mu-E_v)/(k_BT)}\), with \(N_v\) defined by the corresponding integral. At fixed temperature multiplication removes the chemical potential entirely:

\[np=N_cN_v e^{-(E_c-E_v)/(k_BT)}=n_i^2 .\]

An intrinsic neutral crystal requires \(n=p=n_i\). With ionized donors and acceptors it instead requires \(n-p=N_D^+-N_A^-\), because electron and acceptor charges are negative. This equation and the product law determine the two carrier densities. Heavy doping can move the chemical potential into a band, where the exponential approximation must be replaced by the full occupation integral.

Derivation and applications

Measure minority-carrier lifetime from a spatial decay

Illuminate a narrow stripe at the edge of a long, uniformly doped sample. In an \(n\)-type crystal the illumination creates mobile minority holes, which diffuse away and recombine. Let \(u(x)=p(x)-p_0\) be the excess hole density above its equilibrium value. Consider steady, weak injection into the half-space \(x>0\). Assume a constant diffusivity \(D_p\), linear recombination with lifetime \(\tau_p\), and negligible electric field. The stripe maintains a fixed excess at \(x=0\), and the bulk away from it has no further generation.

\[\Gamma_p=-D_p\frac{du}{dx},\]
\[\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial\Gamma_p}{\partial x}-\frac{u}{\tau_p}.\]

The first equation gives flux of particles per area per time. The second counts particles in a thin slice. A declining flux with distance supplies the holes that recombine inside the slice. Setting the time derivative to zero gives an equation with two exponential solutions,

\[\frac{d^2u}{dx^2}=\frac{u}{L_p^2},\]
\[L_p=\sqrt{D_p\tau_p},\]
\[u(x)=u(0)e^{-x/L_p}.\]

The exponentially increasing solution violates the equilibrium boundary condition far from the stripe. The remaining length \(L_p\) gives the depth over which the population falls by a factor \(e\). Multiplying the surface flux by the positive charge \(e\) gives the hole current density into the bulk. Integrating the loss over the half-space independently recovers this flux,

\[\Gamma_p(0)=\frac{D_p u(0)}{L_p}=\int_0^\infty\frac{u(x)}{\tau_p}\,dx .\]

A second nearby surface, significant drift, or recombination changing with injection can all alter the spatial profile. Plotting the logarithm of excess signal against position tests the single-exponential assumption.

Worked example

Predict the current required at an illuminated edge

For \(D_p=12.0\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}\), \(\tau_p=3.00\,\mathrm{\mu s}\) and \(u(0)=2.00\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\), the length is \(0.00600\,\mathrm{cm}\), or \(60.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The surface flux is \(4.00\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}s^{-1}}\), giving \(6.41\,\mathrm{mA/cm^2}\). A stripe extending over \(0.100\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) of injecting area supplies \(0.641\,\mathrm{mA}\) to this bulk recombination channel.

Second application

Infer a lifetime without calibrating the signal amplitude

In a second specimen, a signal proportional to excess density drops by a factor of four between positions separated by \(90.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). Its diffusion length is \(90.0/\ln4=64.9\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). An independently measured \(D_p=8.00\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}\) gives \(\tau_p=(0.00649)^2/8.00=5.27\,\mathrm{\mu s}\). The ratio cancels a common detector gain, provided the equilibrium background was subtracted first.

Integrate the occupation near a band edge

For a three-dimensional parabolic conduction band, the density of states per volume grows as \(\sqrt{E-E_c}\). If \(E_c-\mu\) is several \(k_BT\), the Fermi function is well approximated by \(e^{-(E-\mu)/k_BT}\). Substituting \(x=(E-E_c)/k_BT\) in the carrier integral produces a temperature-dependent prefactor and an exponential separation from the band edge. Carrying out the corresponding hole integral at the valence-band maximum gives

\[n=N_c e^{-(E_c-\mu)/k_BT},\qquad p=N_v e^{-(\mu-E_v)/k_BT},\qquad np=N_cN_v e^{-E_g/k_BT}=n_i^2 .\]

Here \(N_c,N_v\) include spin and any relevant valley degeneracies, \(E_g=E_c-E_v\), and \(n_i\) is the intrinsic concentration. In a parabolic band the prefactors scale as \(T^{3/2}\). The product relation assumes thermal equilibrium with a common chemical potential. Under illumination electrons and holes can have different quasi-Fermi levels; using the same mass-action product there requires additional justification.

Close the equations with charge neutrality

In an intrinsic uniform sample \(n=p=n_i\). Equating the two exponentials gives \(\mu=(E_c+E_v)/2+(k_BT/2)\ln(N_v/N_c)\). The intrinsic chemical potential is at midgap only if the effective state counts match. With fully ionised donors and acceptors, neutrality reads \(n-p=N_D-N_A=D\). Combining it with mass action gives \(n=(D+\sqrt{D^2+4n_i^2})/2,\ p=n_i^2/n\). For large negative \(D\), computing \(p\) first from the corresponding positive root avoids subtracting nearby numbers.

For \(D=10^{16}\) cm⁻³ and \(n_i=10^{10}\) cm⁻³, the electron density is essentially \(10^{16}\) cm⁻³ while \(p\) is about \(10^4\) cm⁻³. Adding net donors moves the Fermi level upward and suppresses holes. At low temperature incomplete ionisation can matter, and at high donor concentration the nondegenerate approximation can fail. At sufficiently high temperature the intrinsic density can grow comparable to the dopant density. Use both balance equations when comparing data across such a crossover.

From a drift balance to a measured signal

In a single isotropic parabolic band a relaxation-time model balances electric acceleration against momentum relaxation. For electrons \(m^*\dot v=-eE-m^*v/\tau\), so the steady drift velocity is \(-e\tau E/m^*\). Define the positive mobility \(u_n=e\tau/m^*\). Multiplying by electron charge and density gives current parallel to the field. With holes present, \(\sigma=e(nu_n+pu_p)\). A conductivity alone supplies a product of concentration and mobility and cannot separate the two.

For one carrier type with charge \(q\), zero transverse current implies \(E_y=v_x B_z\). Substituting \(j_x=n q v_x\) yields \(R_H=E_y/(j_xB_z)=1/(nq)\) in this simple model. Combining its sign and magnitude with \(\sigma\) gives a density and mobility. If both types contribute, in weak fields the two-band expression becomes \(R_H=(p u_p^2-nu_n^2)/[e(pu_p+nu_n)^2]\). The mobility squared makes a highly mobile minority carrier disproportionately visible. Interpreting every measured Hall coefficient as the inverse density can therefore misidentify both carrier count and dominant mechanism.

Set up electrostatics at a junction between two dopings

A junction between p-type and n-type regions can sustain an electric field in thermal equilibrium. On contact, carriers diffuse down their concentration gradients. The uncovered, immobile dopant ions leave a negative region on the p side and a positive one on the n side. This space charge creates a field opposing further majority-carrier diffusion. Consider an abrupt, one-dimensional junction in one nondegenerate semiconductor. Assume complete ionization, uniform dopant densities \(N_A,N_D\), no applied bias and neutral regions far from the junction.

The equilibrium Fermi level is constant. Equivalently, Boltzmann carrier densities on opposite sides and the mass-action relation \(np=n_i^2\) imply a built-in voltage magnitude \(V_{\rm bi}=(k_BT/e)\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)\). In the depletion approximation mobile carriers are neglected across widths \(x_p\) and \(x_n\) adjoining the junction. Requiring field zero at both outer edges enforces charge neutrality \(N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n\). Inside either side \(dE/dx=\rho/\epsilon\) is constant, so the field profile consists of two straight segments meeting at its maximum magnitude. Integrating the triangular field profile gives

\[V_{\rm bi}=\frac12 E_{\max}W,\qquad W=x_p+x_n=\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon V_{\rm bi}}e\left(\frac1{N_A}+\frac1{N_D}\right)}.\]

Most of the width lies on the lightly doped side because more volume there is needed to uncover the same fixed charge per area. This relation follows from charge balance and need not be imposed as another boundary condition.

Predict a width and a measurable differential capacitance

Take silicon-like \(\epsilon=11.7\epsilon_0\), \(n_i=1.00\times10^{16}\) m⁻³, \(N_A=1.00\times10^{23}\) m⁻³ and \(N_D=1.00\times10^{22}\) m⁻³ at 300 K. The logarithm is \(\ln(10^{13})=29.93\), giving \(V_{\rm bi}=0.774\) V. The depletion width is \(0.332\) μm, partitioned as \(x_n=0.302\) μm and \(x_p=0.0302\) μm. The peak electric field is \(2(0.774)/(0.332\,\mu{\rm m})=4.66\times10^6\) V/m. The built-in voltage is an internal electrostatic difference; equilibrium contact potentials complicate interpreting it as a terminal voltmeter reading.

A reverse bias of magnitude 0.500 V increases the voltage across the depletion layer to \(1.274\) V and multiplies its width by \(\sqrt{1.274/0.774}=1.28\). Differentiating the uncovered charge per area with respect to reverse bias gives a differential capacitance per area \(\epsilon/W\). It falls from about \(0.312\) to \(0.244\) mF/m² in this sweep. A plot of reciprocal squared capacitance against reverse voltage is therefore approximately linear for uniform doping. A curved graph can indicate a graded interface, traps or departure from the simple depletion approximation.

The abrupt-junction model predicts how the measured capacitance changes if the device area is doubled with doping and bias held fixed. Terminal capacitance doubles, while the inferred depletion width and field maximum stay fixed. Perimeter leakage and fringe capacitance instead scale differently with lateral device size, so comparing multiple diode areas can help isolate the interior junction contribution. Only after this separation is it reasonable to interpret a slope of the inverse squared capacitance as a dopant-density measurement. Frequency dependence provides another diagnostic since slowly responding traps can alter a low-frequency capacitance without changing the static depletion geometry.

Locate the crossover from doped to intrinsic conduction

Assume fully ionised net donors of density \(D>0\) and a nondegenerate equilibrium semiconductor. Set \(r=n_i/D\); solve neutrality and mass action together rather than discarding minority carriers when the intrinsic density rises.

Chapter 6 · Predicting a gap and testing it in a periodic medium

Combine the translation argument, a measurable band edge and an independent numerical calculation to distinguish a gap from a folded crossing.

From first principles

Infer pass bands and stop bands from a repeated cell

For a normally incident monochromatic plane wave in a lossless layered dielectric, record its tangential electric and magnetic fields in a two-component state. Interface conditions transmit these same tangential fields between adjacent layers. Propagation through one complete cell of period \(a\) is a linear map represented by a matrix \(M(\omega)\). Forward and backward propagation in each lossless layer have reciprocal phase factors, so the cell transfer matrix has determinant one. A Bloch state reproduces its field after one cell up to a multiplier:

\[Mv=\lambda v,\]
\[\lambda=e^{ika},\]
\[\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}M)\lambda+1=0 .\]

The second root is \(1/\lambda\). Adding the two roots and using the phase relation gives the band condition

\[\cos(ka)=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr} M(\omega).\]

For a real lossless layered system the trace is real. Where its half lies between −1 and 1 there are real wave numbers. Outside this interval the two multipliers have different magnitudes and one solution decays with each successive cell. In a uniform material the trace simply reproduces homogeneous propagation: folding the dispersion into the first zone can make branches touch without opening a gap. Index contrast allows repeated reflections and can create finite stop bands.

Derivation and applications

Find the long-wavelength optical index of a layered cell

A finite wavelength can resolve two different dielectrics in a repeating stack. Far below the first band edge, what uniform medium would reproduce the phase accumulated per cell? Consider lossless, nonmagnetic layers of thickness \(d_1,d_2\), real refractive indices \(n_1,n_2\), and cell length \(a=d_1+d_2\). At normal incidence the field is tangential to the interfaces. Define \(k_0=\omega/c\) and the phase thickness \(\delta_j=k_0n_jd_j\). For Bloch wavevector \(K\), the exact trace relation is

\[\cos(Ka)=\cos\delta_1\cos\delta_2-\frac12\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}+\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)\sin\delta_1\sin\delta_2.\]

On the acoustic-like branch through zero frequency, all three phase angles are small. Keep the quadratic terms in each cosine and the product of the linear terms in the sines. Constant terms on both sides cancel. Substitution of the optical phases gives

\[(Ka)^2\simeq k_0^2\left[n_1^2d_1^2+n_2^2d_2^2+(n_1^2+n_2^2)d_1d_2\right].\]

The mixed term records the interfaces even at long wavelengths. Factor the bracket as \(a(n_1^2d_1+n_2^2d_2)\) and take the forward-propagating root. Writing the first layer’s thickness fraction as \(f=d_1/a\) yields

\[n_{\rm eff}=\lim_{\omega\to0}\frac{K}{k_0}=\sqrt{fn_1^2+(1-f)n_2^2}.\]

This is the thickness-weighted average of the relative permittivities under a square root. It agrees with a tangential electric field that is nearly uniform across a small cell. As a thickness fraction tends to unity the remaining material’s index is recovered. Near a band edge the omitted fourth-order terms in the trace matter, so a constant effective index cannot predict the gap.

Worked example

Measure the phase of a deeply subwavelength stack

A cell contains \(10.0\,\mathrm{nm}\) of index 2.00 and \(30.0\,\mathrm{nm}\) of index 1.50. Its effective index is \(\sqrt{0.25(4)+0.75(2.25)}=1.64\). At vacuum wavelength \(4.00\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), ten cells accumulate Bloch phase \(2\pi n_{\rm eff}(0.400/4.00)=1.03\) radians. The larger layer phase per cell is just \(0.071\) radians, consistent with the expansion.

Second application

Infer a layer fraction from a low-frequency slope

Another stack uses indices 3.00 and 1.50. Its long-wavelength measured phase velocity is \(c/2.10\). Solving the squared-index average gives \(f=(2.10^2-1.50^2)/(3.00^2-1.50^2)=0.320\). A \(50.0\,\mathrm{nm}\) cell should contain \(16.0\,\mathrm{nm}\) of the high-index material. A microscope can check that inference independently of the optical phase measurement.

OneSimulation laboratory · Open a band gap in a periodic dielectric

Transfer a field by one period

Consider a lossless stack of two nonmagnetic dielectrics at normal incidence. Layer \(j\) has real refractive index \(n_j\), thickness \(d_j\) and optical phase \(\delta_j=2\pi f n_j d_j/c\). Within a layer the electric field is a sum of forward and backward waves; the corresponding magnetic field has the opposite sign for the backward wave. Eliminating the wave amplitudes between the two faces gives the layer transfer matrix for electric field and magnetic field scaled by the vacuum impedance (one time-phase convention):

\[M_j=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta_j&i\sin\delta_j/n_j\\i n_j\sin\delta_j&\cos\delta_j\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det M_j=1 .\]

The tangential fields are continuous at a simple dielectric interface. Thus the matrix of one complete cell of size \(a=d_1+d_2\) is the product of the two layer matrices. Its eigenvalues multiply to one. For propagation in an infinite periodic stack the Bloch multipliers must be \(e^{\pm ika}\). Their sum fixes the half-trace:

\[\cos(ka)=\cos\delta_1\cos\delta_2-\tfrac12(n_1/n_2+n_2/n_1)\sin\delta_1\sin\delta_2 .\]

If the right side lies outside \([-1,1]\), no real \(k\) exists. One solution decays and the reciprocal one grows along the chosen direction. A semi-infinite passive medium selects the decaying solution inside a stop band. A finite sample can still transmit an evanescent field, especially when it contains few cells.

Derive a check that the numerical solver could fail

Set \(n_1=n_2=n\). The right side reduces to \(\cos(\delta_1+\delta_2)\) by the addition formula. The first two folded branches meet where the combined phase is \(\pi\), at \(fa/c=1/(2n)\). There is no interface reflection and no forbidden interval. With \(n=\sqrt{13}\) both branches should meet at about 0.138675. Two nearly equal eigenvalues on a finite mesh must be compared with the numerical resolution before reporting a narrow gap.

As another analytic limit, take each layer a quarter wavelength thick at the same frequency, so \(\delta_1=\delta_2=\pi/2\). The half-trace is \(-\tfrac12(r+r^{-1})\), \(r=n_1/n_2\). Its magnitude exceeds one whenever \(r\ne1\). For \(r=2\) the decaying multiplier has magnitude \(1/2\) per cell. The attenuation here arises from coherent reflections in a lossless model. Small transmission need not mean that electromagnetic energy was dissipated.

Read the finite-element eigenvalues against the calculation

In the OneSimulation band experiment, keep the layer fractions fixed and change only the second permittivity. For the pair (13,12) the two reported zone-edge frequencies are 0.1396 and 0.1433 in reduced units; the gap is about 2.6% of their mean. Compare this separation with the zero-contrast result and with the two roots of the half-trace equation at \(-1\). For the much larger contrast (13,1), using a weak-perturbation approximation would be hard to justify. The transfer matrix still includes repeated scattering exactly for these ideal layers.

When investigating an unfamiliar contrast, halve the spatial mesh size and repeat the edge calculation. Track modes by their fields as well as by sorted eigenvalue if two branches nearly meet. For the scalar one-dimensional normal-incidence problem the observed stop band has a precise meaning. It is an interval of optical frequencies for that propagation geometry. Inferring a complete three-dimensional gap would require other directions and polarisations. The link to electronic bands is the shared translation eigenvalue and coherent coupling at a crossing; electronic filling and the optical transfer-matrix test answer different physical questions.

Turn an infinite stop band into finite-stack reflectance

The forbidden Bloch interval of an infinite periodic medium does not imply perfect reflection from a finite sample. Its termination and number of periods matter. Consider normal incidence on alternating lossless dielectric layers with refractive indices \(n_H,n_L\), each of optical thickness \(\lambda_0/4\) at the selected vacuum wavelength. Treat all permeabilities as \(\mu_0\) and use normalized optical admittance \(Y=n\). Across a quarter-wave layer the characteristic matrix mapping the fields on its far face to its entrance has zero diagonal and off-diagonal entries \(i/Y\) and \(iY\), with an overall phase convention fixed consistently.

For a terminating medium of admittance \(Y_{\rm load}\) the resulting input admittance is \(Y_{\rm layer}^2/Y_{\rm load}\). Applying this inversion first to the rear low-index layer and then to the front high-index layer multiplies load admittance by \((n_H/n_L)^2\). After \(N\) identical pairs, with the high-index layer first as seen by incident light,

\[Y_{\rm in}=n_s(n_H/n_L)^{2N},\qquad R=\left|\frac{n_0-Y_{\rm in}}{n_0+Y_{\rm in}}\right|^2 .\]

Here \(n_s\) is the substrate index and \(n_0\) the incident medium index. This design-frequency result follows from phase accumulation in each layer. For a general wavelength the matrices are no longer simple inversions and both diagonal entries must be retained. Interchanging the order of high and low layers in a finite stack also changes the input admittance and reflection phase.

Select a period count for a required mirror

Let \(n_0=n_s=1.00\), \(n_H=2.00\) and \(n_L=1.50\) at \(\lambda_0=600\) nm. Quarter-wave physical thicknesses are \(600/(4\times2.00)=75.0\) nm and \(600/(4\times1.50)=100\) nm. Four pairs give \(Y_{\rm in}=(4/3)^8=9.99\) and \(R=[(9.99-1)/(9.99+1)]^2=0.669\). Eight pairs give \(Y_{\rm in}\simeq99.8\) and \(R=0.961\). Thus a fairly clear infinite-medium stop band can coexist with substantial transmission through a short specimen.

For at least 99.0 percent reflectance at the design wavelength the required admittance ratio is \((1+\sqrt{0.990})/(1-\sqrt{0.990})\simeq398\). Solving \((4/3)^{2N}\ge398\) gives \(N\ge10.4\), hence eleven complete pairs. If the indices become equal and match both external media, \(Y_{\rm in}=n_0\) for any period count and the reflection vanishes. This finite-stack result tests a different observable from an infinite cell eigenfrequency. It connects the gap calculation to a transmission or reflectance measurement while exposing the additional data, particularly termination and thickness, needed for that prediction.

At the chosen wavelength the reflection amplitude for high-index-first ordering is negative because the effective admittance exceeds the incident admittance. Measuring reflected intensity alone loses this phase sign. Interference with a calibrated reference reflector can recover the sign and identify termination differences even when two samples have similar reflectances. This matters when the periodic mirror forms one end of a resonant cavity: the reflection phase contributes to the total round-trip phase and can move the resonance frequency. A pair of finite mirrors can therefore share a stop-band range while giving different cavity phase conditions.

Derive the quarter-wave stop-band width

The finite mirror is easy to evaluate at the design wavelength. The same stack admits an analytic expression for the width of its infinite first stop band. In both layers the optical phase thickness is \(\delta=(\pi/2)(\omega/\omega_0)\) when refractive indices are treated as nondispersive. The one-period transfer matrix has unit determinant and half-trace \(\cos(Ka)\), where \(K\) is the Bloch wavenumber and \(a\) the physical period. Multiplication of the two layer matrices gives

\[\cos(Ka)=\cos^2\delta-\frac12\left(\frac{n_H}{n_L}+\frac{n_L}{n_H}\right)\sin^2\delta .\]

At band centre the half-trace is less than minus one for unequal indices, so \(K\) has a nonzero imaginary component. At either neighbouring edge set the half-trace equal to minus one and solve for \(\delta\). The two edge phases are \(\pi/2\pm\arcsin[(n_H-n_L)/(n_H+n_L)]\) for \(n_H>n_L\). Their separation gives fractional angular-frequency width

\[\frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0}=\frac4\pi\arcsin\left(\frac{n_H-n_L}{n_H+n_L}\right).\]

With indices 2.00 and 1.50 this is \(0.183\). A central frequency of 500 THz thus has edges near 454 and 546 THz. The frequency interval is symmetric about the centre in this ideal quarter-wave construction; the corresponding wavelength interval is asymmetric because wavelength is reciprocal to frequency. As the two indices merge, the edge separation vanishes even though an artificially repeated unit cell still has a Brillouin-zone boundary.

At centre the decaying Bloch amplitude falls by \(n_L/n_H=0.750\) per period. A physical period of 175 nm therefore gives a field-amplitude decay length \(175/\ln(2/1.5)=608\) nm. This infinite-medium length explains the strong dependence of the finite mirror on period count. The external-medium matching factors are still needed to turn that internal decay into the actual reflected intensity.

Problem sheet 3 · Distinguishing carriers, reflection and attenuation

Test an inference about a material with an independent balance, a limiting case or a second method.

1. Conductivity and Hall data

A film has \(R_H=-6.25\times10^{-4}\) m³/C and \(\sigma=160\) S/m. Assume one isotropic carrier band and \(e=1.60\times10^{-19}\) C. (a) Determine carrier sign and density. (b) Determine the mobility and drift speed at 50 V/m. (c) Explain what becomes ambiguous if a second band is populated.

Recover the two independent quantities

The sign indicates electrons in this model; \(n=1/(e|R_H|)=1.00\times10^{22}\) m⁻³. The mobility is \(\sigma |R_H|=0.100\) m²/(V·s), giving drift speed 5.0 m/s opposite the electric field. With two carrier types the conductivity is a mobility-weighted sum while the Hall numerator contains a signed mobility-squared combination. The two observations alone cannot identify four concentrations and mobilities.

2. Compensation and the mass-action law

Use the positive concentration root

Chapter 7 · Transport, scattering and the mean free path

Connect an equilibrium band structure to a current-carrying distribution and determine what a measured resistivity says about scattering.

From first principles

Derive a transport coefficient from momentum balance

Measure an electrical current in a homogeneous, isotropic conductor with one type of mobile carrier. Approximate the band by a parabolic effective mass \(m^*\), and suppose collisions relax the mean drift momentum with a constant time \(\tau\). The random equilibrium velocities average to zero. A weak spatially uniform electric field gives a carrier of signed charge \(q\) a systematic force \(q\mathbf E\), so the drift equation is

\[m^*\frac{d\mathbf v_d}{dt}=q\mathbf E-\frac{m^*}{\tau}\mathbf v_d .\]

For constant field, set the time derivative to zero after the transient has decayed. Multiplying the resulting drift by charge and by the number density \(n\) converts it into current per area:

\[\mathbf j=nq\mathbf v_d=\frac{nq^2\tau}{m^*}\mathbf E,\]
\[\rho_{\rm el}=\frac{m^*}{nq^2\tau}.\]

The current is along the field for either sign of charge because both the force and the transported charge reverse. A measured resistivity determines a combination of density, mass and scattering time. Holding \(n\) and \(m^*\) fixed makes changes in resistivity a probe of scattering. An activated carrier density in a semiconductor can change the same measurement through a different mechanism.

Derivation and applications

Separate collision time from carrier density with alternating current

A steady conductivity determines a product of carrier density and relaxation time. A field that reverses during a collision time puts an additional timescale into the measurement. Model a spatially uniform field in a single isotropic parabolic band. Take carrier charge \(-e\), mass \(m^*\), density \(n\), and a constant momentum relaxation time \(\tau\). Neglect changes of the equilibrium density, interband absorption and internal field gradients. For a harmonic drive use \(E(t)=\operatorname{Re}[E_0e^{-i\omega t}]\). The mean drift velocity obeys

\[m^*\frac{dv}{dt}=-eE-\frac{m^*v}{\tau},\]
\[(1/\tau-i\omega)v_0=-\frac{e}{m^*}E_0 .\]

Multiplying \(v_0\) by the mobile charge density gives the current density amplitude. The complex conductivity relates it to the applied field,

\[\sigma(\omega)=\frac{J_0}{E_0}=\frac{\sigma_0}{1-i\omega\tau},\]
\[\sigma_0=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m^*}.\]

Multiplication by the complex conjugate of the denominator separates the response that dissipates work from the response a quarter cycle out of phase,

\[\sigma'=\frac{\sigma_0}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\]
\[\frac{\sigma''}{\sigma'}=\omega\tau,\]
\[\langle JE\rangle=\tfrac12\sigma'|E_0|^2 .\]

The power expression uses a peak electric field amplitude. Dissipation drops at high frequency because carriers have less time to develop drift before the driving field reverses. For the time convention above a positive conductivity phase corresponds to a delayed current maximum. The magnitude and phase can test the single-time assumption independently when measured over a range of frequencies.

Worked example

Read a collision time from a phase measurement

At \(f=2.00\,\mathrm{THz}\) a film gives \(\sigma''/\sigma'=0.500\). Thus \(\tau=0.500/(2\pi f)=39.8\,\mathrm{fs}\). If \(\sigma'=8.00\times10^4\,\mathrm{S/m}\), the inferred steady conductivity is \(1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm{S/m}\). With \(m^*=0.250m_e\), the implied density is \(\sigma_0 m^*/(e^2\tau)=2.23\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\).

Second application

Predict the heating under a fixed peak field

A second material has \(\sigma_0=2.50\times10^5\,\mathrm{S/m}\) and \(\tau=0.200\,\mathrm{ps}\). At \(f=1/(2\pi\tau)=0.796\,\mathrm{THz}\) its dissipative conductivity is half its steady value. For a peak field of \(20.0\,\mathrm{V/m}\) it absorbs \(0.5(1.25\times10^5)(20.0)^2=2.50\times10^7\,\mathrm{W/m^3}\). The simultaneous quadrature current equals the in-phase current in amplitude but contributes zero cycle-averaged heating.

Drive an occupation out of equilibrium

In a uniform metal the equilibrium occupation \(f_0(E)\) is symmetric in velocity and has zero current. An electric field changes the crystal momentum through \(\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=-e\mathbf E\). A simple collision model relaxes the occupation toward equilibrium with time \(\tau\). To first order in a steady uniform field, the Boltzmann equation gives \(\delta f=e\tau(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v)\,\partial f_0/\partial E\), where \(e>0\). Since the energy derivative is negative, states moving opposite the field become more occupied. Multiplying by electron charge gives a conventional current along the applied field.

Collecting velocities in that current yields, for an isotropic band including both spins,

\[\sigma=\frac{e^2}{3}\int v(E)^2\tau(E)D_V(E)\left(-\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial E}\right)dE .\]

Here \(D_V\) is density of states per unit volume. The factor one third averages a squared velocity component in three dimensions. The derivative of the occupation restricts conduction in a degenerate metal to energies near \(E_F\). A high density of states at one energy is insufficient on its own to predict high conductivity; velocities and transport lifetimes also enter.

Recover the Drude result as a special case

At low temperature replace \(-\partial f_0/\partial E\) by a sharp weight at \(E_F\). With a parabolic band and constant \(\tau\), \(v_F^2=2E_F/m^*\) and \(D_V(E_F)=3n/(2E_F)\), giving \(\sigma=ne^2\tau/m^*\). For \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(m^*=m_e\) and resistivity \(1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\), the inferred lifetime is \(2.46\times10^{-14}\) s. With \(v_F=1.57\times10^6\) m/s the transport mean free path is about 39 nm. This is much larger than an atomic spacing yet small relative to many bulk specimen dimensions.

Do not multiply \(\tau\) by the much smaller average drift speed to estimate the mean free path. Scattering changes the momentum of already fast electrons; the tiny net imbalance in their occupations gives the much slower average drift. Likewise a transport lifetime weights deflections by how much they relax a directed current. Small-angle scattering can broaden a quantum state considerably while relaxing directed momentum relatively slowly.

Interpret a temperature sweep

Static impurities can give an approximately temperature-independent residual resistivity, while phonons add temperature-dependent scattering. If independent mechanisms have approximately additive transport rates, \(\rho\simeq\rho_{\rm imp}+\rho_{\rm ph}(T)\). This form is a model with a roughly common transport mass and carrier density. A temperature sweep through a carrier-density crossover or a change in band population calls for more information, such as Hall data. At low temperatures boundary scattering can become important once the nominal bulk mean free path approaches the sample width.

Compare heat and charge transport

Within a degenerate, nearly elastic model the same carriers also move excess energy. Integrating the squared energy relative to the chemical potential across the narrow thermal shell gives the Wiedemann–Franz ratio \(\kappa_{\rm el}/(\sigma T)\simeq\pi^2 k_B^2/(3e^2)\). In SI units the ratio is about \(2.44\times10^{-8}\) W Ω K⁻². A measurement of total thermal conductivity includes phonons as well; subtracting this estimated electronic contribution is model dependent. A discrepancy can indicate inelastic scattering, lattice heat conduction or a breakdown of the simple band description.

Resolve electrical transport by carrier energy

A temperature gradient drives particles above and below the chemical potential in different directions. If their contributions to electrical current cancel exactly, no thermoelectric field is needed. Any asymmetry in the transport of the two groups leaves a field at open circuit. Introduce the energy-resolved transport function \(\Xi(E)\), proportional to the density of states times mean squared velocity along the measured direction times relaxation time. For ordinary electron charge \(-e\) with \(e>0\), define transport moments

\[L_j=\int dE\,\Xi(E)(E-\mu)^j[-\partial f/\partial E],\qquad S=-\frac{L_1}{eTL_0}.\]

This sign convention makes a simple electron band with increasing transport function have negative Seebeck coefficient. The derivative of the Fermi function is sharply centred on \(\mu\) in a degenerate material, with width of order \(k_BT\). Expand \(\Xi(E)=\Xi(\mu)+(E-\mu)\Xi'(\mu)+\cdots\). Odd moments of the symmetric thermal window vanish. Then \(L_0\simeq\Xi(\mu)\), while the first nonzero contribution to \(L_1\) is \(\Xi'(\mu)(\pi^2/3)(k_BT)^2\). This yields the low-temperature Mott relation

\[S\simeq-\frac{\pi^2k_B^2T}{3e}\left.\frac{d\ln\Xi}{dE}\right|_{E_F}.\]

A large conductivity by itself does not determine thermopower. Multiplying the whole transport function by a constant doubles conductivity but leaves its logarithmic slope and the leading Seebeck coefficient unchanged. The result assumes an electronic diffusive regime with sufficiently smooth energy dependence over the thermal window. Drag by nonequilibrium phonons or an abrupt band edge can violate these assumptions.

Infer the energy dependence of scattering

For a three-dimensional parabolic electron band let \(E\) be measured upward from its bottom. The density of states scales as \(E^{1/2}\) and the squared speed as \(E\). If \(\tau(E)\propto E^r\), the transport function scales as \(E^{3/2+r}\). At \(T=100\) K and \(E_F=0.200\) eV the ratio \(k_BT/E_F=0.0431\). With energy-independent scattering time the relation gives \(S=-(\pi^2/3)(k_B/e)(0.0431)(1.5)=-18.3\) μV/K. A 5.00 K temperature difference therefore produces a thermovoltage of magnitude about \(91.5\) μV in a measurement correcting for the reference leads.

If an independent band measurement supports the same Fermi energy but the observed coefficient is \(-24.4\) μV/K, the inferred exponent is \(3/2+r\simeq2.00\) or \(r\simeq0.50\) in this model. This inference uses a derivative and is sensitive to additional bands. For a particle–hole symmetric transport function around \(\mu\) the odd moment vanishes and the predicted diffusive thermopower is zero even when electrical conductivity is large.

The Seebeck coefficient is usually measured relative to metallic leads, and equilibrium contact potentials are present even at zero temperature difference. Reversing the thermal gradient reverses the sought thermovoltage while leaving a fixed voltage offset unchanged. Taking half the difference of two reversed readings removes such an offset. A temperature-dependent reference lead contribution still has to be calibrated or subtracted. Without that measurement, extracting a scattering exponent from a few tens of microvolts can assign properties of the lead material to the sample even if the transport-function derivation itself is correct.

Taking readings at several gradient magnitudes further tests linearity of the fitted Seebeck response.

Chapter 8 · Cyclotron motion and quantised magnetic orbits

Relate a magnetic-field measurement to band curvature, level spacing and a cross-section of the Fermi surface.

From first principles

Find the orbit and energy spacing imposed by a magnetic field

Consider an electron in an isotropic parabolic band with effective mass \(m^*\), no electric field and a uniform magnetic induction \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\). The Lorentz force is perpendicular to velocity, so it changes the direction of the transverse velocity without changing its magnitude. Taking absolute values in the radial force balance gives

\[\frac{m^* v_\perp^2}{r_c}=e v_\perp B,\]
\[r_c=\frac{m^*v_\perp}{eB},\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m^*}.\]

To obtain energy levels one also needs quantum mechanics. Choose \(\mathbf A=(0,Bx,0)\) and electron charge \(-e\) with \(e>0\). The Hamiltonian \((\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2/(2m^*)\) is independent of \(y,z\), so their canonical momenta can be treated as fixed in a plane-wave state. Complete the square:

\[H=\frac{p_x^2}{2m^*}+\frac{m^*\omega_c^2}{2}\left(x+\frac{p_y}{eB}\right)^2+\frac{p_z^2}{2m^*}.\]

This is a harmonic oscillator about a centre set by \(p_y\). Its transverse levels have energies \(\hbar\omega_c(n+1/2)\); longitudinal translation remains free. Spin splitting, anisotropic band curvature and collision broadening require further information. The same frequency occurs in the classical orbit and the quantum level spacing for distinct reasons made explicit by these two constructions.

Derivation and applications

Determine which mass a cyclotron resonance measures

An orbit on an anisotropic band need not be circular. Consider a weak uniform magnetic induction \(B\) along \(z\), and motion in a single band with elliptical constant-energy contours in the \(xy\) plane. Neglect scattering over an orbit, electric fields, and spin splitting. Write the local dispersion as \(E=\hbar^2 k_x^2/(2m_x)+\hbar^2 k_y^2/(2m_y)\), measured from its minimum. For charge \(-e\), the semiclassical force law and the velocity components give

\[\dot k_x=-\frac{eB}{m_y}k_y,\]
\[\dot k_y=\frac{eB}{m_x}k_x .\]

Taking a second derivative cancels the other component. Both coordinates oscillate at the same fundamental angular frequency even though their amplitudes differ,

\[\ddot k_x=-\frac{e^2B^2}{m_xm_y}k_x,\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m_c},\]
\[m_c=\sqrt{m_xm_y}.\]

A geometric measurement leads to the same mass. The two semiaxes of the contour at energy \(E\) are \(\sqrt{2m_iE}/\hbar\). Its reciprocal-space area \(A(E)\) and energy derivative are

\[A(E)=\frac{2\pi E}{\hbar^2}\sqrt{m_xm_y},\]
\[\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\frac{dA}{dE}=m_c .\]

The derivative explains how a measurement of orbit area over a small energy range tests the resonance result. Two bands with the same area at one energy can have different derivatives there. For a circle, both principal masses and the cyclotron mass coincide. A single resonance frequency for this orientation measures their geometric mean and leaves their ratio undetermined.

Worked example

Predict the resonance of an elliptical pocket

A layer has \(m_x=0.100m_e\), \(m_y=0.900m_e\) and is placed in \(B=1.50\,\mathrm T\). The cyclotron mass is \(0.300m_e\), so the ordinary frequency is \(f_c=eB/(2\pi m_c)=140\,\mathrm{GHz}\). The period is \(7.14\,\mathrm{ps}\). The long reciprocal-space semiaxis lies along \(y\) and is three times the short one, even though both components complete their cycle in exactly the same time.

Second application

Use two nearby contour areas as a mass measurement

In a different sample the orbit area rises by \(0.200\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}\) when energy rises by \(20.0\,\mathrm{meV}\). Treat the measured slope as locally constant. In SI units \(dA/dE=2.00\times10^{17}/(3.204\times10^{-21})\,\mathrm{m^{-2}J^{-1}}\). The area formula gives \(m_c=1.10\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}=0.121m_e\). An independent determination of \(m_x=0.0600m_e\) would then imply \(m_y=m_c^2/m_x=0.245m_e\) at the displayed precision.

Follow a wave packet in a band

A narrow wave packet in a well-isolated band moves with \(\mathbf v=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}E\). In a static uniform magnetic field the semiclassical force law is \(\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=-e\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\). Thus \(\nabla E\cdot\dot{\mathbf k}=0\): the energy is constant because a magnetic field does no work. The wavevector component along \(\mathbf B\) is also constant. The orbit in reciprocal space lies along the intersection of a constant-energy surface and a plane perpendicular to the field. Tilting the field samples different sections of a nonspherical Fermi surface.

For an isotropic parabolic band, motion transverse to the field is circular at \(\omega_c=eB/m^*\). The real-space orbit radius is \(v_\perp/\omega_c\). One can measure an effective mass from the resonant angular frequency without separately knowing the elastic scattering time. Whether the motion survives long enough to be resolved depends on \(\omega_c\tau\). A very short scattering time smears the resonance and suppresses oscillatory magnetotransport.

Derive the quantised spacing

For a two-dimensional electron choose \(A_y=Bx\) and \(A_x=0\). Since the Hamiltonian for charge \(-e\) is \(H=[p_x^2+(p_y+eBx)^2]/(2m^*)\), a state of definite \(p_y=\hbar k_y\) leaves a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator in \(x\), centred at \(-\hbar k_y/(eB)\). Its levels are \(E_j=(j+1/2)\hbar\omega_c\), neglecting spin. At fixed level, changing \(k_y\) moves the orbit centre without changing its energy. The centres allowed inside a rectangle of area \(A\) count to \(eBA/h\) per spin, up to edge corrections. In three dimensions motion along the field adds a dispersing kinetic-energy term.

Take \(B=5\) T and \(m^*=0.10m_e\). The orbital spacing is about 5.79 meV, equivalent to \(k_B T\) at 67 K. At 4 K the thermal scale is about 0.345 meV, smaller than the spacing. An observation still requires sufficiently weak disorder broadening and appropriate carrier filling. As the field changes, both the level spacing and the number of states carried by each level change.

Extract a reciprocal-space area from a measurement

For a closed semiclassical orbit of area \(A_k(E)\), quantisation gives \(A_k=2\pi eB(j+\gamma)/\hbar\), where the phase offset \(\gamma\) depends on the orbit. Successive levels crossing the Fermi energy give oscillations periodic in reciprocal field with frequency \(F=\hbar A_k(E_F)/(2\pi e)\), expressed in tesla. For \(F=100\) T, \(A_k\simeq9.55\times10^{17}\) m⁻². A circular section of that area has radius \(5.51\times10^8\) m⁻¹. In a three-dimensional metal dominant oscillations come from extremal sections. Inferring a full Fermi volume from one frequency requires additional assumptions about shape and symmetry; measurements at several orientations can test them.

Count orbit centres and Hall filling

Landau level spacing determines the energy cost of changing orbital index. A second question is how many independent states fit into each level. Take a rectangular two-dimensional layer of area \(L_xL_y\), magnetic field \(B\) normal to it and Landau gauge \(A_y=Bx\). Translation symmetry along \(y\) gives wavevectors \(k_y=2\pi j/L_y\). In the transverse Hamiltonian the term \((\hbar k_y+eBx)^2/(2m^*)\) describes a harmonic oscillator with centre \(x_0=-\hbar k_y/(eB)\).

To place an orbit centre inside the sample, \(k_y\) must range over an interval of length \(eBL_x/\hbar\). Dividing by the allowed spacing \(2\pi/L_y\) gives \(N_\phi=eBL_xL_y/h\) bulk states per spin-resolved orbital level, neglecting edge corrections. Each magnetic flux quantum \(h/e\) adds one state per level. For sheet density \(n_s\) define filling factor \(\nu=n_sh/(eB)\). This counts occupied spin-resolved levels when it is an integer; if a residual spin degeneracy is unresolved, levels arrive in pairs.

In an ideal low-temperature integer quantum Hall state, with the chemical potential in a mobility gap, longitudinal dissipation can vanish and Hall conductance magnitude is \(\nu e^2/h\). The classical expression \(n_se/B\) has the same value at exact integer filling, but alone cannot explain persistence of plateaus over a finite interval of field. Localization and edge transport are essential for that observation.

Locate an integer filling in a measurement

A two-dimensional electron system has \(n_s=3.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻² and effective mass \(0.067m_e\). The field for total filling \(\nu=2\) is \(B=n_sh/(2e)=6.20\) T. The number of states per area per spin-resolved level is \(eB/h=1.50\times10^{15}\) m⁻², half the measured sheet density. The orbital spacing at this field is \(\hbar eB/m^*=10.7\) meV. At 4.2 K the thermal energy is 0.362 meV, so thermal smearing is smaller than the orbital gap; disorder and spin structure still matter.

The ideal Hall resistance magnitude on the \(\nu=2\) plateau is \(h/(2e^2)=12.9\) kΩ. A weak current of \(0.500\) μA would produce a transverse voltage magnitude of \(6.45\) mV. Increasing the field at fixed density decreases filling and can move the Fermi level into another broadened level. A simultaneous longitudinal voltage measurement is indispensable: a Hall voltage of the right numerical size at a single field can also occur in a classical conductor. A broad flat Hall trace together with a longitudinal-resistance minimum gives stronger evidence for quantized transport.

Changing the sample area at constant sheet density and magnetic field increases the number of occupied particles and the number of orbit centres in the same proportion. Filling factor and the ideal Hall resistance therefore remain unchanged, even though total particle count can be very different. In contrast, a gate that varies sheet density at fixed field moves the layer through successive fillings. The expected density spacing per spin-resolved level is \(eB/h\). A gate-calibrated scan can thus test Landau degeneracy independently of the cyclotron energy spacing inferred from optical or thermal observations.

Chapter 9 · Magnetic moments and susceptibility

Derive thermal magnetisation for a two-state moment and distinguish its signature from the response of a filled electronic sea.

From first principles

Obtain the Curie law by counting two magnetic orientations

Take many identical, independent localized moments per volume \(n\), each with component \(+\mu\) or \(-\mu\) along an applied induction \(B\). The magnetic energies are \(-\mu B\) and \(+\mu B\). At temperature \(T\) their probabilities are proportional to their Boltzmann factors. With \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\), normalize the two weights and subtract their contributions to the mean:

\[Z=e^x+e^{-x},\]
\[\langle m_z\rangle=\mu\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}=\mu\tanh x .\]

Multiply by number density to get magnetization. In weak fields or at high temperatures \(x\ll1\), so the leading term in \(\tanh x\) gives

\[M\simeq\frac{n\mu^2 B}{k_BT},\]
\[\chi=\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\simeq\frac{\mu_0 n\mu^2}{k_BT}.\]

The susceptibility expression uses a weakly magnetic sample with \(B\simeq\mu_0 H\) and negligible demagnetizing correction. At low temperature and fixed nonzero field both the expansion and the \(1/T\) extrapolation fail. The exact two-state average saturates at \(n\mu\) because no moment can contribute more than \(\mu\). Exchange between neighbours can also invalidate the independent weights.

Derivation and applications

Distinguish the saturation moment from the Curie moment

An ion with angular momentum quantum number \(J\) can have more than two projections on a magnetic field. Consider independent identical ions of number density \(n\) in thermal equilibrium, with isotropic Landé factor \(g\) and negligible splitting at zero field. Label the levels \(m=-J,\ldots,J\) so that their moments along the applied induction \(B\) are \(g\mu_Bm\). Adjacent Zeeman levels are then separated by \(g\mu_B B\). Introduce \(x=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)\). Weighing each level with its Boltzmann factor gives

\[Z=\sum_{m=-J}^{J}e^{mx},\]
\[M=ng\mu_B\,\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial x}.\]

In weak fields each zero-field state is almost equally likely. The zeroth-order sum of \(m\) vanishes by symmetry. The next term in the numerator is \(x\sum m^2\), while the denominator is \(2J+1\) to this order. Using the sum of squares of the allowed projections gives

\[\langle m\rangle\simeq x\frac{\sum_{m=-J}^J m^2}{2J+1}=\frac{J(J+1)}{3}x .\]

For a dilute specimen take \(B\simeq\mu_0H\). The dimensionless volume susceptibility is the derivative of \(M\) with respect to magnetic field strength \(H\). In strong fields the highest projection alone survives. These limits identify two different combinations of the microscopic parameters,

\[\chi=\frac{\mu_0 n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_BT},\]
\[M_{\rm sat}=ng\mu_B J .\]

The Curie-law effective moment is \(g\mu_B\sqrt{J(J+1)}\), whereas the aligned projection is \(g\mu_BJ\). Their ratio approaches one for large \(J\). A susceptibility fit requires \(Jx\ll1\); a saturation measurement requires the opposite tendency and sufficiently weak interactions between moments.

Worked example

Predict the response of dilute spin-one ions

Take \(J=1\), \(g=2.00\), \(n=1.00\times10^{26}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), and \(T=10.0\,\mathrm K\). The aligned magnetization is \(1.85\times10^3\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The Curie formula gives \(\chi=2.09\times10^{-4}\). At \(B=0.100\,\mathrm T\), \(Jx=0.0134\), and the predicted magnetization is only \(\chi B/\mu_0=16.6\,\mathrm{A/m}\), consistent with weak polarization.

Second application

Identify a moment from two calibrated measurements

Suppose independent concentration measurements in another material yield a saturation moment of \(3.00\mu_B\) per ion and an effective Curie moment of \(3.87\mu_B\). The squared ratio is \(1+1/J=(3.87/3.00)^2=1.66\), giving \(J\simeq1.50\). The saturation value then gives \(g\simeq2.00\). Susceptibility alone fixes only a product of the factor \(g\) and the size of the angular momentum.

A moment has an energy as well as a direction

Consider \(N\) independent identical magnetic moments in volume \(V\), each with allowed projection \(\pm\mu\) along a uniform applied induction \(B\). The energies are \(\mp\mu B\); the state aligned with the field has lower energy. For \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) the one-moment partition function is \(Z_1=2\cosh(\beta\mu B)\). From \(\langle m_z\rangle=k_BT\,\partial_B\ln Z_1\) obtain

\[M=n\mu\tanh(\mu B/k_BT),\qquad n=N/V .\]

The full curve approaches \(n\mu\) at high field or low temperature. In the weak-field regime \(\mu B\ll k_BT\), expansion of the hyperbolic tangent gives \(M\simeq n\mu^2 B/(k_BT)\). Under weak magnetisation and negligible demagnetising corrections use \(B\simeq\mu_0H\); the dimensionless SI susceptibility \(\partial M/\partial H\) is then \(\chi=\mu_0n\mu^2/(k_BT)\). Be explicit about differentiating with respect to \(B\) or \(H\), which carry different units.

Work a moment count from two measurements

Suppose a dilute two-state sample exhibits saturation magnetisation \(9.27\times10^3\) A/m and the moments are known to have \(\mu=\mu_B=9.27\times10^{-24}\) J/T. The implied density is \(1.00\times10^{27}\) m⁻³. At 10 K and 1 T the argument \(\mu B/k_BT\) is 0.0672, safely in the linear range for a rough estimate: \(M\simeq622\) A/m. At the same temperature a field of about 14.9 T gives argument one and \(M/M_\mathrm{sat}=0.762\). Fitting a straight line up to that field would underestimate the zero-field slope.

If the moment magnitude is unknown, saturation determines \(n\mu\) and the small-field slope supplies \(n\mu^2\). Taken together they can separate density from moment within this model. A mixture of moment species, orbital contributions or interactions can defeat this two-equation inference. Plotting curves at several temperatures against \(B/T\) tests the independent-moment scaling without needing to know the overall density beforehand.

Compare with itinerant spin response

For a weakly perturbed degenerate metal, the two spin species shift by \(\mp\mu B\). Almost all states below \(E_F\) remain doubly occupied, so only a thin shell at the Fermi surface changes its net spin. At low temperature and nearly constant total density of states \(D_V(E_F)\), the spin imbalance is approximately \(D_V(E_F)\mu B\), giving \(M\simeq\mu^2D_V(E_F)B\). The leading Pauli response is nearly temperature independent, in contrast to the \(1/T\) scaling of independent localised moments. This argument addresses spin susceptibility; orbital diamagnetism also contributes to a real measured susceptibility.

Diagnose a small impurity contribution

If bulk data show a roughly constant offset plus a low-temperature upturn, test a simple additive model \(\chi=\chi_0+C/T\) over a weak-field interval. Plot \(\chi\) against \(1/T\) rather than taking an inverse before subtracting the offset. An upturn can arise from a dilute impurity population even when the host electronic sea dominates heat capacity. If scaling fails systematically, examine interactions between moments; the next chapter shows how cooperative ordering changes the small-field susceptibility.

Cool local moments by adiabatic demagnetization

Independent moments have an entropy that depends on the ratio of magnetic field to temperature. For a two-state moment of magnitude \(\mu\), energies are \(\mp\mu B\). Define \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\). The one-moment partition function is \(2\cosh x\), average energy is \(-\mu B\tanh x\), and the magnetic entropy follows from \(S=k_B\ln Z+U/T\),

\[\frac{S_{\rm spin}}{Nk_B}=\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x .\]

At zero field for fixed positive temperature the moment has two equiprobable orientations and contributes \(k_B\ln2\). At large \(x\) nearly all moments favour the lower state and the spin entropy tends to zero. If field is increased at fixed temperature, this reduction of spin entropy expels heat to a bath. Now isolate the material and reduce the field quasistatically. In the ideal spin-dominated limit entropy remains fixed, so \(x\) remains fixed and \(T/B\) is constant.

Thermal isolation and sufficiently slow variation are both required. The first prevents external heat from entering, and the second allows internal degrees of freedom to follow equilibrium. Other stores of entropy, particularly lattice vibrations or mobile electrons, participate in an actual specimen. The pure spin scaling can then be modified even when the process as a whole is perfectly adiabatic.

Estimate temperature and cooling capacity

Take \(N=1.00\times10^{21}\) independent moments each of magnitude \(\mu_B\), initially at 2.00 K. Magnetize isothermally from negligible field to 3.00 T, using \(\mu_B/k_B=0.6717\) K/T. The final magnetic parameter is \(x=1.01\), giving spin entropy per particle about \(0.362k_B\) compared with the initial \(0.693k_B\). The heat passed to the bath in that reversible isothermal step is \(T N k_B(0.693-0.362)=9.14\) mJ. Work is supplied by the field source during preparation.

Isolate and reduce field from 3.00 T to 0.300 T. Holding \(x\) fixed predicts \(T_f=2.00(0.300/3.00)=0.200\) K. Further field reduction in the ideal noninteracting equations would approach zero temperature. Real moments interact, however, and internal effective fields or ordering lift their near-zero-field degeneracy. Thermal leaks and finite relaxation rates also become increasingly important. A practical test records temperature against field on several ramp rates; dependence on the rate can distinguish irreversible heating from a reproducible correction to the equilibrium entropy model. The final cold sample can absorb only finite heat before its moments become disordered, even if the predicted endpoint temperature is very low.

A second check comes from magnetization along the ideal isolated sweep. Since \(M=N\mu\tanh x\) at fixed volume and \(x\) is conserved, the spin magnetization should stay almost constant while both the external field and the temperature fall. In contrast, an isothermal field decrease reduces \(x\) and magnetization. Measuring temperature alone cannot establish that the sample really followed an isentropic spin path. A simultaneous magnetic reading tests the fixed-population interpretation. Deviations concentrated at the weakest fields can signal interactions between moments rather than a general failure of the high-field entropy estimate.

Chapter 10 · Exchange, spontaneous magnetisation and critical response

Use a mean-field model and a free-energy expansion to explain how a macroscopic moment can arise in zero applied field.

From first principles

How an interaction permits spontaneous magnetization

Put an Ising spin \(s_i=\pm1\) on each lattice site and use a ferromagnetic bond energy \(-J s_i s_j\) with \(J>0\). If every site has \(z\) neighbours, replacing their actual spins by the common mean \(m=\langle s\rangle\) assigns a trial spin energy \(-s(zJm+\mu B)\). Thermal averaging this two-level problem must reproduce the same mean used to construct the local field:

\[m=\frac{\sum_{s=\pm1}s\, e^{s(zJm+\mu B)/(k_BT)}}{\sum_{s=\pm1}e^{s(zJm+\mu B)/(k_BT)}}=\tanh\!\left(\frac{zJm+\mu B}{k_BT}\right).\]

At zero applied field the right-hand side is odd, so \(m=0\) always solves the equation. To decide when two nearby nonzero solutions appear, compare the slope at the origin with one, then retain the cubic correction. Write \(a=zJ/(k_BT)\):

\[m=am-\tfrac13a^3m^3+\cdots,\]
\[T_c^{\rm MF}=zJ/k_B,\]
\[m^2\simeq3(a-1)/a^3 .\]

Just below this mean-field critical temperature either sign of \(m\) is possible even in zero applied field. The approximation replaces correlated neighbours by an average. In short-range one-dimensional systems that omission can even change whether a finite-temperature transition exists.

Derivation and applications

Calculate a correlation length without a mean-field transition

Mean-field theory replaces the neighbours of each spin by a common average. A chain gives a case in which the energy of a local reversal can instead be counted exactly. Take an open chain of Ising spins \(s_i=\pm1\), zero external field, and ferromagnetic nearest-neighbour interaction \(J>0\). The energy is \(-J\sum_i s_is_{i+1}\). Specify the first spin and all bond variables \(b_i=s_i s_{i+1}\). This is a one-to-one description of every configuration. Since the energy is a sum over these independent binary bonds, each bond has weights \(e^{K}\) and \(e^{-K}\), where \(K=J/(k_BT)\).

\[p_{\rm wall}=P(b_i=-1)=\frac{1}{1+e^{2K}},\]
\[\langle b_i\rangle=1-2p_{\rm wall}=\tanh K .\]

A wall costs \(2J\) compared with an aligned bond. To compare two spins separated by \(r\) bonds, multiply the intervening \(b_i\). Every interior spin then occurs twice and drops out. Bond independence makes the average of this product particularly simple,

\[\langle s_i s_{i+r}\rangle=\left\langle\prod_{j=i}^{i+r-1} b_j\right\rangle=(\tanh K)^r .\]

Writing the result as an exponential in the physical separation \(ra\), where \(a\) is the site spacing, gives

\[\xi=-\frac{a}{\ln(\tanh K)},\]
\[\xi\simeq\frac a2 e^{2K}\quad (K\gg1).\]

At low temperature the last expression follows by expanding \(\tanh K\simeq1-2e^{-2K}\). At every positive temperature the probability of a wall remains positive. Thus widely separated spins lose correlation in this short-range one-dimensional model. A finite sample shorter than \(\xi\) can still look ordered during a measurement.

Worked example

Estimate decorrelation over a microscope field

Let \(J/k_B=20.0\,\mathrm K\), \(T=10.0\,\mathrm K\) and \(a=0.400\,\mathrm{nm}\). The wall probability is \(1/(1+e^4)=0.0180\), giving about 18 walls per 1000 bonds on average. The exact correlation length is \(10.9\,\mathrm{nm}\). Across \(20.0\,\mathrm{nm}\), or 50 bonds, the measured spin correlation should be approximately \((\tanh2)^{50}=0.160\).

Second application

Infer exchange from a measured spatial decay

In a different chain the correlation drops to one half over eight bonds at \(T=30.0\,\mathrm K\). Each step has correlation \(2^{-1/8}=0.9170\). Inverting \(K=\tfrac12\ln[(1+\langle b\rangle)/(1-\langle b\rangle)]\) gives \(J/k_B=47.1\,\mathrm K\). A local image counting opposite neighbours should then find a fraction \((1-0.9170)/2=0.0415\), an independent test using adjacent spins.

Replace neighbouring moments by their average

An independent moment has zero mean at zero field for any positive temperature. In a ferromagnet aligned neighbours can lower the exchange energy. Consider Ising variables \(s_i=\pm1\) with energy \(-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_i s_j-\mu B\sum_i s_i\), \(J>0\). Each distinct nearest-neighbour bond is counted once. In a uniform mean-field approximation replace a neighbour's spin by \(m=\langle s\rangle\). For coordination number \(z\), an individual spin then feels an effective energy bias \(zJm+\mu B\). Applying the two-state result to that spin requires self-consistency:

\[m=\tanh[(zJm+\mu B)/(k_BT)] .\]

At \(B=0\), \(m=0\) always solves the equation. The slope of the right-hand side at the origin is \(zJ/(k_BT)\). Above the mean-field transition temperature \(T_c=zJ/k_B\) only the disordered solution exists. Below it two symmetry-related nonzero stable solutions appear. The sign selected in a macroscopic region can depend on a small field and on its history.

Approach the transition from either side

For small field above \(T_c\), expand \(\tanh x\simeq x\). This gives \(m\simeq\mu B/[k_B(T-T_c)]\), a Curie–Weiss susceptibility. A positive intercept of an inverse-susceptibility plot can indicate dominant ferromagnetic exchange over a suitable high-temperature interval. It is not an exact measurement of the true ordering temperature, particularly when fluctuations, geometry or competing interactions matter.

Just below \(T_c\), at zero field, expand through the cubic term. Writing \(t=T_c/T\), the nonzero solution has \(m^2\simeq3(t-1)/t^3\), approaching \(3(T_c-T)/T_c\) sufficiently near the transition. The square-root onset is a mean-field prediction. A finite-temperature transition of this type does not exist for a one-dimensional short-range Ising chain in the thermodynamic limit, despite the same simple self-consistency calculation predicting one. Neglecting the cost and entropy of fluctuations can qualitatively change a prediction.

Interpret the curvature of a free energy

A local free energy even under \(m\mapsto-m\) at zero field may be expanded as \(f=\tfrac12a(T-T_c)m^2+\tfrac14 b m^4-hm\), \(a,b>0\). At \(h=0\), the minima sit at 0 above the transition and at \(m=\pm\sqrt{a(T_c-T)/b}\) below it. A positive second derivative indicates local stability, and the differential response \(dm/dh=1/f''\) grows as the curvature weakens. For \(a(T-T_c)=-1,b=4\) in scaled units, the minima are at \(\pm0.5\), have curvature 2 and are lower than the intervening maximum by 0.0625 in free-energy density units.

From a local moment to a macroscopic measurement

Different regions can select opposite stable magnetisations. Domain walls cost exchange and anisotropy energy, while domain formation can reduce the magnetic field energy outside a specimen. A small measured zero-field net moment can therefore coexist with substantial local order. A static uniform free-energy graph alone cannot predict coercivity: wall pinning, nucleation, defects and measurement times affect a hysteresis loop. Temperature-dependent susceptibility, local probes and field-cycling measurements answer complementary questions about the same ordered sample.

Derive the profile and energy of a domain wall

Two domains magnetized in opposite easy-axis directions can coexist, but rotating the magnetization in between costs exchange and anisotropy energy. Consider a uniaxial ferromagnet with magnetization magnitude fixed and angle \(\theta(x)\) relative to the easy axis varying along one spatial coordinate. Assume a planar 180-degree wall in a geometry where additional magnetostatic costs can be neglected for this profile. With exchange stiffness \(A\) in J/m and anisotropy density \(K\) in J/m³, the wall energy per unit area is

\[\sigma_w=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[A(\partial_x\theta)^2+K\sin^2\theta]\,dx .\]

A narrow wall saves anisotropy energy but makes the spatial gradient steep. A broad wall saves exchange energy but places more material away from the preferred axis. Variation gives \(2A\theta''=2K\sin\theta\cos\theta\). Multiply by \(\theta'\) and integrate once. Since both gradient and anisotropy term vanish deep inside either domain, the integration constant is zero, yielding \(A(\theta')^2=K\sin^2\theta\). Exchange and anisotropy costs are locally equal on the minimum-energy profile.

Take increasing \(\theta\) between zero and \(\pi\) and define \(\Delta=\sqrt{A/K}\). Separation gives \(d\theta/\sin\theta=dx/\Delta\). With the wall centred at zero, \(\tan(\theta/2)=\exp(x/\Delta)\). Inserting the first integral back into the wall energy and changing variable from \(x\) to \(\theta\) gives \(\sigma_w=2K\Delta\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta=4\sqrt{AK}\). The profile has long tails, so a quoted wall width must state its convention; \(\pi\Delta\) is a frequently used slope-based width rather than a sharp boundary.

Compare two anisotropies at fixed exchange stiffness

Use \(A=1.00\times10^{-11}\) J/m and \(K=1.00\times10^5\) J/m³. The scale \(\Delta\) is 10.0 nm and conventional width \(\pi\Delta\) is 31.4 nm. Wall energy is \(4.00\) mJ/m². A planar patch of area \(1.00\) μm² costs \(4.00\times10^{-15}\) J, even though the two far domains have the same zero-field bulk energy.

If a second specimen at the same temperature has four times the anisotropy and the same exchange stiffness, the predicted width halves while the cost per area doubles. The combination of width and energy could therefore independently estimate \(A\) and \(K\), whereas either measurement by itself fixes only a ratio or a product. In the limit \(K\to0\) the preferred axis loses meaning and the wall spreads indefinitely in this model. Finite sample size, magnetostatic fields, boundaries and defects then set spatial scales omitted from the one-dimensional calculation.

One can also express the measured magnetization components explicitly. With the local moment in a fixed rotation plane, the easy-axis component is proportional to \(\cos\theta=-\tanh(x/\Delta)\) and the transverse component to \(\sin\theta=1/\cosh(x/\Delta)\). The easy-axis profile passes smoothly through zero even though the moment magnitude remains fixed throughout the wall. A probe sensitive only to that component might incorrectly interpret the central zero as suppression of ferromagnetism. Measuring a transverse component or a quantity sensitive to the full moment tests the distinction between a rotating order parameter and a vanishing one.

Distinguish a soft magnetic response from a loss of stability

In zero applied field the lowest stabilising even expansion is \(f=a m^2/2+m^4/4\) in these units. Vary the quadratic coefficient through zero. Equilibrium requires both stationarity and positive curvature at a minimum.

Chapter 11 · Superconductivity, magnetic screening and flux quantisation

Separate persistent current from magnetic flux expulsion and derive two measurable length and flux scales of a superconducting state.

From first principles

Connect a material current law to magnetic screening

A vanishing electrical resistance by itself allows an already existing current to persist. To predict the equilibrium field profile in a thick superconductor one needs additional physical input. In a uniform isotropic superconductor with no vortices the second London relation connects the curl of the supercurrent to the induction. Here \(n_s\) counts carriers of mass \(m\) and signed charge \(q\). Combine this constitutive law with static Ampère’s law:

\[\nabla\times\mathbf j_s=-\frac{n_s q^2}{m}\mathbf B,\]
\[\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j_s .\]

Take a second curl of the field law and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). The sign from the vector identity cancels that in the London relation:

\[\nabla^2\mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf B}{\lambda_L^2},\]
\[\lambda_L=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0n_s q^2}}.\]

Fill \(x>0\) with the material and impose a parallel surface field \(B(0)=B_0\). With no field growth deep in the bulk, only one exponential survives: \(B(x)=B_0e^{-x/\lambda_L}\). Thus the single measured length constrains a ratio \(m/(n_s q^2)\). This construction assumes linear local response and a field low enough for the vortex-free state and its prescribed surface condition to apply.

Derivation and applications

Determine how deeply a parallel field penetrates a finite slab

The exponential London solution assumes a sample extending far beyond a single exposed surface. A film has two surfaces whose screening currents can overlap. Consider an infinite slab of total thickness \(2d\), occupying \(-d<x<d\). Apply a static induction \(B_a\) parallel to its faces, along \(z\), and assume a uniform London penetration length \(\lambda\). Work in the vortex-free Meissner state with fields small enough for linear response. Translational invariance parallel to the slab gives the boundary problem

\[\frac{d^2 B_z}{dx^2}=\frac{B_z}{\lambda^2},\]
\[B_z(-d)=B_z(d)=B_a .\]

A general solution has a hyperbolic cosine and a hyperbolic sine. Reflection about the midplane makes the field even, which removes the sine term. The surface field fixes the remaining coefficient. Ampère’s law then determines the screening current, whose sign reverses at the midplane,

\[B_z(x)=B_a\frac{\cosh(x/\lambda)}{\cosh(d/\lambda)},\]
\[J_y(x)=-\frac{B_a}{\mu_0\lambda}\frac{\sinh(x/\lambda)}{\cosh(d/\lambda)}.\]

A probe at the centre and a probe integrating flux over the cross-section read different quantities. The latter reports the thickness average obtained by integrating the field profile,

\[\frac{\overline B}{B_a}=\frac{1}{2dB_a}\int_{-d}^d B_z(x)\,dx=\frac{\lambda}{d}\tanh(d/\lambda).\]

For a thick slab the mean fraction tends to \(\lambda/d\), consistent with two active surface layers. For a thin slab it is \(1-(d/\lambda)^2/3\) to leading order. Thin samples can therefore exhibit weak parallel-field exclusion even when the material is fully superconducting throughout their volume.

Worked example

Compare central and average field in a thick slab

A sample of thickness \(600\,\mathrm{nm}\) with \(\lambda=100\,\mathrm{nm}\) has \(d/\lambda=3\). In \(B_a=2.00\,\mathrm{mT}\) its central field is \(2.00/\cosh3=0.199\,\mathrm{mT}\). The mean induction is \(2.00\,\tanh(3)/3=0.663\,\mathrm{mT}\). The higher mean follows directly from the substantial contribution of material close to both exposed faces.

Second application

Extract a penetration length using a local probe

A probe halfway through a \(120\,\mathrm{nm}\) film measures \(0.800 B_a\). The central-field solution gives \(d/\lambda=\operatorname{arcosh}(1.25)=0.693\), so \(\lambda=86.6\,\mathrm{nm}\). The same film should transmit a mean fraction \(\tanh(0.693)/0.693=0.866\). Comparing this value with an independent flux integral tests the assumptions of uniform thickness and a single penetration length.

Compare two magnetic histories

A normal conductor dissipates energy from a sustained current. A superconductor can support persistent current and, in an appropriate low-field equilibrium state, expels magnetic flux from its bulk. Imagine cooling a specimen through its transition in a field and subsequently measuring its interior. Perfect conductivity alone would imply that a previously existing flux could remain frozen. Equilibrium flux expulsion supplies additional evidence about the state; sample shape, vortices and pinning affect what a real experiment detects. A resistance measurement and a magnetisation measurement therefore probe different properties.

Derive the London screening length

Let mobile condensate carriers have density \(n_s\), mass \(m_s\) and charge \(q_s\), using a consistent convention for particles or pairs. In a bulk region without vortices the London relation is \(\nabla\times\mathbf j_s=-(n_s q_s^2/m_s)\mathbf B\). Combine this with magnetostatic Ampère's law \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j_s\) and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). Taking a curl gives

\[\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2,\qquad \lambda_L=\sqrt{m_s/(\mu_0n_s q_s^2)} .\]

For a half-space \(x>0\) with surface field parallel to the surface and deep interior field zero, \(B(x)=B(0)e^{-x/\lambda_L}\). At depth \(3\lambda_L\), 4.98% of the surface amplitude remains. Taking \(n_s=2.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ electrons and electron mass in the simple carrier convention gives \(\lambda_L\approx37.6\) nm. Real effective masses, temperature-dependent superfluid density and nonlocality can change this value. Mixing pair density with single-electron charge would introduce a systematic factor error.

Quantise a circulating phase

A coherent condensate has a phase \(\phi\) whose change around a closed path must be \(2\pi n\) for integer \(n\) if the complex order parameter is single-valued and nonzero along the path. The gauge-invariant momentum is \(m_s\mathbf v_s=\hbar\nabla\phi-q_s\mathbf A\). Integrating around a loop relates circulation and flux. If a path lies sufficiently deep in a thick ring that its current density is negligible, the velocity term vanishes and enclosed flux comes in units \(\Phi_0=h/|q_s|\). For paired carriers \(|q_s|=2e\) the unit is \(2.068\times10^{-15}\) Wb. In thin rings where the kinetic term matters, it is the full fluxoid combination that is quantised.

Use length scales to interpret the experiment

The penetration depth characterises field screening. A separate coherence length \(\xi\) measures the distance over which the condensate amplitude recovers after a perturbation. In bulk Ginzburg–Landau theory near the transition, the ratio \(\lambda/\xi\) distinguishes type I and type II at \(1/\sqrt2\). In a type-II material between the lower and upper critical fields, flux can penetrate in quantised vortices with normal-like cores surrounded by screening currents. A specimen in this mixed state can retain superconducting regions while admitting magnetic flux. Stating field, temperature and cooling history is essential when interpreting the observed signal.

Locate the upper critical field from the linearized order parameter

The London equation assumes an established superconducting density. Near the loss of superconductivity at a high applied field, the order parameter itself becomes small and its spatial variation matters. For a bulk type-II material near its transition use Ginzburg–Landau free-energy density terms \(\alpha|\psi|^2+\beta|\psi|^4/2+|(-i\hbar\nabla-q_s\mathbf A)\psi|^2/(2m_s)\) with \(\alpha<0\), \(\beta>0\) and pair charge magnitude \(|q_s|=2e\). Very close to the upper critical field the density is small, field screening is weak and a uniform induction \(B\) is a suitable leading approximation.

Minimizing the free energy with respect to \(\psi^*\) and discarding the cubic term gives a charged-particle magnetic eigenproblem. The lowest transverse kinetic eigenvalue is the lowest Landau energy \(\hbar|q_s|B/(2m_s)\), achieved with no longitudinal variation. A nontrivial small solution is possible at the onset when this cancels \(\alpha\). Defining \(\xi^2=\hbar^2/(2m_s|\alpha|)\) and \(\Phi_0=h/|q_s|\) gives

\[B_{c2}=\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi\xi^2}.\]

Thus a coherence length inferred from upper critical induction measures the scale of condensate recovery. Penetration depth instead controls field screening in an already developed condensate. The GL derivation is controlled near the critical temperature and for orbital suppression. Strong paramagnetic pair breaking or pronounced anisotropy can complicate the interpretation of a measured high-field transition.

Connect critical induction to vortex spacing

At a specified temperature close to the transition a bulk specimen loses its superconducting response at \(B_{c2}=1.20\) T. The inferred coherence length is \(\sqrt{2.068\times10^{-15}/(2\pi\times1.20)}=16.6\) nm. An independent penetration-depth measurement at the same temperature yields \(\lambda=120\) nm, giving \(\lambda/\xi=7.24\), above the type-II boundary. Using lengths measured at unrelated temperatures would weaken that check.

At mean induction 0.200 T within the mixed state each singly quantized vortex carries one flux quantum. Its share of sample area is \(\Phi_0/B=1.03\times10^{-14}\) m². For a triangular lattice that area also equals \(\sqrt3\,a_v^2/2\), giving vortex spacing \(a_v=109\) nm. Increasing induction fourfold halves the spacing if the array remains in the mixed state and retains its geometry. Near \(B_{c2}\) cores overlap and an isolated-vortex picture becomes unreliable even though the linearized onset scale remains useful. Comparing a measured spacing with \(\Phi_0/B\) tests flux counting independently of the inferred coherence length and of the magnetic field profile around any single vortex.

A useful unit check compares the superconducting flux quantum with the electron flux scale from ordinary Landau-level counting. A condensate pair has twice the charge magnitude of one electron, giving half the flux quantum for a phase winding. At 0.200 T a square micrometre should contain about \(BA/\Phi_0=96.7\) singly quantized vortices away from edges. Counting vortices in an image therefore gives a bulk average induction estimate independent of a local peak-field calibration. A surface barrier, pinning or an insufficiently large field of view can account for a local deviation from the average.

Imaging successively larger regions tests how well such local counts represent the bulk.

Chapter 12 · Pairing, excitation gaps and what a spectrum measures

Use a two-component excitation model to predict spectroscopic signatures, then distinguish three physically different gaps encountered across the course.

From first principles

See how pairing opens a spectroscopic gap

Begin with a single normal-state band and let \(\xi\) be its energy measured from the chemical potential. A minimal mean-field model for a uniform paired state mixes an electron amplitude with a hole amplitude through a complex pairing energy \(\Delta\). The two diagonal energies have opposite signs. To find its stationary excitations write the eigenvalue condition:

\[H=\begin{pmatrix}\xi&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\det(H-EI)=(\xi-E)(-\xi-E)-|\Delta|^2=0 .\]

It follows that the two branches are \(E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi^2+|\Delta|^2}\). At the former Fermi surface \(\xi=0\), even the cheapest positive excitation costs \(|\Delta|\). To predict a tunnelling spectrum for constant isotropic gap and nearly constant normal density of states, count the normal states mapping into an energy interval \(dE\). The Jacobian from \(\xi=\pm\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) gives the normalized density, with the electron and hole weights combined:

\[\frac{D_s(E)}{D_n(0)}=\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}}\quad (|E|>|\Delta|).\]

There are no states within the ideal gap at this level of description. Finite temperature, lifetime broadening and instrumental resolution change the measured conductance near its edges. The model assumes a nonzero \(\Delta\); it predicts consequences of pairing without deriving the attractive interaction that supports it.

Derivation and applications

Predict the weights of a particle peak and its partner

A pairing gap determines excitation energies, but a momentum-resolved spectrum also measures how strongly an electron couples to each peak. Consider a clean, uniform superconductor with a real positive gap \(\Delta\), at temperature low enough to neglect thermal broadening. Measure the normal-state energy \(\xi\) at one wavevector from the chemical potential. In the electron and hole basis the positive-energy eigenvector has components \(u,v\). The first row of its eigenvalue equation requires

\[(\xi-E)u+\Delta v=0,\]
\[\frac{v}{u}=\frac{E-\xi}{\Delta},\]
\[E=\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}.\]

Normalize the vector to unit probability. Since \((E-\xi)(E+\xi)=\Delta^2\), the squared amplitude ratio can be written without subtracting two nearly equal energies. The resulting coherence factors are

\[|u|^2=\frac{1}{1+(E-\xi)/(E+\xi)}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{\xi}{E}\right),\]
\[|v|^2=\frac12\left(1-\frac{\xi}{E}\right).\]

The negative-energy eigenvector is orthogonal to the positive one and consequently has electron weight \(|v|^2\). In the ideal single-particle spectral function at this momentum the two sharp peaks have unit total weight,

\[A(\epsilon)=|u|^2\delta(\epsilon-E)+|v|^2\delta(\epsilon+E),\]
\[\int A(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon=1 .\]

Thus the weights exchange as the wavevector passes through the normal Fermi surface. At \(\xi=0\) the two weights are equal. Well above the surface the positive-energy peak carries almost all the weight. To compare both sides quantitatively an experiment must account for occupation and matrix elements; ordinary photoemission alone samples the occupied side most directly.

Worked example

Resolve a weak partner peak

At a selected momentum let \(\xi=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=4.00\,\mathrm{meV}\). The spectral energies are \(+5.00\) and \(-5.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), with respective electron weights \(0.800\) and \(0.200\). At a momentum with the opposite \(\xi\) and the same gap the two peak energies stay fixed while their weights reverse. Such a weight exchange tests particle–hole mixing more specifically than the peak separation alone.

Second application

Reconstruct a gap using corrected peak areas

In a different sample the peaks are separated by \(6.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), and corrected electron spectral areas have positive-to-negative ratio \(3:2\). Their normalized weights are \(0.600\) and \(0.400\), so \(\xi/E=0.200\). With \(E=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) this gives \(\xi=0.600\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=\sqrt{3.00^2-0.600^2}=2.94\,\mathrm{meV}\). Half the separation overestimates the gap here because the probed momentum lies away from the normal Fermi surface.

Build excitations on a paired state

For a conventional fully gapped superconductor, a mean-field description mixes particle and hole amplitudes near the Fermi surface. With \(\xi_k=\epsilon_k-\mu\) and gap parameter \(\Delta\), a simplified matrix for a time-reversed pair of states is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\xi_k&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi_k\end{pmatrix}\binom{u_k}{v_k}=E\binom{u_k}{v_k},\qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta|^2}.\]

A positive-energy quasiparticle has minimum energy \(|\Delta|\) where the normal band crossed the chemical potential. Breaking a pair into two excitations can cost at least \(2|\Delta|\). These are different thresholds, so the probe and transition being discussed must be specified. The matrix displays level repulsion, reminiscent of the nearly free electron calculation, but its basis states and the physical origin of the coupling are different.

Transform a density of states

Suppose the normal-state density is approximately constant over the gap scale and the gap is isotropic. For \(E>|\Delta|\), \(|\xi|=\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) and \(d|\xi|/dE=E/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\). Counting the paired branches relative to the normal-state convention yields \(D_s(E)/D_n=|E|/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) outside the gap and zero inside in the ideal zero-broadening model. The edge singularity is integrable. States removed from the gap reappear near its edges. Thermal broadening, disorder, nodes or an anisotropic gap can change the measured shape.

A low-temperature tunnel junction with a normal counterelectrode of nearly constant density can use differential conductance as a probe of this spectrum, subject to matrix-element and broadening effects. Peaks at biases \(\pm1.5\) mV suggest \(|\Delta|\simeq1.5\) meV in this geometry. Peak separation on the voltage axis is 3.0 mV. Using the weak-coupling BCS ratio \(2|\Delta(0)|/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.53\) gives \(T_c\simeq9.86\) K. Measure \(T_c\) independently; agreement with this estimate supports the simple model, while deviation calls for closer investigation.

Select a second observation that distinguishes the mechanism

A nearly free electron band gap separates eigenvalues near a zone boundary. For conduction it also matters where the chemical potential sits. A superconducting gap opens at the Fermi surface on entering an ordered state, and has magnetic screening and a characteristic temperature dependence. A photonic stop band instead excludes real propagating wavevectors in a frequency range for the specified periodic optical medium. All three can produce missing spectral weight or reduced transmission. Their energies, frequency units, occupation rules and control parameters differ.

Return to the numerical optical stack with a prediction: making both layers identical closes its first stop band even though the artificial zone boundary remains. For a proposed superconducting gap, repeat the tunnelling and magnetisation measurements as temperature crosses the transition. For an electronic band edge, relate spectroscopy to filling, dispersion and transport. This combination of an analytic limit, a controlled change and an independent observable is what makes an interpretation of a spectrum persuasive.

Read the phase difference across a superconducting weak link

A single-particle tunnelling spectrum can reveal an excitation gap. A weak junction between two superconductors can also carry a coherent pair current that responds directly to their relative condensate phase. Use the gauge-invariant phase difference \(\varphi\), including the vector-potential line integral across the junction. For a simple tunnel junction in the low-transparency regime, its leading coupling energy is \(U_J=-E_J\cos\varphi\). Its \(2\pi\) periodicity reflects invariance under an integer phase winding and the energy minimum at aligned phase for a conventional junction.

A potential difference \(V\) between the condensates causes their relative phase to advance at \(\dot\varphi=2eV/\hbar\), choosing a consistent orientation of current and voltage. Combining electrical power \(IV\) with the rate of change of junction energy gives \(IV=E_J\sin\varphi\,\dot\varphi\), hence

\[I=I_c\sin\varphi,\qquad I_c=\frac{2eE_J}{\hbar}.\]

Under a steady subcritical current the phase can remain constant while \(V=0\). At constant nonzero voltage the phase winds and the ideal coherent current oscillates with frequency \(f_J=2e|V|/h\). These two statements distinguish a DC pair current with no terminal voltage from an AC pair response under bias. A real junction includes quasiparticle conduction and capacitance, so its observed total current can contain additional components.

Convert a microwave frequency into a voltage

A junction biased at 5.00 μV has Josephson oscillation frequency \(f_J=(2e/h)V=2.42\) GHz. Illuminate the junction with a stable microwave reference of frequency 10.0 GHz. If its mean phase advance locks to one full winding per reference cycle, then \(\langle\dot\varphi\rangle=2\pi f_{\rm ref}\), requiring mean voltage \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)=20.7\) μV. Locking to two windings per cycle gives 41.4 μV. Such voltage steps probe phase dynamics and the charge associated with coherent transfer.

Suppose the same device has measured critical current \(I_c=30.0\) nA at the operating temperature. Its coupling energy is \(\hbar I_c/(2e)=9.87\times10^{-24}\) J, corresponding to \(E_J/k_B=0.715\) K. At a bath temperature of 50 mK thermal energy is appreciably smaller, although phase fluctuations can still depend on capacitance and the surrounding circuit. If either electrode is driven normal, the equilibrium phase-link energy disappears and the ideal supercurrent relation ceases to apply. Observing quantized phase locking and an excitation gap therefore accesses two different signatures of the superconducting state; their characteristic frequencies need not be numerically identical.

The two Josephson relations yield a further calibration test that does not require measuring energy directly. At fixed microwave frequency, the voltage separation of successive locking steps is \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)\) independently of junction area in the ideal phase model. Increasing junction area can change total critical current and the visibility or width of current intervals over which locking persists, yet should leave the quantized voltage spacing fixed. Looking at spacing and current width separately distinguishes uncertainty in the reference frequency from a change in the junction coupling or its electromagnetic environment.

Chapter 13 · How alternating bonds split a one-dimensional band

Two periodic chains contain precisely the same number of atoms at the same spacing. One has equal bonds and the other alternates strong and weak bonds. The difference changes which electron energies can propagate, even without changing the on-site energy of any atom.

Choose a primitive cell that repeats the bonds

Let a cell of length \(a\) contain orbitals A and B. An electron hops between A and B in the same cell with real amplitude \(t_1>0\), and between B and A in the next cell with amplitude \(t_2>0\). Use one orbital per site, neglect interactions and set both on-site energies to zero. We can absorb an overall hopping sign into the B basis state. Translation by one cell multiplies each component by \(e^{ika}\), although the relative amplitudes on A and B remain unknown. Writing those amplitudes as \(A,B\) turns the real-space nearest-neighbour equations into

\[EA=(t_1+t_2e^{-ika})B,\]
\[EB=(t_1+t_2e^{ika})A.\]

The cell boundary crossed by the second hopping accounts for its phase. The two coefficients are complex conjugates as required by Hermiticity. Substitute the second relation into the first without dividing by energy. A nonzero component then requires \(E^2=(t_1+t_2e^{ika})(t_1+t_2e^{-ika})\). The two energies at each wavevector are

\[E_\pm(k)=\pm\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2+2t_1t_2\cos ka}.\]

For positive hoppings their magnitude is largest at \(k=0\), namely \(t_1+t_2\), and smallest at \(ka=\pi\), namely \(|t_1-t_2|\). The gap is \(2|t_1-t_2|\). At one spinless electron per cell the lower band is full, so this is also the independent-particle excitation gap in a large periodic chain.

Read the band formation from two limits

Take \(t_1=1.0\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(t_2=0.6\,\mathrm{eV}\). The lower band runs from \(-1.6\) to \(-0.4\,\mathrm{eV}\), the upper from \(0.4\) to \(1.6\,\mathrm{eV}\). The gap is \(0.8\,\mathrm{eV}\), while each bandwidth is \(1.2\,\mathrm{eV}\). If \(t_2\) tends to zero, the cell becomes an isolated pair with two discrete bonding energies \(\pm t_1\). Bands flatten because cells can no longer exchange probability. At \(t_1=t_2=t\), the spectrum becomes \(\pm2t|\cos(ka/2)|\) in the reduced zone. The two pieces meet at its edge. The two-site construction then folds the simple uniform chain into a smaller wavevector interval.

One can also read off a velocity. Away from a crossing, differentiate \(E_\pm\) to obtain \(v_\pm=\mp a t_1t_2\sin(ka)/[\hbar|E_\pm|]\). At extrema of a gapped band it vanishes. At the equal-hopping crossing the derivative written as a ratio has a removable directional ambiguity, because the ordered upper and lower bands meet with opposite slopes.

Exchange the two bonds

Keep \(t_1\) fixed and move \(t_2\) through it. Compare both the gap and the bandwidth. Interchanging the numerical values of the two hoppings exactly preserves the bulk energies. The experiment in the next chapter distinguishes these patterns by opening the chain at a specified bond. A termination supplies physical information that the unordered hopping pair and the energy graph alone cannot encode.

Chapter 14 · Finding an end state one bond at a time

The bulk spectrum of an alternating chain is unchanged when strong and weak bonds exchange places. A boundary with a named first site breaks this equivalence. The zero-energy equations provide its wavefunction without diagonalising a large matrix.

Start at an exposed A site

Use the alternating chain with intracell link \(t_1\) and intercell link \(t_2\), both positive. End the left edge at \(A_1\). The first B site connects \(A_1\) and \(A_2\). Seek an eigenstate at \(E=0\) supported only on A sites. Each A-site equation then holds because all neighbouring B amplitudes vanish. Each B-site equation requires cancellation between its two neighbouring A amplitudes. Hence

\[0=t_1 A_j+t_2 A_{j+1},\]
\[A_j=(-t_1/t_2)^{j-1}A_1.\]

Relative signs matter in the cancellation, even though a local probability measurement sees only squared magnitudes. On a semi-infinite chain the geometric series for the norm converges only for \(r=t_1/t_2<1\). Normalising gives \(|A_1|^2=1-r^2\). The amplitude decay length satisfies \(e^{-a/\ell}=r\), so \(\ell=a/\ln(t_2/t_1)\). The probability decay length is half that. As the bulk gap closes, the decay length diverges and the bound state spreads into the interior.

Impose the far boundary too

A finite chain can have \(M\) A sites and \(M-1\) B sites, ending on A at both edges. Every B site still imposes exactly the recurrence above, leaving one overall free amplitude. The resulting state has exactly zero energy for any positive hoppings. Its finite norm is the sum of \(M\) successive powers of \(r^2\). At \(r=1\) the apparent zero over zero in the closed formula is resolved by summing \(M\) unit terms. The state then has probability \(1/M\) at every A site.

When \(r>1\) its norm remains finite in this finite sample and the weight accumulates at the right edge. An equal-sublattice finite chain ending on B instead has an additional boundary equation. Two exponentially localised end solutions can then hybridise, shifting their energies slightly above and below zero. Equal sublattice counts alone provide no exact zero eigenvalue. A change of boundary conditions can therefore alter the interpretation of a measured near-zero peak.

Connect the recurrence to the bulk phase

The off-diagonal matrix element \(h=t_1+t_2e^{ika}\) traces a circle centred at \(t_1\) with radius \(t_2\) as \(k\) crosses the Brillouin zone. If \(t_2>t_1\) it winds once around zero. If \(t_2<t_1\) it misses zero. Passing between the two with fixed cell choice requires the gap \(2|h|\) to close. A diagonal potential on just one sublattice would break the off-diagonal symmetry used to pin energies to zero. The winding statement and the boundary argument require a consistent assignment of the unit cell and termination.

For \(r=0.5\) the first three semi-infinite A probabilities are 0.75, 0.1875 and 0.046875. For \(r=0.90\), the first site has only 0.19 and the amplitude decay length increases from \(1.44a\) to \(9.49a\). Use the finite odd chain below to move the density from one end to the other. At reciprocal hopping ratios the two distributions must be mirror images. Check this using the endpoint readouts.

Chapter 15 · Why a honeycomb lattice has Dirac cones

In an alternating chain the two paths from A to B can cancel at a special wavevector. The honeycomb lattice adds a third path. Their cancellation now occurs at isolated points in a two-dimensional Brillouin zone and produces a linear low-energy dispersion.

Add the three Bloch phases

Each honeycomb A site has three nearest B neighbours at displacements \(d(0,1)\), \(d(\sqrt3/2,-1/2)\), and \(d(-\sqrt3/2,-1/2)\), where \(d\) is the bond length. For equal real hopping magnitude \(t\), sum the translation phase for each displacement. The common off-diagonal factor is

\[f(\boldsymbol k)=e^{ik_yd}+2e^{-ik_yd/2}\cos(\sqrt3 k_xd/2),\]
\[E_\pm=\pm t|f|.\]

This uses a basis phase convention that includes the orbitals' intra-cell positions. A rephasing can change the phase of \(f\) but leaves its magnitude invariant. Along \(k_y=0\), cancellation occurs where \(1+2\cos(\sqrt3 k_xd/2)=0\). Choose \(K=(4\pi/(3\sqrt3d),0)\). The counterpart at \(-K\) is inequivalent up to primitive reciprocal vectors. Near either valley many wavevectors represent distinct states even when their energies almost coincide.

Linearise at a zero, then test the approximation

Write \(\boldsymbol k=K+\boldsymbol q\) with \(qd\ll1\). At \(K\) the cosine equals \(-1/2\) and the sine \(\sqrt3/2\). Differentiating the cosine term with respect to \(k_x\) consequently gives \(-3d/2\). Differentiating the two exponential factors with respect to \(k_y\) gives \(id+id/2=3id/2\). The constant terms cancel. Therefore

\[f(K+\boldsymbol q)\simeq \tfrac{3d}{2}(-q_x+iq_y),\]
\[E_\pm\simeq\pm\hbar v_F|\boldsymbol q|,\]
\[v_F=\frac{3td}{2\hbar}.\]

With \(t=2.7\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(d=0.142\,\mathrm{nm}\) and \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{eV\,fs}\), the speed is \(0.874\,\mathrm{nm/fs}=8.74\times10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\). At \(qd=0.05\) the leading energy magnitude is \(0.2025\,\mathrm{eV}\). The energy varies linearly with momentum displacement even though the microscopic Hamiltonian contains only hopping between neighbours. The two components describe sublattice amplitude, distinct from the real electron spin.

Break the equivalence of the sublattices

Assign on-site energies \(+\Delta\) to A and \(-\Delta\) to B. The characteristic equation of the two-by-two Hamiltonian becomes \(E^2=\Delta^2+t^2|f|^2\). A gap \(2|\Delta|\) opens at K. For \(\Delta=0.10\,\mathrm{eV}\), the edge sits at \(0.10\,\mathrm{eV}\) and the predicted conduction energy at \(qd=0.05\) becomes about \(0.226\,\mathrm{eV}\). Far enough from the origin the slopes recover their nearly linear values. Close to it, expansion of the square root gives \(E_+\simeq|\Delta|+\hbar^2v_F^2q^2/(2|\Delta|)\). A band-edge mass \(|\Delta|/v_F^2\) has emerged. The expansion requires \(\hbar v_Fq\ll|\Delta|\).

Compare the exact positive-energy cut along \(q_x\) with the local cone in the graph. Unequal deviations to the right and left of K are allowed by the lattice and cannot be captured by the lowest-order isotropic cone. The reflected cut at the other valley restores the time-reversal relation.

Chapter 16 · Counting carriers and weighing a cyclotron orbit

A conical band has no constant quadratic band mass at its touching point. A carrier on a finite-energy orbit still has a measurable cyclotron mass. Counting states fixes the Fermi circle and determines how this mass changes when a gate adds charge.

Count states in a two-dimensional disc

Put a two-dimensional sheet of area \(A=L_xL_y\) under periodic boundary conditions. Neighbouring allowed wavevectors differ by \(2\pi/L_x\) and \(2\pi/L_y\), so each state occupies reciprocal-space area \((2\pi)^2/A\). At zero temperature fill all conduction states up to radius \(k_F\), measured from a Dirac point. There are two spin states and two inequivalent valleys in ideal monolayer graphene, giving total degeneracy \(g=4\). Density relative to charge neutrality is therefore

\[n=g\frac{\pi k_F^2}{(2\pi)^2}=\frac{k_F^2}{\pi},\]
\[E_F=\hbar v_F\sqrt{\pi n}.\]

For electron and hole densities use \(|n|\) under the square root and give \(E_F\) the sign of \(n\) relative to the touching point. Differentiating the state count with respect to energy gives the density of states per area \(D(E)=g|E|/[2\pi(\hbar v_F)^2]\). It tends to zero at an ideal neutral cone. Thermal broadening, disorder and a gap all change what a finite-resolution probe observes near neutrality.

Let a perpendicular field bend the orbit

For a wavepacket in a band with weak magnetic field, \(\hbar\dot{\boldsymbol k}=-e\,\boldsymbol v\times\boldsymbol B\), where \(e\) denotes the positive elementary charge. An isotropic band has velocity normal to a circular constant-energy contour. The orbit is traversed with speed \(|\dot{\boldsymbol k}|=e v_F B/\hbar\). Dividing its circumference \(2\pi k_F\) by that speed gives the period. Express its angular frequency as \(eB/m_c\) to identify

\[m_c=\frac{\hbar k_F}{v_F}=\frac{|E_F|}{v_F^2}.\]

The mass measures the time needed to travel around a contour. It depends on the radius even when the magnitude of the group velocity stays fixed. For a general band area \(\mathcal A(E)\), neighbouring contours separated perpendicular to themselves by \(dk_\perp\) differ in energy by \(\hbar v\,dk_\perp\). Thus \(|d\mathcal A/dE|=\oint dl/(\hbar v)\), which gives \(m_c=\hbar^2|d\mathcal A/dE|/(2\pi)\) by integrating the same equation of motion.

Convert a gate density into an energy and a mass

At \(n=10^{12}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}=10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}\) and \(v_F=10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\), one obtains \(k_F=1.772\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(E_F=0.117\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(m_c=1.87\times10^{-32}\,\mathrm{kg}=0.0205m_e\). Quadrupling the density doubles the Fermi energy and cyclotron mass. By contrast a single isotropic parabolic band \(E=\hbar^2 k^2/(2m)\) has the same counting geometry but constant \(m_c=m\). Transport mobility or disorder scattering time does not enter the state count, although scattering can obscure the cyclotron motion.

Change velocity and signed density. Raising the velocity at fixed density increases \(|E_F|\) while reducing the mass because the orbit radius stays fixed and the wavepacket moves faster. Exactly at charge neutrality, a single classical Fermi orbit no longer describes the carriers. Finite-field quantum levels replace the continuum picture in the next chapter.

Chapter 17 · Quantising the orbit and finding the zero Landau level

In a magnetic field the two kinetic momentum components cannot be specified simultaneously. In a conical band their commutator turns the two sublattice equations into coupled oscillator equations. Their spectra differ by one state, leaving an unpaired level at zero energy.

Construct the oscillator from kinetic momentum

In one valley of a gapless monolayer use the long-wavelength Hamiltonian \(H=v_F(\sigma_x\Pi_x+\sigma_y\Pi_y)\), with Pauli matrices acting on sublattices. For an electron of charge \(-e\), \(\boldsymbol\Pi=\boldsymbol p+e\boldsymbol A\). In a uniform perpendicular field \(B>0\), applying \([p_j,A_k]=-i\hbar\partial_j A_k\) gives \([\Pi_x,\Pi_y]=-ie\hbar B\). Introduce \(a=(\Pi_x-i\Pi_y)/\sqrt{2e\hbar B}\). Its adjoint uses the opposite sign, giving \([a,a^\dagger]=1\). The magnetic length \(\ell_B=\sqrt{\hbar/(eB)}\) sets the spatial size of the oscillator.

\[H=v_F\sqrt{2e\hbar B}\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\a^\dagger&0\end{pmatrix},\]
\[H^2=2e\hbar Bv_F^2\begin{pmatrix}aa^\dagger&0\\0&a^\dagger a\end{pmatrix}.\]

In the B component the squared Hamiltonian has oscillator numbers \(0,1,2,\ldots\). In the A component \(aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+1\), so its sequence starts at one. For integer \(n\ge1\) combine \(|n-1\rangle\) on A with \(\pm|n\rangle\) on B in equal magnitudes. Acting with \(H\) confirms energies \(E_{n,\pm}=\pm v_F\sqrt{2e\hbar Bn}\). For \(n=0\), the B-only spinor with \(|0\rangle\) is annihilated by \(a\). Its energy vanishes. Valley and field conventions can exchange which sublattice carries that state.

Count independent centres at the same energy

Choose gauge \(A_y=Bx\) and retain translation along \(y\). Quantisation gives \(k_y=2\pi j/L_y\). The kinetic momentum \(\Pi_y=\hbar k_y+eBx\) places the orbit centre at \(x_0=-\hbar k_y/(eB)\). Successive centres differ by \(2\pi\ell_B^2/L_y\). Counting centres in a width \(L_x\) gives \(L_xL_y/(2\pi\ell_B^2)\), or \(eB/h\) states per area for each spin and valley, apart from edge corrections. This degeneracy is independent of level index. Spin splitting, lattice effects and interactions are set aside here.

At \(B=1\,\mathrm T\), \(v_F=10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_B=25.7\,\mathrm{nm}\) and the first positive level is \(36.3\,\mathrm{meV}\) above zero. The next sits at \(51.3\,\mathrm{meV}\). The spacing between the first two positive levels is consequently only 15.0 meV. At 4 T all these energies double while the number of orbit centres per unit area quadruples. A parabolic band would instead give equally spaced levels at fixed field.

Identify the measurable dependence

Compare the first few positive levels as field changes. Dividing \(E_n\) by \(\sqrt{Bn}\) should return one material-dependent coefficient. An optical measurement sees transitions between occupied and available states, subject to matrix-element selection rules. A measured transition energy therefore need not coincide with the energy of an individual level. At zero field this discrete-level construction loses its normalisable magnetic oscillator; the limiting energies on the graph describe the approach from finite field.

Chapter 18 · How the same electrons carry heat and charge

A metal with high electrical conductivity often also conducts heat well. The quantitative relation involves an open-circuit measurement, the thermal width of the Fermi surface, and the energy dependence of the states able to carry current.

Weigh the states that can carry a current

Use an isotropic band of quasiparticles with energy \(E\), velocity magnitude \(v(E)\), density of states per volume \(D(E)\) and elastic relaxation time \(\tau(E)\). In a weak electric field or temperature gradient the departure from local equilibrium is proportional to the relaxation time times the applied driving term. For a given transport direction the angular average gives \(\langle v_x^2\rangle=v^2/3\) in three dimensions. Define \(\Sigma(E)=D(E)v(E)^2\tau(E)/3\). Electrical current weights a particle by its charge \(q=-e\), while heat current weights it by \(E-\mu\). Three moments consequently suffice for the two linear responses:

\[K_j=\int\Sigma(E)(E-\mu)^j[-\partial_E f(E)]\,dE,\]
\[j=0,1,2.\]

Let \(\mathcal E\) denote the electric/electrochemical field with any chemical-potential gradient properly included. The resulting currents along one direction are \(j_e=q^2K_0\mathcal E-qK_1\nabla T/T\) and \(j_Q=qK_1\mathcal E-K_2\nabla T/T\). The factor \(E-\mu\) in the thermal drive follows from differentiating the Fermi function with respect to temperature. The repeated first moment is a manifestation of reciprocal coupling of electrical and thermal transport in this zero-field model.

Impose the actual electrical boundary condition

A voltage probe draws negligible charge current. Set \(j_e=0\) to obtain \(\mathcal E=S\nabla T\) with \(S=K_1/(qTK_0)\). Substitute this field into the heat current to get \(\kappa_e=(K_2-K_1^2/K_0)/T\). Under the positive measure \(\Sigma(-f')dE/K_0\), the bracket divided by \(K_0\) is the variance of \(E-\mu\). Hence the open-circuit electronic thermal conductivity is nonnegative. Measuring with zero imposed electric field would retain a thermally driven charge current and a different heat-flow coefficient.

For a degenerate metal with smooth \(\Sigma\), the weight \(-f'\) is a narrow even peak. Replacing \(\Sigma\) by \(\Sigma(\mu)\) gives \(K_1=0\), \(K_0=\Sigma(\mu)\), and \(K_2=(\pi^2/3)(k_BT)^2\Sigma(\mu)\). The dimensionless second moment follows by integrating \(x^2/[4\cosh^2(x/2)]\) over the real axis. With \(\sigma=q^2K_0\),

\[\frac{\kappa_e}{\sigma T}=L_0=\frac{\pi^2k_B^2}{3e^2}=2.44\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,\Omega/K^2}.\]

At \(T=100\,\mathrm K\), a metal with \(\sigma=2.0\times10^7\,\mathrm{S/m}\) has estimated electronic \(\kappa_e=48.8\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\). A total measured conductivity of 60 would leave about \(11\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\) for other carriers if the assumptions hold. Phonons, inelastic scattering or strong energy dependence may change that inference.

Select a narrow range of transport energies

A model energy filter \(\Sigma\propto\exp[-(x-c)^2/(2w^2)]\), with \(x=(E-\mu)/(k_BT)\), \(c,w\) dimensionless, introduces a tunable departure from this metallic limit. The Seebeck coefficient is \((k_B/q)\langle x\rangle\) and the normalised Lorenz ratio is \(\operatorname{var}(x)/(\pi^2/3)\). A very narrow filter centred above the chemical potential produces strong signed thermopower but little heat transport at zero charge current, because current-carrying particles have almost identical excess energies. Those statements refer to the ratio and thermal moments; the absolute conductivity also depends on the overall transmission.

Chapter 19 · Why a polar crystal has two optical phonon frequencies

A uniform relative displacement of positive and negative ions creates an electric polarisation. A longitudinal vibration induces a macroscopic restoring field while a transverse displacement has a different electrical boundary condition. An infrared spectrum can reveal both frequencies.

Separate the ionic and electronic contributions

For one infrared-active optical mode let \(u\) be relative displacement per primitive cell, \(M\) the effective reduced mass, \(Z^*\) the effective charge in coulombs, and \(N\) the number of cells per volume. The oscillator equation driven by the macroscopic field is \(M(\ddot u+\gamma\dot u+\omega_{\rm TO}^2u)=Z^*E\). The zero-macroscopic-field stiffness defines the transverse optical frequency in this oscillator model. Bound electronic response at these frequencies supplies a real relative background permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\). The ionic polarisation is \(P_{\rm ion}=NZ^*u\), so \(D=\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty E+P_{\rm ion}\).

With time convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\), solving for \(u\) gives

\[\epsilon_r(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{S}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\]
\[S=\frac{N(Z^*)^2}{\epsilon_0 M}.\]

The signed effective charge appears twice, once as drive and once in the dipole density. Its square therefore sets oscillator strength. At zero frequency the ions can follow the field and \(\epsilon_s=\epsilon_\infty+S/\omega_{\rm TO}^2\). Far above the phonon resonance but below electronic absorption the ionic term is small, making \(\epsilon_\infty\) the observed plateau.

Apply Gauss's law to a longitudinal vibration

Consider a long-wavelength, nonzero wavevector \(\boldsymbol k\) with no external free charge. Gauss's law gives \(\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol D=0\). For a longitudinal field \(D_L=0\), so the ions create \(E_L=-NZ^*u/(\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty)\). Substituting this opposing field into the undamped oscillator equation adds a restoring term. Thus

\[\omega_{\rm LO}^2=\omega_{\rm TO}^2+S/\epsilon_\infty,\]
\[\frac{\omega_{\rm LO}^2}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2}=\frac{\epsilon_s}{\epsilon_\infty}.\]

Equivalently the longitudinal collective mode is the zero of the lossless dielectric function. The transverse optical frequency is its pole. For a single isotropic mode this is the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation. With several infrared modes, anisotropy, damping or mobile free carriers, assigning one measured peak and one dielectric ratio to a single oscillator needs further justification.

Connect a reflectance band with the two modes

In the lossless limit rewrite \(\epsilon_r=\epsilon_\infty(\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega^2)/(\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2)\). Between the two frequencies this is negative. A transverse electromagnetic wave satisfies \(k^2=\epsilon_r\omega^2/c^2\), so its wavenumber becomes imaginary there in a nonmagnetic lossless crystal. At a planar vacuum boundary a semi-infinite crystal therefore has unit ideal reflectance over this interval. Finite damping makes the refractive index complex and allows absorption, smoothing the edges. The two mode frequencies have been deduced from mechanical response and electric boundary conditions; an electromagnetic wave interacting with the lattice is a coupled phonon–polariton excitation.

For \(f_{\rm TO}=6.0\,\mathrm{THz}\), \(\epsilon_\infty=4\) and \(\epsilon_s=9\), \(f_{\rm LO}=9.0\,\mathrm{THz}\). At 7.5 THz in the lossless limit \(\epsilon_r=4(81-56.25)/(36-56.25)=-4.89\). Below the mode, normal-incidence static-index reflectance is \([(\sqrt9-1)/(\sqrt9+1)]^2=0.25\). Above the phonon plateau the corresponding background reflectance approaches \(1/9\). Applied study

Use lattice symmetry to connect vibrations, electronic bands and gaps in a periodic optical material.

From a row of atoms to a dispersion relation

Why can a crystal carry waves of some frequencies but exclude others? Start with the simplest solid. Identical masses \(m\) sit at sites \(x_j=ja\), where \(a\) is the lattice spacing. Each atom is coupled to its nearest neighbours by springs of stiffness \(K\). Small longitudinal displacements \(u_j\) obey

\[m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}).\]

A displacement pattern can reproduce itself after a translation by one cell, apart from a phase. Insert \(u_j=Ue^{i(kja-\omega t)}\) and cancel the common factor. This yields \(\omega^2=(4K/m)\sin^2(ka/2)\). Two wave numbers that differ by \(2\pi/a\) assign the same phase to every lattice site. One interval, the first Brillouin zone \(-\pi/a\le k\le\pi/a\), therefore contains all distinct patterns.

For long waves the dispersion relation becomes \(\omega\simeq a\sqrt{K/m}\,|k|\). A narrow wave packet travels at the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\). This speed approaches the sound speed near the zone centre and falls to zero at the edge. There the neighbours move oppositely in a standing pattern.

How a periodic perturbation opens a gap

In a periodic potential a single electron has Bloch states \(\psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}v_k(x)\), with \(v_k(x+a)=v_k(x)\). For a free electron, the waves at \(+\pi/a\) and \(-\pi/a\) have the same energy \(E_0\). Their wave numbers differ by a reciprocal lattice vector. A periodic potential can mix them.

Retain these two waves and absorb the mean potential into \(E_0\). The diagonal entries of their Hamiltonian are both \(E_0\). Its off-diagonal entries are \(V_G\) and its complex conjugate, the Fourier components of the potential that couple the waves. The characteristic equation \((E_0-E)^2-|V_G|^2=0\) gives \(E_\pm=E_0\pm|V_G|\). A coupling of 0.12 eV opens a 0.24 eV splitting at this crossing. The two standing waves place their probability density differently relative to the ions.

Band filling matters. At zero temperature a band with available neighbouring states at the Fermi energy can respond to a weak electric field. An ideal crystal with a full valence band separated from empty states by an energy gap needs excitations across that gap for electronic conduction. To infer such a gap in a real material, check the band extrema throughout the zone.

Read a gap in an optical lattice

Light in alternating transparent layers also obeys a Bloch condition. A transfer matrix maps the electric and magnetic fields across one complete cell. For a lossless cell at normal incidence its determinant is one, so the two eigenvalues are reciprocal. A propagating wave has eigenvalues \(e^{\pm ika}\), hence half the matrix trace equals \(\cos(ka)\). An absolute half-trace greater than one leaves no real wave number at that frequency. The field then decays into a thick stack.

Consider equal layers with the same relative permittivity 13 and relative permeability one. They behave as a uniform medium with \(n=\sqrt{13}\). At the zone edge \(2\pi nf/c=\pi/a\), so the reduced frequency \(fa/c=1/(2\sqrt{13})=0.1387\). The folded branches touch. Give the second layer a different refractive index and light reflects at each interface. Near the zone edge successive reflections add coherently, lifting this degeneracy. This gap is an interval of optical frequencies. The electronic example above measures an interval of energies.
