Distinguish a physical precession from numerical drift

A bound orbit circulates many times about its centre. A small change in the angle of successive closest approaches could be physical or could be accumulated phase error. Construct an exactly solvable family of precessing orbits that lets the two effects be compared without assuming a reference trajectory from another code.

Replace time by the measured polar angle

A particle of mass \(m\) moves in a static central potential \(V(r)=-k/r+\alpha/(2r^2)\) with \(k>0\). The radial force is \(-k/r^2+\alpha/r^3\). Angular momentum \(L=mr^2\dot\phi\) is constant. Put \(u=1/r\) and differentiate with respect to \(\phi\), denoting this derivative by a prime. The identities \(\dot r=-(L/m)u'\) and \(\ddot r=-(L^2/m^2)u^2u''\) follow by the chain rule. Subtracting the centripetal term \(r\dot\phi^2=(L^2/m^2)u^3\) from radial acceleration gives Binet's equation.

\[u''+(1+\delta)u=\frac{mk}{L^2},\]
\[\delta=\frac{m\alpha}{L^2}.\]

The sign of the perturbation matters. Positive \(\alpha\) gives a repulsive short-range correction, increasing radial frequency per unit angle. A mildly negative correction decreases it. This family is a test potential; a measured nonzero advance in nature need not have this particular cause.

Read apses from a harmonic equation

For \(1+\delta>0\), write \(\nu=\sqrt{1+\delta}\), \(u_c=mk/[L^2(1+\delta)]\) and impose \(u(0)=u_c(1+e)\), \(u'(0)=0\) with \(0<e<1\). Then \(u=u_c[1+e\cos(\nu\phi)]\). The radial minimum corresponds to a maximum of \(u\). Its next occurrence is at angle \(2\pi/\nu\), so the change of line of apsides per radial period is

\[\Delta\omega=2\pi\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\delta}}-1\right).\]

For \(\delta=0.04\), successive pericentres rotate backwards by approximately 7.0 degrees. At \(\delta=0\) the Kepler ellipse closes. For a strictly circular orbit the algebraic radial frequency has a well-defined small-oscillation limit, but the direction of pericentre of the observed circle has no unique value. A nearly circular orbit therefore has a genuine experimental difficulty even when the integration is accurate.

Compute the bias of a discrete orbit exactly

Shift \(v=u-u_c\). On a uniform angular grid of spacing \(h\), the centred second difference gives

\[v_{j+1}=2v_j-v_{j-1}-(h\nu)^2v_j.\]

Insert the trial \(v_j=e^{ij\psi}\) and simplify:

\[\cos\psi=1-\tfrac12(h\nu)^2,\]
\[\nu_h=\frac{2}{h}\arcsin(h\nu/2).\]

The numerical mode oscillates if \(h\nu<2\). Inside that range it is too fast, since the arcsine exceeds its argument. A bounded orbit can thus accumulate a negative apsidal error. For an unperturbed Kepler case and a 30-degree grid, the numerical angular frequency is roughly 1.012. Treating this as a physical result would infer a retrograde precession of roughly four degrees per revolution. Refining \(h\) halves the angular step and quarters the leading error, because \(\nu_h=\nu+\nu^3h^2/24+\cdots\).

The experiment plots radial phase after multiple nominal revolutions and reports the implied apsidal angles. Vary the perturbation at fixed step first, then halve the step at fixed perturbation. Distinguish the discrepancy that persists in the continuum from the discrepancy that can be extrapolated away. Starting all curves at pericentre makes the phase convention identical.

Hold the force exactly Newtonian. Compare the accumulated numerical phase on a 30° grid and 6° grid. The exact radial phase stays at zero in both runs. Use the exported values to estimate how the phase error scales when the angular step is divided by five.

Tutorial

A measured radial period is 364 degrees of azimuthal advance. In this family, which sign of \(\alpha\) is indicated and what is \(\delta\)?

Work through the solution

The radial cycle takes more than 360 degrees, so the radial frequency per unit angle is below one. The inferred correction is attractive,

\[\delta=(360/364)^2-1\simeq-0.02186.\]

A simulation reports advances of −1.20, −0.60 and −0.45 degrees at successive halvings of the angular step. What leading continuum estimate follows?

Work through the solution

With second-order phase bias, the final difference 0.15 equals three times the remaining magnitude of that bias. Adding 0.05 to −0.45 gives −0.40 degrees. The earlier pair gives the same estimate. An additional refinement tests the assumed scaling.

Can a long observation of a closed numerically integrated orbit rule out a time-step error in the orbital period?

Work through the solution

No. Closure depends on a ratio of radial to azimuthal phase advances. Both may be biased in the same proportion. Independently time successive crossings and compare with an analytic period or a finer integration. Different observables can hide different errors.
