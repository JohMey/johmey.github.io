Mechanics in the laboratory

Turn a measured trajectory or force into a test of a mechanical law. Derive the observable, propagate it numerically and determine which parameters the experiment can resolve.

    1. Measure gravity with a pendulum of finite amplitude

      A metre-long pendulum is an appealing clock because its small-angle period depends only on length and gravity. A precise clock can nevertheless give a precisely wrong value of gravity. Derive the full period, trace the systematic error to an identifiable approximation and design a measurement that distinguishes a length error from a release-angle error.

    2. Measure inertia and gravity with two suspension axes

      A rigid pendulum can oscillate from either of two knife edges. If the periods agree at distinct distances from the centre of mass, the spacing of the edges determines gravity. Derive the cancellation, identify a second way equal periods can arise and find why a poorly chosen geometry magnifies timing errors.

    3. Distinguish a physical precession from numerical drift

      A bound orbit circulates many times about its centre. A small change in the angle of successive closest approaches could be physical or could be accumulated phase error. Construct an exactly solvable family of precessing orbits that lets the two effects be compared without assuming a reference trajectory from another code.

    4. Which track reaches a lower point first?

      A bead is released from rest and slides without friction along a track between two points. A straight track is shortest, yet another path can deliver the bead sooner. Derive the travel-time functional, solve its boundary problem and compare actual times while changing the horizontal distance independently of the vertical drop.

    5. When can shaking a support stabilise an upside-down pendulum?

      A rapidly oscillating pivot can stabilise a pendulum near its normally unstable upright orientation. Develop the slowly varying effective potential, test its assumptions with the full time-dependent equation and compare the observable that each calculation predicts.

    6. When does a smooth circular guide lose contact?

      A bead moves inside a vertical circular guide after receiving a known speed at the bottom. Predict whether it turns around, separates from the guide or completes the circle. A normal-force measurement can distinguish these outcomes before a simple top-height energy test can.
