Which track reaches a lower point first?

A bead is released from rest and slides without friction along a track between two points. A straight track is shortest, yet another path can deliver the bead sooner. Derive the travel-time functional, solve its boundary problem and compare actual times while changing the horizontal distance independently of the vertical drop.

Choose the coordinate appearing in the speed law

Let \(y\) increase downwards from the release point, \(x\) increase along the horizontal and \(g\) be uniform. A small frictionless bead conserves mechanical energy, so its speed at depth \(y\) is \(\sqrt{2gy}\). For a track expressed as \(y(x)\) the time element is line element divided by speed. Hence

\[T[y]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2g}}\int_0^X \sqrt{\frac{1+y'^2}{y}}\,dx,\]
\[y(0)=0,\]
\[y(X)=Y.\]

A straight line has distance \(\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\) and component of acceleration \(gY/\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\), giving \(T_{\mathrm{line}}=\sqrt{2(X^2+Y^2)/(gY)}\). The advantage of a steeper early path is to build speed at the part of the trajectory where the bead would otherwise remain slow. A path with a longer distance can take less time because distances at different depths cost different amounts of time.

Use a first integral to remove a differential equation

Write the integrand excluding its constant multiplier as \(F=\sqrt{1+y'^2}/\sqrt y\). Since it contains no explicit \(x\), the Euler–Lagrange equation implies \(F-y'F_{y'}=\) constant. Differentiating this expression with respect to \(x\) shows the cancellation explicitly:

\[dF/dx=F_y y'+F_{y'}y'';\]

the derivative of the second term removes \(F_{y'}y''\) and leaves \(y'(F_y-dF_{y'}/dx)=0\). Substituting the integrand gives \(y(1+y'^2)=2a\) for some positive length \(a\).

Set \(y=a(1-\cos u)\). On the descending part \(y'=\cot(u/2)\). Integrating \(dx=\tan(u/2)dy\) yields

\[x=a(u-\sin u).\]

The signed derivative continues through zero at \(u=\pi\) and produces an ascending segment for \(\pi<u<2\pi\). A track may pass below the final point, then climb towards it while spending speed acquired earlier.

Solve for the actual endpoint and clock reading

Divide the endpoint coordinates to eliminate \(a\). The angle \(u_f\) solves \((u_f-\sin u_f)/(1-\cos u_f)=X/Y\) on the first arch. The ratio starts at zero and increases without bound; bisection gives a robust root for positive finite \(X/Y\). Recover \(a=Y/(1-\cos u_f)\). Along the cycloid \(ds=2a\sin(u/2)du\), while \(v=2\sqrt{ga}\sin(u/2)\). Their ratio is finite even at the cusp where both vanish:

\[T_{\mathrm{cyc}}=u_f\sqrt{a/g}.\]

For \(Y=1\) m and \(X=\pi/2\) m, \(u_f=\pi\) and \(a=0.5\) m. The cycloid time is approximately 0.709 s and the straight track needs about 0.841 s. The endpoint is the bottom of this particular cycloid. Increasing the horizontal offset beyond \(\pi Y/2\) makes the first-arch solution turn upwards before it arrives.

Keep track shape geometrically similar and double every length. By which factor does the travel time change?

Work through the solution

The parameter angle is fixed by a length ratio and \(a\) doubles. The time grows by \(\sqrt2\). The straight track time obeys the same scaling. A measured different exponent would suggest another dimensional scale in the apparatus.

Why might a numerical quadrature applied directly in \(x\) at the first sample point report infinite time?

Work through the solution

The release speed vanishes and the track has a vertical tangent. The local time integral remains finite. The cycloid parametrisation cancels both vanishing factors analytically. Evaluating a singular endpoint as an ordinary sample in a trapezoidal rule is an integration error.

How can two paths be compared if a common release mechanism imparts a small initial speed \(v_0\)?

Work through the solution

Energy now gives

\[v(y)=\sqrt{v_0^2+2gy}.\]

Recompute the time functional using that speed and the actual initial condition. Subtracting an arbitrary time offset from both runs cannot undo a depth-dependent modification of travel speed.
