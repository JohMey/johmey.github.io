Euler–Poinsot motion, torque-free nutation and the intermediate axis

A freely rotating body has fixed angular momentum in space, yet the components recorded by three body-mounted gyroscopes can vary periodically or linger near an unstable rotation. Solve the nonsymmetric top from its invariants, distinguish its body-component period from its spatial orientation, and extract a stability time scale from a measured inertia tensor.

Derive the body equations from a spatial conservation law

Choose principal axes fixed in a rigid body and label its moments \(I_1<I_2<I_3\). In the co-rotating basis the angular-momentum components are \(L_i=I_i\omega_i\). For any vector expressed in that basis, its inertial derivative is its component derivative plus \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L\). With no external torque the inertial derivative is zero. Taking components gives

\[I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\]
\[I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\]
\[I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2 .\]

Multiply these equations by the corresponding \(\omega_i\) and sum: the coefficients of \(\omega_1\omega_2\omega_3\) cancel, giving constant \(E=\sum I_i\omega_i^2/2\). Multiplying instead by \(I_i\omega_i\) gives constant \(L^2=\sum I_i^2\omega_i^2\). In body angular-momentum space the motion lies on the intersection of a sphere and an energy ellipsoid. At fixed \(L\), the possible energies range from \(L^2/(2I_3)\) to \(L^2/(2I_1)\). The intermediate value \(L^2/(2I_2)\) divides two families of closed curves and contains the unstable principal-axis orientations.

In Poinsot's construction the inertia ellipsoid in angular-velocity space rolls on an invariable plane normal to the fixed spatial angular momentum. The ellipsoid corresponding to a given energy is \(\sum I_i\omega_i^2=2E\). Its normal at the instantaneous angular velocity is proportional to \(\mathbf L\); the tangent plane obeys \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf x=2E\). In space that plane therefore has fixed distance \(2E/L\) from the origin. Its instantaneous point in the body along \(\boldsymbol\omega\) has zero instantaneous rotational velocity because the vector cross product of angular velocity with itself is zero. These facts underlie the rolling construction. A photographed body axis traces a different curve from the moving contact point or from a body-frame gyro trace.

Test all three principal rotations locally

For rotation \((0,0,\Omega)\), linearise the first two Euler equations in transverse perturbations. Eliminating one component gives \(\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega^2[(I_3-I_2)(I_3-I_1)/(I_1I_2)]\omega_1\). Small transverse deviations oscillate. Performing the analogous process about \(I_1\) also gives a restoring sign. About \((0,\Omega,0)\) the sign reverses and the growing exponent is \(\lambda=|\Omega|\sqrt{(I_2-I_1)(I_3-I_2)/(I_1I_3)}\). The unstable rotation is still an exact solution when both perturbations vanish identically. Instability describes the evolution of arbitrarily small disturbances, not a spontaneous violation of the torque-free equations.

For moments proportional to \(1,2,3\) and intermediate-axis rotation \(\Omega=6.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(\lambda=3.46\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and the linear e-folding time is \(0.289\,{\rm s}\). A perturbation with a growing-mode component \(10^{-3}\) of the spin reaches roughly \(10^{-1}\) in \(\ln(100)/\lambda=1.33\,{\rm s}\). Its nonlinear fate remains bounded by the momentum sphere and energy ellipsoid. An exponential growth fit carried past the first large reorientation would therefore misrepresent the global motion. Initial perturbations projected entirely on the decaying linear eigenvector can delay the first visible flip.

Solve the low-energy family with elliptic functions

For \(L^2/(2I_3)<E<L^2/(2I_2)\) the trajectory surrounds the largest-inertia axis. Start at a crossing with \(\omega_2=0,\omega_1=A>0,\omega_3=C>0\). Solving the two invariants gives \(A^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_1(I_3-I_1)]\) and \(C^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_3(I_3-I_1)]\). When \(\omega_1\) reaches zero the other transverse component has maximum \(B^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_2(I_3-I_2)]\). Use \(s=\omega_2/B\). Eliminating the other squares with the invariants gives \(\omega_1^2=A^2(1-s^2)\), \(\omega_3^2=C^2(1-k_e^2s^2)\). Euler's second equation now reduces to

\[\dot s^2=\nu^2(1-s^2)(1-k_e^2s^2),\]
\[\nu^2=\frac{(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)}{I_1I_2I_3},\]
\[k_e^2=\frac{(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)}{(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)} .\]

Separating variables defines \(u=\nu t=\int_0^s dw/\sqrt{(1-w^2)(1-k_e^2w^2)}\). Its inverse is Jacobi's \({\rm sn}(u,k_e)\). The continuously signed square roots are \({\rm cn}\) and \({\rm dn}\), giving \((\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3)=(A\,{\rm cn},B\,{\rm sn},C\,{\rm dn})\). This construction defines the special functions from the mechanical quadrature instead of assuming them as a table of answers. At \(k_e=0\) the inverse is a sine and the stabilising motion is harmonic. As \(k_e\to1\), the integral to \(s=1\) diverges logarithmically and the period grows without bound.

The body-frame period is \(T_b=4K(k_e)/\nu\), with \(K\) the same integral evaluated at one. Some software defines elliptic functions in terms of parameter \(m=k_e^2\) rather than modulus \(k_e\). Supplying one to an implementation expecting the other produces a plausible curve with the wrong period. Check the derivative at the zero crossing \(\dot\omega_2(0)=B\nu\) and the time of its first maximum \(K/\nu\) before fitting a long trace. The third component \({\rm dn}\) repeats after half the full period, so a single principal-axis gyro can report a dominant frequency twice that of the full body vector.

Reconstruct the observed nutation angle

Orient the inertial vertical along \(\mathbf L\). The angle between body axis three and this fixed direction obeys \(\cos\theta=L_3/L=I_3\omega_3/L\). For \(I_1,I_2,I_3=(1,2,3)\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) and initial \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0.50,0,1.00)\,{\rm rad/s}\), the invariants give \(L^2=9.25\), \(2E=3.25\) in SI, \(A=B=0.50\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(C=1.00\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(\nu=1.00\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), and \(k_e^2=1/12\). Thus the nutation angle changes from \(\arccos(3/\sqrt{9.25})\approx9.46^\circ\) to \(\arccos[3\sqrt{11/12}/\sqrt{9.25}]\approx19.2^\circ\). A torque is unnecessary for this change in the angle of a body axis relative to fixed \(\mathbf L\).

The principal-axis components alone determine body orientation only when supplemented with an initial attitude and the kinematic equation. For the Euler-angle convention with polar axis \(\mathbf L\), the azimuthal rate of body axis three is \(\dot\phi=L(L_1^2/I_1+L_2^2/I_2)/(L_1^2+L_2^2)\) away from the polar singularity. It follows by differentiating the body-axis unit vector in space and projecting onto its azimuthal direction. A complete period of the body components therefore leaves a finite spatial rotation about fixed \(\mathbf L\). Return of the three gyro readings does not imply that a painted mark has returned to its starting position in the laboratory.

Solve the high-energy family and join both sides at the separatrix

For \(L^2/(2I_2)<E<L^2/(2I_1)\) the closed curves on the angular-momentum sphere surround axis one. The component \(\omega_1\) now keeps its sign while \(\omega_3\) changes sign through a cycle. As in the first family, choose a phase with \(\omega_2=0\) and both other components positive. The two invariants give the same initial \(A^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_1(I_3-I_1)]\) and \(C^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_3(I_3-I_1)]\). Now the maximum of \(\omega_2\) occurs when \(\omega_3=0\), so \(B^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_2(I_2-I_1)]\). Which component reaches zero is the physical branch choice that determines the arrangement of elliptic functions.

Let \(s=\omega_2/B\). The invariant equations give \(\omega_3^2=C^2(1-s^2)\), \(\omega_1^2=A^2(1-k_h^2s^2)\). Substitution in the second Euler equation yields the same elliptic quadrature but with \(\nu_h^2=(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)/(I_1I_2I_3)\) and \(k_h^2=(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)/[(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)]\). All components are given by \((A\,{\rm dn},B\,{\rm sn},C\,{\rm cn})(\nu_h t,k_h)\), where the notation means each function is evaluated at the same time and modulus. On this energy side \(0<k_h<1\). The low-energy modulus formula evaluated here would exceed one, signalling that the original choice of component scaling and physical extrema must be changed.

For the same inertias \(1,2,3\), choose initial \((1,0,0.20)\,{\rm rad/s}\). Then \(L^2=1.36,\ 2E=1.12\), and the high-energy inequality holds since \(2E>L^2/I_2=0.68\). The formulas give \(A=1,\ B=\sqrt{0.12}=0.3464,\ C=0.20,\ \nu_h=\sqrt{1/3}=0.5774\,{\rm s^{-1}},\ k_h^2=0.12\). The minimum \(\omega_1\) is \(A\sqrt{1-k_h^2}=0.9381\,{\rm rad/s}\), so that principal gyro never reverses even though the third one does. A body-marking aligned with axis three can undergo a conspicuous reversal while the dominant axis-one spin changes only modestly. These component predictions are more specific than an energy or angular-speed trace.

At \(2E=L^2/I_2\), both moduli approach one. The definitions give \({\rm sn}(u,1)=\tanh u\), \({\rm cn}(u,1)={\rm dn}(u,1)={\rm sech}\,u\). One separatrix solution therefore has \(\omega_2=B\tanh(\nu t)\) with \(\omega_1=A\,{\rm sech}(\nu t)\) and \(\omega_3=C\,{\rm sech}(\nu t)\), up to signs and time shifts consistent with Euler's equations. It connects opposite steady orientations along axis two as \(t\) goes from negative to positive infinity. Its asymptotic approach is exponential, with \(\nu\) equal to the small-disturbance growth exponent at the intermediate-axis equilibrium after inserting \(B=L/I_2\). A nearby periodic orbit spends much of its time near the unstable spin and flips more rapidly between those intervals.

Reconstruct spatial attitude without a singular azimuth

Let \(R(t)\) map body components into a laboratory basis. For body angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega_b\), its kinematic equation is \(\dot R=R[\boldsymbol\omega_b]_\times\), where the skew matrix acts by a cross product. Starting from measured \(R(0)\), propagate by multiplying by the rotation appropriate to each short time interval. Exponentiating the skew matrix for an interval with constant estimated body angular velocity preserves orthogonality and determinant exactly. Applying a naive forward-Euler matrix addition \(R+hR[\omega]_\times\) generally stretches the basis, even if the angular velocity components came from an exact elliptic solution.

For a constant rotation about axis three at one radian per second, a single Euler step of duration \(0.10\,{\rm s}\) takes a transverse unit vector to one of length \(\sqrt{1+0.10^2}=1.00499\). After one hundred such steps the transverse length would grow by \((1.01)^{50}\approx1.64\). A quaternion or matrix exponential avoids that artificial stretch. Normalising each axis separately is insufficient if the axes lose orthogonality; check \(R^TR\) against identity and \(\det R\) against one. Also compute \(R(I_1\omega_1,I_2\omega_2,I_3\omega_3)^T\) at every time. It should give the same spatial vector in a torque-free run.

A measured attitude time series allows a different inference from fitting Euler angles separately. Use relative rotations between successive images to estimate angular velocity in the correct body or spatial frame. A camera can momentarily lose sensitivity to yaw when a marked axis points towards it; simultaneous orthogonal views or multiple non-collinear marks avoid that degeneracy. The gyro and attitude measurements together distinguish a true physical precession from an Euler-angle wrap by \(2\pi\). Synchronise their clocks before comparing the phase of a nutation extremum, since even a small time delay produces a plausible-looking mismatch near a fast flip.

Infer inertia ratios from a free-rotation record

A calibrated triad records \(\omega_i(t)\) in axes thought to be principal. Euler's equations predict that \(\dot\omega_1/(\omega_2\omega_3)\) is the constant \((I_2-I_3)/I_1\) wherever the denominator is appreciable, and similarly for cyclic indices. Instead of dividing noisy derivatives near zeros, integrate each component over a time interval. For example \(\omega_1(t_2)-\omega_1(t_1)=a_1\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\omega_2\omega_3\,dt\). Fit \(a_1\) over several intervals and repeat for \(a_2,a_3\). Select intervals with different angular configurations so that products do not all average nearly to zero. The ratios depend on inertia shape; multiplying every moment by a common constant leaves a torque-free angular-velocity record unchanged.

For a record giving \(a_1=-1.0\), \(a_2=1.0\) and \(a_3=-1/3\), solve the homogeneous equations \(I_2-I_3=-I_1\), \(I_3-I_1=I_2\), \(I_1-I_2=-I_3/3\). They give \(I_1:I_2:I_3=1:2:3\). An independent angular-momentum or torque calibration is needed to set the absolute scale. A known short applied torque impulse that changes one principal angular velocity by a measured amount can do this, provided the other gyroscopic contributions over that impulse are small or accounted for. A fit that estimates all absolute inertias from angular velocity alone can have an apparently excellent residual and still be structurally underdetermined.

If the sensors are not along principal axes the inertia matrix contains off-diagonal entries. The coordinate-free body equation is \(I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega)=0\). Each measured time sample gives equations linear in the constant entries of the symmetric inertia tensor. With sufficiently rich motion one may estimate these entries up to scale, then diagonalise the fitted tensor to recover principal directions. Pure rotation along one axis gives insufficient excitation to reveal three distinct directions. Comparing a family of initial spins with a single tensor provides a stronger validity test than fitting a different set of effective principal inertias to each record.

Relate a body-frame period to a measured geometric phase

After a closed polhode in body angular-momentum space the laboratory orientation differs by a rotation about the fixed spatial momentum. That phase can be separated into a term accumulated at a dynamical rate and a term depending on the oriented solid angle of the body momentum path. For a fixed \(L\), the angular-velocity component along the momentum is \(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\hat L=2E/L\), since \(\mathbf L\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=2E\). Over one body period it contributes \(2ET_b/L\). Parallel transport of a transverse reference direction along the unit momentum curve contributes minus the oriented solid angle enclosed, up to whole turns and the chosen orientation of the sphere. The resulting net attitude phase is \(2ET_b/L-\Omega_{\rm solid}\pmod{2\pi}\).

The geometric part can be visualised by carrying a local tangent basis along the closed curve. Its two basis vectors are constrained to stay perpendicular to the moving unit momentum and are transported with no imposed twist around that direction. A return to the initial unit momentum need not return that basis to its starting orientation. For a latitude circle on the unit sphere the enclosed cap area is \(2\pi(1-\cos\theta)\); shrinking the path to a point makes the geometric phase vanish modulo \(2\pi\). This is why comparing a painted mark after a nutation only to an integrated scalar spin speed can give a discrepancy even with exact torque-free motion.

In practice the total phase can be measured without choosing a cap convention. Fit the attitude at two times separated by the full gyro period, form \(R(t+T_b)R(t)^T\), and extract the relative rotation around the known fixed spatial momentum. Independently integrate \(2E/L\) over that interval and compare the difference modulo \(2\pi\) with the signed spherical area traced by normalised body momentum. A factor-of-two error in the identified period, common if only \(\omega_3\) is recorded in the low-energy family, generally leaves the relative attitude rotation axis different from \(\mathbf L\). The axis check can reveal the error before assigning a numerical geometric phase.

Identify which inertias can exist in an actual rigid body

Three positive principal moments inserted into Euler's equations define a mathematical top, but the moments of a physical nonnegative mass distribution also satisfy triangle inequalities. In principal coordinates \(I_1=\int(y^2+z^2)\,dm\), \(I_2=\int(z^2+x^2)\,dm\), \(I_3=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm\). Therefore \(I_1+I_2-I_3=2\int z^2\,dm\ge0\), and likewise by cyclic permutation. Equality means all mass is confined to the corresponding coordinate plane. The convenient example \(1,2,3\) lies on that boundary and can be realised by an ideal planar lamina. A fitted ratio \(1,2,3.5\) would imply negative mean squared distance along one direction and should prompt a calibration or modelling check.

A uniform thin rectangular plate of mass \(M\) and side lengths \(b\) along body axis one and \(a\) along body axis two has \(I_1=Ma^2/12,\ I_2=Mb^2/12,\ I_3=M(a^2+b^2)/12\). Choosing \(b=\sqrt2\,a\) gives exactly \(1:2:3\). For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(b=0.283\,{\rm m}\), the moments are approximately \(0.0020,0.0040,0.0060\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). The component equations for these moments give the same time histories as the scaled numerical example for identical initial angular velocities, while the actual angular momentum and energy are smaller by a factor 500. This gives a physical specimen for checking principal-axis stability without requiring a massive rotor.

Adding a small mass at a known location changes both inertia and possibly the centre of mass. If a symmetric pair of equal small masses is attached at \(\pm d\hat e_3\), their combined mass \(m_a\) adds \(m_a d^2\) to \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), nothing to \(I_3\), and leaves the centre fixed. For the plate above and \(m_a=0.10\,{\rm kg}, d=0.10\,{\rm m}\), the new moments are \(0.0030,0.0050,0.0060\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). The intermediate-axis growth exponent at fixed \(\Omega\) changes from \(|\Omega|/\sqrt3\) to \(|\Omega|\sqrt{(0.005-0.003)(0.006-0.005)/(0.003\cdot0.006)}=|\Omega|/3\). A reversible mass-placement experiment tests the inertia dependence of instability, not merely the existence of a visually dramatic flip.

Estimate distance from a separatrix using dwell time

Near the intermediate-axis energy, elliptic modulus approaches one on either side. Writing \(d=1-k_e^2\) on the low-energy family gives \(K\sim\ln(4/\sqrt d)\). Thus \(T_b\sim4\ln(4/\sqrt d)/\nu\) for a slowly varying scale \(\nu\). Changing \(d\) by a factor of one hundred increases the expected body period by approximately \(4\ln10/\nu\). A tiny difference in the initial inertia-weighted energy can produce a readily observed increase in the time between reorientation events while the maximum angular speed scarcely changes. The logarithm follows from passages near a hyperbolic equilibrium, where the signed unstable perturbation grows exponentially.

If \(\nu=3.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(d=10^{-4}\), the leading estimate is \(T_b=(4/3)\ln400\approx7.99\,{\rm s}\). Reducing \(d\) to \(10^{-6}\) gives \(T_b\approx11.06\,{\rm s}\). Inverting this relation makes estimates of \(d\) exponentially sensitive to a period offset. An uncertain start or stop event of a few tenths of a second, or a weak external torque during a long dwell, can affect an inferred separatrix energy considerably. Report the uncertainty of \(E-L^2/(2I_2)\) as well as the fitted period when claiming close approach to the unstable family.

One experimental check is to use both the crossing speed of \(\omega_2=0\) and the time between successive such crossings. At a low-energy upward crossing the slope has magnitude \(B\nu\); it remains finite near the separatrix even while the period diverges. A simple global slowdown from increased bearing drag tends to modify the crossing speed as well as the dwell. A correctly prepared near-separatrix torque-free motion has relatively rapid crossings separated by long intervals close to intermediate-axis rotation. The distinction is visible in the time domain and does not depend on estimating a derivative at the slowest, noisiest part of the record.

A fit places a nominally torque-free experiment slightly above the separatrix when \(E,L\) are computed from gyros, but below it when inferred from the oscillating component pattern. What should be checked first?

Work through the solution

Recalculate both invariants from the same time stamps, calibrated orientation and inertia axes. The energy classification subtracts comparable quantities and can be sensitive to cross-axis bias. The low-energy family keeps its third component's sign, whereas the high-energy family keeps its first component's sign with the stated ordering. Verify that a full body period was actually recorded and that sign changes were resolvable. Persistent disagreement after uncertainty and sampling checks can indicate a slow torque, changing mass distribution or a misspecified principal-axis alignment.

Quantify a small external torque before fitting a free-top period

Suppose the laboratory angular momentum has magnitude \(3.0\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and an approximately constant transverse torque \(0.010\,{\rm N\,m}\) acts for a \(6.0\,{\rm s}\) body period. The accumulated transverse impulse is \(0.060\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), enough to tilt the spatial angular-momentum direction by roughly \(0.020\,{\rm rad}\), or \(1.15^\circ\). Its magnitude changes only to second order if the torque remains perpendicular to the initial momentum. A check using only \(L^2\) could therefore show a small relative error while the invariable plane has moved perceptibly.

Use two marked inertial reference directions to track the spatial momentum vector and plot its Cartesian increments against the integrated measured torque. When gravity acts about a support whose line passes through the mass centre, its torque vanishes ideally, but a small offset can supply just such a transverse disturbance. Reversing the initial spin while retaining the support geometry helps distinguish a fixed parasitic torque from gyro bias. If the spatial direction changes appreciably during one long near-separatrix dwell, fitting a torque-free elliptic period to the complete signal becomes inaccurate even if the early, fast gyro crossings agree with the free model.

Tutorial on a torque-free body's time scales

What period should be measured for the transverse gyro in the worked example? Use the small-parameter expansion \(K=(\pi/2)[1+m/4+9m^2/64+\cdots]\) with \(m=k_e^2\).

Work through the solution

Substitution of \(m=1/12\) gives \(K\approx1.605\) and \(T_b\approx6.42\,{\rm s}\). The third-axis component repeats after about \(3.21\,{\rm s}\), while the second component first reaches its positive maximum at \(1.61\,{\rm s}\). Checking all three features tests the phase relation among the gyros and helps identify a sensor axis swap.

For a body with \(I_1=I_2<I_3\), how do Euler's equations simplify and which feature of the asymmetric example disappears?

Work through the solution

The third body component remains exactly constant, and the first two rotate sinusoidally at body-frame frequency \((I_3-I_1)|\omega_3|/I_1\). The polar angle to fixed angular momentum is constant. A body axis can still precess in space, but the varying torque-free polar nutation caused by distinct transverse inertias disappears. Taking this limit in the low-energy solution gives \(k_e\to0\) consistently.

A gyro experiment reports slow amplitude decay along with fixed spatial angular momentum. Can that arise in a strictly rigid, torque-free model conserving kinetic energy?

Work through the solution

The two rigid-body invariants fix the allowed intersection curve, so a persistent drift between such curves requires an effect beyond that ideal model or a changing calibration. Internal dissipation in a deformable body can decrease kinetic energy while conserving total spatial angular momentum, favouring rotation about the largest-inertia axis. An external torque could instead change angular momentum. Measuring both invariants helps distinguish the two explanations.
