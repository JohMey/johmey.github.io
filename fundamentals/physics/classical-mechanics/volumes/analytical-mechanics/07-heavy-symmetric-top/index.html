The heavy symmetric top, precession branches and the sleeping state

Gravity supplies a torque to a top pivoted away from its centre of mass. Its axis may precess steadily, nutate between two turning angles, or trace loops. Derive all three from the same angular kinetic energy and determine when an upright spinning top can remain stable.

Project gravity onto a body with one fixed point

Let \(I_1=I_2=I\) and \(I_3\) be moments about the pivot, with the centre of mass a distance \(l\) along the symmetry axis. Use Euler angles \(\theta,\phi,\psi\), where \(\theta\) measures the tilt from upward laboratory vertical, \(\phi\) measures the azimuth of the symmetry axis, and \(\psi\) spins the body about that axis relative to the nodal line. The centre-of-mass height is \(l\cos\theta\), so gravity contributes \(V=Mgl\cos\theta\). In the body the spin component of angular velocity is \(\omega_3=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta\). The squared transverse component is \(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta\). Consequently

\[L=\tfrac12 I(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)+\tfrac12 I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2-Mgl\cos\theta .\]

The angles \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) are absent themselves. Their momenta \(L_z=I\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta\) and \(L_3=I_3\omega_3\) are constants. Gravity torque about the pivot is perpendicular to the vertical and to the body symmetry axis, consistent with these two conservation laws. The entire vector \(\mathbf L\) need not be constant. Distinguish the axial spin \(\omega_3\) measured by a body gyro from the Euler rate \(\dot\psi\). A precessing top can have appreciable \(\dot\phi\cos\theta\); using the painted-mark rate in a spin estimate without specifying the reference can bias a precession prediction.

Eliminate cyclic velocities to find the nutation potential

Solve \(\dot\phi=(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)/(I\sin^2\theta)\) and \(\dot\psi=L_3/I_3-\dot\phi\cos\theta\). The energy can now be written \(E=L_3^2/(2I_3)+I\dot\theta^2/2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta)\), with

\[V_{\rm eff}(\theta)=Mgl\cos\theta+\frac{(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}.\]

The second term is not an additional gravitational interaction. It is the transverse rotational energy required by the two fixed angular momenta at each tilt. A root of \(E-L_3^2/(2I_3)-V_{\rm eff}=0\) is a polar turning angle; a simple root is generally reached and reversed in finite time. A minimum gives stable regular precession under sufficiently small nutations at fixed conserved momenta. The apparent divergence at a pole disappears if the numerator vanishes in the same limit. The possibility of the upright sleeping state therefore requires

\[L_z=L_3.\]

Putting \(u=\cos\theta\) gives a polynomial first integral. With \(E'=E-L_3^2/(2I_3)\), one obtains

\[I^2\dot u^2=2I(E'-Mglu)(1-u^2)-(L_z-L_3u)^2=P_3(u).\]

The accessible range lies within \([-1,1]\) where \(P_3\ge0\). The physical turning points can be found without assigning an Euler azimuth at a pole. A nutation period is \(2I\int_{u_{\min}}^{u_{\max}}du/\sqrt{P_3(u)}\). Computing the azimuthal advance over the same cycle requires averaging \(\dot\phi(u)\) with the same time weight. An arithmetic average of the two turning-point precession rates misses the longer dwell times near the endpoints.

Find slow and fast regular precession

For constant tilt and constant \(\dot\phi=\Omega\), the \(\theta\) equation at a nonzero tilt gives

\[I\Omega^2\cos\theta-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0.\]

This relation follows directly by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to \(\theta\), using \(\partial_\theta[\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta]=-\dot\phi\sin\theta\). It is quadratic in precession speed, so the often-used \(\Omega\simeq Mgl/L_3\) is only one limit. For a top above the horizontal plane and \(L_3>0\), two positive precession branches exist if \(L_3^2\ge4IMgl\cos\theta\). Their sum is \(L_3/(I\cos\theta)\), and their product is \(Mgl/(I\cos\theta)\).

Take \(I=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(I_3=0.010\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(Mgl=0.200\,{\rm N\,m}\),

\[L_3=0.200\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s},\]
\[\theta=60^\circ.\]

The quadratic is \(0.010\Omega^2-0.200\Omega+0.200=0\), with roots \(1.056\) and \(18.94\,{\rm rad/s}\). Both have the same material spin \(\omega_3=20\,{\rm rad/s}\), but their vertical angular momenta are different. The slow branch has \(L_z=0.020(1.056)(0.75)+0.200(0.5)=0.1158\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\); the fast branch has about \(0.384\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\). Preparing a tilt and a spin alone therefore does not select a unique regular precession. The initial angular velocity of the tilted axis also matters.

Distinguish looping, cusped and monotone azimuth traces

During nutation the sign of \(\dot\phi\) is the sign of \(L_z-L_3u\). If \(u_*=L_z/L_3\) lies strictly between the turning points, the azimuth reverses along each nutation and the axis can trace loops on the unit sphere. If \(u_*\) equals a turning point, both \(\dot\theta\) and \(\dot\phi\) can vanish together there, producing a cusp in the ideal trace. If the numerator keeps one sign the azimuth advances monotonically even as tilt oscillates. Seeing an apparent loop in a two-dimensional camera projection is insufficient by itself; recover the three-dimensional orientation or check the signed azimuthal rate.

One classic release starts with a tilted axis that initially has no angular velocity except spin about its own direction. Then \(L_z=L_3\cos\theta_0\), \(\dot\phi=0\), \(\dot\theta=0\). The gravitational torque starts tilting the axis down; on returning to that initial turning point the projected angular speed again momentarily vanishes. By contrast, giving the top the correct nonzero precession velocity at release puts it on a regular precession branch with no initial nutation. Identical tilt and spin measurements can thus yield dramatically different trajectories if the release mechanism imparts a different transverse angular velocity.

Derive the upright stability threshold

At \(L_z=L_3\), reduce the ratio \((1-\cos\theta)^2/\sin^2\theta\) to \((1-\cos\theta)/(1+\cos\theta)\). Near zero tilt the effective potential is \(Mgl+[L_3^2/(8I)-Mgl/2]\theta^2+O(\theta^4)\). A positive coefficient gives an energy minimum and requires \(L_3^2>4IMgl\). For the preceding \(I,Mgl\) this critical angular momentum is \(0.1265\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), or an axial spin speed \(12.65\,{\rm rad/s}\) for the chosen \(I_3\). A high spin stabilises the upper position by coupling tilt to conservation of angular momentum. The purely gravitational potential by itself has a maximum there.

The fixed-\(L_z=L_3\) quadratic energy curvature describes a polar disturbance within that momentum class. Near the axis \(\phi,\psi\) individually become coordinate singularities. To analyse arbitrary small perturbations, use horizontal components of the unit axis and linearise their coupled gyroscopic equations. The two circular precession frequencies at the pole solve \(I\Omega^2-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\), giving the same discriminant. Exactly at threshold the linear frequencies coalesce, and nonlinear terms or dissipation can dominate a finite disturbance. Distinguishing a stable ideal top from an experimentally long-lived slowly slowing top requires monitoring spin over the entire observation interval.

Measure the nutation frequency of a regular precession branch

Take any regular precession tilted away from the poles, with prescribed constants \(L_z,L_3\). A small perturbation of the polar angle gives \(I\ddot\eta+V_{\rm eff}''(\theta_0)\eta=0\), so the nutation frequency at fixed momenta follows from the second derivative of the effective potential. Differentiating while holding the instantaneous \(\dot\phi\) fixed would give a different result, because a displaced top with the same momenta must change its precession rate. This distinction matters when preparing a perturbation: a small tap that adds a transverse angular impulse can change the constants as well as the tilt.

A convenient explicit formula follows by writing \(F(\theta)=L_z-L_3\cos\theta\),

\[w(\theta)=F/(I\sin^2\theta).\]

Then \(V_{\rm eff}'=\sin\theta[-Mgl+L_3w-Iw^2\cos\theta]\). At a regular solution the bracket vanishes. Differentiate \(w\) as \(w'=(L_3/I-2w\cos\theta)/\sin\theta\), substitute into the derivative of the bracket, and obtain

\[\omega_{\rm nut}^2=\frac{V_{\rm eff}''(\theta_0)}{I}=\left(\frac{L_3}{I}-2\Omega\cos\theta_0\right)^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta_0.\]

For the \(60^\circ\) example,

\[L_3/I=10.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

On the slow branch \(\Omega=1.056\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), this gives \(\omega_{\rm nut}\approx8.99\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), or a nutation period of \(0.699\,{\rm s}\). On the fast branch \(\Omega=18.94\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), it gives approximately \(18.7\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), or \(0.336\,{\rm s}\). Both tilted regular solutions have positive fixed-momentum curvature in this example. Comparing just the precession rates would miss the different small oscillations around their two reduced equilibria.

Suppose a small angular displacement conserves \(L_z,L_3\) on the slow branch. The correlated rate shift is \(w'\delta\theta\). At the example point \(w'=(10-1.056)/\sin60^\circ=10.33\,{\rm s^{-1}/rad}\). A nutation of \(0.020\,{\rm rad}\) therefore modulates the precession rate by about \(0.207\,{\rm rad/s}\), a substantial fraction of its slow steady value. A low-resolution video that measures azimuth per frame can show a conspicuous modulation even when the nutation angle itself is visually small. Filtering only one angle and fitting it with constant angular speed would hide this dynamical correlation.

Recover a nutation trajectory from energy and two turn angles

Suppose a measured top has \(I=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(Mgl=0.20\,{\rm J}\), \(L_3=0.20\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), and is released without precession or nutation at \(\cos\theta_0=0.50\). Its constants are \(L_z=0.10\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and \(E'=0.10\,{\rm J}\). The polar polynomial becomes

\[P_3(u)=0.04(0.10-0.20u)(1-u^2)-(0.10-0.20u)^2.\]

Factoring out \(0.10-0.20u\) leaves \(0.04(1-u^2)-(0.10-0.20u)\). The other roots satisfy \(0.04u^2-0.20u+0.06=0\), giving \(u\simeq0.3206\) and \(u\simeq4.68\). Only the first lies within the physical interval. Thus the tilt runs from \(60^\circ\) to about \(71.3^\circ\), and the unphysical cubic root must be discarded.

At the initial \(u=0.50\), the azimuthal numerator vanishes and the top momentarily has no precession. At the lower turning \(u\simeq0.3206\), the precession rate is \((0.10-0.20u)/[0.020(1-u^2)]\approx2.0\,{\rm rad/s}\). These two measurements together discriminate the cusped release from regular slow precession. A detector reporting only a mean precession rate near one radian per second could confuse them. Timing the first turning angle after release offers an independent check of \(Mgl/I\), while a direct gyro constrains \(L_3/I_3\).

To evaluate the nutation quadrature numerically without endpoint difficulties, substitute \(u=u_{\min}+(u_{\max}-u_{\min})\sin^2\alpha\). As \(\alpha\) approaches either endpoint, \(du\) cancels the simple square-root singularity from \(P_3\). Integrate once with numerator one for time and once with the extra factor \(\dot\phi(u)\) for azimuth. Repeat after halving the quadrature spacing. An exactly repeated root signals a limiting regular solution or a separatrix; the simple-turning substitution alone cannot regularise an actual logarithmically divergent period. In the cusped trajectory above the angular trace is continuous at the polar turning, even though the tangent to that trace can vanish.

Use horizontal tilt coordinates at the sleeping pole

Near the upright orientation the separate Euler angles \(\phi,\psi\) are ill conditioned. Instead let the symmetry-axis unit vector be \(\mathbf n=(x,y,1)+O(x^2+y^2)\) in an inertial frame with \(z\) upward. Here \(x,y\) are small dimensionless tilts, not translations. The instantaneous transverse angular velocity is \(\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n}\approx(-\dot y,\dot x,0)\), because \((\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n})\times\mathbf n=\dot{\mathbf n}\). The laboratory angular momentum to first order is \(I\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n}+L_3\mathbf n\). Its derivative equals the gravitational torque \(Mgl\,\hat z\times\mathbf n\). Equating the two horizontal components yields

\[I\ddot x+L_3\dot y-Mgl\,x=0,\]
\[I\ddot y-L_3\dot x-Mgl\,y=0.\]

Let \(w=x+iy\). These combine into \(I\ddot w-iL_3\dot w-Mgl\,w=0\). Substituting \(w\propto e^{i\Omega t}\) returns \(I\Omega^2-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\) without ever assigning azimuth to the exact pole. For spin strictly above threshold, a general small tilt is a superposition of two circular motions of the two real frequencies. Their vector sum traces a wobble. For spin below threshold, the two frequencies form a complex conjugate pair and one mode grows exponentially. A perfectly vertical initial state can remain exactly vertical in an ideal simulation on either side, so stability requires a nonzero test perturbation.

Take a below-threshold spin \(L_3=0.100\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) with the earlier \(I=0.020\) and \(Mgl=0.200\). The real precession rate is \(L_3/(2I)=2.50\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and the exponential growth rate is

\[\sqrt{4IMgl-L_3^2}/(2I)=1.94\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

If the growing-mode component starts at \(1\,{\rm mrad}\), a \(20\,{\rm mrad}\) excursion takes about \(\ln20/1.94=1.55\,{\rm s}\) within the linear model. Below threshold the top need not collapse immediately after release. A short video can show several degrees of apparently orderly precession before growing tilt becomes unmistakable.

Just above threshold the mode frequencies are nearly degenerate. Decomposing noisy initial conditions into two circular amplitudes then involves division by their small frequency separation. A finite tilt impulse can produce a large beat envelope even though both ideal modes remain bounded. At exactly the repeated root the general linear solution contains \(t e^{i\Omega t}\) as well as \(e^{i\Omega t}\); neutral-looking roots alone do not ensure boundedness. This distinction matters when inferring a critical spin from finite observation windows. Report the initial perturbation and the tilt threshold used to define loss of stability.

Infer the centre-of-mass offset from controlled precession

If regular precession can be prepared at a known tilt, the quadratic balance gives

\[Mgl=L_3\Omega-I\Omega^2\cos\theta.\]

Neglecting the second term is customary only on a sufficiently slow branch. With the slow \(60^\circ\) case the neglected term is about \(0.0112\,{\rm N\,m}\), or \(5.6\%\) of the true gravitational torque scale. Inferring the centre-of-mass offset by using \(l=L_3\Omega/(Mg)\) would overestimate \(l\) by this fraction. On the fast branch the two terms nearly cancel and a small error in \(I\) or \(\Omega\) can be magnified substantially in the inferred \(Mgl\).

For a top of measured mass \(0.50\,{\rm kg}\) and \(Mgl=0.200\,{\rm N\,m}\), the offset is \(0.0408\,{\rm m}\). An uncertainty of \(0.002\,{\rm N\,m}\) in gravitational torque corresponds to about \(0.41\,{\rm mm}\) before mass and local gravity uncertainty. Repeat at several known tilts while measuring body spin and laboratory precession individually. A consistent \(l\) across slow and fast branches checks the kinetic-energy model, whereas a gradual decrease of apparent \(L_3\) during a single run suggests pivot drag or aerodynamic torque. A static suspension measurement of the centre of mass is a useful independent reference when estimating inertias from dynamic data.

Verify the torque law with a horizontal gyroscope

An instructive limiting experiment supports one end of a spinning wheel axle at a pivot, leaving its symmetry axis nearly horizontal. Choose the centre of mass along the free end of the axle and define the wheel's axial angular momentum by the right-hand rule. The gravitational torque is \(\boldsymbol\tau=Mgl\,\hat z\times\mathbf n\). If \(\mathbf L\) is approximately \(L_3\mathbf n\), a regular precession at laboratory angular speed \(\Omega\) gives

\[\dot{\mathbf L}\approx L_3\Omega\,\hat z\times\mathbf n.\]

Equating the torque gives

\[\Omega=Mgl/L_3.\]

At exactly horizontal tilt this agrees with the finite root of the full quadratic; slightly away from horizontal the small transverse angular momentum affects the quantitative result.

For a wheel with \(I_3=0.012\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), axial spin \(40\,{\rm rad/s}\), mass \(0.40\,{\rm kg}\) and pivot offset \(0.060\,{\rm m}\),

\[L_3=0.480\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s},\]
\[Mgl=0.235\,{\rm N\,m}.\]

Horizontal precession is \(0.490\,{\rm rad/s}\), one revolution in \(12.8\,{\rm s}\). Reversing the spin reverses the precession direction. Moving the pivot to the other side of the centre also reverses the gravitational torque for a fixed axis convention. Testing each reversal separately helps distinguish the true gyroscopic response from a static torque due to a tilted support or a twisting suspension lead.

The phrase that gravity causes the wheel to stay up hides a vertical support force and a changing horizontal angular momentum. The pivot supplies most or all of the vertical force required to support the wheel's weight. With steady horizontal precession, the centre of mass also accelerates horizontally toward the precession axis with magnitude \(l\Omega^2\). Thus the pivot must supply a horizontal radial reaction \(Ml\Omega^2\). For the numerical wheel this is only \(0.0058\,{\rm N}\), much smaller than the \(3.92\,{\rm N}\) vertical support. A torque signal can be easy to see through slow precession while the corresponding horizontal reaction is weak. A load cell measuring only vertical force would not directly measure the gravitational torque acting about the pivot.

Spin friction alters the interpretation of a long run. Suppose the measured axial spin falls from \(40\) to \(35\,{\rm rad/s}\) over one minute and the axis remains close to horizontal regular precession. The predicted precession speed rises from \(0.490\) to \(0.561\,{\rm rad/s}\), even though mechanical energy is being dissipated. This is not negative damping. Less axial angular momentum must change direction under essentially the same gravitational torque, so its direction turns more rapidly. If the spin-down time becomes comparable with a nutation period, an instantaneous regular-precession approximation is no longer sufficient; include dissipative torques in the full equations and measure both tilt and angular momentum.

Extract polar turning points from uncertain orientation data

A camera supplies a unit direction \(\mathbf n(t)\), while body and pivot calibrations supply \(I,I_3,Mgl\). To infer \(L_3\), either measure the gyro component along \(\mathbf n\) or combine two independent orientation marks so that spin about the axis is observable. A single unmarked axis direction cannot reveal that spin. Vertical angular momentum follows from \(L_z=I(\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n})_z+L_3 n_z\). This expression remains regular at a vertical pose and avoids differentiating an azimuth divided by a small \(\sin\theta\). Estimating \(\dot{\mathbf n}\) still requires timing and finite-difference checks, especially near a fast turn.

The transverse kinetic energy is \(I|\dot{\mathbf n}|^2/2\), so \(E'=I|\dot{\mathbf n}|^2/2+Mgl\,n_z\). These vector formulas allow each time sample to supply independent estimates of the invariants. Compute their median or uncertainty-weighted values over a torque-free-about-vertical segment, then locate the physical roots of \(P_3(u)\). Reject roots outside \([-1,1]\) before converting to angles, and propagate measured parameter ranges into intervals for the roots. Near a repeated root a small coefficient error can split the pair substantially or apparently remove it. A nearly steady precession should therefore be assessed using the dynamic measurement uncertainty, not classified solely by the discriminant of a fitted cubic.

A direction-only camera sees apparently steady \(60^\circ\) precession at \(1.0\,{\rm rad/s}\). Can it infer the axial spin without measuring a second body mark?

Work through the solution

In a precisely known ideal regular state the torque balance gives \(L_3=(I\Omega^2\cos\theta+Mgl)/\Omega\), so one can infer spin from a dynamical model and calibrated inertias. Using \(I=0.020\), \(Mgl=0.200\) gives

\[L_3=0.210\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}.\]

That is an indirect inference, sensitive to gravity calibration, pivot torque, and the assumption of exactly regular precession. The orientation of one axis alone supplies no purely kinematic measurement of rotation about itself. A second mark or gyro offers an independent check rather than fitting the same torque law twice.

A numerical implementation rescales \(\mathbf n\) to unit length at every step and observes bounded plotted angles. Does this establish correct top dynamics?

Work through the solution

Unit length is only the configuration constraint. Reconstruct \(L_z,L_3,E'\), compare polar turning points with the cubic, and repeat at smaller time step. The vector can stay exactly on the sphere while its azimuth and nutation phase drift, or while a spurious torque transfers axial angular momentum. A support torque and gravitational work budget, with a clear reference for the potential, test complementary aspects that normalisation alone cannot enforce.

Check the fixed-pivot inertia before drawing an instability boundary

A laboratory data sheet may quote the transverse inertia about the centre of mass, whereas the Lagrangian here needs the inertia about the pivot. With a pivot on the symmetry axis a distance \(l\) from the centre, the parallel-axis relation gives \(I=I_{\rm cm}+Ml^2\) for each transverse direction, while \(I_3\) remains unchanged. For \(M=0.50\,{\rm kg}\), \(l=0.080\,{\rm m}\), and \(I_{\rm cm}=0.010\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), the correct value is \(0.0132\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). Neglecting the shift underpredicts the critical spin by a factor \(\sqrt{0.010/0.0132}=0.870\).

Moving the pivot changes both the gravitational torque scale \(Mgl\) and the transverse inertia. Doubling \(l\) does not merely multiply the stability threshold by \(\sqrt2\) if \(Ml^2\) is appreciable. Compute the new \(I\) and then the product that enters \(L_{3,\rm crit}^2=4IMgl\). A two-pivot experiment with one unchanged rotor provides a direct test of the parallel-axis correction and checks why a table of freely rotating principal moments cannot be inserted unaltered into a supported top calculation.

Tutorial on a heavy top

With the same constants, impose a tilt of \(90^\circ\). Does the regular precession formula become singular when \(\cos\theta=0\)?

Work through the solution

The unsimplified quadratic becomes \(-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\), so the finite regular precession is

\[\Omega=Mgl/L_3=1.0\,{\rm rad/s}.\]

A quadratic formula written with \(2I\cos\theta\) in the denominator masks this regular solution. The other branch becomes unbounded as the horizontal configuration is approached from the side with positive cosine at fixed spin. Keep the original equation when taking a degenerate limit.

A top undergoes nutation with \(u\) between \(0.30\) and \(0.70\), with \(L_z=0.11\) and \(L_3=0.20\) in consistent angular-momentum units. Does the azimuth reverse?

Work through the solution

The numerator of \(\dot\phi\) vanishes at \(u=0.55\), inside the allowed interval. For \(u<0.55\) precession is positive in this convention; for \(u>0.55\) it is negative. A camera recording the full axis should therefore find an azimuthal reversal twice per nutation period. The polar velocity need not vanish at those two reversals, so these instants are distinct from the polar turning points.

A frictionless rigid-top simulation reports exact \(E,L_z,L_3\) but returns a nutation frequency differing by ten percent from an independent measurement. Name two checks that use more than the conserved values.

Work through the solution

Compute the turning points from the cubic and compare the measured times between them against its quadrature, then reconstruct the azimuthal phase over the same cycle. Check whether the measured spin was \(\omega_3\) or a painted-mark Euler rate and whether the inertias include the parallel-axis contribution about the pivot. A numerical method can conserve constants while producing a phase error, and an inconsistent initial state can preserve different constants perfectly.
