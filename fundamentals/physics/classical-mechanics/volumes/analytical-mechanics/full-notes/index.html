  2. Moving constraints, boundary work and a changing energy

    A pendulum with a reeled string can gain energy while its support force does no virtual work. A bead on a spinning guide can accelerate without gravity. Follow the work done by the apparatus, derive the relation between a reduced energy and laboratory energy, and identify which quantity a measurement tests.

  3. Noether's theorem, boundary terms and measurable constants

    A constant of motion can survive when neither laboratory energy nor angular momentum is separately conserved. Construct the conserved quantity from an infinitesimal transformation of the action. Then use boosts, a screw symmetry, and a rotating potential to determine what measured combinations should stay fixed.

  4. Kepler orbits, the eccentricity vector and the loss of apsidal closure

    An orbit can conserve energy and angular momentum yet gradually rotate its periapsis. The inverse-square problem possesses one more conserved vector that fixes the direction of closest approach. Derive it, turn a single position and velocity measurement into a conic, and measure what a weak perturbation does to the inferred apsidal direction.

  5. Scattering from a central potential and what angular data can recover

    A detector records angles, whereas the force law refers to separations that were never directly observed. Derive the forward map from incoming impact parameter to angle, distinguish the several trajectories a detector may count in one angular bin, and invert sufficiently complete signed deflection data under stated hypotheses.

  6. Euler–Poinsot motion, torque-free nutation and the intermediate axis

    A freely rotating body has fixed angular momentum in space, yet the components recorded by three body-mounted gyroscopes can vary periodically or linger near an unstable rotation. Solve the nonsymmetric top from its invariants, distinguish its body-component period from its spatial orientation, and extract a stability time scale from a measured inertia tensor.

  7. The heavy symmetric top, precession branches and the sleeping state

    Gravity supplies a torque to a top pivoted away from its centre of mass. Its axis may precess steadily, nutate between two turning angles, or trace loops. Derive all three from the same angular kinetic energy and determine when an upright spinning top can remain stable.

  8. Symplectic transformations, Poisson brackets and the geometry of measurements

    Two phase-space plots can enclose the same volume while predicting different responses to an impulse. Derive the stronger condition that makes a change of variables canonical, test it on measured linear maps and use Poisson brackets to propagate observables without solving separately for every coordinate.

  9. Action, angle and the slowing clock at a separatrix

    A phase portrait contains enough information to extract a period by differentiating an area. Use that fact to build a clock for finite-amplitude pendulum motion, determine precisely where slow-parameter invariance fails, and distinguish a librating trajectory from one that makes complete rotations.

  10. Canonical perturbations, small denominators and resonant capture

    A weak periodic force can produce a bounded ripple on most trajectories and a large change on a narrow band of initial conditions. Derive the averaging transformation away from resonance, construct the slow resonant dynamics where that transformation fails, and estimate the physically observable width and time scale of a resonance.

  11. Action density, stress waves and the boundary that reflects them

    A chain of coupled masses becomes an elastic field when the wavelength is long compared with the spacing. Derive the field equation and its boundary traction from the same variation, follow energy and stress across a junction, and decide whether a measured pulse is limited by material waves or by numerical dispersion.

  12. Relativistic collisions, centre-of-momentum energy and thresholds

    Two particles with the same total laboratory energy can have very different energy available for changing their internal state. Determine the energy and motion of the centre-of-momentum system, reconstruct two-body elastic scattering, and follow how a detector threshold transforms between frames.

  13. Which path is shortest across the surface of a cone?

    A point can move along a conical surface while its azimuth and height both change. Unroll the geometry and predict the shortest path between two marked points. The measurable minimum radius distinguishes the geodesic from a line drawn along a horizontal circle.

  14. Motion in a medium and momentum carried across a boundary

    Two beads can approach exactly the same terminal speed with recognisably different histories. A sand cart can slow down while the total horizontal momentum of sand and cart stays fixed. Both observations begin by specifying which material the equation of motion follows.

  15. From a repeating trajectory to a chaotic attractor

    A damped pendulum under a periodic drive can settle into the same orbit on each run yet visit a different point on successive drive periods. A return map supplies the right clock for distinguishing a new period from a slowly decaying transient.

  16. From differential acceleration to three-dimensional strain and stress

    An accelerometer in a falling laboratory can read zero while a pair of falling test masses moves apart. A crystal can support different longitudinal and transverse speeds in the same direction. Both measurements require the spatial variation of a vector field, represented by a tensor.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 1. Virtual work and the forces hidden by a constraint

A bead follows a wire, a pendulum remains the same length, and a rolling wheel obeys a relation between translation and rotation. Their reduced equations describe the motion but can conceal the largest forces in the apparatus. Determine which forces disappear under a virtual displacement, recover their values, and decide when the assumed motion ceases to be physically admissible.

Hold the clock fixed when defining a virtual displacement

A system of particles has Cartesian positions \(\mathbf r_i\) and applied forces \(\mathbf F_i\). A holonomic constraint is an equation \(g_\alpha(\mathbf r_1,\ldots,\mathbf r_N,t)=0\). At a fixed instant an admissible infinitesimal change of configuration obeys \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\). The physical velocities obey a different relation, \(\sum_i\nabla_i g_\alpha\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_i+\partial_tg_\alpha=0\). A virtual displacement compares geometrically allowed configurations at the same time. It has no prescribed duration and makes no claim that the system's momentum actually points along it.

The distinction becomes measurable for a bead on a moving horizontal table. Every allowed virtual displacement has zero vertical component, so the table's upward normal force gives zero virtual work. An upward-moving table can nevertheless raise the bead and perform positive ordinary work. Ordinary power involves the actual velocity, which includes the table motion. A careless statement that constraint forces do no work confuses two different operations. For a fixed ideal frictionless surface the real velocity is tangent as well, and vanishing virtual work then does imply vanishing ordinary power.

Let \(\mathbf R_i\) be the reaction forces and use \(m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i+\mathbf R_i\). Multiply by an admissible \(\delta\mathbf r_i\) and sum over particles. We call the constraints ideal when \(\sum_i\mathbf R_i\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\) for every allowed virtual displacement. Eliminating those reactions gives d'Alembert's principle,

\[\sum_i(\mathbf F_i-m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0.\]

This is a dynamical equation expressed through a static-looking virtual-work test. It applies instantaneously along the motion. It does not assert that the total force vanishes or that the acceleration is an independent virtual displacement. One advantage is that the detailed construction of a rigid support need not be known to determine the admissible tangential acceleration; its force can be calculated afterwards when designing the support.

Pass from virtual work to equations for coordinates

Choose independent coordinates \(q_a\) with \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r_i(q,t)\) and independent virtual increments \(\delta q_a\). The velocity is \(\sum_a\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i\,\dot q_a+\partial_t\mathbf r_i\). With kinetic energy \(T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i^2/2\), differentiation at fixed \(q,t\) gives

\[\partial_{\dot q_a}T=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Differentiate in time. The product rule produces an acceleration term and a second term involving \(d(\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i)/dt\). The latter equals \(\partial_{q_a}\dot{\mathbf r}_i\), with the generalised velocities held fixed, and supplies \(\partial_{q_a}T\). Hence

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_a}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_a}=\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf r_i}{\partial q_a}.\]

The projected applied force is

\[Q_a=\sum_i\mathbf F_i\cdot\partial_{q_a}\mathbf r_i.\]

Independence of the coordinate variations in d'Alembert's principle gives one equation \(d(\partial_{\dot q_a}T)/dt-\partial_{q_a}T=Q_a\) per coordinate. When \(\mathbf F_i\) comes from a potential \(V(q,t)\), the conservative contribution is \(-\partial_{q_a}V\). Other contributions such as a measured resisting torque remain explicit. A coordinate expressed as an angle has a generalised force in joules per radian; a coordinate expressed as a length has one in newtons. Checking \(Q_a\,dq_a\) as work avoids mistaking a generalised force for a Cartesian component.

For a simple pendulum of length \(l\) and mass \(m\), choose \(\theta\) measured from downward vertical. Then \(T=ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\) and \(V=mgl(1-\cos\theta)\). The equation \(ml^2\ddot\theta=-mgl\sin\theta\) follows at once. The string tension vanished under the angular displacement. It has not been shown to vanish physically. Projecting Newton's equation onto the direction from the bead toward the pivot gives

\[T_{\rm string}=mg\cos\theta+ml\dot\theta^2.\]

This second observable is sensitive to speed even when the reduced angular acceleration depends only on angle.

Recover multipliers before choosing coordinates

Cartesian coordinates keep reaction directions explicit. Put all coordinates into a vector \(x\), let \(M\) be the mass matrix and let \(G\) contain the rows \(\nabla g_\alpha\). The tangent virtual displacements are precisely those satisfying \(G\delta x=0\). A reaction doing zero virtual work must be a linear combination of the normal covectors, \(R=G^T\lambda\), when the constraints are independent. Newton's equation becomes

\[M\ddot x=F+G^T\lambda.\]

Differentiate the constraints twice along the trajectory: \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\), where \(\gamma\) includes the known velocity, curvature and explicit time terms. Substituting for \(\ddot x\) determines reactions from a much smaller system,

\[(GM^{-1}G^T)\lambda=-\gamma-GM^{-1}F.\]

For any vector \(c\), \(c^TGM^{-1}G^Tc=(G^Tc)^TM^{-1}(G^Tc)\) is positive if \(G\) has independent rows and particle masses are positive. Dependent constraint equations make the individual multipliers ambiguous. For instance writing both \(g=0\) and \(2g=0\) declares the same geometrical restriction twice. Only the combined reaction is then determined. The magnitude of a raw multiplier also depends on the units and normalisation of \(g\). Reporting the force \(G^T\lambda\) makes comparisons between alternative constraint descriptions meaningful.

Use \(g=(x^2+y^2-l^2)/2=0\) for a planar pendulum, with \(y\) upward, so the gradient is the radius vector \(\mathbf r\). Newton's equation reads \(m\ddot{\mathbf r}=-mg\hat y+\lambda\mathbf r\), where the symbol \(g\) in the gravitational term denotes the acceleration magnitude. The acceleration constraint \(\mathbf r\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}=-v^2\) gives

\[\lambda=(mgy-mv^2)/l^2.\]

The inward tension is \(-\lambda l=mv^2/l-mgy/l\). Since \(y=-l\cos\theta\), this is the radial-force result already obtained. The multiplier in this convention has units of force per length. Using the radial-distance constraint instead would make its magnitude a force.

Background · What if the support can push but cannot pull?

Replacing a surface by an equality constraint assumes the object stays in contact. A particle sliding along the inside of a smooth vertical circular track illustrates the additional physical test. At the top, gravity and the inward normal reaction together must supply the centripetal acceleration.

\[N=\frac{mv^2}{R}-mg,\qquad N\geq0.\]

At a top speed below \(\sqrt{gR}\) the equality-constrained solution requires negative \(N\). An ordinary track cannot exert that pull. Its normal force then falls to zero and the particle must follow free flight until it meets the track again. A bead threaded on a wire could receive the oppositely directed force and would follow a different trajectory. Both models have the same geometrical path during contact.

Recognise an inequality at a contact

A bead threaded on a stiff loop can experience reaction in either direction, whereas a string can pull only. A surface without adhesion can push only. Derive the constrained motion, calculate its reaction, and compare the required sign with the physical mechanism. When a calculated tension turns negative, replace the constrained trajectory by free flight until a new contact event. Continuing to integrate the pendulum equation would retain a mathematically valid circle and a physically unavailable pushing string.

A mass starts at the bottom of a frictionless vertical loop with speed \(v_b\). Energy conservation gives \(v_{\rm top}^2=v_b^2-4gl\), since the top is \(2l\) higher. A string just remaining taut at the top requires \(T_{\rm top}=mv_{\rm top}^2/l-mg\ge0\), giving \(v_b^2\ge5gl\). The energy-only criterion for reaching the elevation is \(v_b^2\ge4gl\); the interval between these two values illustrates why a trajectory test must include reactions. With \(l=0.80\,{\rm m}\), the threshold bottom speed is \(6.26\,{\rm m/s}\). At this threshold the bottom tension is \(6mg\), six times the weight, even though the tension is about to vanish at the top.

Recover the forces on a wedge that is free to slide

Place a wedge of mass \(M\) on a smooth horizontal floor and a block of mass \(m\) on its smooth upper face. Let the slope descend to the right at angle \(\alpha\). Choose \(X\) as the wedge's horizontal position and \(s\) as distance down the face. The block's coordinates apart from fixed offsets are \(x=X+s\cos\alpha,\ y=-s\sin\alpha\). Its relative velocity points down the slope but its absolute acceleration need not. The kinetic energy contains a mixed term \(m\dot X\dot s\cos\alpha\). Dropping that term is equivalent to holding the wedge stationary while allowing it to recoil, two incompatible mechanical models.

The reduced equations are \((M+m)\ddot X+m\ddot s\cos\alpha=0\) and \(\ddot s+\ddot X\cos\alpha=g\sin\alpha\). The first is conservation of total horizontal momentum, since the block–wedge normal force is internal and the floor is frictionless. Elimination gives

\[\ddot s=(M+m)g\sin\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha),\]
\[\ddot X=-mg\sin\alpha\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

The acceleration down the moving face exceeds \(g\sin\alpha\) because the wedge moves left as the block slides right. Nevertheless the absolute vertical acceleration of the block remains less than or equal to free fall because the upper face pushes upward.

To find the block's normal force, project its absolute acceleration onto the outward unit normal \((\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)\). The part proportional to \(\ddot s\) cancels, leaving \(\ddot X\sin\alpha\). Newton's equation along that direction gives

\[N=m(g\cos\alpha+\ddot X\sin\alpha)=mMg\cos\alpha/(M+m\sin^2\alpha).\]

With \(M=3.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=1.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(\alpha=30^\circ\), one finds \(\ddot s=6.04\,{\rm m/s^2}\),

\[\ddot X=-1.31\,{\rm m/s^2},\]
\[N=7.84\,{\rm N}.\]

The normal based on a stationary wedge would be \(8.50\,{\rm N}\). A force sensor under the moving face could distinguish the two models even when a short record of relative slope displacement cannot resolve the difference.

Which reaction does no virtual work here? The normal force on the block has virtual work \(N\hat n\cdot(\delta X\hat x+\delta s\hat t)=N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\). The opposite normal force on the wedge gives \(-N\sin\alpha\,\delta X\), so the sum cancels. Considering only the block and asserting zero work from the normal for arbitrary \(\delta X\) would be incorrect. At the real velocities the same reaction transfers energy between the bodies even though its net internal power on their pair is zero. The floor's normal reaction balances the vertical momentum change of the whole system. During the slide it is \((M+m)g-m\ddot s\sin\alpha\), less than total weight while the block accelerates downwards.

Take the limits as an additional test. At fixed \(m,\alpha\), increasing \(M\) to infinity returns the familiar fixed-wedge results. At a nearly horizontal face, downslope acceleration and wedge recoil vanish, restoring the full block weight as normal force. At a vertical face the normal vanishes and the block falls. These limits hold because the contact has no friction and can be maintained with a nonnegative normal. A moving wedge of independently prescribed acceleration would replace the horizontal momentum equation by a control input, changing both the relative acceleration and the support force.

Distinguish rolling constraints from purely geometric restrictions

A cylinder of radius \(R\), mass \(m\) and axial inertia \(I\) rolls without slip down a fixed rough incline. Choose coordinate \(s\) down the incline and spin angle \(\phi\) in the corresponding sense. The constraint \(\dot s=R\dot\phi\) is integrable in this one-dimensional motion, so it can be inserted as \(s-R\phi=\mathrm{constant}\). The resulting kinetic energy is \((m+I/R^2)\dot s^2/2\). The reduced equation gives

\[\ddot s=g\sin\alpha/[1+I/(mR^2)].\]

Reconstruct the static friction from translation, \(f=g m\sin\alpha-m\ddot s\), directed up the slope, and compare it with the available maximum \(\mu_s mg\cos\alpha\).

For a homogeneous solid cylinder \(I=mR^2/2\), the speed-up is \(2g\sin\alpha/3\) and friction magnitude \(mg\sin\alpha/3\). A ring of the same mass and radius has \(I=mR^2\), half the gravitational downslope acceleration and half the downslope weight component as friction. At \(30^\circ\) the minimum static friction coefficients are respectively \(\tan30^\circ/3=0.192\) and \(\tan30^\circ/2=0.289\). Two objects on the same surface can therefore diverge qualitatively because the ring slips while the cylinder still rolls. Treating an assumed no-slip relation as exact without testing that inequality can produce an unphysical result despite apparently consistent energy conservation.

The friction on a rigid body rolling without slip on a fixed surface has zero power because its instantaneous point of contact is stationary. The force still changes both linear and angular momentum. On a moving conveyor the contact velocity is no longer zero in the laboratory; friction can supply mechanical energy even while the relative contact velocity vanishes. For a wheel steering on a plane the restrictions \(\dot x=R\dot\phi\cos\psi,\ \dot y=R\dot\phi\sin\psi\) depend on heading \(\psi\). In general they cannot be integrated into two configuration equations relating \(x,y,\phi,\psi\) globally. d'Alembert's fixed-time allowed variations still project out ideal rolling reactions, but replacing those velocity constraints by invented holonomic coordinates gives the wrong dynamics. The distinction can be seen geometrically by rolling and steering through a closed sequence of steering operations that leaves a net displacement.

Find loads on two supports of a suspended bar

A rigid horizontal bar of mass \(m\) and length \(2a\) is supported at its ends by two vertical inextensible light cords. At one instant both cords are vertical and the bar is horizontal. An extra downward load of magnitude \(F\) is applied at horizontal coordinate \(s\) relative to the centre. Ask first for a static experiment. The two vertical reaction forces \(T_L,T_R\) obey force balance \(T_L+T_R=mg+F\) and moment balance \(a(T_R-T_L)=sF\). This gives

\[T_R=(mg+F+sF/a)/2,\]
\[T_L=(mg+F-sF/a)/2.\]

The lever arms encode geometry that the vertical balance alone cannot provide.

The same result can be obtained from virtual variations if the reactions are kept as multipliers until the end. For small vertical translation \(y\) of the centre and small tilt \(\theta\), the endpoint vertical displacements are \(\delta y-a\delta\theta\) on the left and \(\delta y+a\delta\theta\) on the right. The load point displacement is \(\delta y+s\delta\theta\). Before eliminating the endpoint constraints, project all applied and reaction forces against arbitrary independent \(\delta y,\delta\theta\); vanishing of both coefficients gives the preceding balances. After imposing both endpoint constraints directly, these two variations vanish and the reduced model for vertical motion contains no equation from which the support loads can be read. Removing a degree of freedom can remove precisely the observable of interest unless reactions are reconstructed.

For \(m=2.0\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.40\,{\rm m}\), \(F=12\,{\rm N}\) and \(s=0.25\,{\rm m}\), the total load is \(31.62\,{\rm N}\) and the load difference \(7.50\,{\rm N}\). Hence \(T_R=19.56\,{\rm N}\), \(T_L=12.06\,{\rm N}\). Exchanging the load from right to left reverses the difference while preserving the sum. This symmetry is a calibrating manoeuvre for two load cells with different offsets: subtract readings from mirrored configurations to isolate the lever-arm response, and compare their sum to the known total weight and applied force.

Now imagine three vertical supports at \(-a,0,+a\). Their three tensions enter only two independent balances. Perfectly rigid constraints determine no unique allocation to the centre support. The ambiguity is physical; the stiffnesses, preloads and small height differences of real supports decide the result. With three identical unstrained linear springs and a centred load \(F\), each spring extends equally if the bar stays level, so each carries one third of the total weight. If the middle spring is twice as stiff and all have the same unloaded height, it carries one half and each outer spring one quarter. Both answers satisfy exactly the same force and moment balances. Reporting a unique value from a singular ideal multiplier system would manufacture an unmeasured constitutive assumption.

Test an acceleration constraint against drift of the position itself

Solving \(G\ddot x+\gamma=0\) guarantees the correct instantaneous normal acceleration at a state lying on the constraint manifold with tangent velocity. It does not magically repair earlier position or velocity errors in approximate time integration. For a particle constrained to a circle, monitor \(g=(r^2-l^2)/2\), \(\dot g=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v\), and the computed force. A small tangential displacement can give tiny instantaneous force error while the radius gradually drifts. Integrating an acceleration-level constraint twice without a position or velocity correction can cause substantial mismatch over many revolutions.

A simple projection illustrates what each correction does. Normalise an approximate position vector to length \(l\), then remove the radial component of velocity by \(\mathbf v\leftarrow\mathbf v-\hat r(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)\). The corrected point and tangent satisfy the constraints, but the velocity correction removes kinetic energy \(m(\hat r\cdot\mathbf v)^2/2\), and the position change can alter potential energy. Projection is therefore a numerical operation whose physical invariant errors must be measured. A structure-preserving constrained integrator builds the position and momentum conditions into the step and often gives better long-time behaviour. Compare convergence of reaction, constraint residual and energy separately instead of treating a rounded radius as proof of a correct force.

A pendulum on a free cart tests the complete mass matrix

Mount a pendulum pivot on a cart of mass \(M\) moving horizontally on a smooth track. The bob has mass \(m\), length \(l\) and downward angle \(\theta\). Choose the cart position \(X\) and use bob coordinates \(X+l\sin\theta,-l\cos\theta\). The kinetic energy is \((M+m)\dot X^2/2+ml\dot X\dot\theta\cos\theta+ml^2\dot\theta^2/2\). Two different off-diagonal mass terms would contradict its origin as a quadratic form; the same factor \(ml\cos\theta\) multiplies the companion acceleration in each equation. This is a useful check on a manually assembled multibody model.

The equations are

\[(M+m)\ddot X+ml(\ddot\theta\cos\theta-\dot\theta^2\sin\theta)=0,\]
\[l\ddot\theta+\ddot X\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0.\]

In the small-angle regime the horizontal centre of mass moves uniformly. Choosing zero total momentum gives

\[\dot X\simeq-ml\dot\theta/(M+m).\]

Substitute its derivative into the angular equation to obtain

\[\ddot\theta+[(M+m)g/(Ml)]\theta=0.\]

Releasing the support raises the oscillation frequency because the effective angular inertia falls while the gravitational stiffness stays the same. The result is a statement about the relative pendulum angle, measured from an inertial vertical, with an unforced horizontally moving support.

For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(m=0.20\,{\rm kg}\) and \(l=0.50\,{\rm m}\), the small-angle period is

\[2\pi\sqrt{Ml/[(M+m)g]}=1.229\,{\rm s}.\]

Fixing the cart with a clamp gives \(1.419\,{\rm s}\) instead. At zero total horizontal momentum the cart displacement from its equilibrium is approximately \(-0.125\,\theta\) metres, so a \(0.10\,{\rm rad}\) angle corresponds to \(12.5\,{\rm mm}\) of recoil. Simultaneous position measurements on cart and bob should reconstruct a horizontal centre of mass that is stationary to within tracking uncertainty. A missing cart inertia or a wrongly filled cross term can alter both period and recoil; checking just one number may not identify which error occurred.

The reaction on the moving bob can again be recovered. Its radial acceleration along the outward string direction is \(\ddot X\sin\theta-l\dot\theta^2\). Thus the tension is \(T=m[g\cos\theta-\ddot X\sin\theta+l\dot\theta^2]\). The acceleration of the support appears explicitly. Reusing the fixed-pivot formula at all angles would omit a term that vanishes accidentally at the bottom. To test the sign, observe a rightward tilted pendulum whose pivot accelerates right: the support's acceleration has an outward projection along the tilted string, reducing the inward tension needed for the same relative motion. Substituting a directly measured cart acceleration provides an independent load-cell prediction.

Decide which force a sensor actually reports

Reaction forces can be reported as tension along a member, normal traction at a contact, total support load on a floor, or a single Cartesian component read by a load cell. A rigid-body idealisation alone does not specify a sensor's orientation or the weight of its housing. For a fixed pendulum, for example, the string carries tension \(T\), while a vertical force channel at its pivot reads the vertical string contribution \(T\cos\theta\). A horizontal channel reads \(T\sin\theta\). The force on the pivot is opposite the force exerted on the bob. Selecting the wrong sign or leaving out the fixture's own weight can create a discrepancy even when the dynamical calculation is correct.

Suppose an ideal load cell with its sensitive axis vertical is tared with the fixture installed but the \(0.40\,{\rm kg}\) bob removed. When the bob is held at \(60^\circ\) by a separate purely tangential actuator and is otherwise stationary, the radial balance gives

\[T=mg\cos60^\circ=1.96\,{\rm N}.\]

The pivot's vertical reading attributable to the string is \(0.981\,{\rm N}\). The tangential actuator supplies the remaining vertical support as well as a horizontal component. Once the actuator releases, instantaneously the same tension and reading hold because radial speed is still zero. At the bottom of the subsequent swing the load jumps to the dynamical value obtained earlier, even though no upper fixture mass has changed.

A student hangs the bob at the same \(60^\circ\) angle using a separate horizontal cord instead of a tangential actuator. Should the pivot cell read the preceding \(0.981\,{\rm N}\)?

Work through the solution

The horizontal cord contributes no vertical support. Static balance now requires \(T\cos60^\circ=mg\), giving \(T=7.85\,{\rm N}\) and vertical pivot contribution \(3.92\,{\rm N}\). The horizontal holding force has a nonzero radial projection and changes the radial-force balance. After the horizontal cord is removed the tension immediately changes to \(mg\cos60^\circ\) because no momentum impulse is required for a finite jump in force. A schematic that shows only the bob angle is insufficient to infer the support load without saying how the angle is maintained.

In the free cart experiment bearing drag is weak but measurable. Can an observed frequency shift distinguish it from a wrongly assumed cart mass?

Work through the solution

A viscous drag changes the horizontal momentum balance and allows the formerly stationary centre of mass to move as momentum leaks to the track. A mass error in a drag-free model changes the relative period and recoil ratio without supplying an actual external impulse. Track the complete centre of mass using independently weighed masses, compare its momentum change with the integrated bearing force, and measure the decay envelope. The frequency alone is a single scalar and can often be fitted by several combinations of missing mass, spring compliance and drag.

Locate contact loss on a smooth convex surface

A small block starts sliding from near rest just to one side of the top of a smooth fixed sphere of radius \(R\). Measure angle \(\theta\) from upward vertical. Energy gives \(v^2=2gR(1-\cos\theta)\) during contact, while the inward radial equation reads \(mg\cos\theta-N=mv^2/R\). Thus \(N=mg(3\cos\theta-2)\). Contact first vanishes at \(\cos\theta=2/3\), or \(48.2^\circ\). For a sphere of radius \(0.50\,{\rm m}\), this occurs after a vertical drop \(R/3=0.167\,{\rm m}\) and at speed \(1.81\,{\rm m/s}\). A block balanced exactly at the top at exact rest is an unstable equilibrium in the ideal model; a small initial displacement or speed initiates the measured trajectory.

Once the block leaves, the same spherical coordinates can still be used to describe position, but \(r=R\) is no longer an equation of motion. Continue with the measured tangential velocity and free gravitational acceleration. Enforcing the sphere condition beyond release would make the computed normal negative and predict a surface that pulls on the block. The zero normal, the geometric release angle and the subsequent ballistic arc provide three independent observables for the same force reconstruction.

Tutorial on measuring reactions

A \(0.40\,{\rm kg}\) pendulum of length \(0.75\,{\rm m}\) is released from rest at \(60^\circ\) from downward vertical. Predict the load-cell reading at the pivot when it passes the bottom. What part does the reduced equation alone omit?

Work through the solution

The height change is

\[l(1-\cos60^\circ)=0.375\,{\rm m}.\]

Energy conservation gives

\[v^2=2g(0.375)=7.36\,{\rm m^2/s^2}.\]

At the bottom the tension is \(mg+mv^2/l=7.85\,{\rm N}\), twice the weight. The tangential acceleration there vanishes because gravity points along the string, but the radial acceleration remains nonzero. A sensor measuring the string force sees this centripetal contribution directly.

A bead lies on a smooth moving plane with upwards acceleration \(a_z\). Which acceleration first makes a passive contact impossible if the bead initially shares the plane's motion?

Work through the solution

Vertical Newton balance requires \(N-mg=ma_z\), or \(N=m(g+a_z)\). At \(a_z=-g\) the reaction vanishes. A more negative plane acceleration would require the plane to pull the bead down, so contact is lost and the bead follows free fall until the separation closes. Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 2. Moving constraints, boundary work and a changing energy

A pendulum with a reeled string can gain energy while its support force does no virtual work. A bead on a spinning guide can accelerate without gravity. Follow the work done by the apparatus, derive the relation between a reduced energy and laboratory energy, and identify which quantity a measurement tests.

Parameterise the motion of a guide as well as the bead

Let a prescribed moving guide define positions \(\mathbf r(q,t)\). Its relative velocity along the guide is \(\mathbf r_q\dot q\) and its imposed local velocity is \(\mathbf r_t\), where \(t\) is differentiated at fixed coordinate. The kinetic energy \(T=m|\mathbf r_q\dot q+\mathbf r_t|^2/2\) includes a quadratic term in \(\dot q\), a mixed term linear in \(\dot q\), and a term independent of \(\dot q\). Dropping the last two merely because the guide motion is prescribed generally changes the bead equation. A term independent of \(\dot q\) may still depend on its position \(q\).

For \(L=T-V\) define \(p=\partial_{\dot q}L\) and \({\cal E}=p\dot q-L\). Differentiating along a solution of the Euler–Lagrange equation gives \(d{\cal E}/dt=-\partial_tL\) in the absence of extra nonconservative generalised forces. This identity involves the partial time derivative at fixed \(q,\dot q\). The laboratory energy is

\[E_{\rm lab}=m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2+V.\]

Direct substitution gives \({\cal E}=E_{\rm lab}-m\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r_t\) for a single particle and ordinary position potential. Thus a time-independent reduced Lagrangian can conserve a quantity different from the laboratory mechanical energy. Which observer supplies a force, and which independent variables are held fixed, determine the energy being tracked.

Work supplied by a steadily rotating straight slot

A smooth horizontal guide turns about its inner end with prescribed angle \(\varphi=\Omega t\). Let \(r\) be the bead's distance from the pivot. The velocity is \(\dot r\hat r+r\Omega\hat\varphi\), and the reduced Lagrangian is \(m(\dot r^2+\Omega^2r^2)/2\). Its Euler–Lagrange equation is

\[\ddot r=\Omega^2r.\]

With \(r(0)=r_0,\dot r(0)=0\) the solution is \(r_0\cosh\Omega t\) until the bead leaves the slot. In the laboratory no force acts along the radial tangent of the slot, yet radial acceleration in polar coordinates is \(\ddot r-r\Omega^2\), precisely zero. The tangential acceleration \(2\Omega\dot r\) is provided by the slot wall.

The wall reaction \(R_\varphi=2m\Omega\dot r\) exerts a torque \(rR_\varphi\) on the bead. The driving motor balances the opposite reaction on the slot and supplies power \(\Omega rR_\varphi=2m\Omega^2r\dot r\). Differentiating \(E_{\rm lab}=m(\dot r^2+\Omega^2r^2)/2\) and using the radial equation gives the same power. The reduced energy \(m(\dot r^2-\Omega^2r^2)/2\) is constant. Its difference from \(E_{\rm lab}\) equals \(-\Omega L_z\), with \(L_z=mr^2\Omega\). This is a consequence of uniform imposed rotation, not a negative physical rotational kinetic energy.

For \(m=0.10\,{\rm kg}\), \(r_0=0.050\,{\rm m}\) and \(\Omega=4.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), consider \(t=0.25\,{\rm s}\). The radial position is \(77.2\,{\rm mm}\), radial speed \(0.235\,{\rm m/s}\), and wall reaction \(0.188\,{\rm N}\). The motor supplies about \(0.0580\,{\rm W}\) at that instant. Laboratory energy has risen from \(0.00200\,{\rm J}\) to approximately \(0.00752\,{\rm J}\). Measuring only the angular motor speed would miss the incoming mechanical work. The torque on the drive shaft or the changing moment of inertia supplies the other observable required for an energy budget.

A pendulum with prescribed length

Let a point pendulum have length \(l(t)>0\) and angle \(\theta\) from downward vertical. Using \(x=l\sin\theta\), \(y=-l\cos\theta\) gives \(T=m(\dot l^2+l^2\dot\theta^2)/2\) and \(V=-mgl\cos\theta\) for a fixed gravitational datum. The angular Euler–Lagrange equation becomes

\[\frac{d}{dt}(ml^2\dot\theta)=-mgl\sin\theta,\]
\[\ddot\theta+2\frac{\dot l}{l}\dot\theta+\frac{g}{l}\sin\theta=0 .\]

The term proportional to \(\dot l\dot\theta\) comes directly from differentiating angular momentum about the pivot. With increasing length the angular speed at fixed angular momentum decreases; reeling in has the opposite effect. It can look like damping or amplification in the angular equation even with no friction. A mechanical energy balance distinguishes the reversible exchange with a reel from energy lost as heat.

The radial Newton equation, taking outward as positive, is \(m(\ddot l-l\dot\theta^2)=mg\cos\theta-T_{\rm string}\). Hence \(T_{\rm string}=m(g\cos\theta+l\dot\theta^2-\ddot l)\). The tension power on the bob is \(-T_{\rm string}\dot l\). This has the correct sign when a heavy nearly vertical bob is lowered slowly: \(\dot l>0\) and the gravitational potential decreases as the string takes energy out. At the bottom of a swing the tension is larger, so a reel shortening the string there does more positive work per unit length than it would at a turning point. Timing a cyclic length change can pump an oscillation while the guide returns to precisely its initial length.

With \(m=0.50\,{\rm kg}\), \(l=1.0\,{\rm m}\), \(\dot\theta=2.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), and the bob at the bottom, a short interval of constant-rate shortening at \(\dot l=-0.030\,{\rm m/s}\) has tension \(6.91\,{\rm N}\) and delivers \(0.207\,{\rm W}\). When the bob is momentarily at rest at \(30^\circ\), the tension for the same length and zero radial acceleration is \(4.25\,{\rm N}\). Reversing the reel rate there would remove only \(0.127\,{\rm W}\). A complete finite cycle requires integrating the time-varying tensions and checking the acceleration during each reel reversal.

Translate a pivot and separate real forcing from a coordinate choice

For a pivot translating horizontally at prescribed \(X(t)\) and constant string length \(l\), write \(x=X+l\sin\theta\), \(y=-l\cos\theta\). The kinetic energy includes \(ml\dot X\dot\theta\cos\theta\). In the angular Euler–Lagrange equation the terms proportional to \(\dot X\dot\theta\sin\theta\) cancel, leaving \(l\ddot\theta=-g\sin\theta-\ddot X\cos\theta\). A constant pivot velocity therefore does not force the relative angle. Replacing the pivot velocity by its magnitude as a driving force would wrongly violate Galilean invariance. A horizontal pivot acceleration does force the angle; it appears as the opposite inertial acceleration in the translating frame.

For \(|\theta|\ll1\) and \(X=X_0\cos\omega t\), the equation becomes

\[\ddot\theta+\omega_0^2\theta=(X_0\omega^2/l)\cos\omega t,\]
\[\omega_0^2=g/l.\]

At drive frequencies well below \(\omega_0\),

\[\theta\simeq-\ddot X/g.\]

The bob nearly aligns with the effective gravity in the moving frame. Far above \(\omega_0\), the particular solution is approximately \(-X/l\); then the bob's absolute horizontal coordinate \(X+l\theta\) changes little even while the pivot moves. Near resonance the undamped steady particular amplitude is inappropriate, and the initial transient or a physical damping model matters.

Calculate work done by a moving wall in an elastic collision

A particle of mass \(m\) moves along a line and collides elastically with a wall whose prescribed laboratory velocity is \(U\) during the short contact. In the instantaneous wall frame the incoming relative velocity \(v^--U\) reverses, so

\[v^+=2U-v^-.\]

In the laboratory the impulse is \(J=m(v^+-v^-)=2m(U-v^-)\) and the kinetic energy change is \(\Delta K=m[(v^+)^2-(v^-)^2]/2=UJ\). The energy gained by the particle equals wall velocity times the impulse delivered by the wall. A perfectly elastic contact still changes laboratory kinetic energy when the wall moves, since elasticity was defined for the relative normal velocity.

Suppose a particle of mass \(0.20\,{\rm kg}\) moves right at \(3.0\,{\rm m/s}\) into a right wall moving left at \(0.50\,{\rm m/s}\). Its final velocity is \(-4.0\,{\rm m/s}\), and the impulse on the particle is \(-1.40\,{\rm N\,s}\). The particle's kinetic energy rises from \(0.90\) to \(1.60\,{\rm J}\), exactly \(UJ=0.70\,{\rm J}\). The actuator keeping the wall on schedule supplies the energy. When the same wall recedes to the right at \(0.50\,{\rm m/s}\), the outgoing velocity is \(-2.0\,{\rm m/s}\) and kinetic energy falls by \(0.50\,{\rm J}\). The difference is not symmetric under reversal of wall speed at fixed incoming particle velocity, because that reversal also changes the relative approach speed.

Now trap a particle between a fixed wall and a length \(l(t)\) varying slowly compared with the collision period. To leading order a right-wall collision changes the particle's speed by \(-2\dot l\), and such collisions occur about once in \(2l/v\). Hence \(dv/dt\simeq-(v/l)\dot l\), so \(vl\) is approximately constant. The one-dimensional action around a full bounce cycle is

\[J_{\rm act}=(2\pi)^{-1}(2mvl)=mvl/\pi.\]

This elementary wall argument explains the adiabatic action without requiring the smooth-force approximation at the instantaneous collision. If the wall speed varies quickly within a round trip, the collision phases matter and the averaged invariant can fail.

Starting with \(l_0=0.40\,{\rm m}\) and speed \(4.0\,{\rm m/s}\), slow compression to \(0.30\,{\rm m}\) predicts \(v\simeq5.33\,{\rm m/s}\). Its kinetic energy increases by a factor \(16/9\), supplied through the moving boundary. A pressure sensor at the wall measures impulse per time, \(2mv\) times the collision frequency \(v/(2l)\), giving average force \(mv^2/l\) at leading order. Multiplying this force by the inward speed gives the power required by the predicted energy law. An instantaneous compression while the particle is elsewhere would instead change no kinetic energy until a later collision. The final length alone does not determine the energy unless the rate protocol is specified.

Follow a string through a moving boundary in configuration and time

For a vertical moving pivot with height \(Y(t)\), coordinates \(x=l\sin\theta\), \(y=Y-l\cos\theta\) give kinetic energy \(m[\dot Y^2+2l\dot Y\dot\theta\sin\theta+l^2\dot\theta^2]/2\). Derive the angular equation by differentiating \(p_\theta=ml^2\dot\theta+ml\dot Y\sin\theta\). The terms containing \(\dot Y\dot\theta\cos\theta\) cancel as in horizontal translation; the remaining equation is

\[l\ddot\theta=-(g+\ddot Y)\sin\theta.\]

A vertical acceleration changes the restoring coefficient multiplicatively, unlike a horizontal displacement whose small-angle effect is an additive forcing term. The distinction is visible even when the two pivot motions have identical amplitudes and frequencies.

For \(Y=A\cos\Omega t\), the effective gravity oscillates as \(g-A\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\). The perfectly vertical solution remains an exact solution at any drive amplitude. A small deviation need not remain small, because its linear equation has a periodic coefficient. An observed growth of swing from a tiny initial disturbance near twice the natural frequency is a parametric effect. A camera viewing the pivot and bob together can separate relative angle from large imposed vertical displacement. Estimating angular amplitude from absolute bob height alone introduces a component at the drive frequency unrelated to the swing.

At modest drive \(A=1.0\,{\rm mm}\), \(\Omega=20\,{\rm rad/s}\), the pivot acceleration amplitude is \(0.40\,{\rm m/s^2}\), about four percent of gravity. A pendulum of length \(0.10\,{\rm m}\) has small-angle frequency \(9.90\,{\rm rad/s}\), putting this drive near twice its natural frequency despite the small displacement. For an exactly stationary vertical bob relative to the pivot, the string tension is \(m(g+\ddot Y)\) as long as that value is positive. A force measurement thus contains a direct drive-frequency signal even before transverse oscillation develops. Combining an angle sensor with a load cell helps distinguish contact loss from growth of parametric oscillation.

A fast vertical drive can also modify the slow stability of the inverted position. A quantitative treatment requires separating a fast small angular ripple from the slow tilt and averaging its feedback, with the support acceleration and frequency satisfying the relevant ordering. The instantaneous sign of \(g+\ddot Y\) alone is insufficient to establish the stability of the inverted solution. An upright motion that briefly samples a favourable sign each cycle may still drift away over many cycles. Compare initial perturbations at several drive phases and fit the envelope over complete periods before drawing a stability conclusion.

Treat the drive itself as a degree of freedom

A prescribed rotating slot conceals the energy source behind a fixed pattern speed. Replace the motor by a free rotating turntable with moment of inertia \(I_0\), and let its angle \(\varphi\) evolve dynamically. In the horizontal frictionless problem the total kinetic energy is \(m\dot r^2/2+(I_0+mr^2)\dot\varphi^2/2\). The angle is cyclic, so the total angular momentum \(P=(I_0+mr^2)\dot\varphi\) is constant. As the bead moves out, the turntable slows. The radial equation remains \(\ddot r=r\dot\varphi^2\), but its coefficient now decreases according to the total angular momentum instead of remaining externally fixed.

Eliminate \(\dot\varphi\) from the total energy to find

\[E=m\dot r^2/2+P^2/[2(I_0+mr^2)].\]

The reduced radial potential decreases outwards and feeds radial kinetic energy, but the total energy available is finite. Differentiating the potential gives a radial acceleration \(P^2r/(I_0+mr^2)^2\), consistent with the original radial equation. One should not simply insert \(\dot\varphi=P/(I_0+mr^2)\) into the original kinetic Lagrangian before varying \(r\); the correct fixed-momentum Routh reduction subtracts \(P\dot\varphi\), reversing the sign of the eliminated rotational term. The energy and Lagrangian methods agree once their controlled quantities are distinguished.

With \(I_0=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(m=0.10\,{\rm kg}\), \(r_0=0.10\,{\rm m}\), initial angular speed \(4.0\,{\rm rad/s}\) and zero radial speed, total momentum is \(0.084\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and energy \(0.168\,{\rm J}\). At \(r=0.30\,{\rm m}\) the rotation rate has fallen to \(0.084/0.029=2.90\,{\rm rad/s}\). The remaining radial kinetic energy is \(0.168-0.084^2/(2\cdot0.029)=0.0463\,{\rm J}\), giving radial speed \(0.962\,{\rm m/s}\). Under a prescribed constant angular speed of four, the radial first integral instead gives \(\dot r=4\sqrt{r^2-r_0^2}=1.13\,{\rm m/s}\) there. The two runs started with identical positions and velocities but have different power connections.

The drive can also be modelled between those limits. Let a servo apply torque \(\tau_{\rm drive}=K_{\rm s}(\Omega_{\rm command}-\dot\varphi)\). Then \(d[(I_0+mr^2)\dot\varphi]/dt=\tau_{\rm drive}\), and the rate of increase of total mechanical energy is \(\tau_{\rm drive}\dot\varphi\). A high control gain with finite response time can approximate the prescribed-speed case over part of a trajectory, yet a rapid radial change can cause a measurable speed sag. Recording shaft torque and speed together tests the drive rather than attributing its lag to a fictitious radial friction on the bead.

Infer drive work from a closed loop of length and measured tension

The reel-pendulum experiment produces synchronised records of string force and length. Over a period in which the length returns to its original value, the work on the bob from the reel is \(-\oint T\,dl\). A graph of tension against length can enclose a signed area although the string force at each instant is perpendicular to the bob's allowed virtual angular displacement. That loop area measures energy transferred by the imposed control. If the bob returns to its full mechanical state and there are no other energy sources, any net positive reel work must go into losses. If only the string length returns, the bob's energy can increase cycle after cycle.

Suppose the reel shortens by \(20\,{\rm mm}\) during a high-tension interval averaging \(7.0\,{\rm N}\), then pays out the same length while the mean tension is \(4.5\,{\rm N}\). The net mechanical work on the bob is \(0.020(7.0-4.5)=0.050\,{\rm J}\) for that cycle. A torque sensor on a reel of changing winding radius must convert torque to tension with the actual instantaneous radius. A force signal delayed relative to the length encoder can create a spurious enclosed area, especially when tension changes rapidly near a turning point. Repeating the measurement slowly with a known hanging calibration weight provides a direct check of polarity and timing.

Derive rapid-pivot stabilisation rather than replacing gravity by its mean

The vertical-pivot equation can be written \(\ddot\theta+[g/l-(A\Omega^2/l)\cos\Omega t]\sin\theta=0\). The time average of the oscillatory coefficient is zero. Nevertheless a high-frequency pivot can alter slow stability because the small fast ripple in \(\theta\) correlates with the fast acceleration. Let \(\theta=\Theta+\xi\), where \(\Theta\) varies little in a drive period and \(\xi\) is small. Keeping the leading rapidly oscillating term gives \(\ddot\xi\simeq(A\Omega^2/l)\sin\Theta\cos\Omega t\), hence \(\xi\simeq-(A/l)\sin\Theta\cos\Omega t\). Its amplitude scales with displacement ratio \(A/l\), so large acceleration and small angular ripple can coexist.

Expand \(\sin(\Theta+\xi)\simeq\sin\Theta+\xi\cos\Theta\), insert the fast solution, and average. Terms linear in the cosine average to zero but the product of ripple and imposed acceleration gives a mean cosine square of one half. To leading order the slow equation is

\[\ddot\Theta+\frac{g}{l}\sin\Theta+\frac{A^2\Omega^2}{2l^2}\sin\Theta\cos\Theta=0.\]

It derives from an effective potential per angular inertia \(U_{\rm eff}/(ml^2)=-(g/l)\cos\Theta+[A^2\Omega^2/(4l^2)]\sin^2\Theta\), up to a constant. Linearising near \(\Theta=\pi+\eta\) gives

\[\ddot\eta+[A^2\Omega^2/(2l^2)-g/l]\eta=0.\]

The inverted state is stable within the averaged approximation if \(A^2\Omega^2>2gl\). The restoring contribution depends on squared pivot speed amplitude. Replacing effective gravity by its simple time average would miss it entirely.

Choose a rigid pendulum arm with \(l=0.20\,{\rm m}\), drive amplitude \(A=0.0040\,{\rm m}\), and \(\Omega=600\,{\rm s^{-1}}\). The small displacement ratio is \(0.020\), the pivot speed amplitude is \(2.4\,{\rm m/s}\), and \(A^2\Omega^2=5.76\,{\rm m^2/s^2}\), above \(2gl=3.92\,{\rm m^2/s^2}\). The predicted slow angular frequency about the inverted state is \(\sqrt{5.76/(2\cdot0.20^2)-9.81/0.20}=4.79\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), far below the drive frequency. A slow ripple of tilt \(0.10\,{\rm rad}\) has a leading drive-frequency micromotion of roughly \(0.0020\,{\rm rad}\). A camera sampling at an unfortunate phase can hide this micromotion or alias it into apparent slow drift.

The rigid arm in this example is essential. An ordinary string cannot push its bob when an averaged upright configuration requires alternating compression and tension throughout a fast cycle. Check the actual instantaneous radial reaction and the physical actuator's ability to deliver the large acceleration. Also check drive-period, arm elasticity and support resonance before identifying an observed stable wobble with the averaged rigid-arm prediction. The leading formula assumes small ripple and a clear separation between \(\Omega\) and all slow angular frequencies; near threshold the slow restoring coefficient is a small difference and higher-order effects or friction become relatively important.

Distinguish two ways of moving an endpoint in Hamilton's principle

When a constraint itself is explicitly time dependent, the identity \(\delta g=\nabla g\cdot\delta x=0\) describes a virtual change at fixed time on the moving surface. A variational problem may instead allow the endpoint time to change. If a terminal point moves along a prescribed spacetime curve \(q_b(t)\), the endpoint variation includes \(dq_b=\dot q_b\,dt\); it is not an arbitrary tangent displacement at fixed laboratory clock. The boundary term in the on-shell action is \(p\,dq-H\,dt\). Reading these two terms separately tells which physical terminal conditions a derivation has assumed.

For a free particle of mass \(m\) moving in one dimension from the origin at time zero to \(q\) at time \(T\), the extremal action is \(S=m q^2/(2T)\). Its derivatives are \(S_q=mq/T=p\) and \(S_T=-mq^2/(2T^2)=-H\). If the terminal marker moves as \(q_b(T)=uT\), the action of the connecting extremal is \(mu^2T/2\). Its derivative along that endpoint line is \(p u-H=mu^2/2\), exactly the direct derivative. Holding the endpoint position fixed while differentiating instead gives \(-mu^2/2\). Both derivative experiments are legitimate, but the different signs demonstrate why one must state what is fixed.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 3. Noether's theorem, boundary terms and measurable constants

A constant of motion can survive when neither laboratory energy nor angular momentum is separately conserved. Construct the conserved quantity from an infinitesimal transformation of the action. Then use boosts, a screw symmetry, and a rotating potential to determine what measured combinations should stay fixed.

Vary a path and its time labels

Let \(q_i(t)\) be a path for \(L(q,\dot q,t)\). Consider a continuous family of transformations \(t'=t+\varepsilon\tau(q,t)\), \(q'_i(t')=q_i(t)+\varepsilon\xi_i(q,t)\), to first order in a constant parameter. The transformation at the corresponding new time has displacement \(\xi_i\); at the original time the path perturbation is \(\eta_i=\xi_i-\dot q_i\tau\). Distinguishing these variations prevents a time translation from being mistaken for a vertical displacement in a configuration-versus-time plot. Differentiating the transformed coordinates gives

\[\delta\dot q_i/\varepsilon=\dot\xi_i-\dot q_i\dot\tau,\]

with dots on the generator functions meaning derivatives along the path.

The transformed integration element is \(dt'=(1+\varepsilon\dot\tau)dt\). A variational symmetry need only preserve the action up to an endpoint term. If its first-order change in the Lagrangian times the time element is \(\varepsilon\,dF/dt\,dt\), the equations of motion with fixed endpoints are unchanged. Substitute the variations to write

\[\sum_i\left(L_{q_i}\xi_i+p_i\dot\xi_i-p_i\dot q_i\dot\tau\right)+L_t\tau+L\dot\tau=\frac{dF}{dt},\]
\[p_i=L_{\dot q_i}.\]

On a physical path \(L_{q_i}=\dot p_i\). Combine the first two terms into \(d(\sum p_i\xi_i)/dt\). Define \(H=\sum p_i\dot q_i-L\); its derivative on a solution is

\[\dot H=-L_t.\]

The terms with \(\tau\) then combine into \(-d(H\tau)/dt\). Thus \(Q=\sum_i p_i\xi_i-H\tau-F\) is conserved. Reversing the sign of the generator reverses the sign of the charge, which has no bearing on conservation. Scaling the group parameter also rescales the charge, so specifying the transformation matters when reporting its units.

Recover the familiar examples and test broken symmetry

For a common infinitesimal translation along fixed unit vector \(\hat n\), every particle has \(\xi_a=\hat n\), \(\tau=0\). A potential depending solely on pair separations is invariant, so \(F=0\). The conserved charge is total momentum along \(\hat n\). For an infinitesimal rotation about the origin,

\[\xi_a=\hat n\times\mathbf r_a;\]

the scalar triple product gives \(\hat n\cdot\sum_a\mathbf r_a\times\mathbf p_a\), the chosen angular momentum component. For time translation \(\tau=1,\xi=0\), an explicitly time-independent Lagrangian gives \(Q=-H\). In each case the symmetry refers to the system including the prescribed external fields. Internal pair forces may conserve momentum while a fixed trap exerting an external force breaks the translation symmetry of the particles.

The algebra also gives an exact diagnostic for an approximate symmetry. If the variation of \(Ldt\) differs from the boundary term by \(\varepsilon{\cal D}\,dt\), the same steps give

\[\dot Q={\cal D}.\]

In a weak potential \(V(x)=V_0+\epsilon U(x)\), translation of \(x\) gives

\[\dot p_x=-\epsilon U'(x).\]

A drifting computed momentum is therefore not automatically a failure of the time integrator. Compare its change with the integrated physical symmetry-breaking force. Reversing the small external bias helps separate the first-order physical drift from a numerical offset that does not reverse.

Derive a boost charge that needs an endpoint term

Consider a free Newtonian particle in one dimension and a small Galilean boost \(x'=x+\varepsilon t\). Here \(\xi=t\), \(\tau=0\), and \(L=m\dot x^2/2\) changes to first order by \(m\dot x\varepsilon=d(mx\varepsilon)/dt\). The Lagrangian is not pointwise invariant, but its change is a total derivative. The charge is \(Q=pt-mx\); its constancy is equivalent to \(x-(p/m)t\) remaining equal to the initial position. Dropping the endpoint term would give \(pt\), whose derivative is generally the nonzero momentum itself.

For mutually interacting particles with translationally invariant internal forces, summing the argument gives

\[Q=P t-MR_{\rm cm}.\]

Constancy of total momentum and boost charge together imply that the centre of mass moves uniformly along a straight line. Take two pucks of masses \(0.20\) and \(0.30\,{\rm kg}\) with \(x_1(0)=0\), \(x_2(0)=1.0\,{\rm m}\), and velocities \(2.0\) and \(-0.5\,{\rm m/s}\). Their total momentum is \(0.25\,{\rm kg\,m/s}\) and centre of mass starts at \(0.60\,{\rm m}\). At \(t=0.40\,{\rm s}\), including any intervening isolated elastic collision, it must be at \(0.80\,{\rm m}\). The boost charge \(0.25t-0.50R_{\rm cm}\) remains \(-0.30\,{\rm kg\,m}\). Internal impact changes the individual momenta but preserves both total quantities.

Rotate and translate together in a helical landscape

Let a particle move in cylindrical coordinates in a potential \(V(r,z-c\varphi)\), with the combination physically interpreted on a helical branch or within a periodic potential compatible with \(2\pi\) rotation. Rotation through a small angle \(\varepsilon\) combined with translation \(z\to z+c\varepsilon\) leaves the potential unchanged. The kinetic energy \(m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\varphi^2+\dot z^2)/2\) also stays unchanged. The generator has \(\xi_\varphi=1,\xi_z=c\), hence \(Q=p_\varphi+c p_z\) is conserved. The constant \(c\) has units of length per radian, so both terms have angular-momentum units.

Differentiate directly as a check. Writing \(u=z-c\varphi\), Hamilton's equations give

\[\dot p_\varphi=+c\,V_u,\]
\[\dot p_z=-V_u.\]

Each can change, yet the combination has zero derivative. A guided particle receiving a burst of axial momentum can exchange a specific amount of angular momentum with the helical potential. For \(c=0.030\,{\rm m}\) and \(\Delta p_z=0.080\,{\rm kg\,m/s}\), the relation predicts \(\Delta p_\varphi=-0.0024\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\). A circularly symmetric potential would conserve angular momentum on its own and represents a different field configuration.

Exchange energy and angular momentum in a rotating pattern

A potential \(V(r,\varphi-\Omega t)\) with constant pattern speed is unchanged when time increases by \(\varepsilon\) and the particle coordinates rotate by \(\Omega\varepsilon\). Take \(\tau=1,\xi_\varphi=\Omega\). The conserved combination is \(H-\Omega p_\varphi\), up to the sign chosen for \(Q\). A torque from the rotating apparatus can change ordinary energy and ordinary angular momentum. Their changes obey \(dH=\Omega\,dp_\varphi\) along the trajectory when the Hamiltonian contains only the prescribed rotating potential as explicit time dependence. The external power equals angular speed times the torque acting through that pattern.

Suppose \(\Omega=12\,{\rm rad/s}\) and a particle gains \(0.015\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) in axial angular momentum during a passage past the rotating potential. Its mechanical energy must gain \(0.180\,{\rm J}\). A numerical run reporting \(0.195\,{\rm J}\) calls for accounting for other time dependence, sampling uncertainty or integration error. If \(\Omega\) itself slowly varies, introduce a pattern angle \(\alpha(t)\), \(\Omega=\dot\alpha\). One still finds \(\dot H=\Omega\dot p_\varphi\), but \(d(H-\Omega p_\varphi)/dt=-\dot\Omega p_\varphi\). Testing an exact rotating-frame invariant with a ramped motor would therefore diagnose the wrong experiment.

Find the conserved momentum in a magnetic field directly from the action

A charge \(q\) of mass \(m\) moves in a uniform static field \(B\hat z\). Choose vector potential \(A_y=Bx\), scalar potential zero. Its Lagrangian in the perpendicular plane is \(L=m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)/2+qBx\dot y\). The position \(y\) is cyclic, so \(p_y=m\dot y+qBx\) is constant. The physical force is also invariant under translations in \(x\), although \(x\) appears in this chosen Lagrangian. Under \(x\to x+\varepsilon\), its change is \(qB\dot y\,\varepsilon=d(qBy\varepsilon)/dt\). The corresponding Noether charge is \(p_x-qBy=m\dot x-qBy\). Thus both spatial translation symmetries survive once the endpoint term is included.

Write \(\omega_c=qB/m\), which retains the sign of the charge. Rearranging the two constants gives the coordinates of the guiding centre \(X_c=x+\dot y/\omega_c,\ Y_c=y-\dot x/\omega_c\). Differentiate using \(m\ddot x=qB\dot y,\ m\ddot y=-qB\dot x\) to see both are independent of time. A uniform field changes velocity direction but does no work. In a force experiment the centre of the measured circular arc, its radius and its angular orientation can be estimated without measuring any vector potential. The endpoint terms explain why two gauge choices can assign different canonical momenta while predicting the same observed guiding centre.

There is a useful algebraic surprise. Denote the translation charges by \(K_x=p_x-qBy\) and \(K_y=p_y\) in the chosen gauge. Their canonical bracket is \(\{K_x,K_y\}=-qB\). Each is conserved, yet the bracket does not vanish. The result is a constant, so Jacobi's identity is satisfied. The mechanical effect of their infinitesimal flows on positions commutes; a central constant in the charge bracket has zero Hamiltonian vector field. Conservation, commutation of the generated coordinate motions, and zero bracket of the generating functions are related but distinct statements. This example supplies a direct check when applying symmetry counting in phase space.

For an illustrative particle with \(q/m=2.0\,{\rm C/kg}\), \(B=0.50\,{\rm T}\), initial \((x,y)=(0.20,0.10)\,{\rm m}\) and velocity \((0.30,-0.40)\,{\rm m/s}\), the signed cyclotron frequency is \(1.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}\). The guiding centre is \((-0.20,-0.20)\,{\rm m}\), and the radius from that centre is \(0.50\,{\rm m}\). The speed is \(0.50\,{\rm m/s}\), consistent with radius \(v/|\omega_c|\). Replacing the field by its negative at the same initial state moves the guiding centre to the opposite side of the initial velocity and reverses the sense of rotation. A constant numerical offset in the vector potential should leave both reconstructed centres unchanged.

Use scaling to decide when a formal transformation creates a charge

Many equations of motion have scaling relations. A map taking one solution into another need not preserve the action up to an endpoint term, and therefore need not supply a Noether charge. To test an example, let \(L=T-V\) in three dimensions with \(V\) homogeneous of degree \(n\), so \(V(\lambda\mathbf r)=\lambda^nV(\mathbf r)\). Take \(\mathbf r\to e^\varepsilon\mathbf r\), \(t\to e^{a\varepsilon}t\). Kinetic energy times \(dt\) scales as \(e^{(2-a)\varepsilon}\), while potential energy times \(dt\) scales as \(e^{(n+a)\varepsilon}\). Equating the exponents gives \(a=(2-n)/2\), a map that rescales the entire action by a common factor and preserves stationary paths. Exact invariance of the action additionally requires the common exponent to vanish, giving \(a=2,n=-2\).

For a potential proportional to \(1/r^2\), the continuous symmetry has \(\xi=\mathbf r,\tau=2t\), \(F=0\), and charge \(D=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p-2tH\). Direct differentiation gives \(d(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p)/dt=2T-\mathbf r\cdot\nabla V=2T+2V=2H\), verifying conservation. At fixed positive energy this particular scalar product grows linearly in time. This does not require every coordinate to grow linearly; it follows from the scaling structure of the combined kinetic and potential energies. Collision singularities may limit the interval on which a classical solution exists.

For a Newtonian potential \(n=-1\), the path scaling gives \(a=3/2\), precisely the scaling of orbital periods with orbital size. The common action exponent is \(1/2\), so this scaling does not produce the preceding conserved \(D\). Instead the virial identity \(d(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p)/dt=2T+V\) implies \(2\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle=0\) on a bounded periodic trajectory after time averaging. This distinction can be checked numerically on one elliptical orbit: the virial combination generally oscillates during a cycle, whereas its integrated full-cycle average vanishes. Mistaking a scaling of solutions for exact invariance of the action would turn that averaged result into a false instantaneous conservation law.

Derive a centre-of-energy statement from a boost charge

For a free relativistic particle in one spatial dimension use \(L=-mc^2\sqrt{1-\dot x^2/c^2}\). An infinitesimal Lorentz boost, with a parameter carrying velocity units and the sign convention \(x'=x+\varepsilon t\), has generators \(\xi=t,\tau=x/c^2\). Proper time is invariant, so \(Ldt\) is invariant and \(F=0\). The Noether charge is \(K=pt-Hx/c^2\), where \(H=\gamma mc^2\), \(p=\gamma m\dot x\). Its derivative vanishes because \(p=H\dot x/c^2\). At low speed \(H/c^2\) approaches \(m\), and the Newtonian boost charge reappears up to relativistic corrections.

For an isolated set of freely propagating particles away from local collisions, sum the charges to obtain

\[K=P t-(E_{\rm tot}/c^2)X_E,\]

where \(X_E=\sum E_i x_i/E_{\rm tot}\) is the centre of energy. If collisions conserve total energy and momentum and occur at the same spacetime point for their participants, the summed charge passes continuously across them. Its constancy implies

\[dX_E/dt=c^2P/E_{\rm tot}.\]

In an interacting field problem energy stored between particles also belongs in the weighted centre; assigning every bit of energy to particle positions can violate the balance. A field-carrying interaction illustrates why the Newtonian centre of mass alone is insufficient in a relativistic energy accounting.

Imagine equal positive rest masses at \(x=0\) and \(x=2.0\,{\rm m}\). At the observation instant their energies are \(2mc^2\) and \(mc^2\), with the more energetic particle moving to the right and the second at rest. Their ordinary mass centre is \(1.0\,{\rm m}\), while their energy centre is \(2/3\,{\rm m}\). The total momentum is \(\sqrt3\,mc\), the total energy \(3mc^2\), so the centre of energy moves at \(c/\sqrt3\). The numerical difference is not a correction to the measured coordinates but a change in the physical quantity whose motion follows a boost symmetry. A centre-of-momentum frame for the pair moves at the same speed.

Diagnose symmetry from noisy finite-time balances

In an experiment one rarely has symbolic access to every component of the Lagrangian. A practical symmetry test measures the candidate charge at two times and independently integrates the physical source expected to change it. For the rotating potential, use \(R=\Delta H-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Omega(t)\,dL_z\). If \(\Omega\) is measured on the same clock as the trajectory, the integral can be estimated by summing the mean \(\Omega\) across each angular-momentum increment. This avoids taking a second time derivative of position merely to reconstruct the torque. An equivalent form obtained by parts is \(\Delta(H-\Omega L_z)+\int L_z\,d\Omega\). Both versions should agree if endpoint times and signs are consistent.

Take a short programmed ramp in which \(\Omega\) increases linearly from \(10\) to \(12\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(L_z\) from \(0.030\) to \(0.050\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) over the same time. If the rotating pattern is the only external work source, the energy change is mean speed times angular-momentum change,

\[11(0.020)=0.220\,{\rm J}.\]

The product \(\Omega L_z\) changes by \(0.600-0.300=0.300\,{\rm J}\), so \(H-\Omega L_z\) falls by \(0.080\,{\rm J}\). The missing amount equals

\[-\int L_z\,d\Omega=-0.040(2)=-0.080\,{\rm J}.\]

These two balances provide a nontrivial sign test when the drive speed is varied deliberately.

When measurements are noisy, an observed discrepancy must be compared with the uncertainties of both charge and physical source. An erroneous angular zero in a central problem can affect the torque reconstruction while leaving a scalar speed unchanged; a simple time shift between the motor encoder and position camera can mimic an extra source of work. Change the drive direction, repeat with constant speed, and estimate the effect of clock offsets using resampled records. These experiments test the specific symmetry under investigation. A plot that appears almost flat on an arbitrary vertical scale supplies neither a physical tolerance nor a discrimination among competing error sources.

Derive a conservation law for an elastic field

Noether's argument also applies when the coordinates form a continuous field \(u(x,t)\). Consider a uniform string or rod with density per length \(\rho_l\), displacement field \(u\) and stiffness coefficient \(C\), so \({\cal L}=\rho_l u_t^2/2-Cu_x^2/2\). A constant shift \(u\to u+\varepsilon\) changes no strain and leaves the density invariant. The Euler–Lagrange equation becomes \(\partial_t(\rho_lu_t)+\partial_x(-Cu_x)=0\), a local conservation law. For an axial rod the density \(\rho_l u_t\) is ordinary longitudinal momentum per unit length, and the boundary term is axial traction with its outward sign. A uniform displacement symmetry survives a free translation of the whole rod but is broken if an end displacement is imposed relative to a clamp.

Integrate from \(x=0\) to \(L\). The total physical momentum changes as \(dP/dt=Cu_x(L)-Cu_x(0)\). A fixed end can exert a nonzero reaction and spoil conservation of the rod's momentum even when it does no power because its actual velocity vanishes. The distinction between a momentum source and an energy source is experimental: a rigid wall reflecting a pulse can accept net impulse while taking no mechanical energy. Including the support in the system restores the corresponding balance. Checking only the field density's invariance in the interior neglects boundary conditions, which are part of a physical symmetry.

The uniformity of material parameters also gives an invariance under translations of the material coordinate \(x\) in an infinite or periodic homogeneous domain. The associated density is proportional to \(-\rho_lu_tu_x\), often called wave pseudomomentum under this convention. Derive its local balance by multiplying \(\rho_lu_{tt}=Cu_{xx}\) by \(-u_x\) and applying the product rule. One obtains

\[\partial_t(-\rho_lu_tu_x)+\partial_x[Cu_x^2/2+\rho_lu_t^2/2]=0.\]

This conserved quantity differs from the ordinary momentum found from shifting \(u\) itself. A small-amplitude sinusoidal travelling wave can have zero mean material velocity over one period yet positive mean transport of this wave quantity.

For \(u=A\cos(kx-\omega t)\),

\[u_t=\omega A\sin(kx-\omega t),\]
\[u_x=-kA\sin(kx-\omega t).\]

The cycle-averaged wave density is \(\rho_l\omega kA^2/2\) while ordinary momentum density averages to zero. With \(\rho_l=0.20\,{\rm kg/m}\), \(A=1.0\,{\rm mm}\), \(k=5.0\,{\rm m^{-1}}\), and \(\omega=100\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), the average wave density is \(5.0\times10^{-5}\,{\rm kg/s}\). Its units are momentum per unit length, but equal dimensions do not make the two conserved charges interchangeable. To identify the charge, specify whether the symmetry shifts every displacement equally or shifts the material-coordinate pattern.

Derive an identity without assuming the equations of motion

The usual on-shell proof substitutes the Euler–Lagrange equations just before a total derivative appears. Retaining the difference gives an equally useful off-shell identity. Define the residual \(R_i=d p_i/dt-\partial L/\partial q_i\). For a true variational symmetry and any differentiable trial path, repeat the calculation with \(\eta_i=\xi_i-\dot q_i\tau\). The result is

\[dQ/dt=\sum_i R_i\eta_i.\]

Thus the drift of a Noether charge evaluated on an approximate path is a particular projection of its dynamical residual. A path can have zero drift of one charge while violating equations in directions to which that charge is insensitive.

For a free particle in two dimensions, perfect conservation of \(p_x\) enforces zero residual acceleration in the \(x\) direction but says nothing about a spurious force in \(y\). Conversely tracking two independent translational charges checks both components. Energy conservation tests a different projection, \(dH/dt=\dot{\mathbf q}\cdot\mathbf R\) for a time-independent Lagrangian. An error force always perpendicular to velocity can leave the speed and energy unchanged while bending an originally straight trajectory. That is one reason an invariant energy graph cannot replace a force residual or a comparison of trajectories.

Separate a discrete symmetry from a continuous one

A potential \(V(q)=a(q^2-b^2)^2\) is invariant under the discrete reflection \(q\mapsto-q\). Reflecting both position and velocity maps any solution into another solution with equal energy. There is no continuously tunable parameter that takes the identity smoothly to this isolated reflection while preserving the same one-dimensional potential at every intermediate step. No infinitesimal translation generator follows from this symmetry, and the momentum generally changes along either path. A pair of measured trajectories launched from mirrored initial data should match after reflection, but neither trajectory is required to have zero or constant momentum.

For \(a=1\) and \(b=1\) in consistent scaled units, release particles from \(q=0.50\) and \(-0.50\) at rest. The force is \(-4q(q^2-1)\), giving initial accelerations \(+1.50/m\) and \(-1.50/m\). The two accelerations are mirror images, not individually zero. A numerical method that accidentally introduces a constant bias force breaks the reflection comparison and shifts the two turning times in opposite senses. Energy can remain nearly conserved with respect to a slightly tilted effective potential, so trajectory pairing is a complementary diagnostic. Reflection symmetry imposes a relationship between solutions even when Noether's continuous-symmetry construction supplies no new conserved scalar.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 4. Kepler orbits, the eccentricity vector and the loss of apsidal closure

An orbit can conserve energy and angular momentum yet gradually rotate its periapsis. The inverse-square problem possesses one more conserved vector that fixes the direction of closest approach. Derive it, turn a single position and velocity measurement into a conic, and measure what a weak perturbation does to the inferred apsidal direction.

Reduce the two-body system without freezing either mass

Two bodies of masses \(m_1,m_2\) have positions \(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2\). Define total mass \(M=m_1+m_2\), centre \(\mathbf R=(m_1\mathbf r_1+m_2\mathbf r_2)/M\), relative coordinate \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2\), and reduced mass \(\mu=m_1m_2/M\). Substitution into the kinetic energy cancels the cross terms, giving \(M\dot{\mathbf R}^2/2+\mu\dot{\mathbf r}^2/2\). Newtonian gravitational potential is \(-\kappa/r\) with \(\kappa=Gm_1m_2\). The centre moves uniformly when isolated, while the relative motion obeys \(\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\kappa\hat r/r^2\). Dividing by the reduced mass gives the acceleration parameter \(k=\kappa/\mu=GM\); replacing it by \(Gm_1\) is the test-particle approximation.

Use specific energy \(\epsilon=v^2/2-k/r\) and specific angular momentum \(\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v\), both per unit reduced mass. A central force makes \(\dot{\mathbf h}=0\), fixing the plane of nonradial motion. The area of the triangle swept in time \(dt\) is \(r^2d\varphi/2=h\,dt/2\), yielding the constant areal rate. The radial energy is \(\dot r^2/2+h^2/(2r^2)-k/r\). Its turning points determine the accessible radii, but energy and \(h\) alone cannot specify where the major axis lies in the chosen inertial plane.

Construct a vector whose derivative vanishes

The derivative of \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf h\) is \((-k\hat r/r^2)\times\mathbf h\). Use \(\mathbf h=r^2\dot\varphi\,\hat z\) with an oriented plane in which motion is positive. Since \(\hat r\times\hat z=-\hat\varphi\), the derivative becomes

\[k\dot\varphi\hat\varphi=k\,d\hat r/dt.\]

Consequently the eccentricity vector

\[\mathbf e=\frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf h}{k}-\hat r\]

is constant. The dimensional Runge–Lenz vector is proportional to this choice: for relative momentum \(\mathbf p=\mu\mathbf v\),

\[\mathbf A=\mathbf p\times\mathbf L-\mu\kappa\hat r=\mu\kappa\mathbf e.\]

Every term in the specific expression is dimensionless. The step equating the derivative to \(k\,d\hat r/dt\) uses exactly the inverse-square radial force. General central forces retain areal velocity but lose this cancellation.

Dot \(\mathbf e\) with \(\hat r\). The triple product \((\mathbf v\times\mathbf h)\cdot\hat r=h^2/r\) gives

\[e\cos(\varphi-\varpi)=h^2/(kr)-1,\]

where \(\varpi\) is the fixed direction of \(\mathbf e\). Thus \(r=p/[1+e\cos(\varphi-\varpi)]\), \(p=h^2/k\), the conic in focus-centred polar coordinates. Squaring \(\mathbf e\) and eliminating \(v^2\) with the energy gives

\[e^2=1+2\epsilon h^2/k^2.\]

Bound ellipses have \(\epsilon<0,e<1\), parabolic escape has \(\epsilon=0,e=1\), and positive energy gives hyperbolas for nonzero angular momentum. Radial trajectories require separate care because their orbital plane and apsidal angle are degenerate.

Recover the orbit from a single state vector

In units chosen so \(k=1\), a measured relative position is \((2,0,0)\) and velocity is \((0.2,0.6,0)\). Compute \(h_z=1.2\), \(\epsilon=(0.04+0.36)/2-1/2=-0.30\), and \(p=1.44\). The cross product \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf h=(0.72,-0.24,0)\) gives \(\mathbf e=(-0.28,-0.24,0)\), with \(e=\sqrt{0.136}\approx0.369\). The direction of periapsis is about \(220.6^\circ\) from the positive \(x\) axis. At the measured point \(\dot r>0\), so the body is travelling from periapsis toward apoapsis. Dropping the radial component of velocity would rotate the eccentricity vector and change the inferred shape.

The semimajor axis is

\[a=-k/(2\epsilon)=1.667.\]

Periapsis and apoapsis radii \(a(1-e)\) and \(a(1+e)\) are approximately \(1.05\) and \(2.28\), consistent with the observed radius of two. The orbital period is \(2\pi\sqrt{a^3/k}=13.52\) time units. Derive this last result by dividing ellipse area \(\pi ab\) by the areal speed \(h/2\). Using \(b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\) and \(h^2=ka(1-e^2)\) cancels eccentricity exactly. This shows why changing the initial radial velocity can change shape and orientation while the period depends only on binding energy.

Determine time along an ellipse and check limiting cases

Introduce the eccentric anomaly \(E\) by \(x=a(\cos E-e)\), \(y=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E\) in axes centred on the focus, with \(x\) pointing toward periapsis. Then \(r=a(1-e\cos E)\). Compute \(x\,dy-y\,dx=ab(1-e\cos E)\,dE\) and equate it to \(h\,dt\). Integrating from periapsis gives

\[n(t-t_p)=E-e\sin E,\]

where \(n=\sqrt{k/a^3}\). This relation turns orbital geometry into an observation clock. The true anomaly is generally different from the linearly advancing mean anomaly because the body sweeps angle faster at small radius.

For \(e=0.6\) and the time \(t-t_p=T/4\), the mean anomaly is \(\pi/2\). Newton iteration for \(F(E)=E-0.6\sin E-\pi/2\) uses denominator \(1-0.6\cos E\), which stays positive. Starting near \(E=2.0\) yields \(E\simeq2.091\) radians and \(r\simeq1.30a\). The orbit has already moved beyond its semimajor radius at a quarter period. At \(e=0\), \(E\) equals the mean and true anomalies up to an arbitrary origin, and \(\mathbf e=0\) has no definable periapsis direction. Estimating a periapsis angle for a nearly circular noisy orbit is therefore poorly conditioned even when the radius and period are well measured.

Perturb the force and measure apsidal precession

For any central acceleration \(f(r)\hat r\), put \(u=1/r\). Since \(\dot\varphi=hu^2\), radial velocity is \(-h\,du/d\varphi\). Substituting into the radial acceleration gives Binet's equation \(u''+u=-f(1/u)/(h^2u^2)\), where primes denote angular derivatives. Choose \(f(r)=-k/r^2-\alpha/r^3\). Then \(u''+(1-\alpha/h^2)u=k/h^2\). If \(0<\alpha<h^2\), a radial oscillation requires angular advance \(2\pi/\sqrt{1-\alpha/h^2}\), greater than one full turn. Successive periapses advance by that value minus \(2\pi\), approximately \(\pi\alpha/h^2\) for a weak perturbation.

With \(\alpha/h^2=0.010\), the exact precession per radial cycle is approximately \(0.03165\,{\rm rad}=1.81^\circ\). Ordinary energy and angular momentum remain constant in this conservative central potential. The Kepler eccentricity vector evaluated from instantaneous position and velocity now changes. Track periapses using zeros of the radial velocity with positive radial acceleration, then compare their inertial angles. A numerical Kepler integrator can precess even for \(\alpha=0\); refine the time step and repeat the unperturbed control before assigning a small precession to the physical perturbation.

Infer an orbit using quantities that remain well conditioned

Suppose positions and velocities are measured with finite precision. A circular orbit has exact eccentricity vector zero, so even tiny perpendicular perturbations can swing the direction of that vector through large angles. Keep its Cartesian components when computing a fit near circularity. For an initially transverse circular state at radius \(r\) and speed \(v_c=\sqrt{k/r}\), perturb the velocity by radial \(\delta v_r\) and tangential \(\delta v_t\). In the local \((\hat r,\hat\varphi)\) basis the eccentricity components are \(rv_t^2/k-1\) and \(-rv_rv_t/k\). At first order they become \(2\delta v_t/v_c\) and \(-\delta v_r/v_c\). This linear response is well behaved even though taking an inverse tangent to quote periapsis longitude can become meaningless.

For a circular speed of \(10.0\,{\rm m/s}\), radial and tangential measurement errors each of \(0.010\,{\rm m/s}\) correspond to component uncertainties about \(0.0010\) and \(0.0020\). A reported eccentricity of \(0.0012\) is not accompanied by a precise preferred direction under those uncertainties. Repeated orbital samples reduce independent noise but do not remove a persistent tangential velocity bias. Measuring the radius and period independently supplies an additional constraint on \(k\) and the orbital energy. A model that regards the primary as fixed also introduces a systematic mass error when the secondary has non-negligible mass.

At the opposite extreme, a near-parabolic orbit can have a well-defined eccentricity direction but a badly conditioned energy estimate. Its energy is the small difference of \(v^2/2\) and \(k/r\). Since \(a=-k/(2\epsilon)\), a small energy uncertainty produces a very large uncertainty in semimajor axis and period. Report the measured state vector, eccentricity vector and energy uncertainty before interpreting an enormous best-fit period. If the energy uncertainty straddles zero, bounded and unbounded conics are both consistent with the measured data. Integrating a single best-fit ellipse for many revolutions does not resolve that ambiguity.

Calculate the velocity impulse to transfer between circular radii

The same two-body invariants reconstruct a transfer between circular trajectories without requiring a time-stepped simulation. Set a lower radius \(r_1\) and an upper radius \(r_2>r_1\). An ellipse tangent to both has periapsis \(r_1\), apoapsis \(r_2\), semimajor axis \(a_t=(r_1+r_2)/2\), and eccentricity \((r_2-r_1)/(r_2+r_1)\). At either apsis its speed follows from energy:

\[v_t(r)=\sqrt{k(2/r-1/a_t)}.\]

A first instantaneous tangential velocity change moves the particle from its lower circular trajectory to the transfer ellipse. A second tangential change at the opposite apsis matches the upper circular speed. These formulas describe impulses idealised as short compared with the orbital period.

Choose normalised \(k=r_1=1\), \(r_2=2\). Initial speed is one, transfer semimajor axis \(1.5\), and periapsis speed \(\sqrt{4/3}=1.1547\). The first change is \(0.1547\). At the upper radius the transfer speed is \(\sqrt{1/3}=0.57735\), while the circular speed there is

\[1/\sqrt2=0.70711.\]

The second change must also be forward, by \(0.12976\). The flight time between the two impulses is \(\pi\sqrt{1.5^3}=5.77\) time units, half the transfer period. Simply increasing the initial speed until the path reaches radius two does not make it circular upon arrival. Matching position and angular momentum at the final boundary requires the second change.

Verify the energy increment from an impulse independently. A change \(\Delta\mathbf v\) at fixed position gives

\[\Delta\epsilon=\mathbf v\cdot\Delta\mathbf v+|\Delta\mathbf v|^2/2.\]

At the lower radius our impulse raises \(\epsilon\) from \(-0.5\) to \(-1/3\), an increase of \(1/6\). Substitution of the rounded \(0.1547\) gives the same value. The same velocity increment generally changes energy more when applied where the initial speed is larger and aligned with the impulse. This is a statement about mechanical work at different initial states, not an exception to conservation between impulses.

A laboratory analogue uses a central potential engineered over a limited radius range. A small finite-duration tangential push changes radius as well as speed during the push. To check the impulse approximation compute the change in angular momentum as \(\int\mathbf r\times\mathbf a_{\rm applied}\,dt\) and energy as \(\int\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a_{\rm applied}\,dt\). Compare these with the instantaneous predictions and shorten the pulse while holding its integrated velocity change fixed. If the return radius still drifts, map the force against radius to distinguish a finite-pulse error from an actual deviation from inverse-square attraction.

Continue the time law across the escape boundary

A hyperbolic Kepler orbit can be described without calling a divergent ellipse period a clock. Define a positive hyperbolic length \(a_h=k/(2\epsilon)\) for \(\epsilon>0\), and let \(e>1\). With periapsis on the positive \(x\) axis use \(x=a_h(e-\cosh H)\),

\[y=a_h\sqrt{e^2-1}\sinh H.\]

Then \(r=a_h(e\cosh H-1)\), and the swept-area relation gives

\[t-t_p=\sqrt{a_h^3/k}(e\sinh H-H).\]

The hyperbolic anomaly \(H\) increases monotonically from negative to positive along the passage. At large positive \(H\) the ratio \(dr/dt\) approaches \(\sqrt{k/a_h}=\sqrt{2\epsilon}\), the asymptotic speed predicted by the conserved energy.

At the parabolic boundary \(e=1,\epsilon=0\), put \(D=\tan(\varphi/2)\) with \(\varphi\) measured from periapsis. If \(q_p\) is the periapsis distance, the semilatus rectum is \(p=2q_p\) and \(r=q_p(1+D^2)\). Since \(dt=r^2d\varphi/h\), \(d\varphi=2dD/(1+D^2)\) and \(h=\sqrt{2kq_p}\), integration gives Barker's relation \(t-t_p=\sqrt{2q_p^3/k}(D+D^3/3)\). This expression is finite on either side of the periapsis for finite radius, even as the time to reach infinity diverges. A parabolic passage therefore has an entirely useful local time-of-flight law despite having no period.

For \(k=1\), \(q_p=1\) in scaled units, a parabolic trajectory reaches \(\varphi=90^\circ\) at \(D=1,\ r=2\). Its elapsed time is

\[4\sqrt2/3=1.886.\]

Using the instantaneous angular speed at periapsis for the entire quarter turn would predict \((\pi/2)/\sqrt2=1.111\), much too short. The areal speed stays constant, but angular speed decreases by a factor of four by the time radius has doubled. Integrating \(r^2d\varphi/h\) along the conic captures that slowdown directly.

Near zero energy, formulas involving a semimajor axis of enormous magnitude can lose numerical accuracy through cancellation. For example \(r=a(1-e\cos E)\) involves subtracting two near-equal terms near periapsis in a high-eccentricity ellipse. Expressions in \(q_p=a(1-e)\) and half-angle quantities, such as \(r=q_p+2ae\sin^2(E/2)\), avoid one damaging subtraction. A state-based numerical propagation should preserve angular momentum, compare the periapsis passage time with a quadrature, and test both small positive and small negative energies. Agreement only in the well-bound ellipse regime is a weak test of an algorithm used for near-escape trajectories.

Relate the Runge–Lenz direction to a velocity-space circle

The spatial Kepler conic has a simpler partner in velocity space. In the orbital plane,

\[d\mathbf v/d\varphi=\mathbf a/\dot\varphi=(-k\hat r/r^2)/(h/r^2)=-(k/h)\hat r.\]

Since \(d\hat\varphi/d\varphi=-\hat r\), integration gives

\[\mathbf v=(k/h)\hat\varphi+\mathbf v_c,\]

where \(\mathbf v_c\) is a constant vector. Thus the velocity vector traces a circle of radius \(k/h\), or part of that circle on an unbound orbit. At periapsis a prograde orbit has speed \((k/h)(1+e)\), so the circle centre is shifted in the prograde tangential direction by \(ke/h\). The offset direction is perpendicular to the eccentricity vector.

One can recover \(k/h\) and \(e\) from a set of calibrated velocity samples by fitting a circle in the orbital plane and comparing its radius with the offset of its centre from zero velocity. This method still needs the correct inertial frame: adding a constant observer velocity translates every velocity-space point, moving the fitted centre while preserving the circle radius. A time sequence of relative velocities between the two masses removes a common boost. If the force departs from inverse square, the derivative \(d\mathbf v/d\varphi\) acquires radius dependence and the velocity circle generally distorts. Simultaneously checking the spatial conic and the velocity circle makes a more discriminating test than plotting either shape alone.

Derive which central orbits remain nearly closed

A circular orbit at radius \(r_c\) in a smooth potential per mass \(U(r)\) has \(h^2=r_c^3U'(r_c)\). Its angular frequency obeys \(\Omega^2=U'(r_c)/r_c\). Small radial deviations at fixed \(h\) see the effective potential \(U+h^2/(2r^2)\), so their squared epicyclic frequency is

\[\kappa_r^2=U''(r_c)+3U'(r_c)/r_c.\]

During one radial oscillation the orbital angle advances roughly \(2\pi\Omega/\kappa_r\). If the two frequencies differ, the apsidal orientation generally rotates, even though energy and angular momentum remain constants. This local result distinguishes near-circular closure from the much stronger claim that all bounded orbits close for a given force law.

For an attractive radial acceleration \(-k r^{-s}\), \(U'=k r^{-s}\) and \(\kappa_r^2=(3-s)\Omega^2\). A stable circular orbit requires \(s<3\). For inverse square, \(s=2\), radial and angular frequencies agree, so the apsis returns to its starting orientation each revolution. For an isotropic harmonic potential the force is proportional to \(r\), so \(s=-1\) and \(\kappa_r=2\Omega\). There are two radial minima per full turn; its closed ellipse is centred on the force centre rather than focused there. A rational near-circular ratio at one energy or radius does not by itself prove global closure for all eccentricities. The Runge–Lenz derivation supplied much stronger information for the inverse-square case.

Suppose a weak field calibration drift changes the local logarithmic force slope from \(s=2\) to \(s=1.98\), approximately constant over a narrow radial range. The near-circular radial frequency is \(1.00995\Omega\). Consecutive closest approaches are separated by \(2\pi/\sqrt{1.02}\approx6.221\,{\rm rad}\), giving a retrograde change about \(0.062\,{\rm rad}\) per radial period. The sign is opposite the advance induced by the additional attractive inverse-cube contribution considered before. Estimating the radial oscillation frequency and orbital frequency independently can therefore diagnose deviations that an energy test would miss. At larger eccentricity the sampled force covers a broader radial interval and the local-power approximation must be replaced by the full turning-point integral.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 5. Scattering from a central potential and what angular data can recover

A detector records angles, whereas the force law refers to separations that were never directly observed. Derive the forward map from incoming impact parameter to angle, distinguish the several trajectories a detector may count in one angular bin, and invert sufficiently complete signed deflection data under stated hypotheses.

Build the deflection integral from a radial turning point

Use relative mass \(\mu\), asymptotic relative speed \(v_\infty\), incoming energy \(E=\mu v_\infty^2/2>0\) and impact parameter \(b\ge0\). With a central interaction that tends to zero at infinity, angular momentum is \(\ell=\mu v_\infty b\). For a single repulsive encounter let \(r_0\) be the closest approach. Energy gives

\[\dot r^2=v_\infty^2[1-U(r)/E-b^2/r^2];\]

\(r_0\) is the outer turning root reached from infinity. Dividing \(\dot\varphi=\ell/(\mu r^2)\) by the radial speed and integrating over the outgoing half trajectory gives

\[\Phi(b)=\int_{r_0}^\infty \frac{b\,dr}{r^2\sqrt{1-U(r)/E-b^2/r^2}},\]
\[\chi(b)=\pi-2\Phi(b).\]

The straight, free trajectory sweeps \(\Phi=\pi/2\) between closest approach and infinity and gives zero deflection. For repulsive bend away from the centre this sign convention gives positive \(\chi\). A weak attraction can give negative signed deflection even though a detector reporting the angle between incoming and outgoing velocities may supply only its magnitude. If the path winds by more than a complete turn, angular data modulo \(2\pi\) also lose winding information. The square-root singularity in the integral for an ordinary turning point is integrable. A substitution \(r=r_0+s^2\) makes the near-endpoint quadrature regular. A double turning point requires separate analysis because it can make the angular advance diverge.

Incoming trajectories with parameters between \(b\) and \(b+db\) represent annular area \(2\pi b\,db\) for an axially symmetric beam. At a detected polar angle \(\theta\) the solid angle of a ring is \(2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\). Conservation of incoming counts gives

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\sum_j\frac{b_j}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db_j}{d\theta}\right|.\]

The sum includes all incoming impact parameters reaching that angular bin. Correcting measured counts requires incident flux, target number, angular acceptance and efficiency. The formula is a density in solid angle. Its apparent divergence at an isolated angle need not imply an infinite number in a finite acceptance bin. Integrate the density against the actual bin solid angle or use the preimage areas of the bin directly.

Solve a hard sphere and an inverse-square repulsion

A hard sphere of effective exclusion radius \(a\) specularly reverses the incoming normal velocity at the impact surface. The angle between incoming and outgoing directions is \(\theta=2\arccos(b/a)\) for \(b<a\). Therefore \(b=a\cos(\theta/2)\),

\[|db/d\theta|=(a/2)\sin(\theta/2).\]

The factors cancel to give \(d\sigma/d\Omega=a^2/4\), independent of polar angle, and total cross section \(\pi a^2\). Equal polar angular bins do not give equal counts: their solid angles differ by \(\sin\theta\). This geometric distinction often accounts for a measured histogram that does not look uniform even for a constant differential cross section.

For \(U(r)=K/r\) with \(K>0\), the orbit is a repulsive hyperbola. The analytic relation is

\[\tan(\theta/2)=K/(2Eb).\]

It can be derived from the energy and the angle through the deflection integral: put \(u=b/r\) so that \(\Phi=\int_0^{u_0}du/\sqrt{1-(K/Eb)u-u^2}\). Complete the square and evaluate the arcsine at the root to get

\[\Phi=\pi/2-\arctan[K/(2Eb)].\]

Define \(d=K/(2E)\); then \(b=d\cot(\theta/2)\),

\[d\sigma/d\Omega=d^2/[4\sin^4(\theta/2)].\]

High precision near the forward direction is physically limited by finite beam size, detector acceptance and any screening of the long-range interaction.

For \(d=2.0\,{\rm mm}\) in an abstract mechanical analogue, a deflection of \(60^\circ\) corresponds to \(b=3.46\,{\rm mm}\), and the differential cross section is \(16\,{\rm mm^2/sr}\). The cross section for deflections greater than \(60^\circ\) is the area \(\pi b^2=37.7\,{\rm mm^2}\). The differential value and integrated area answer different laboratory questions. Doubling energy at fixed interaction halves \(d\), quarters the cross section into a fixed angular interval and reduces the deflection at a fixed incoming \(b\). Measuring only a total count without specifying angular threshold is inadequate for a long-range interaction.

Recover impact parameter from angular counting under monotonicity

Suppose the deflection decreases continuously from \(\pi\) at \(b=0\) to zero at large \(b\). Integrating counts for all angles at least \(\theta\) then gives precisely the incident disk \(\pi b(\theta)^2\). The recovered map satisfies

\[b(\theta)^2=2\int_\theta^\pi(d\sigma/d\Omega)(\theta')\sin\theta'\,d\theta'.\]

Applying this formula to constant hard-sphere intensity gives \(b^2=a^2(1+\cos\theta)/2\), the geometrical result. It also exposes the information needed from a detector: a finite upper cutoff can leave the reconstruction with an unknown disk-area offset.

Failing monotonicity, the same cumulative counts give the total area of all incoming regions producing deflections above threshold, which can be a collection of annuli. No unique \(b(\theta)\) follows from that one number. Near a smooth extremum \(\theta(b)=\theta_r+c(b-b_r)^2/2+\cdots\), two branches contribute inverse square-root factors proportional to \(1/\sqrt{|\theta-\theta_r|}\). A finite bin contains finite counts because integrating that singularity gives a square root. Tagging the incoming impact parameter in a controlled beam or measuring trajectory time delay can separate branches invisible in a final-angle histogram.

Invert a signed deflection function at one energy

Assume smooth \(U(r)\) with \(\rho(r)=r\sqrt{1-U(r)/E}\) strictly increasing on the radial interval sampled by the trajectories. The turning condition is

\[\rho(r_0)=b.\]

Write \(W(\rho)=\ln[r(\rho)/\rho]\) and transform the deflection integral to \(\Phi=b\int_b^\infty[d\ln r/d\rho]\,d\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2-b^2}\). The term from \(d\ln\rho/d\rho\) gives the free angle \(\pi/2\); the remainder has \(\chi=-2b\int_b^\infty W'(\rho)\,d\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2-b^2}\). This is an Abel-type map. Multiply by \(1/\sqrt{b^2-s^2}\) and integrate from \(b=s\) to infinity. Interchanging the integrals produces the inner integral

\[\int_s^\rho\frac{b\,db}{\sqrt{b^2-s^2}\sqrt{\rho^2-b^2}}=\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

Substitute \(b^2=s^2+(\rho^2-s^2)\sin^2\alpha\) to check the identity. With \(W(\infty)=0\) the inversion is \(W(s)=\pi^{-1}\int_s^\infty\chi(b)\,db/\sqrt{b^2-s^2}\). Recover \(r=s e^{W(s)}\) and finally \(U(r)=E[1-(s/r)^2]\) parametrically. This derivation explains the restrictions. A trajectory only samples radii outside its turning point, the signed angle must retain branch information, and the map to \(\rho\) must be single valued. Unknown large-\(b\) tails influence the integral even when only comparatively small radii are of interest.

Derive the weak-deflection formula from a straight incoming trajectory

When the deflection is small one can calculate the transverse impulse along the undeflected line before evaluating a nonlinear radial integral. Put the incoming velocity along \(x\) and the impact point at \(y=b>0\), so the unperturbed trajectory is \(x=v_\infty t\), \(r=\sqrt{x^2+b^2}\). The transverse force is \(-U'(r)b/r\). Dividing its time integral by the incoming momentum \(\mu v_\infty\) gives the signed leading deflection

\[\chi(b)\simeq-\frac{b}{E}\int_b^\infty\frac{U'(r)\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-b^2}} .\]

The factor follows from replacing \(dt\) by \(dx/v_\infty\), using symmetry between positive and negative \(x\), and the identity \(dx=r\,dr/\sqrt{r^2-b^2}\). A decreasing positive potential gives a positive angle, consistent with repulsive scattering in the chapter's convention. The approximation neglects longitudinal momentum change at leading order; a finite speed change far from the interaction would also be inconsistent with elastic energy conservation. Near a turning barrier or for a large angle the original deflection integral must replace this straight path.

An analytically tractable finite-range model is \(U(r)=U_0\exp[-r^2/(2a^2)]\). Along the incoming straight line the transverse force is \((U_0 b/a^2)\exp[-(x^2+b^2)/(2a^2)]\). The Gaussian \(x\) integral gives

\[\chi(b)\simeq\sqrt{\pi/2}(U_0/E)(b/a)\exp[-b^2/(2a^2)].\]

A central hit has zero transverse force by symmetry if the particle crosses through the weak smooth potential rather than being reflected. Distant particles see little interaction, so the deflection must peak at an intermediate impact parameter. Differentiation places the maximum at \(b=a\), with height \(\sqrt{\pi/(2e)}U_0/E\).

For \(U_0/E=0.10\) the peak is about \(0.0760\,{\rm rad}=4.36^\circ\), comfortably small for this leading calculation. Two impact-parameter families can contribute to one smaller nonzero angle. For example \(b=0.50a\) gives \(0.0553\,{\rm rad}\); a trajectory with \(b\) between \(1.5a\) and \(1.7a\) produces a similar deflection on the declining branch. A detector counting more events near four degrees may be recording the merging of these branches, rather than a particularly large local force at the turning separation. Reconstructing \(b(\theta)\) from monotonically accumulated disk areas would be invalid for this smooth repulsive bump even though every encounter is elastic.

Measure the energy dependence to distinguish the weak limit. For fixed \(b/a\) the leading angle scales as \(1/E\). Doubling energy halves the predicted peak angle. A finite spread of incident energies therefore broadens the observed rainbow before detector angular resolution is considered. A position-tagged incident beam can furnish \(\chi(b)\) point by point; a detector measuring final angles alone mixes weakly and more strongly penetrating paths. Adding incoming-position information changes what inverse problem is identifiable, without improving the detector's angular precision.

Limit a scattering inference using finite range and measurement bandwidth

The Abel reconstruction requires data to arbitrarily large \(b\). A finite experiment measures up to \(b_{\max}\), and the unmeasured tail contributes an integral to \(W(s)\). For the example of a known asymptotic \(\chi=C/b\), the tail from \(b_{\max}\) to infinity evaluates to \(C\,\arcsin(s/b_{\max})/(\pi s)\) for \(s<b_{\max}\). Substitute \(b=s/\cos\alpha\) to obtain this result. For \(s\ll b_{\max}\), it approaches \(C/(\pi b_{\max})\). Setting the tail to zero therefore introduces an offset across the reconstructed log-radius even at small \(s\). Bounded angular noise and uncertainty in the tail model should be propagated separately.

As a numerical illustration take \(C=5.0\,{\rm mm}\),

\[b_{\max}=100\,{\rm mm},\]
\[s=20\,{\rm mm}.\]

The missing contribution is about \(0.0160\), corresponding to roughly a \(1.6\%\) multiplicative error in reconstructed radius if other terms were exact. At that same \(s\), the leading whole weak-tail value \(C/(2s)=0.125\) would be biased appreciably by this omission. A model with a short exponential tail would give a quite different correction. Adding a physically motivated tail with an uncertainty range is more informative than extending a noisy measured curve by an unrestricted polynomial.

The lower integration endpoint has a square-root weight. Uniform samples in \(b\) can be wasteful near a selected reconstruction radius and coarse interpolation there can dominate the result. Substitute \(b=s\cosh u\) to obtain

\[db/\sqrt{b^2-s^2}=du.\]

The Abel integral becomes a straightforward integral of \(\chi(s\cosh u)\) over \(u\), with a finite upper limit if the data stop at \(b_{\max}\). This substitution removes a numerical singularity but adds no information about missing impact parameters. Validate a reconstruction by inserting its inferred potential back into the original forward integral and predicting angular measurements withheld from the fit. Agreement with the very data used in the inversion alone can conceal an incorrect tail or a misassigned branch.

Convert centre-of-momentum angles into laboratory angles

An ordinary collision experiment often launches a particle of mass \(m_1\) into a stationary particle of mass \(m_2\). In Newtonian elastic scattering the centre-of-mass speed is \(V=m_1v_{\rm in}/(m_1+m_2)\), while the projectile's speed in that frame is \(u_1=m_2v_{\rm in}/(m_1+m_2)\). Rotate that velocity by the centre-of-mass angle \(\chi\) in the scattering plane, then add the centre velocity back. The outgoing laboratory velocity components are \(V+u_1\cos\chi\) longitudinally and \(u_1\sin\chi\) transversely. Consequently \(\theta_{\rm lab}=\operatorname{atan2}(\sin\chi,m_1/m_2+\cos\chi)\). The centre-of-mass solid-angle density derived from the impact parameter does not transfer unchanged to a lab angular histogram.

If \(m_1=m_2\), the identity \(1+\cos\chi=2\cos^2(\chi/2)\) gives \(\theta_{\rm lab}=\chi/2\) except when the projectile stops and loses a well-defined direction. A heavy projectile can be restricted to a narrow forward cone. Let \(r=m_1/m_2>1\). Differentiating the laboratory angle gives

\[d\theta_{\rm lab}/d\chi=(1+r\cos\chi)/(1+2r\cos\chi+r^2).\]

It vanishes at \(\cos\chi=-1/r\). At that angle \(\sin\theta_{\max}=1/r\); hence \(r=2\) limits the projectile to \(\theta_{\max}=30^\circ\). Two centre-of-mass angles can generate the same nonzero laboratory angle below this maximum. This laboratory-frame fold may appear even for a monotone centre-of-mass repulsive potential.

Energy helps separate the branches. The outgoing laboratory projectile energy divided by its incident energy is \([m_1^2+m_2^2+2m_1m_2\cos\chi]/(m_1+m_2)^2\). For \(m_1=2m_2\), head-on reversal in the momentum frame gives energy fraction \(1/9\), while forward scattering gives one. Both can land near the laboratory beam direction. A detector reporting angle only cannot distinguish a barely deflected fast projectile from a strongly deflected slow one emerging along almost the same line. Recording time of flight to a downstream detector or measuring the recoil supplies the missing information. Instrumental and kinematic multiplicity should therefore be addressed before attributing a two-branch angular signal to an exotic force law.

Ask what a flight-time measurement adds to angle data

At a given impact parameter choose a large comparison sphere of radius \(R\) enclosing a short-range potential. The transit time from its incoming boundary to the outgoing boundary is \(t_U(R,b)=2v_\infty^{-1}\int_{r_0}^R dr/\sqrt{1-U/E-b^2/r^2}\). The free reference at the same \(b\) is \(t_0=2\sqrt{R^2-b^2}/v_\infty\). Their difference is a classical time delay under a precisely specified pair of surfaces. It combines a different path length with the altered speed along the path. A repulsive barrier can cause either a shorter geometrical penetration or longer dwell near a turning point, so the sign cannot be inferred merely from the word repulsive.

For an impenetrable sphere of radius \(a>b\), the particle moves at free speed on each straight segment. The radial turning point is replaced by specular contact at \(a\), so \(t_{\rm hard}=2[\sqrt{R^2-b^2}-\sqrt{a^2-b^2}]/v_\infty\). The delay is \(-2\sqrt{a^2-b^2}/v_\infty\). A reported time delay then needs its reference geometry and tail model, just as a measured forward angle needs its angular acceptance. Reconstruct a finite-depth potential well from two deflections

A circularly symmetric square well provides a forward problem in which the trajectory is straight in each region and changes direction at the potential boundary. \[v_\infty\sin i=v_{\rm in}\sin r_i,\]

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 6. Euler–Poinsot motion, torque-free nutation and the intermediate axis

A freely rotating body has fixed angular momentum in space, yet the components recorded by three body-mounted gyroscopes can vary periodically or linger near an unstable rotation. Solve the nonsymmetric top from its invariants, distinguish its body-component period from its spatial orientation, and extract a stability time scale from a measured inertia tensor.

Derive the body equations from a spatial conservation law

Choose principal axes fixed in a rigid body and label its moments \(I_1<I_2<I_3\). In the co-rotating basis the angular-momentum components are

\[L_i=I_i\omega_i.\]

For any vector expressed in that basis, its inertial derivative is its component derivative plus \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L\). With no external torque the inertial derivative is zero. Taking components gives

\[I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\]
\[I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\]
\[I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2 .\]

Multiply these equations by the corresponding \(\omega_i\) and sum: the coefficients of \(\omega_1\omega_2\omega_3\) cancel, giving constant \(E=\sum I_i\omega_i^2/2\). Multiplying instead by \(I_i\omega_i\) gives constant \(L^2=\sum I_i^2\omega_i^2\). In body angular-momentum space the motion lies on the intersection of a sphere and an energy ellipsoid. At fixed \(L\), the possible energies range from \(L^2/(2I_3)\) to \(L^2/(2I_1)\). The intermediate value \(L^2/(2I_2)\) divides two families of closed curves and contains the unstable principal-axis orientations.

In Poinsot's construction the inertia ellipsoid in angular-velocity space rolls on an invariable plane normal to the fixed spatial angular momentum. The ellipsoid corresponding to a given energy is

\[\sum I_i\omega_i^2=2E.\]

Its normal at the instantaneous angular velocity is proportional to \(\mathbf L\); the tangent plane obeys \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf x=2E\). In space that plane therefore has fixed distance \(2E/L\) from the origin. Its instantaneous point in the body along \(\boldsymbol\omega\) has zero instantaneous rotational velocity because the vector cross product of angular velocity with itself is zero. These facts underlie the rolling construction. A photographed body axis traces a different curve from the moving contact point or from a body-frame gyro trace.

Test all three principal rotations locally

For rotation \((0,0,\Omega)\), linearise the first two Euler equations in transverse perturbations. Eliminating one component gives

\[\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega^2[(I_3-I_2)(I_3-I_1)/(I_1I_2)]\omega_1.\]

Small transverse deviations oscillate. Performing the analogous process about \(I_1\) also gives a restoring sign. About \((0,\Omega,0)\) the sign reverses and the growing exponent is \(\lambda=|\Omega|\sqrt{(I_2-I_1)(I_3-I_2)/(I_1I_3)}\). The unstable rotation is still an exact solution when both perturbations vanish identically. Instability describes the evolution of arbitrarily small disturbances, not a spontaneous violation of the torque-free equations.

For moments proportional to \(1,2,3\) and intermediate-axis rotation \(\Omega=6.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(\lambda=3.46\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and the linear e-folding time is \(0.289\,{\rm s}\). A perturbation with a growing-mode component \(10^{-3}\) of the spin reaches roughly \(10^{-1}\) in \(\ln(100)/\lambda=1.33\,{\rm s}\). Its nonlinear fate remains bounded by the momentum sphere and energy ellipsoid. An exponential growth fit carried past the first large reorientation would therefore misrepresent the global motion. Initial perturbations projected entirely on the decaying linear eigenvector can delay the first visible flip.

Solve the low-energy family with elliptic functions

For \(L^2/(2I_3)<E<L^2/(2I_2)\) the trajectory surrounds the largest-inertia axis. Start at a crossing with \(\omega_2=0,\omega_1=A>0,\omega_3=C>0\). Solving the two invariants gives

\[A^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_1(I_3-I_1)],\]
\[C^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_3(I_3-I_1)].\]

When \(\omega_1\) reaches zero the other transverse component has maximum \(B^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_2(I_3-I_2)]\). Use \(s=\omega_2/B\). Eliminating the other squares with the invariants gives

\[\omega_1^2=A^2(1-s^2),\]
\[\omega_3^2=C^2(1-k_e^2s^2).\]

Euler's second equation now reduces to

\[\dot s^2=\nu^2(1-s^2)(1-k_e^2s^2),\]
\[\nu^2=\frac{(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)}{I_1I_2I_3},\]
\[k_e^2=\frac{(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)}{(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)} .\]

Separating variables defines \(u=\nu t=\int_0^s dw/\sqrt{(1-w^2)(1-k_e^2w^2)}\). Its inverse is Jacobi's \({\rm sn}(u,k_e)\). The continuously signed square roots are \({\rm cn}\) and \({\rm dn}\), giving \((\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3)=(A\,{\rm cn},B\,{\rm sn},C\,{\rm dn})\). This construction defines the special functions from the mechanical quadrature instead of assuming them as a table of answers. At \(k_e=0\) the inverse is a sine and the stabilising motion is harmonic. As \(k_e\to1\), the integral to \(s=1\) diverges logarithmically and the period grows without bound.

The body-frame period is

\[T_b=4K(k_e)/\nu,\]

with \(K\) the same integral evaluated at one. Some software defines elliptic functions in terms of parameter \(m=k_e^2\) rather than modulus \(k_e\). Supplying one to an implementation expecting the other produces a plausible curve with the wrong period. Check the derivative at the zero crossing \(\dot\omega_2(0)=B\nu\) and the time of its first maximum \(K/\nu\) before fitting a long trace. The third component \({\rm dn}\) repeats after half the full period, so a single principal-axis gyro can report a dominant frequency twice that of the full body vector.

Reconstruct the observed nutation angle

Orient the inertial vertical along \(\mathbf L\). The angle between body axis three and this fixed direction obeys \(\cos\theta=L_3/L=I_3\omega_3/L\). For \(I_1,I_2,I_3=(1,2,3)\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) and initial \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0.50,0,1.00)\,{\rm rad/s}\), the invariants give \(L^2=9.25\), \(2E=3.25\) in SI, \(A=B=0.50\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(C=1.00\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(\nu=1.00\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), and \(k_e^2=1/12\). Thus the nutation angle changes from \(\arccos(3/\sqrt{9.25})\approx9.46^\circ\) to \(\arccos[3\sqrt{11/12}/\sqrt{9.25}]\approx19.2^\circ\). A torque is unnecessary for this change in the angle of a body axis relative to fixed \(\mathbf L\).

The principal-axis components alone determine body orientation only when supplemented with an initial attitude and the kinematic equation. For the Euler-angle convention with polar axis \(\mathbf L\), the azimuthal rate of body axis three is \(\dot\phi=L(L_1^2/I_1+L_2^2/I_2)/(L_1^2+L_2^2)\) away from the polar singularity. It follows by differentiating the body-axis unit vector in space and projecting onto its azimuthal direction. A complete period of the body components therefore leaves a finite spatial rotation about fixed \(\mathbf L\). Return of the three gyro readings does not imply that a painted mark has returned to its starting position in the laboratory.

Solve the high-energy family and join both sides at the separatrix

For \(L^2/(2I_2)<E<L^2/(2I_1)\) the closed curves on the angular-momentum sphere surround axis one. The component \(\omega_1\) now keeps its sign while \(\omega_3\) changes sign through a cycle. As in the first family, choose a phase with \(\omega_2=0\) and both other components positive. The two invariants give the same initial \(A^2=(2EI_3-L^2)/[I_1(I_3-I_1)]\) and \(C^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_3(I_3-I_1)]\). Now the maximum of \(\omega_2\) occurs when \(\omega_3=0\), so

\[B^2=(L^2-2EI_1)/[I_2(I_2-I_1)].\]

Which component reaches zero is the physical branch choice that determines the arrangement of elliptic functions.

Let \(s=\omega_2/B\). The invariant equations give

\[\omega_3^2=C^2(1-s^2),\]
\[\omega_1^2=A^2(1-k_h^2s^2).\]

Substitution in the second Euler equation yields the same elliptic quadrature but with \(\nu_h^2=(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)/(I_1I_2I_3)\) and \(k_h^2=(I_3-I_2)(L^2-2EI_1)/[(I_2-I_1)(2EI_3-L^2)]\). All components are given by \((A\,{\rm dn},B\,{\rm sn},C\,{\rm cn})(\nu_h t,k_h)\), where the notation means each function is evaluated at the same time and modulus. On this energy side \(0<k_h<1\). The low-energy modulus formula evaluated here would exceed one, signalling that the original choice of component scaling and physical extrema must be changed.

For the same inertias \(1,2,3\), choose initial \((1,0,0.20)\,{\rm rad/s}\). Then \(L^2=1.36,\ 2E=1.12\), and the high-energy inequality holds since \(2E>L^2/I_2=0.68\). The formulas give

\[A=1,\ B=\sqrt{0.12}=0.3464,\ C=0.20,\ \nu_h=\sqrt{1/3}=0.5774\,{\rm s^{-1}},\ k_h^2=0.12.\]

The minimum \(\omega_1\) is \(A\sqrt{1-k_h^2}=0.9381\,{\rm rad/s}\), so that principal gyro never reverses even though the third one does. A body-marking aligned with axis three can undergo a conspicuous reversal while the dominant axis-one spin changes only modestly. These component predictions are more specific than an energy or angular-speed trace.

At \(2E=L^2/I_2\), both moduli approach one. The definitions give

\[{\rm sn}(u,1)=\tanh u,\]
\[{\rm cn}(u,1)={\rm dn}(u,1)={\rm sech}\,u.\]

One separatrix solution therefore has \(\omega_2=B\tanh(\nu t)\) with \(\omega_1=A\,{\rm sech}(\nu t)\) and \(\omega_3=C\,{\rm sech}(\nu t)\), up to signs and time shifts consistent with Euler's equations. It connects opposite steady orientations along axis two as \(t\) goes from negative to positive infinity. Its asymptotic approach is exponential, with \(\nu\) equal to the small-disturbance growth exponent at the intermediate-axis equilibrium after inserting \(B=L/I_2\). A nearby periodic orbit spends much of its time near the unstable spin and flips more rapidly between those intervals.

Reconstruct spatial attitude without a singular azimuth

Let \(R(t)\) map body components into a laboratory basis. For body angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega_b\), its kinematic equation is

\[\dot R=R[\boldsymbol\omega_b]_\times,\]

where the skew matrix acts by a cross product. Starting from measured \(R(0)\), propagate by multiplying by the rotation appropriate to each short time interval. Exponentiating the skew matrix for an interval with constant estimated body angular velocity preserves orthogonality and determinant exactly. Applying a naive forward-Euler matrix addition \(R+hR[\omega]_\times\) generally stretches the basis, even if the angular velocity components came from an exact elliptic solution.

For a constant rotation about axis three at one radian per second, a single Euler step of duration \(0.10\,{\rm s}\) takes a transverse unit vector to one of length \(\sqrt{1+0.10^2}=1.00499\). After one hundred such steps the transverse length would grow by \((1.01)^{50}\approx1.64\). A quaternion or matrix exponential avoids that artificial stretch. Normalising each axis separately is insufficient if the axes lose orthogonality; check \(R^TR\) against identity and \(\det R\) against one. Also compute \(R(I_1\omega_1,I_2\omega_2,I_3\omega_3)^T\) at every time. It should give the same spatial vector in a torque-free run.

A measured attitude time series allows a different inference from fitting Euler angles separately. Use relative rotations between successive images to estimate angular velocity in the correct body or spatial frame. A camera can momentarily lose sensitivity to yaw when a marked axis points towards it; simultaneous orthogonal views or multiple non-collinear marks avoid that degeneracy. The gyro and attitude measurements together distinguish a true physical precession from an Euler-angle wrap by \(2\pi\). Synchronise their clocks before comparing the phase of a nutation extremum, since even a small time delay produces a plausible-looking mismatch near a fast flip.

Infer inertia ratios from a free-rotation record

A calibrated triad records \(\omega_i(t)\) in axes thought to be principal. Euler's equations predict that \(\dot\omega_1/(\omega_2\omega_3)\) is the constant \((I_2-I_3)/I_1\) wherever the denominator is appreciable, and similarly for cyclic indices. Instead of dividing noisy derivatives near zeros, integrate each component over a time interval. For example \(\omega_1(t_2)-\omega_1(t_1)=a_1\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\omega_2\omega_3\,dt\). Fit \(a_1\) over several intervals and repeat for \(a_2,a_3\). Select intervals with different angular configurations so that products do not all average nearly to zero. The ratios depend on inertia shape; multiplying every moment by a common constant leaves a torque-free angular-velocity record unchanged.

For a record giving \(a_1=-1.0\), \(a_2=1.0\) and \(a_3=-1/3\), solve the homogeneous equations \(I_2-I_3=-I_1\), \(I_3-I_1=I_2\), \(I_1-I_2=-I_3/3\). They give \(I_1:I_2:I_3=1:2:3\). An independent angular-momentum or torque calibration is needed to set the absolute scale. A known short applied torque impulse that changes one principal angular velocity by a measured amount can do this, provided the other gyroscopic contributions over that impulse are small or accounted for. A fit that estimates all absolute inertias from angular velocity alone can have an apparently excellent residual and still be structurally underdetermined.

If the sensors are not along principal axes the inertia matrix contains off-diagonal entries. The coordinate-free body equation is

\[I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega)=0.\]

Each measured time sample gives equations linear in the constant entries of the symmetric inertia tensor. With sufficiently rich motion one may estimate these entries up to scale, then diagonalise the fitted tensor to recover principal directions. Pure rotation along one axis gives insufficient excitation to reveal three distinct directions. Comparing a family of initial spins with a single tensor provides a stronger validity test than fitting a different set of effective principal inertias to each record.

Relate a body-frame period to a measured geometric phase

After a closed polhode in body angular-momentum space the laboratory orientation differs by a rotation about the fixed spatial momentum. That phase can be separated into a term accumulated at a dynamical rate and a term depending on the oriented solid angle of the body momentum path. For a fixed \(L\), the angular-velocity component along the momentum is \(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\hat L=2E/L\), since \(\mathbf L\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=2E\). Over one body period it contributes \(2ET_b/L\). Parallel transport of a transverse reference direction along the unit momentum curve contributes minus the oriented solid angle enclosed, up to whole turns and the chosen orientation of the sphere. The resulting net attitude phase is \(2ET_b/L-\Omega_{\rm solid}\pmod{2\pi}\).

The geometric part can be visualised by carrying a local tangent basis along the closed curve. Its two basis vectors are constrained to stay perpendicular to the moving unit momentum and are transported with no imposed twist around that direction. A return to the initial unit momentum need not return that basis to its starting orientation. For a latitude circle on the unit sphere the enclosed cap area is \(2\pi(1-\cos\theta)\); shrinking the path to a point makes the geometric phase vanish modulo \(2\pi\). This is why comparing a painted mark after a nutation only to an integrated scalar spin speed can give a discrepancy even with exact torque-free motion.

In practice the total phase can be measured without choosing a cap convention. Fit the attitude at two times separated by the full gyro period, form \(R(t+T_b)R(t)^T\), and extract the relative rotation around the known fixed spatial momentum. Independently integrate \(2E/L\) over that interval and compare the difference modulo \(2\pi\) with the signed spherical area traced by normalised body momentum. A factor-of-two error in the identified period, common if only \(\omega_3\) is recorded in the low-energy family, generally leaves the relative attitude rotation axis different from \(\mathbf L\). The axis check can reveal the error before assigning a numerical geometric phase.

Identify which inertias can exist in an actual rigid body

Three positive principal moments inserted into Euler's equations define a mathematical top, but the moments of a physical nonnegative mass distribution also satisfy triangle inequalities. In principal coordinates \(I_1=\int(y^2+z^2)\,dm\),

\[I_2=\int(z^2+x^2)\,dm,\]
\[I_3=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm.\]

Therefore \(I_1+I_2-I_3=2\int z^2\,dm\ge0\), and likewise by cyclic permutation. Equality means all mass is confined to the corresponding coordinate plane. The convenient example \(1,2,3\) lies on that boundary and can be realised by an ideal planar lamina. A fitted ratio \(1,2,3.5\) would imply negative mean squared distance along one direction and should prompt a calibration or modelling check.

A uniform thin rectangular plate of mass \(M\) and side lengths \(b\) along body axis one and \(a\) along body axis two has \(I_1=Ma^2/12,\ I_2=Mb^2/12,\ I_3=M(a^2+b^2)/12\). Choosing \(b=\sqrt2\,a\) gives exactly \(1:2:3\). For \(M=0.60\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(b=0.283\,{\rm m}\), the moments are approximately \(0.0020,0.0040,0.0060\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). The component equations for these moments give the same time histories as the scaled numerical example for identical initial angular velocities, while the actual angular momentum and energy are smaller by a factor 500. This gives a physical specimen for checking principal-axis stability without requiring a massive rotor.

Adding a small mass at a known location changes both inertia and possibly the centre of mass. If a symmetric pair of equal small masses is attached at \(\pm d\hat e_3\), their combined mass \(m_a\) adds \(m_a d^2\) to \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), nothing to \(I_3\), and leaves the centre fixed. For the plate above and \(m_a=0.10\,{\rm kg}, d=0.10\,{\rm m}\), the new moments are \(0.0030,0.0050,0.0060\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\). The intermediate-axis growth exponent at fixed \(\Omega\) changes from \(|\Omega|/\sqrt3\) to \(|\Omega|\sqrt{(0.005-0.003)(0.006-0.005)/(0.003\cdot0.006)}=|\Omega|/3\). A reversible mass-placement experiment tests the inertia dependence of instability, not merely the existence of a visually dramatic flip.

Estimate distance from a separatrix using dwell time

Near the intermediate-axis energy, elliptic modulus approaches one on either side. Writing \(d=1-k_e^2\) on the low-energy family gives \(K\sim\ln(4/\sqrt d)\). Thus \(T_b\sim4\ln(4/\sqrt d)/\nu\) for a slowly varying scale \(\nu\). Changing \(d\) by a factor of one hundred increases the expected body period by approximately \(4\ln10/\nu\). A tiny difference in the initial inertia-weighted energy can produce a readily observed increase in the time between reorientation events while the maximum angular speed scarcely changes. The logarithm follows from passages near a hyperbolic equilibrium, where the signed unstable perturbation grows exponentially.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 7. The heavy symmetric top, precession branches and the sleeping state

Gravity supplies a torque to a top pivoted away from its centre of mass. Its axis may precess steadily, nutate between two turning angles, or trace loops. Derive all three from the same angular kinetic energy and determine when an upright spinning top can remain stable.

Project gravity onto a body with one fixed point

Let \(I_1=I_2=I\) and \(I_3\) be moments about the pivot, with the centre of mass a distance \(l\) along the symmetry axis. Use Euler angles \(\theta,\phi,\psi\), where \(\theta\) measures the tilt from upward laboratory vertical, \(\phi\) measures the azimuth of the symmetry axis, and \(\psi\) spins the body about that axis relative to the nodal line. The centre-of-mass height is \(l\cos\theta\), so gravity contributes \(V=Mgl\cos\theta\). In the body the spin component of angular velocity is \(\omega_3=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta\). The squared transverse component is \(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta\). Consequently

\[L=\tfrac12 I(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)+\tfrac12 I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2-Mgl\cos\theta .\]

The angles \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) are absent themselves. Their momenta \(L_z=I\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta\) and \(L_3=I_3\omega_3\) are constants. Gravity torque about the pivot is perpendicular to the vertical and to the body symmetry axis, consistent with these two conservation laws. The entire vector \(\mathbf L\) need not be constant. Distinguish the axial spin \(\omega_3\) measured by a body gyro from the Euler rate \(\dot\psi\). A precessing top can have appreciable \(\dot\phi\cos\theta\); using the painted-mark rate in a spin estimate without specifying the reference can bias a precession prediction.

Eliminate cyclic velocities to find the nutation potential

Solve \(\dot\phi=(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)/(I\sin^2\theta)\) and \(\dot\psi=L_3/I_3-\dot\phi\cos\theta\). The energy can now be written \(E=L_3^2/(2I_3)+I\dot\theta^2/2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta)\), with

\[V_{\rm eff}(\theta)=Mgl\cos\theta+\frac{(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}.\]

The second term is not an additional gravitational interaction. It is the transverse rotational energy required by the two fixed angular momenta at each tilt. A root of \(E-L_3^2/(2I_3)-V_{\rm eff}=0\) is a polar turning angle; a simple root is generally reached and reversed in finite time. A minimum gives stable regular precession under sufficiently small nutations at fixed conserved momenta. The apparent divergence at a pole disappears if the numerator vanishes in the same limit. The possibility of the upright sleeping state therefore requires

\[L_z=L_3.\]

Putting \(u=\cos\theta\) gives a polynomial first integral. With \(E'=E-L_3^2/(2I_3)\), one obtains

\[I^2\dot u^2=2I(E'-Mglu)(1-u^2)-(L_z-L_3u)^2=P_3(u).\]

The accessible range lies within \([-1,1]\) where \(P_3\ge0\). The physical turning points can be found without assigning an Euler azimuth at a pole. A nutation period is \(2I\int_{u_{\min}}^{u_{\max}}du/\sqrt{P_3(u)}\). Computing the azimuthal advance over the same cycle requires averaging \(\dot\phi(u)\) with the same time weight. An arithmetic average of the two turning-point precession rates misses the longer dwell times near the endpoints.

Find slow and fast regular precession

For constant tilt and constant \(\dot\phi=\Omega\), the \(\theta\) equation at a nonzero tilt gives

\[I\Omega^2\cos\theta-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0.\]

This relation follows directly by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to \(\theta\), using \(\partial_\theta[\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta]=-\dot\phi\sin\theta\). It is quadratic in precession speed, so the often-used \(\Omega\simeq Mgl/L_3\) is only one limit. For a top above the horizontal plane and \(L_3>0\), two positive precession branches exist if \(L_3^2\ge4IMgl\cos\theta\). Their sum is \(L_3/(I\cos\theta)\), and their product is \(Mgl/(I\cos\theta)\).

Take \(I=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(I_3=0.010\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(Mgl=0.200\,{\rm N\,m}\),

\[L_3=0.200\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s},\]
\[\theta=60^\circ.\]

The quadratic is \(0.010\Omega^2-0.200\Omega+0.200=0\), with roots \(1.056\) and \(18.94\,{\rm rad/s}\). Both have the same material spin \(\omega_3=20\,{\rm rad/s}\), but their vertical angular momenta are different. The slow branch has \(L_z=0.020(1.056)(0.75)+0.200(0.5)=0.1158\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\); the fast branch has about \(0.384\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\). Preparing a tilt and a spin alone therefore does not select a unique regular precession. The initial angular velocity of the tilted axis also matters.

Distinguish looping, cusped and monotone azimuth traces

During nutation the sign of \(\dot\phi\) is the sign of \(L_z-L_3u\). If \(u_*=L_z/L_3\) lies strictly between the turning points, the azimuth reverses along each nutation and the axis can trace loops on the unit sphere. If \(u_*\) equals a turning point, both \(\dot\theta\) and \(\dot\phi\) can vanish together there, producing a cusp in the ideal trace. If the numerator keeps one sign the azimuth advances monotonically even as tilt oscillates. Seeing an apparent loop in a two-dimensional camera projection is insufficient by itself; recover the three-dimensional orientation or check the signed azimuthal rate.

One classic release starts with a tilted axis that initially has no angular velocity except spin about its own direction. Then \(L_z=L_3\cos\theta_0\), \(\dot\phi=0\), \(\dot\theta=0\). The gravitational torque starts tilting the axis down; on returning to that initial turning point the projected angular speed again momentarily vanishes. By contrast, giving the top the correct nonzero precession velocity at release puts it on a regular precession branch with no initial nutation. Identical tilt and spin measurements can thus yield dramatically different trajectories if the release mechanism imparts a different transverse angular velocity.

Derive the upright stability threshold

At \(L_z=L_3\), reduce the ratio \((1-\cos\theta)^2/\sin^2\theta\) to \((1-\cos\theta)/(1+\cos\theta)\). Near zero tilt the effective potential is \(Mgl+[L_3^2/(8I)-Mgl/2]\theta^2+O(\theta^4)\). A positive coefficient gives an energy minimum and requires \(L_3^2>4IMgl\). For the preceding \(I,Mgl\) this critical angular momentum is \(0.1265\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), or an axial spin speed \(12.65\,{\rm rad/s}\) for the chosen \(I_3\). A high spin stabilises the upper position by coupling tilt to conservation of angular momentum. The purely gravitational potential by itself has a maximum there.

The fixed-\(L_z=L_3\) quadratic energy curvature describes a polar disturbance within that momentum class. Near the axis \(\phi,\psi\) individually become coordinate singularities. To analyse arbitrary small perturbations, use horizontal components of the unit axis and linearise their coupled gyroscopic equations. The two circular precession frequencies at the pole solve \(I\Omega^2-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\), giving the same discriminant. Exactly at threshold the linear frequencies coalesce, and nonlinear terms or dissipation can dominate a finite disturbance. Distinguishing a stable ideal top from an experimentally long-lived slowly slowing top requires monitoring spin over the entire observation interval.

Measure the nutation frequency of a regular precession branch

Take any regular precession tilted away from the poles, with prescribed constants \(L_z,L_3\). A small perturbation of the polar angle gives \(I\ddot\eta+V_{\rm eff}''(\theta_0)\eta=0\), so the nutation frequency at fixed momenta follows from the second derivative of the effective potential. Differentiating while holding the instantaneous \(\dot\phi\) fixed would give a different result, because a displaced top with the same momenta must change its precession rate. This distinction matters when preparing a perturbation: a small tap that adds a transverse angular impulse can change the constants as well as the tilt.

A convenient explicit formula follows by writing \(F(\theta)=L_z-L_3\cos\theta\),

\[w(\theta)=F/(I\sin^2\theta).\]

Then \(V_{\rm eff}'=\sin\theta[-Mgl+L_3w-Iw^2\cos\theta]\). At a regular solution the bracket vanishes. Differentiate \(w\) as \(w'=(L_3/I-2w\cos\theta)/\sin\theta\), substitute into the derivative of the bracket, and obtain

\[\omega_{\rm nut}^2=\frac{V_{\rm eff}''(\theta_0)}{I}=\left(\frac{L_3}{I}-2\Omega\cos\theta_0\right)^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta_0.\]

For the \(60^\circ\) example,

\[L_3/I=10.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

On the slow branch \(\Omega=1.056\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), this gives \(\omega_{\rm nut}\approx8.99\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), or a nutation period of \(0.699\,{\rm s}\). On the fast branch \(\Omega=18.94\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), it gives approximately \(18.7\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), or \(0.336\,{\rm s}\). Both tilted regular solutions have positive fixed-momentum curvature in this example. Comparing just the precession rates would miss the different small oscillations around their two reduced equilibria.

Suppose a small angular displacement conserves \(L_z,L_3\) on the slow branch. The correlated rate shift is \(w'\delta\theta\). At the example point \(w'=(10-1.056)/\sin60^\circ=10.33\,{\rm s^{-1}/rad}\). A nutation of \(0.020\,{\rm rad}\) therefore modulates the precession rate by about \(0.207\,{\rm rad/s}\), a substantial fraction of its slow steady value. A low-resolution video that measures azimuth per frame can show a conspicuous modulation even when the nutation angle itself is visually small. Filtering only one angle and fitting it with constant angular speed would hide this dynamical correlation.

Recover a nutation trajectory from energy and two turn angles

Suppose a measured top has \(I=0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(Mgl=0.20\,{\rm J}\), \(L_3=0.20\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), and is released without precession or nutation at \(\cos\theta_0=0.50\). Its constants are \(L_z=0.10\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and \(E'=0.10\,{\rm J}\). The polar polynomial becomes

\[P_3(u)=0.04(0.10-0.20u)(1-u^2)-(0.10-0.20u)^2.\]

Factoring out \(0.10-0.20u\) leaves \(0.04(1-u^2)-(0.10-0.20u)\). The other roots satisfy \(0.04u^2-0.20u+0.06=0\), giving \(u\simeq0.3206\) and \(u\simeq4.68\). Only the first lies within the physical interval. Thus the tilt runs from \(60^\circ\) to about \(71.3^\circ\), and the unphysical cubic root must be discarded.

At the initial \(u=0.50\), the azimuthal numerator vanishes and the top momentarily has no precession. At the lower turning \(u\simeq0.3206\), the precession rate is \((0.10-0.20u)/[0.020(1-u^2)]\approx2.0\,{\rm rad/s}\). These two measurements together discriminate the cusped release from regular slow precession. A detector reporting only a mean precession rate near one radian per second could confuse them. Timing the first turning angle after release offers an independent check of \(Mgl/I\), while a direct gyro constrains \(L_3/I_3\).

To evaluate the nutation quadrature numerically without endpoint difficulties, substitute \(u=u_{\min}+(u_{\max}-u_{\min})\sin^2\alpha\). As \(\alpha\) approaches either endpoint, \(du\) cancels the simple square-root singularity from \(P_3\). Integrate once with numerator one for time and once with the extra factor \(\dot\phi(u)\) for azimuth. Repeat after halving the quadrature spacing. An exactly repeated root signals a limiting regular solution or a separatrix; the simple-turning substitution alone cannot regularise an actual logarithmically divergent period. In the cusped trajectory above the angular trace is continuous at the polar turning, even though the tangent to that trace can vanish.

Use horizontal tilt coordinates at the sleeping pole

Near the upright orientation the separate Euler angles \(\phi,\psi\) are ill conditioned. Instead let the symmetry-axis unit vector be \(\mathbf n=(x,y,1)+O(x^2+y^2)\) in an inertial frame with \(z\) upward. Here \(x,y\) are small dimensionless tilts, not translations. The instantaneous transverse angular velocity is \(\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n}\approx(-\dot y,\dot x,0)\), because \((\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n})\times\mathbf n=\dot{\mathbf n}\). The laboratory angular momentum to first order is \(I\mathbf n\times\dot{\mathbf n}+L_3\mathbf n\). Its derivative equals the gravitational torque \(Mgl\,\hat z\times\mathbf n\). Equating the two horizontal components yields

\[I\ddot x+L_3\dot y-Mgl\,x=0,\]
\[I\ddot y-L_3\dot x-Mgl\,y=0.\]

Let \(w=x+iy\). These combine into \(I\ddot w-iL_3\dot w-Mgl\,w=0\). Substituting \(w\propto e^{i\Omega t}\) returns \(I\Omega^2-L_3\Omega+Mgl=0\) without ever assigning azimuth to the exact pole. For spin strictly above threshold, a general small tilt is a superposition of two circular motions of the two real frequencies. Their vector sum traces a wobble. For spin below threshold, the two frequencies form a complex conjugate pair and one mode grows exponentially. A perfectly vertical initial state can remain exactly vertical in an ideal simulation on either side, so stability requires a nonzero test perturbation.

Take a below-threshold spin \(L_3=0.100\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) with the earlier \(I=0.020\) and \(Mgl=0.200\). The real precession rate is \(L_3/(2I)=2.50\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and the exponential growth rate is

\[\sqrt{4IMgl-L_3^2}/(2I)=1.94\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

If the growing-mode component starts at \(1\,{\rm mrad}\), a \(20\,{\rm mrad}\) excursion takes about \(\ln20/1.94=1.55\,{\rm s}\) within the linear model. Below threshold the top need not collapse immediately after release. A short video can show several degrees of apparently orderly precession before growing tilt becomes unmistakable.

Just above threshold the mode frequencies are nearly degenerate. Decomposing noisy initial conditions into two circular amplitudes then involves division by their small frequency separation. A finite tilt impulse can produce a large beat envelope even though both ideal modes remain bounded. At exactly the repeated root the general linear solution contains \(t e^{i\Omega t}\) as well as \(e^{i\Omega t}\); neutral-looking roots alone do not ensure boundedness. This distinction matters when inferring a critical spin from finite observation windows. Report the initial perturbation and the tilt threshold used to define loss of stability.

Verify the torque law with a horizontal gyroscope

Derive the matrix condition from Hamilton's equations

Collect \(n\) canonical positions and momenta into \(z=(q_1,\ldots,q_n,p_1,\ldots,p_n)^T\). Write Hamilton's equations as \(\dot z=J\nabla_z H\), where \(J\) has zero diagonal blocks, identity upper right and minus identity lower left. For a time-independent change \(Z=f(z)\) with Jacobian \(S=\partial Z/\partial z\), the chain rule gives

\[\dot Z=S J S^T\nabla_Z H.\]

To preserve the canonical form for every differentiable Hamiltonian require

\[SJS^T=J.\]

For an invertible square matrix this is equivalent to \(S^TJS=J\). In geometric terms the bilinear antisymmetric form \(\omega(u,v)=u^TJv\) has the same value on any transported pair of tangent vectors.

Taking determinants gives \((\det S)^2=1\), and symplectic maps have positive determinant equal to one. One way to see the sign is to take the top exterior power of the preserved two-form: \(\omega^n\) fixes an oriented volume. In one canonical pair the identity \(S^TJS=(\det S)J\) makes determinant one sufficient. In two or more pairs a single volume condition cannot enforce every entry of the matrix condition. For example in ordering \((q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)\), scaling by diagonal entries \((2,1,1,1/2)\) preserves four-dimensional volume. But \(\{Q_1,P_1\}=2\) and \(\{Q_2,P_2\}=1/2\), so neither conjugate pair has the required bracket. A volume-preserving histogram test alone would miss this failure.

Identify brackets of observables and what they measure

Define \(\{A,B\}=(\nabla A)^T J\nabla B=\sum_i(A_{q_i}B_{p_i}-A_{p_i}B_{q_i})\). For any explicitly time-dependent observable \(A(z,t)\), differentiation along Hamiltonian trajectories gives

\[\dot A=\{A,H\}+\partial_t A.\]

The bracket is antisymmetric and obeys the product rule. Computing it with the chain rule in the new variables proves that canonical transformations preserve brackets, even between nonlinear observables. Conversely the fundamental brackets \(\{Q_i,P_j\}=\delta_{ij}\), \(\{Q_i,Q_j\}=\{P_i,P_j\}=0\) are precisely the entries of \(SJS^T=J\).

For a particle set \(L_z=xp_y-yp_x\). Its Hamiltonian flow as a generator sends \(x\) to \(x-\varepsilon y\), \(y\) to \(y+\varepsilon x\) and rotates momenta by the same infinitesimal angle. The angular momentum components satisfy \(\{L_x,L_y\}=L_z\) with cyclic permutations. Derive one entry directly for \(L_x=yp_z-zp_y\) and \(L_y=zp_x-xp_z\). The \(z,p_z\) pair contributes \(xp_y-yp_x\), with the other terms zero. Rotations about two different axes generally fail to commute. The resulting commutator has the form of a rotation about the third axis even though each underlying infinitesimal motion preserves every canonical bracket.

Jacobi's identity \(\{A,\{B,C\}\}+\{B,\{C,A\}\}+\{C,\{A,B\}\}=0\) implies that the bracket of two time-independent conserved quantities is conserved under the same Hamiltonian. If the Hamiltonian is invariant under rotations about \(x\) and \(y\), its invariance about \(z\) follows from this relation and \(\{L_x,L_y\}=L_z\). Conservation alone need not make two observables commute. A central force can conserve all three angular momentum components, but their mutual brackets remain nonzero. For independent commuting integrals in an integrability argument one must check the involution condition explicitly.

Construct a canonical map for any smooth point transformation

Suppose new coordinates depend only on old coordinates, \(Q=f(q)\). Preserve the canonical one-form exactly,

\[p^T dq=P^T dQ=P^T Df\,dq.\]

Thus \(p=(Df)^TP\) and \(P=(Df)^{-T}p\). Applying the same coordinate matrix to momenta works only for a restricted case such as an orthogonal change of coordinates. An extension of position must be accompanied by a reciprocal rescaling of its conjugate momentum. For \(Q=q^2\) on \(q>0\), for example, \(P=p/(2q)\). The free Hamiltonian becomes \(2QP^2/m\); even a physically free particle can acquire a coordinate-dependent expression for its kinetic energy.

Polar coordinates demonstrate why a momentum variable need not equal a measured tangential component. In the plane \(x=r\cos\varphi,y=r\sin\varphi\). Substituting into \(p_xdx+p_ydy\) gives

\[p_r=\mathbf p\cdot\hat r,\]
\[p_\varphi=r\,\mathbf p\cdot\hat\varphi=L_z.\]

The physical tangential momentum is \(p_\varphi/r\). At radius \(0.40\,{\rm m}\) and transverse momentum \(0.60\,{\rm kg\,m/s}\), the canonical angular momentum is \(0.24\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\). Treating the transverse component itself as conjugate to angle would make \(\{\varphi,p_\varphi^{\rm wrong}\}=1/r\). The coordinate singularity at \(r=0\) also prevents these canonical polar variables from covering the entire original phase space with one chart.

Generate shears and test a numerical map

A type-two generating function \(F_2(q,P,t)\) defines \(p=\partial_qF_2\), \(Q=\partial_P F_2\), and \(K=H+\partial_tF_2\) where the transformation can be locally inverted. The differential \(d(F_2-QP)\) equals \(p\,dq-P\,dQ+(K-H)\,dt\). Thus the actions differ only by an endpoint term; taking another exterior derivative at fixed time gives the symplectic form condition. For \(F_2=qP+sP^2/(2m)\), the map is \(p=P,Q=q+sp/m\), free drift over time \(s\). For \(F_2=qP+sV(q)\), the map is \(P=p-sV'(q),Q=q\), a potential kick. Their composition is canonical for any step size even though an alternating drift and kick need not give the exact trajectory.

Take a unit-mass, unit-frequency oscillator with \(h=0.5\). A kick followed by drift gives \(P=p-hq\), \(Q=q+hP=(1-h^2)q+hp\). Its amplification matrix has determinant one and trace \(2-h^2=1.75\), so its eigenvalues form a unit-modulus conjugate pair. The numerical phase per step is \(\arccos(1-h^2/2)=0.50536\), slightly ahead of the exact phase \(0.50000\). Starting at \(q=1,p=0\), the first step gives \(Q=0.75,P=-0.50\), and physical energy becomes \(0.40625\) instead of \(0.5\). Exact bracket preservation does not imply exact conservation of the original energy at every point. If \(h>2\) the same matrix remains symplectic but has a real expanding and contracting eigenvalue; geometric preservation is compatible with numerical instability.

For a numerical implementation with analytic equations unavailable, approximate the Jacobian of a one-step map at selected states by perturbing one canonical component at a time. Compute \(S^TJS-J\) after nondimensionalising coordinates and momenta in conjugate pairs. Repeat with smaller perturbations and at different integration tolerances. A determinant close to one in four dimensions is insufficient. Testing the transported brackets of one position–momentum pair and one cross-pair can reveal a swapped column or misplaced mass factor before a long simulation exhibits visible energy drift.

Track canonical area for a transported experimental ensemble

A narrow group of trajectories lets an experimenter estimate a tangent map without applying a separate perturbation to each coordinate at the same time. Prepare a small covariance ellipse in one canonical pair \(z=(q,p)\), propagate each member through a known linear Hamiltonian device, and compute \(\Sigma_{\rm out}=S\Sigma_{\rm in}S^T\). The ellipse area is proportional to \(\sqrt{\det\Sigma}\), and a symplectic map in one pair preserves that area. Its projections can grow or contract individually: a focusing device can reduce position spread while increasing momentum spread or correlation. Measuring only the two marginal widths ignores their covariance and can falsely suggest area growth.

For a free unit-mass drift of duration two in scaled units,

\[S=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Start with \(\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(1,1)\). The outgoing covariance has entries \(\Sigma_{qq}=5,\Sigma_{pp}=1,\Sigma_{qp}=2\). The apparent rectangle of marginal standard deviations grows by \(\sqrt5\), yet the determinant remains \(5-4=1\). A plot that suppresses the correlation has discarded exactly the information needed for the area test. Noise in the two channels also contributes to observed covariance, so calibrate sensor variances independently before attributing a small apparent growth to physical dissipation or numerical smoothing.

For two pairs it is possible for a perfectly canonical coupling to exchange projected emittances. Take a uniform rotation of the two positions and the same rotation of their conjugate momenta. It is canonical by construction. If the two incoming pairs have different covariance areas, a ninety-degree rotation swaps them completely; at forty-five degrees each outgoing projected pair contains a mixture. The full four-dimensional symplectic structure remains intact. An experimentally available two-dimensional marginal then tests only part of the dynamics. Counting phase-space points in a volume and measuring cross-pair brackets answer different questions about a strongly coupled system.

The eigenvalues of \(J\Sigma\) are invariant under the linear canonical covariance transport. Indeed \(J S=S^{-T}J\) for symplectic \(S\), so \(J\Sigma_{\rm out}=S^{-T}(J\Sigma_{\rm in})S^T\), a similarity transformation. For a positive covariance these eigenvalues occur as conjugate imaginary pairs, and their positive magnitudes give symplectic covariance scales. This algebraic test can distinguish loss of correlations in a fitted transfer matrix from actual exchange between pairs. It assumes linear propagation of the ensemble. A nonlinear canonical map can stretch a finite ellipse into a curved shape whose covariance determinant changes while its exact enclosed area remains preserved.

Use generating functions to find a moving normal-mode basis

Consider a two-dimensional isotropic oscillator and rotate its coordinate axes by a prescribed time-dependent angle \(\alpha(t)\). Let \(Q=R(\alpha)^Tq\), \(P=R(\alpha)^Tp\) for a proper planar rotation. At any fixed time the map is orthogonal and symplectic. To derive the Hamiltonian in these moving variables, use \(F_2(q,P,t)=q^TR(\alpha)P\). Its time derivative at fixed old \(q\) and new \(P\) is \(-\dot\alpha(Q_xP_y-Q_yP_x)\), with the sign corresponding to axes rotating counterclockwise in the laboratory. The transformed Hamiltonian is therefore

\[K=H_0(Q,P)-\dot\alpha L_z.\]

For constant rotation rate and an isotropic potential the new Hamiltonian has no explicit time dependence. Its value is the combination energy minus rotation rate times angular momentum encountered in the Noether argument. Hamilton's equations now include \(-\dot\alpha\,\hat z\times Q\) in \(\dot Q\) relative to the rotated physical velocity. Thus the transformed canonical momentum \(P\) equals the rotated laboratory momentum rather than \(m\dot Q\). Confusing the two yields incorrect initial conditions for a rotating-frame simulation even if the transformation matrix at the starting instant is the identity.

Take \(m=1.0\,{\rm kg}\), a laboratory point initially at \(q=(0.20,0)\,{\rm m}\) with \(p=(0,0.30)\,{\rm kg\,m/s}\), and axes initially aligned but rotating at \(2.0\,{\rm rad/s}\). The canonical \(P_y\) is \(0.30\,{\rm kg\,m/s}\). The derivative of the rotating-frame coordinate is

\[0.30-2.0(0.20)=-0.10\,{\rm m/s}.\]

It moves in the negative rotating-frame \(y\) direction while its laboratory velocity points positive \(y\). The angular momentum is \(0.060\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\), so \(K\) lies \(0.120\,{\rm J}\) below the laboratory Hamiltonian. Both position–momentum transformations preserve brackets; the extra term derives from the moving basis, not a failure of canonical geometry.

An oscillator with two nearly equal natural frequencies provides another reason to track the generating function. If one diagonalises a slowly changing stiffness matrix at each time and keeps only the instantaneous diagonal frequencies, the omitted rotation of the eigenvectors couples the nominal modes through a term of this type. When the eigendirections turn rapidly near a degeneracy, the coupling can be appreciable even if both eigenvalues change little. Measuring the orientation as well as the eigenfrequencies allows the size of this effect to be estimated before fitting amplitude transfer to a spurious dissipation term.

Compare exact phase transport with a dissipative fit

A damped oscillator with \(m\ddot q+c\dot q+kq=0\) has equations \(\dot q=p/m\), \(\dot p=-kq-cp/m\). In the physical \((q,p)\) plane their divergence is \(-c/m\). A small material phase-space area therefore contracts as \(A(t)=A(0)e^{-ct/m}\). No time-independent canonical coordinate change on the same two-dimensional isolated phase space can turn that physical contraction into a Hamiltonian flow preserving area. This conclusion concerns the reduced dissipative description. An expanded system including the degrees of freedom that absorb energy can still be Hamiltonian.

For \(c/m=0.40\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), a pair of independent initial perturbations spanning area one in calibrated coordinates spans only \(0.449\) after two seconds. A numerical step of \(0.10\,{\rm s}\) fitted to that exact map should have determinant \(e^{-0.04}=0.9608\). Forcing its determinant to one during system identification would remove a real dissipative measurement. Conversely a conservative oscillator propagated by forward Euler at matched units and \(h=0.10\) has amplification determinant \(1+h^2=1.01\), corresponding to a spurious area growth. A geometric diagnostic is interpretable only after deciding what physical system and coordinate pair were actually observed.

The pair \(q,v\) differs from canonical \(q,p\) by a constant mass scaling for an ordinary single particle. In one pair with constant mass that scaling does not change a measured same-coordinate before-and-after determinant. For a position-dependent mass, constrained coordinates with a varying metric, or explicit time-dependent changes of measurement scale, volume in raw position–velocity coordinates need not equal canonical volume. For example a free particle expressed in \(Q=q^2\) has canonical momentum \(P=m\dot Q/(4Q)\). A detector estimating phase area in \((Q,\dot Q)\) without the \(Q\)-dependent factor can infer contraction or expansion even when canonical phase area is exactly conserved.

Compute a bracket of mechanical momenta and recover the Lorentz force

For a charge \(e\) in a prescribed vector potential write mechanical momentum \(\boldsymbol\pi=\mathbf p-e\mathbf A(\mathbf q,t)\). The canonical brackets are \(\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}\), but the measured mechanical components satisfy

\[\{\pi_i,\pi_j\}=e(\partial_i A_j-\partial_j A_i)=e\epsilon_{ijk}B_k.\]

At nonzero magnetic field they are not simultaneously canonical momenta conjugate to the Cartesian positions. This result is gauge invariant because it involves only the curl of the vector potential. It offers a physical example where replacing canonical by measured momenta in a software interface changes the matrix of fundamental brackets.

With \(H=\pi^2/(2m)+e\phi\), the time derivative is

\[\dot\pi_i=\{\pi_i,H\}+\partial_t\pi_i.\]

The kinetic-energy bracket contributes \((e/m)(\boldsymbol\pi\times\mathbf B)_i\); the scalar potential contributes \(-e\partial_i\phi\); and the explicit derivative supplies \(-e\partial_t A_i\). Their sum is \(e[\mathbf E+(\boldsymbol\pi/m)\times\mathbf B]_i\). Dropping the last term would make time-dependent electric acceleration depend on gauge. In a static uniform \(B_z\), the transverse mechanical momenta rotate although their energy is constant. Canonical bracket structure therefore contains observable information about the force even before integrating a trajectory.

A useful finite-difference test sets \(A_y=B x\) and samples the map from canonical to mechanical variables around one state. The measured bracket \(\{\pi_x,\pi_y\}=eB\) should be constant in space. A code that insists on zero will attribute the entire magnetic response to an erroneous Hamiltonian gradient. A code using mechanical variables legitimately can instead use their noncanonical bracket matrix; its equations must then preserve the correct transformed structure, not the original constant \(J\). Testing the resulting acceleration at zero velocity and at a transverse velocity distinguishes electric and magnetic terms and catches sign errors in the antisymmetric entries.

Derive a reversible drift–kick–drift scheme and its measured phase

For \(H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)\), exact kinetic flow for a duration \(t\) is \(D_t:(q,p)\mapsto(q+tp/m,p)\). Exact potential flow is \(K_t:(q,p)\mapsto(q,p-tV'(q))\). Each is canonical. Compose \(D_{h/2}K_hD_{h/2}\) in chronological order. Applying the same construction with \(-h\) undoes each substep in reverse order, so the full map is time reversible. Symmetry eliminates the even local-error powers in the effective generator, giving a second-order method for smooth \(V\). The individual Hamiltonian pieces need not commute; if they did, the split map would be exact at any step.

For a unit oscillator, carrying out the algebra gives

\[Q=(1-h^2/2)q+h(1-h^2/4)p,\]
\[P=-h q+(1-h^2/2)p.\]

Verify directly that the determinant is one. The trace gives the same step angle \(2\arcsin(h/2)\) as the first-order kick–drift matrix in its stable range, but the new off-diagonal coefficients make the amplitude errors and time reversal different. Starting from \(q=1,p=0\) at \(h=0.50\), one gets \(Q=0.875,P=-0.50\), energy \(0.5078125\). A negative step returns to the initial values apart from roundoff. Passing this one reversal test says nothing about absolute phase accumulated over hundreds of periods.

The map preserves \(q^2+(1-h^2/4)p^2\) exactly for the harmonic test. At \(h=0.5\) the coefficient of \(p^2\) is \(0.9375\), so the preserved ellipses differ slightly from the circles of physical energy. Their reversible oscillation in physical energy is bounded for \(|h|<2\). The per-step phase is about \(0.50536\) at this step, giving a phase gain \(0.536\) radians after one hundred steps. As \(h\) is halved while physical integration time stays fixed, the overall phase error falls approximately by four. Measuring an energy oscillation, a reversal residual and a return time therefore probes three different properties of one apparently simple map.

Relate the order of two pulses to their Poisson bracket

A canonical transformation close to identity can be built by applying two brief Hamiltonian operations in different orders. Take one free drift and one potential kick, each of short duration \(s\). A drift then a kick gives

\[q'=q+sp/m,\ p'=p-sV'(q+sp/m).\]

A kick then a drift gives

\[p''=p-sV'(q),\ q''=q+s[p-sV'(q)]/m.\]

To second order their coordinate difference is \(q'-q''=s^2V'(q)/m\); their momentum difference is \(p'-p''=-s^2pV''(q)/m+O(s^3)\). The difference vector equals the Hamiltonian vector generated by \(G=pV'(q)/m=\{V,T\}\), multiplied by \(s^2\). The bracket sets the leading effect of reversing the operation order.

For the harmonic oscillator at \(q=1,p=0,m=k=1\) with \(s=0.10\), a drift then kick gives \((1,-0.10)\); kick then drift gives \((0.99,-0.10)\). The coordinate difference \(0.01\) equals \(s^2V'/m\), and the leading momentum difference is zero for this initial condition. At \(q=0,p=1\), the complementary experiment gives a momentum difference of \(-0.01\) and no coordinate difference. Running both initial states tests the two components of the bracket-generated perturbation. A single apparently correct coordinate update would not detect a sign swap in the other component.

An experimental implementation might use a short force pulse as a kick and free propagation between pulses. The pulse must be short enough that motion during the kick is negligible; otherwise its own kinetic drift enters the comparison at the same order as the desired bracket. Extrapolate the measured difference divided by \(s^2\) to small \(s\), and separately measure acquisition noise, which grows in relative importance under that division. Alternating the pulse order cancels many static calibration shifts but does not magically remove timing offsets correlated with the order. Treat the small bracket signal as a physical difference between two specified protocols.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 9. Action, angle and the slowing clock at a separatrix

A phase portrait contains enough information to extract a period by differentiating an area. Use that fact to build a clock for finite-amplitude pendulum motion, determine precisely where slow-parameter invariance fails, and distinguish a librating trajectory from one that makes complete rotations.

Turn a closed phase-space loop into an action variable

For a one-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian \(H(q,p;\lambda)\) at fixed parameter \(\lambda\), take a connected periodic orbit of energy \(E\). Define its action by \(J(E,\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1}\oint p\,dq\), oriented along the actual motion. The units are energy times time. A smooth family of nested librating loops has \(2\pi J\) equal to the enclosed canonical phase area. For circulation on a coordinate cylinder, such as an angle increasing through \(2\pi\), the integral still defines an action even though the loop winds around the cylinder. Its area interpretation and sign need a reference branch.

At fixed \(q,\lambda\), differentiating \(H(q,p(E,q))=E\) gives

\[\partial_Ep=1/H_p=1/\dot q.\]

Differentiating the action integral yields \(\partial_E J=T/(2\pi)\), because \(dq/\dot q=dt\) around the orbit. At ordinary turning points \(\dot q=0\), the derivative integrand has an integrable singularity while the two endpoints of the closed loop agree; one can justify the derivative using a local square-root coordinate. Inverting gives

\[\omega(J)=\partial_JE.\]

If \(\theta\) is the angle conjugate to \(J\), the transformed Hamiltonian is \(E(J)\) and \(\dot\theta=\omega(J)\) is constant. This canonical angle generally differs from the geometric angle reported by a camera.

For a unit-mass harmonic oscillator the energy curve \(p^2+\omega_0^2q^2=2E\) is an ellipse. Its area is \(2\pi E/\omega_0\), giving \(J=E/\omega_0\) and a frequency independent of amplitude. One may take \(q=\sqrt{2J/\omega_0}\sin\theta,\ p=\sqrt{2J\omega_0}\cos\theta\), whose bracket is one. The position turns nonuniformly in coordinate space even though the phase angle advances uniformly. Under a slow change in \(\omega_0\), constant \(J\) predicts \(E\propto\omega_0\) and amplitude proportional to \(1/\sqrt{\omega_0}\). Constant action does not mean constant mechanical energy while work is performed by a changing spring.

Integrate the finite-amplitude pendulum

Let \(I=ml^2\), \(\omega_0^2=g/l\), and measure \(q\) from downward vertical. The Hamiltonian \(H=p^2/(2I)+I\omega_0^2(1-\cos q)\) has separatrix energy \(E_s=2I\omega_0^2\). For a libration of turning angle \(q_m<\pi\), define \(k=\sin(q_m/2)=\sqrt{E/E_s}\). The positive momentum branch is \(p=2I\omega_0\sqrt{k^2-\sin^2(q/2)}\). Set \(\sin(q/2)=k\sin u\). Evaluating four quarter-arcs of the loop gives

\[J_{\rm lib}=\frac{8I\omega_0}{\pi}\big[\mathsf E(k)-(1-k^2)\mathsf K(k)\big],\]
\[T_{\rm lib}=\frac{4}{\omega_0}\mathsf K(k).\]

Here \(\mathsf K(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-k^2\sin^2u)^{-1/2}du\) and \(\mathsf E(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}\,du\). To verify the first formula after substitution, note that \(k^2\cos^2u=(1-k^2\sin^2u)-(1-k^2)\). The transformed integral of \(k^2\cos^2u/\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}\) splits exactly into the two quoted complete elliptic integrals. The period follows either by \(dq/\dot q\) or by differentiating the action. That independent check is useful when numerical differentiation of noisy action estimates amplifies sampling errors.

At small \(k\),

\[\mathsf K=(\pi/2)(1+k^2/4+\cdots),\]
\[T\approx(2\pi/\omega_0)(1+q_m^2/16+\cdots).\]

At \(q_m=60^\circ\), \(k=1/2\) and \(\mathsf K\simeq1.68575\), giving \(T/T_{\rm small}=1.0732\). A pendulum of length \(1.00\,{\rm m}\) then has period about \(2.153\,{\rm s}\), where the small-angle answer is \(2.006\,{\rm s}\). Timing ten periods rather than one helps resolve the accumulated difference. Measuring the arc amplitude from a projected image requires a geometric correction if the swing plane is tilted relative to the camera.

Approach the saddle from either side

At the upright position \(q=\pi\), the potential is maximal and the equilibrium is a hyperbolic saddle in phase space. Put \(q=\pi+s\) with small \(s\); the energy near the saddle is \(H\simeq E_s+p^2/(2I)-I\omega_0^2s^2/2\). For an orbit at \(E_s-\Delta E\), the time spent in a fixed small neighbourhood includes an integral proportional to \(\int ds/\sqrt{s^2-2\Delta E/(I\omega_0^2)}\). As \(\Delta E\) decreases, this develops a logarithm. Equivalently \(\mathsf K(k)\sim\ln[4/\sqrt{1-k^2}]\) as \(k\to1^-\). The separatrix encloses finite canonical area but its period is infinite. Hence \(\partial_EJ\) diverges although \(J\) itself remains finite.

On the positive rotation branch \(E>E_s\), set \(k_r=\sqrt{E_s/E}<1\). A period is the time for \(q\) to increase through \(2\pi\), so there is only one positive branch of momentum in its action integral. Direct integration gives

\[J_{\rm rot}=(2/\pi)\sqrt{2IE}\,\mathsf E(k_r),\]
\[T_{\rm rot}=4\sqrt{I/(2E)}\,\mathsf K(k_r).\]

At very high energy, the potential is a small modulation and these formulas approach free-rotor values \(\sqrt{2IE}\) and \(2\pi\sqrt{I/(2E)}\). At the separatrix the positive rotation action approaches \(4I\omega_0/\pi\), half the limiting libration action \(8I\omega_0/\pi\). The two actions count different closed paths on a cylinder. The factor two is a topological change in the cycle, not a discontinuous jump in the mechanical momentum at a point.

Test slow conservation against the actual orbit period

To treat slowly varying parameters, define instantaneous action–angle coordinates for each fixed \(\lambda\). The generating function \(W(q,J,\lambda)\) acquires explicit time dependence when \(\lambda=\lambda(t)\), adding \(\dot\lambda\,\partial_\lambda W\) to the transformed Hamiltonian. Its angle dependence produces an oscillating \(\dot J=-\dot\lambda\,\partial_\theta\partial_\lambda W\). On a regular periodic orbit, the integral of an angle derivative over a cycle vanishes. If \(\lambda\) changes little during that cycle and there is no resonant complication, this cancellation explains why the action changes little on average. The requirement uses the actual orbit period at the chosen energy. Near a separatrix it cannot be justified merely by comparing a drive rate to the small-amplitude period.

Consider a parameter ramp lasting \(20\,{\rm s}\) for the one-metre pendulum. Near the bottom this covers about ten periods. For \(1-E/E_s=10^{-6}\), the asymptotic \(\mathsf K\simeq\ln4000=8.29\) gives a period of approximately \(10.6\,{\rm s}\), so the same ramp spans less than two cycles. Even longer ramps fail the scale separation sufficiently close to the saddle. If a varying parameter changes the separatrix area until it meets the trajectory, continued use of a single analytic action branch is especially unreliable. A trajectory can pass into circulation or return to libration depending on its phase and the ramp history.

Compute an action without measuring momentum continuously

Suppose a camera tracks one-dimensional positions over one full cycle, and velocity estimates are noisy. For a known position-dependent potential and calibrated mass, an independent measurement of the turning energy allows phase-area integration without differentiating the camera trace. On a bound orbit \(p_\pm(q)=\pm\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}\), so \(J=\pi^{-1}\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}\,dq\). One needs a potential calibration, both turning positions and a common energy datum. An additive constant in the potential cancels when \(E\) is shifted by the same amount. An unrecognised dissipative force makes the inward and outward energy profiles different and invalidates the assumed single closed energy curve.

Take a particle in the potential \(V(q)=a|q|\), \(a>0\), which can approximate opposed constant restoring forces away from a narrow central smoothing zone. Its turning displacements are \(\pm E/a\). Integrate on the positive half and use symmetry to find

\[J=4\sqrt{2m}E^{3/2}/(3\pi a).\]

Differentiating gives

\[T=2\pi\,dJ/dE=4\sqrt{2mE}/a.\]

The same period follows by constant acceleration: starting at rest at a positive turning point, the fall to the origin takes \(\sqrt{2mE}/a\), one quarter period. Agreement across a nondifferentiable potential cusp is a useful check that the action calculation handles the turning boundaries rather than requiring sinusoidal motion.

For \(m=0.10\,{\rm kg}\), \(a=2.0\,{\rm N}\), and \(E=0.20\,{\rm J}\), the amplitude is \(0.10\,{\rm m}\), action is \(0.00849\,{\rm J\,s}\), and period is \(0.400\,{\rm s}\). If the restoring strength \(a\) doubles slowly at fixed action, \(E\propto a^{2/3}\) increases by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\), while the turning amplitude shrinks by \(2^{-1/3}=0.794\). A harmonic-oscillator action rule \(J=E/\omega\) using a single measured frequency would be incorrect here: the actual \(E/\omega=ET/(2\pi)\) exceeds the action by a factor \(3/2\). The equality found for a harmonic oscillator follows from its linear energy–action relation and need not generalise.

Separate the angle accumulated during a cycle from the action it encloses

In a slowly modulated oscillator an almost constant action can coexist with a measurable phase error. Consider \(\ddot q+\omega(t)^2q=0\) with smooth positive \(\omega\). An approximate oscillation is \(q=A(t)\cos\Phi(t)\). At leading order \(\dot\Phi=\omega\). Cancelling the first-order sine term gives \(2\dot A\omega+A\dot\omega=0\), so \(A\sqrt\omega\) is approximately constant. The amplitude law reproduces conservation of \(E/\omega\) after averaging. The accumulated phase is \(\int\omega(t)\,dt\), which can change by many radians during a slow parameter cycle even if the initial and final frequency and action agree.

For \(\omega(t)=10[1+0.10\sin(2\pi t/T_r)]\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(T_r=20\,{\rm s}\), integration over one full ramp gives \(200\,{\rm rad}\) of leading phase. The largest value of \(|\dot\omega|/\omega^2\) is only a few \(10^{-3}\). To first order the final amplitude returns to its initial value, but the position at the end is set by a phase that is generally not an integer multiple of \(2\pi\). A calculation that expects to return to its initial point after the parameter cycle confuses action invariance with angle closure. A small constant frequency calibration error, integrated over a long ramp, can dominate the final phase error while producing little amplitude error.

A sudden frequency switch gives an instructive opposite limit with exact phase dependence. At fixed \(q,p\), changing \(\omega_1\) instantly to \(\omega_2\) changes the action to \(J_2=p^2/(2m\omega_2)+m\omega_2 q^2/2\). Write the initial state at action \(J_1\) using the harmonic angle. Then

\[J_2/J_1=(\omega_1/\omega_2)\cos^2\theta+(\omega_2/\omega_1)\sin^2\theta.\]

For a frequency doubling, the ratio ranges from one half at a passage through the centre to two at a turning point. For a uniformly sampled ensemble the average ratio is \(1.25\), although the pre-switch and post-switch Hamiltonians are both perfectly integrable.

An experiment can therefore test slowness by preparing the same initial action at different phases and comparing the distribution of final actions. A broad phase-dependent distribution for a rapid switch narrows as the ramp becomes slow on a regular family of tori. Near a separatrix a slow external ramp can still yield a broad distribution because the orbital clock itself stalls. The size of the instantaneous energy, measured without phase and the final Hamiltonian, does not distinguish these mechanisms.

Resolve a separatrix in time without integrating arbitrarily close to the saddle

At exactly \(E_s\), energy conservation gives \(p=\pm2I\omega_0\cos(q/2)\) on \(-\pi<q<\pi\). Choose the increasing branch passing through \(q=0\) at \(t=0\). Separation of variables in \(\dot q=2\omega_0\cos(q/2)\) gives

\[\tan(\pi/4+q/4)=e^{\omega_0t}.\]

Therefore \(q(t)=4\arctan(e^{\omega_0 t})-\pi\),

\[p(t)=2I\omega_0\,{\rm sech}(\omega_0t).\]

This exact orbit approaches the upright saddle exponentially at both infinite endpoints. The two endpoints represent the same physical angle on the cylinder even though they differ by \(2\pi\) on an unwrapped plot.

The separatrix momentum falls to one tenth of its maximum after \(\omega_0 t=\operatorname{arcosh}10\approx2.993\). At \(\omega_0=3.13\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) that takes only \(0.956\,{\rm s}\). Reaching one hundredth takes \(\operatorname{arcosh}100/\omega_0\approx1.69\,{\rm s}\). The logarithmic increase in time per retained significant digit explains why an apparently captured upright state can simply be a trajectory very close to the saddle. Measuring angular velocity as well as angular displacement makes the exponential approach easier to distinguish from a truly stationary upright solution.

The phase area enclosed by both separatrix branches can also be recovered directly. Integrating their difference gives

\[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}4I\omega_0\cos(q/2)dq=16I\omega_0.\]

Dividing by \(2\pi\) yields the libration limit \(8I\omega_0/\pi\) obtained using elliptic integrals. By contrast a trajectory winding once on the upper branch integrates only the upper momentum, giving half that value. Testing these two integrals on an unwrapped numerical phase trace can reveal a missing lower turning branch or a routine that silently wraps the angle before accumulating \(p\,dq\).

Find a frequency directly from noisy orbital areas

Suppose a calibrated experiment measures the phase areas \(A(E)=\oint p\,dq\) at several energies. With \(J=A/(2\pi)\), the identity \(dA/dE=T\) says that an area-versus-energy slope directly has units of time. Take three close energies \(0.09,0.10,0.11\,{\rm J}\), with measured areas \(0.180,0.202,0.225\,{\rm J\,s}\). A central difference gives \(T\simeq(0.225-0.180)/0.020=2.25\,{\rm s}\) at the centre. If each area has an independent uncertainty \(0.001\,{\rm J\,s}\), the propagated standard uncertainty of that difference is about \(0.071\,{\rm s}\), before accounting for energy uncertainty and curvature of \(A(E)\).

Decreasing the energy spacing would reduce smooth finite-difference bias but amplify the effect of those same area errors. A local fit over several energies can trade variance against the assumption of a smooth slope. Near a separatrix the actual slope varies rapidly, so a wide polynomial fit may smooth away precisely the period increase being sought. Compare with direct return times at selected energies, or fit the logarithmic form justified by the saddle expansion when the orbit is demonstrably in its asymptotic regime. A measured phase area can be highly accurate while an inferred frequency remains poorly constrained because it is a derivative.

A practical area estimate from ordered camera samples uses the polygon formula \(\tfrac12\sum_i(p_i+p_{i+1})(q_{i+1}-q_i)\), including the return to the initial point with a consistent sign. Very long time sampling can overrepresent the slow turn neighbourhood but need not corrupt a properly oriented geometric line integral. It can make a uniform-clock finite difference of velocity near a turnaround noisy, however. If a dataset includes several cycles with dissipation, do not simply close the final state back to the beginning of the first cycle. Find individual return events, estimate the action per cycle and test whether its change is consistent with measured losses.

Construct the canonical angle on two sides of a turning point

For a bound one-dimensional potential take a reference crossing at the lower turning point and choose the increasing-\(q\) branch. A Hamilton–Jacobi generating integral is

\[W(q,J)=\int_{q_-}^{q}p(q',E(J))\,dq'.\]

Its angle is \(\theta=\partial_JW=(dE/dJ)\int_{q_-}^{q}dq'/\dot q'=\omega t_{\rm flight}\) on that branch. At the upper turning point \(\theta=\pi\) for a time-reversal-invariant kinetic-plus-potential Hamiltonian. On the return branch the same coordinate \(q\) must be assigned the complementary angle \(2\pi-\theta_{\rm outgoing}(q)\), because its momentum has opposite sign. The coordinate alone is insufficient to reconstruct a unique phase.

For a harmonic oscillator beginning at its leftmost position a quarter-amplitude point is crossed at two different times. In coordinates \(q=-A\cos\theta\) take \(q=-A/2\). The increasing branch gives

\[\theta=\pi/3;\]

the returning branch gives \(5\pi/3\). Their velocities have opposite signs. Using the principal arccosine for every time sample forces both branches onto the same interval and makes an apparently discontinuous phase that never completes a cycle. Measuring the sign of velocity or interpolating successive turning events restores the appropriate branch. Near a saddle, tiny velocities make this branch identification particularly sensitive to camera noise.

Once a reliable angle is available, the phase deviation from the uniform prediction \(\theta(t_0)+\omega(t-t_0)\) becomes a useful diagnostic. A static potential with a wrongly calibrated shape can cause a phase error periodic in position even when its return time is fitted correctly. Dissipation usually changes the action as well, while a constant clock error causes a steadily accumulating phase drift. Compare the residual over several cycles at different amplitudes and plot it against reconstructed angle as well as laboratory time. These alternate plots test different physical explanations of the same timing mismatch.

Separate a true separatrix crossing from a change of angle convention

Imagine slowly reducing the pendulum's gravitational strength while a bob initially librates. The separatrix action \(J_s=8I\omega_0/\pi\) decreases with \(\omega_0\). Away from the crossing the orbit approximately maintains its own action, so its energy as a fraction of the instantaneous barrier rises. When the shrinking separatrix action reaches that of the orbit, no closed librating torus of that action remains. The system can enter one of the two circulation branches. Its action convention changes because the relevant closed curve on the cylinder now winds rather than traversing two momentum branches. An apparent numerical factor-of-two jump caused by this convention is distinct from the actual dynamics through the slow saddle neighbourhood.

For an initial libration action equal to \(0.40\) of the original separatrix action at fixed \(I\), a parameter protocol reducing \(\omega_0\) to half its original value leaves \(J/J_s\) about \(0.80\), assuming adiabatic tracking remains valid. Reducing it to \(0.30\) of the initial value would formally give \(J/J_s=1.33\), impossible for a libration. The naive crossing estimate is \(\omega_0=0.40\omega_{0,\rm initial}\), but the timing and selected rotation direction require integrating the actual trajectory near the saddle; the adiabatic argument loses uniform accuracy there. A local measure of \(p\) and unwrapped \(q\) supplies the physical distinction.

A real parameter protocol must specify how gravity strength changes. A pendulum length ramp changes both \(I=ml^2\) and \(\omega_0=\sqrt{g/l}\), so its separatrix area scales as \(I\omega_0=m\sqrt g\,l^{3/2}\). Reducing length to \(0.64\) of its starting value reduces the separatrix action by \(0.64^{3/2}=0.512\), whereas replacing only \(\omega_0\) in a fixed-\(I\) model would predict the opposite trend. A literal change of length also brings radial kinetic energy and actuator work, as derived for the reelled pendulum. Infer work during an adiabatic change from a phase average

Differentiate the action integral at fixed energy with respect to the external parameter \(\lambda\). The energy relation gives \(\partial_\lambda p|_{E,q}=-H_\lambda/H_p\), so \(J_\lambda|_E=-(2\pi)^{-1}\oint H_\lambda\,dt\). Combine this with \(J_E=T/(2\pi)\) and differentiate at fixed \(J\). The result is

\[\partial_\lambda E|_J=\langle\partial_\lambda H\rangle_{\rm time}.\]

A slowly changed parameter therefore does net reversible work at the orbit-averaged rate dictated by its conjugate generalised force. The average must weight time, not distance along the plotted phase curve.

For a harmonic spring with stiffness \(k\), \(H_k=q^2/2\) and \(\langle q^2\rangle=A^2/2=E/k\), giving \(\partial_k E|_J=E/(2k)\). Integrating yields \(E\propto\sqrt k\), consistent with \(J=E/\omega\). A \(4\%\) slow stiffness increase raises energy by about \(1.98\%\) exactly under this law. Changing stiffness at a single turning point would instead add \(4\%\) to the energy for that sudden change. The difference quantifies why observing only identical start and end stiffnesses does not identify the work protocol.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 10. Canonical perturbations, small denominators and resonant capture

A weak periodic force can produce a bounded ripple on most trajectories and a large change on a narrow band of initial conditions. Derive the averaging transformation away from resonance, construct the slow resonant dynamics where that transformation fails, and estimate the physically observable width and time scale of a resonance.

Separate the unperturbed clock from a weak correction

Take an integrable Hamiltonian \(H(I,\theta,t)=H_0(I)+\epsilon H_1(I,\theta,t)\) in action–angle variables. With \(\epsilon=0\), actions are constants and angles advance at \(\boldsymbol\omega(I)=\partial_I H_0\). A perturbation with Fourier component \(h_{\mathbf n}(I)\cos(\mathbf n\cdot\theta-\Omega t)\) drives action changes proportional to its sine. If the phase sweeps quickly through all angles, positive and negative contributions largely cancel. If \(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\omega-\Omega\) is small, the perturbation acts with a coherent sign over a long time. We want a transformation that makes both statements quantitative.

Use a near-identity type-two generating function \(F_2(\theta,J,t)=\theta\cdot J+\epsilon S_1(\theta,J,t)\). Old actions are \(I=J+\epsilon\partial_\theta S_1\), and new angles are \(\psi=\theta+\epsilon\partial_JS_1\). Expand \(H_0(I)=H_0(J)+\epsilon\boldsymbol\omega(J)\cdot\partial_\theta S_1+O(\epsilon^2)\). At first order the new Hamiltonian is \(K=H_0(J)+\epsilon[H_1+\partial_t S_1+\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\partial_\theta S_1]\). Replacing old by new angles inside that bracket produces only higher-order errors. To leave the averaged part \(\langle H_1\rangle\), solve the homological equation

\[(\partial_t+\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\partial_\theta)S_1=-\big(H_1-\langle H_1\rangle\big).\]

For an oscillating cosine with detuning \(\Delta=\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\omega-\Omega\), choose \(S_1=-h_{\mathbf n}\sin(\mathbf n\cdot\theta-\Omega t)/\Delta\). The transformation is useful while the resulting action displacement \(\epsilon|\partial_\theta S_1|\) is small on the relevant action scale and the derivatives entering the angle transformation remain controlled. Small \(\epsilon\) alone is insufficient if \(\Delta\) is even smaller. A singular denominator says that this choice to remove the harmonic is unsuitable, not that the underlying mechanical force becomes infinite.

Obtain a nonlinear frequency shift from the angle average

A free oscillator with mass \(m\), small-amplitude frequency \(\omega_0\), and quartic correction \(\beta q^4/4\) has unperturbed coordinates \(q=\sqrt{2I/(m\omega_0)}\sin\theta\). Substituting gives the correction \(\beta I^2\sin^4\theta/(m^2\omega_0^2)\). Since \(\langle\sin^4\theta\rangle=3/8\), the first-order averaged Hamiltonian is \(K=\omega_0J+3\beta J^2/(8m^2\omega_0^2)\). Its derivative gives

\[\omega=\omega_0+3\beta J/(4m^2\omega_0^2).\]

Expressing \(J\simeq m\omega_0 A^2/2\) in terms of leading oscillation amplitude yields the frequency shift \(3\beta A^2/(8m\omega_0)\). Oscillatory harmonics at \(2\theta,4\theta\) change the waveform but do not contribute directly to this averaged first-order clock.

For \(m=0.20\,{\rm kg}\), \(\omega_0=10.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), \(\beta=80\,{\rm N/m^3}\), and \(A=0.10\,{\rm m}\), the predicted shift is \(0.150\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), about \(1.5\%\). The nonlinear-to-linear force ratio at maximum displacement is \(\beta A^2/(m\omega_0^2)=0.040\), consistent with the expansion. At twice the amplitude the shift quadruples and the expansion parameter grows to \(0.16\). Measuring period over several amplitudes and plotting the frequency against \(A^2\) tests both the slope and the onset of curvature from higher orders. A positive \(\beta\) stiffens the spring; a negative quartic term is only a local approximation unless higher powers restore global confinement.

Solve the slow motion in the resonant frame

Consider a rotor \(H=I^2/(2{\cal I})-\epsilon V\cos(\theta-\Omega t)\), where \({\cal I}\) is moment of inertia and \(\epsilon V>0\) has energy units. Choose \(F_2=(\theta-\Omega t)P\), so \(P=I\),

\[\phi=\theta-\Omega t,\]
\[K=P^2/(2{\cal I})-\Omega P-\epsilon V\cos\phi.\]

Completing the square gives \(K=(P-P_*)^2/(2{\cal I})-\epsilon V\cos\phi+\mathrm{constant}\) with \(P_*={\cal I}\Omega\). This is an exact pendulum in the rotating frame. It retains the resonant cosine instead of attempting to average it away. Phase locking means libration of \(\phi\) around zero; outside its separatrix the relative phase circulates.

The stable fixed point has \(P=P_*,\phi=0\); its small libration frequency is

\[\omega_{\rm lib}=\sqrt{\epsilon V/{\cal I}}.\]

The saddle at \(\phi=\pi\) has energy \(+\epsilon V\) relative to the same constant. At \(\phi=0\) that energy is reached for \(|P-P_*|=2\sqrt{{\cal I}\epsilon V}\), the resonance half-width in action. Its complete width is twice that number. For \({\cal I}=0.050\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(\Omega=8.0\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(\epsilon V=0.0020\,{\rm J}\), the central action is \(0.40\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\); the half-width is \(0.020\,{\rm kg\,m^2/s}\) and small libration period \(31.4\,{\rm s}\). The rotor makes many laboratory turns while its slow relative phase completes one libration.

An initial action inside the quoted half-width is necessary but not sufficient for trapping at every phase. At \(\phi=\pi\), any nonzero \(P-P_*\) is above the saddle energy. Compute the actual rotating-frame energy of the initial state and compare with the saddle. For \(\phi=0,P=0.415\), the kinetic detuning energy is \(0.00225\,{\rm J}\); subtracting \(0.0020\,{\rm J}\) gives \(0.00025\,{\rm J}\), below the saddle. With the same action at \(\phi=\pi\) the value is \(0.00425\,{\rm J}\), above the saddle. Selecting trajectories by mean frequency alone can therefore mix trapped and circulating populations.

Retain a resonant harmonic in a general integrable system

For one action with \(H_0'(I_*)=\Omega\), use the rotating frame and expand \(H_0(I)-\Omega I\) about \(I_*\). The linear term vanishes, leaving \(H_0''(I_*)(I-I_*)^2/2\). Retain the slowly varying resonant cosine and average the rapidly varying harmonics. When \(H_0''\ne0\), this gives the same pendulum normal form with an effective inertia and possibly a reversed sign of the stable phase. The action half-width scales as \(2\sqrt{\epsilon|V/H_0''|}\). Thus a weak perturbation can influence a band of width proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\), parametrically larger than a typical nonresonant action ripple proportional to \(\epsilon\).

For an exactly harmonic oscillator \(H_0=\omega_0 I\) the curvature vanishes. One cannot replace \(H_0''\) with zero in the width formula. An exactly tuned additive drive in a linear oscillator instead produces secular growth until damping, detuning, nonlinearity or a finite drive interval intervenes. In multiple actions, one first transforms to the resonant phase \(\mathbf n\cdot\theta-\Omega t\) and complementary angles, preserving the canonical one-form. Other near-resonances can prevent isolation of a single pendulum. Apparent chaos inferred from a short irregular time trace alone needs additional tests, including resolved phase portraits, convergence under time refinement and identification of competing resonances.

Calculate a phase-locking threshold for a drive that sweeps in frequency

The exact rotating rotor also allows a tractable time-dependent drive frequency. Write the pattern angle as \(\alpha(t)\), with \(\Omega(t)=\dot\alpha\). In its frame \(K=P^2/(2{\cal I})-\Omega(t)P-\epsilon V\cos\phi\). Hamilton's equations give

\[\dot\phi=P/{\cal I}-\Omega(t),\]
\[\dot P=-\epsilon V\sin\phi.\]

Define the detuning momentum \(p=P-{\cal I}\Omega(t)\). Even when \(\dot\phi=0\) at one instant, phase locking throughout a ramp requires \(\dot p=0\) and hence \(\sin\phi_0=-{\cal I}\dot\Omega/(\epsilon V)\). The restoring torque must be sufficient to accelerate the rotor along with the changing pattern.

For constant \(\dot\Omega\) and \(|{\cal I}\dot\Omega|<\epsilon V\), a stable locked phase with \(\cos\phi_0>0\) exists. Perturbing the phase by \(\eta\) gives \(\ddot\eta+(\epsilon V/{\cal I})\cos\phi_0\,\eta=0\), so the libration softens as the required torque approaches its maximum. At threshold the stable and unstable stationary phases meet. Above it no fixed phase can provide the torque needed by the ramp. The mere existence of a stable locked solution still does not imply that an arbitrary initial phase and momentum will be captured in it. In a conservative system initial phase-space location and separatrix crossing are essential.

With the previous \({\cal I}=0.050\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\) and \(\epsilon V=0.0020\,{\rm J}\), the largest trackable constant acceleration in this model is \(0.040\,{\rm rad/s^2}\). At half that value the stable phase lags by \(\pi/6\), and the small relative-phase frequency is

\[0.20\sqrt{\cos(\pi/6)}=0.186\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

Increasing the drive frequency by \(1.0\,{\rm rad/s}\) at this rate takes \(50\,{\rm s}\), only about one and a half libration periods. Adiabatic capture estimates that additionally require many libration periods may therefore be unconvincing even though the elementary torque threshold is satisfied. A measured lag versus sweep rate assesses the static torque condition, while a full phase trajectory tests dynamic capture.

The same equations admit a tilted effective washboard potential in the accelerating frame, \(U_{\rm rel}=-\epsilon V\cos\phi+{\cal I}\dot\Omega\,\phi\), as long as \(\dot\Omega\) is constant. Its potential difference over a whole \(2\pi\) turn is nonzero, so unwrap the relative phase before using its energy. Mapping every phase back to \([-\pi,\pi)\) hides this difference and produces artificial jumps in the inferred energy. In the original variables the physical motor does work. There is no contradiction between a conserved local accelerating-frame energy for a constant ramp and the changing laboratory kinetic energy of a locked rotor.

Derive secular energy exchange in a pair of weakly coupled oscillators

Take two equal masses \(m\) with individual natural frequency \(\omega_0\) and potential coupling \(k_c(q_1-q_2)^2/2\). The normal coordinates \(Q_\pm=(q_1\pm q_2)/\sqrt2\) have frequencies \(\omega_+=\omega_0\),

\[\omega_-=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+2k_c/m}.\]

Release mass one at displacement \(A\) with both velocities and \(q_2\) initially zero. Superposition gives

\[q_1=A[\cos\omega_+ t+\cos\omega_-t]/2,\]
\[q_2=A[\cos\omega_+t-\cos\omega_-t]/2.\]

The slowly varying difference phase transfers oscillation amplitude between the masses over a time much longer than their rapid periods.

For \(\omega_0=10\,{\rm s^{-1}}\) and \(k_c/m=1.0\,{\rm s^{-2}}\), the exact separation is

\[\sqrt{102}-10=0.0995\,{\rm s^{-1}}.\]

The first near-complete exchange occurs at \(t=\pi/|\omega_--\omega_+|\approx31.6\,{\rm s}\). Over a single fast period the displacements can look almost like uncoupled oscillations, despite large later energy redistribution. Perturbing two isolated oscillators while trying to average the coupling over both independent fast angles would discard a term depending on their slow difference phase. Retaining the resonant combination predicts the exchange; diagonalising the linear model supplies a reference solution for that approximation.

Detune the uncoupled springs so their natural frequencies have a separation large compared with \(k_c/(m\omega_0)\). The coupled normal modes then align more closely with the original oscillators, and the energy exchange cannot reach its former depth. In a nonlinear problem the individual frequencies also depend on action, so the energy transfer can detune itself. To interpret an observed beating envelope, measure both normal frequencies and relative phases. An apparent loss of amplitude in oscillator one can be energy stored in oscillator two, distinguishable from damping through recovery at later times and a balance of the total Hamiltonian.

Estimate the accuracy of averaging over a finite observation time

For a nonresonant perturbation with initially slow action change, one can integrate the first-order drive along the unperturbed trajectory over the actual observation window instead of assuming an infinite-time average. For \(H_1=A\cos(\theta-\Omega t)\), \(\dot I=A\sin(\Delta t+\phi_0)\) to leading order with \(\Delta=\omega_0-\Omega\). Thus \(\Delta I(T)=A[\cos\phi_0-\cos(\Delta T+\phi_0)]/\Delta\). For \(\Delta\ne0\) its absolute value remains at most \(2|A/\Delta|\), and its largest possible value over initial phase is \(2|A\sin(\Delta T/2)/\Delta|\). For \(\Delta\to0\) the phase-dependent result tends to \(AT\sin\phi_0\). The finite-time expression is regular right at resonance and shows exactly when a secular term emerges.

For \(A=0.001\,{\rm J}\), \(T=10\,{\rm s}\) and \(\Delta=0.20\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), the maximum-amplitude change in action over initial phase is \(0.00841\,{\rm J\,s}\). At zero detuning the leading largest change is \(0.0100\,{\rm J\,s}\), only modestly larger because this observation window resolves a frequency band of order \(1/T\). For \(\Delta=2\pi/T\) the change after exactly \(T\) vanishes, although the action swung during the interval. A final-minus-initial measurement alone can mistake a completed nonresonant excursion for an entirely unperturbed trajectory. Recording at an additional end time tests that cancellation.

At leading order a force of finite duration produces this sine-over-detuning envelope even in a linear system. It is an observation-time bandwidth, distinct from the dynamical resonance width proportional to \(\sqrt{\epsilon}\) in the nonlinear rotor. Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 11. Action density, stress waves and the boundary that reflects them

A chain of coupled masses becomes an elastic field when the wavelength is long compared with the spacing. Derive the field equation and its boundary traction from the same variation, follow energy and stress across a junction, and decide whether a measured pulse is limited by material waves or by numerical dispersion.

Pass from a discrete rod to an action density

Let equal masses of spacing \(a\) move longitudinally by \(u_j(t)\), with nearest-neighbour spring constant \(k_s\) and mass \(m_s\) per site. Their Lagrangian is \(\sum_j[m_s\dot u_j^2/2-k_s(u_{j+1}-u_j)^2/2]\). For variations smooth on the spacing, \((u_{j+1}-u_j)/a\to u_x\) and \(\sum_j a\,f_j\to\int f(x)\,dx\). Hold the line density \(\rho A=m_s/a\) and axial rigidity \(EA=k_s a\) fixed. The continuum Lagrangian density per unit reference length is

\[{\cal L}=\rho A\,u_t^2/2-EA\,u_x^2/2,\]

with \(A\) area, \(\rho\) volume density and \(E\) Young modulus. Both terms have units of joules per metre. This is a slender-rod model with small strain, primarily uniaxial stress and wavelengths long compared with the cross-sectional dimensions.

Vary the field in the action over \([0,L]\times[t_1,t_2]\). Integration of \(\rho A\,u_t\delta u_t\) by parts in time, with fixed endpoint fields, produces \(-\rho A\,u_{tt}\delta u\). Integration of \(-EAu_x\delta u_x\) by parts in space produces \(+\partial_x(EAu_x)\delta u\) and a remaining term \(-[EAu_x\delta u]_0^L\). Thus the interior equation is \(\rho A u_{tt}=\partial_x(EAu_x)\), and the end force conjugate to displacement is axial force \(N=EAu_x\). Adding external work \(F_L(t)\delta u(L)\) at the right face yields

\[N(L)=F_L.\]

A prescribed displacement instead imposes \(\delta u(L)=0\), leaving its reaction as an output. Keeping this boundary term prevents prescribing displacement and independent traction simultaneously at the same end of a second-order rod.

Determine the energy flux and characteristic impedance

Assume time-independent coefficients. Multiply the field equation by \(u_t\) and rearrange to obtain

\[\partial_t[\rho A u_t^2/2+EAu_x^2/2]+\partial_x[-EAu_xu_t]=0.\]

The energy flux is \(-Nu_t\), with positive sign denoting energy moving towards increasing \(x\). The minus sign follows the convention of positive tensile \(N\): positive mechanical power entering through a right face is \(Nu_t\), which is inward with respect to the rod on its left. This identity controls energy transport across both smooth material changes and sharp joints.

For a homogeneous right-moving wave \(u=F(x-ct)\), \(c=\sqrt{E/\rho}\), particle velocity \(v=u_t=-cF'\) and axial force \(N=EAF'=-Zv\), where \(Z=\rho A c=EA/c\). A left-moving wave has \(N=+Zv\). The impedance is force divided by particle velocity and has units \({\rm kg/s}\). If one instead uses stress, impedance is \(\rho c\) per unit area. Doubling the area of the same material keeps wave speed unchanged but doubles the force–velocity impedance. A transit-time measurement and a reflection measurement therefore provide independent material or geometry information.

For a rod with \(E=70\,{\rm GPa}\), \(\rho=2700\,{\rm kg/m^3}\) and \(A=1.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\), \(c\approx5092\,{\rm m/s}\) and \(Z\approx1375\,{\rm kg/s}\). A right-going compressive pulse with particle speed \(0.020\,{\rm m/s}\) carries axial force \(-27.5\,{\rm N}\), compressive stress \(0.275\,{\rm MPa}\), and instantaneous power \(Zv^2=0.550\,{\rm W}\) across a section. A rectangular approximation lasting \(100\,\mu{\rm s}\) carries about \(55\,\mu{\rm J}\) and occupies \(0.509\,{\rm m}\). The pulse speed is kilometres per second; the material particles move only millimetres per second. Treating these as the same velocity would spoil force, impulse and energy estimates.

Match a bonded interface and distinguish amplitude from power

At a perfect massless bond displacement and axial force are continuous. For an incident right-going velocity amplitude \(v_i\) in rod 1, let \(v_r\) be reflected left-going velocity and \(v_t\) transmitted right-going velocity. Velocity continuity gives

\[v_i+v_r=v_t.\]

Force continuity gives

\[-Z_1v_i+Z_1v_r=-Z_2v_t.\]

Thus

\[r_v=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[t_v=\frac{2Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[{\cal R}=r_v^2,\]
\[{\cal T}=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1}t_v^2=\frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1+Z_2)^2}.\]

The power fractions add to one for real positive impedances. The reflected force coefficient has opposite sign to the reflected velocity coefficient because the wave direction reverses. At a free end \(Z_2\to0\), reflected velocity equals incident velocity, so end velocity doubles while total force vanishes. At a rigid end \(Z_2\to\infty\), velocity reverses and force doubles. The sign a strain gauge reports can therefore differ from that of a velocity-sensitive optical measurement at the same reflection.

A step from the worked rod into one of the same material with twice the cross-sectional area gives \(Z_2=2Z_1\), \(r_v=-1/3\), \(t_v=2/3\), and power fractions \(1/9\) and \(8/9\). A reduced transmitted velocity amplitude does not imply that most energy is reflected. If the interface includes a concentrated mass \(M_b\), force jumps instead obey \(N_2-N_1=M_b\dot v_b\) with signs determined by forces exerted on that mass from either side. At high frequency the reaction of that mass can mimic a much stiffer termination. Frequency-dependent reflection is then an expected boundary effect, rather than evidence that the bulk modulus varies with frequency.

Connect continuum modes to their lattice predecessor

For a periodic chain the exact discrete equation is

\[m_s\ddot u_j=k_s(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}).\]

Substitute \(u_j=U e^{i(kja-\omega t)}\) to obtain

\[\omega=2\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.\]

In the first positive Brillouin zone, \(\omega/(ck)=\sin(ka/2)/(ka/2)\) and group velocity \(d\omega/dk=c\cos(ka/2)\), where \(c=a\sqrt{k_s/m_s}\). The continuum rod retains only the leading low-\(ka\) term. A wave with eight sites per wavelength has phase speed about \(0.9745c\) and group speed \(0.9239c\). A narrowband packet's peak can arrive later than the continuum prediction even when a single crest is timed fairly accurately.

A finite-difference code approximating the continuum rod can produce the same spatial dispersion with its mesh spacing in place of a physical lattice spacing. Distinguish them experimentally or numerically by changing the computational mesh while holding the physical structure fixed. Lattice effects persist as the numerical grid is refined; artificial spatial dispersion diminishes. A separate time-step study is needed if a time integrator also disperses the wave. Comparing both peak arrival and phase at two frequencies tests more of the dynamics than fitting a single transit time.

Derive transverse beam waves from curvature energy

A slender beam bending laterally is governed by a different elastic energy from the axial rod. Let \(w(x,t)\) denote small transverse deflection of its neutral axis. Under the Euler–Bernoulli assumption that cross sections remain plane and normal to that axis, the axial strain a distance \(z\) from the neutral axis is approximately \(-z w_{xx}\). Integrating the local elastic energy \(E\epsilon_{xx}^2/2\) across the section gives \(EI_Aw_{xx}^2/2\) per unit length, where \(I_A=\int z^2\,dA\) is a geometrical second moment of area, with units \({\rm m^4}\). It is distinct from a mass moment of inertia. The kinetic contribution uses line density \(\rho A\) as before.

Varying \({\cal L}=\rho A w_t^2/2-EI_Aw_{xx}^2/2\) requires integrating twice by parts in space. With constant coefficients the interior variation gives

\[\rho A w_{tt}+EI_A w_{xxxx}=0.\]

The boundary terms are \(-[EI_Aw_{xx}\delta w_x]+[(EI_Aw_{xx})_x\delta w]\). Thus slope variations pair with bending moments and displacement variations with shear forces, with signs set by the chosen face orientation. A clamped end prescribes both deflection and slope; a free end sets moment and shear to zero. A simply supported end prescribes deflection and zero bending moment. Using only one boundary condition per end, as for the axial rod, leaves the fourth-order beam problem underdetermined.

A sinusoid \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\) gives

\[\omega=\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}\,k^2.\]

Phase speed grows proportionally to wavenumber and group speed is twice phase speed in this model. A narrow transverse pulse in a long beam consequently spreads even in a perfectly homogeneous non-dissipative continuum. By contrast the axial rod was nondispersive in its long-wavelength continuum approximation. At high enough frequency the simple bending model omits rotary inertia, shear deformation and eventually three-dimensional elastic behaviour. Extrapolating its unbounded speed to arbitrary \(k\) would use the slender, low-frequency approximation far beyond its derivation.

For a simply supported beam with length \(L\), the sine modes satisfy both end conditions and give

\[\omega_n=(n\pi/L)^2\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}.\]

For a rectangular section of width \(w_b\) and thickness \(d\) in the bending direction, \(I_A=w_bd^3/12\) and \(A=w_bd\). Doubling thickness doubles all the natural angular frequencies at fixed \(L,E,\rho\), whereas doubling width alone leaves the ideal frequencies unchanged. The stiffness increases and so does the inertia. A beam with \(L=0.50\,{\rm m}\), \(d=5.0\,{\rm mm}\) and the earlier aluminium properties has \(\sqrt{EI_A/(\rho A)}\approx7.35\,{\rm m^2/s}\), giving fundamental \(46.2\,{\rm Hz}\). The second bending mode is near four times that frequency, a clear difference from the approximately harmonic axial mode sequence.

Match a force pulse to a support spring and dashpot

A right termination exerts force on the rod through a dashpot with mechanical impedance \(Z_L\). For a real positive dashpot impedance the reflected velocity fraction is \(r=(Z-Z_L)/(Z+Z_L)\). Taking \(Z_L=Z\) gives zero reflection at the termination even though energy leaves a finite rod: the dashpot dissipates the arriving power. With \(Z_L=3Z\) one obtains a reflected velocity of \(-1/2\) of the incident value, a reflected energy fraction one quarter and absorbed power fraction three quarters. A free or rigid end also reflects energy, but it absorbs no net power in the ideal limit. A measured weak reflection alone is therefore not proof of weak damping inside the rod.

For a pure end spring of stiffness \(k_L\) attached to a rigid support, the velocity impedance is \(Z_L=k_L/(i\omega)\) under \(e^{i\omega t}\). It is imaginary, so the reflection coefficient has unit magnitude at every frequency and a frequency-dependent phase. At low frequency the spring impedance is large and the end looks nearly fixed. At high frequency its impedance is small and the end looks nearly free within the lumped approximation. An impulsive signal contains many frequencies and can return with a changed waveform even without any transmitted or dissipated energy. A support combining a resistor-like dashpot and a spring gives both amplitude loss and phase variation.

A measurement with strain gauges at two positions can separate forward and backward waves if their path delays are resolved. In a narrow frequency band the two records are linear combinations of advancing and returning phase factors. If the gauge spacing corresponds to an integer number of half wavelengths, these combinations become linearly dependent and the separation is ill-conditioned. Change the spacing, use a broadband pulse, or combine a velocity measurement with a strain measurement at one station so that the two characteristic directions enter with opposite signs. The relation \(v=(v_++v_-)\), \(N=Z(-v_++v_-)\) then gives a direct local decomposition.

Derive sound propagation from mass and momentum conservation

The displacement-action argument has a fluid analogue. Take an initially uniform fluid of density \(\rho_0\) and pressure \(p_0\), moving little from equilibrium in one spatial dimension. Write small perturbations \(\rho',p',v\). Linearised mass conservation is

\[\partial_t\rho'+\rho_0\partial_x v=0.\]

Momentum balance for a short fluid parcel is \(\rho_0\partial_tv=-\partial_xp'\). If compression over an acoustic cycle is approximately isentropic, \(p'=c_s^2\rho'\) with \(c_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\). Differentiate the first equation in time, eliminate the velocity derivative with momentum balance, and obtain \(\partial_t^2p'=c_s^2\partial_x^2p'\).

The thermodynamic derivative matters because pressure can respond differently when compression is slow enough for heat exchange. For a perfect gas with ratio of specific heats \(\gamma\), isentropic perturbations give

\[c_s^2=\gamma p_0/\rho_0.\]

Taking an isothermal derivative would give \(p_0/\rho_0\) instead. For \(p_0=101\,{\rm kPa}\), \(\rho_0=1.20\,{\rm kg/m^3}\), and \(\gamma=1.40\), the isentropic speed is about \(343\,{\rm m/s}\), while the isothermal calculation gives \(290\,{\rm m/s}\). A measured transit time can discriminate between these constitutive conditions. Wall heat exchange in a very narrow tube can alter which approximation is appropriate over a chosen frequency band.

For a right-moving sound wave \(p'=\rho_0c_s v\), with the pressure positive in compression. This differs in sign from the tensile-positive axial force used for the rod, so match physical force conventions before borrowing formulas across the two examples. The acoustic power per area is \(p'v\). For sinusoidal pressure with rms amplitude \(1.0\,{\rm Pa}\) in the stated gas, the mean intensity is

\[p_{\rm rms}^2/(\rho_0 c_s)=2.43\times10^{-3}\,{\rm W/m^2}.\]

A peak amplitude of \(1.0\,{\rm Pa}\) would yield half this number. Writing rms or peak explicitly prevents a routine factor-of-two error when comparing a microphone measurement with an energy flux.

Now examine a thick elastic solid under a narrow compressional disturbance with wavelengths short compared with its transverse dimensions. Lateral deformation can be constrained by surrounding material. Derive orthogonality when a rod changes material along its length

For a rod with positive varying density \(\rho(x)\), area \(A(x)\) and modulus \(E(x)\), a separated free oscillation \(u(x,t)=\phi(x)\cos\omega t\) satisfies

\[-[EA\phi']'=\omega^2\rho A\phi.\]

Suppose two modes \(\phi_n,\phi_m\) satisfy the same homogeneous fixed or free boundary conditions and the correct continuity of displacement and axial force across every perfectly bonded interface. Multiply the first equation by \(\phi_m\), the second by \(\phi_n\), subtract and integrate. Interior terms produced by integration by parts cancel pairwise, including the interface flux terms, and the endpoint terms vanish. Thus

\[(\omega_n^2-\omega_m^2)\int\rho A\phi_n\phi_m\,dx=0.\]

Mode shapes with distinct frequencies are orthogonal under the mass-weighted inner product, not generally under a simple average of displacements measured at uniformly spaced stations. An initial velocity distribution \(v_0(x)\) projects onto mode \(n\) with coefficient \(\int\rho A v_0\phi_n\,dx/\int\rho A\phi_n^2\,dx\). In a rod with a heavy section, the same imposed velocity there stores more energy and carries more modal weight. An experiment interpreting raw camera amplitudes without line-density weighting can misassign energy among modes. Normalising each measured mode shape to unit maximum does not fix the mass weighting needed for energies.

Multiplying one eigenvalue equation by its own shape gives

\[\omega_n^2=\int EA(\phi_n')^2dx/\int\rho A\phi_n^2dx.\]

For an admissible trial function satisfying prescribed displacement conditions, the same quotient bounds the fundamental squared frequency from above when rigid translations are excluded. Consider a uniform fixed–free rod and trial \(\phi=x/L\). The numerator is \(EA/L\) and denominator \(\rho A L/3\), giving \(\omega_{\rm trial}=\sqrt3\,c/L\). The exact fundamental is \(\pi c/(2L)\), so this one-parameter shape overestimates frequency by about \(10.3\%\). It need not satisfy the free-end traction condition itself because that is a natural condition of the variational minimiser; it does need to satisfy the essential fixed-end displacement.

For a free–free rod the constant displacement mode has zero frequency. Using an arbitrary positive trial function without removing its mass-weighted mean can drive a quotient towards zero by adding a large constant. To estimate the first deforming mode impose orthogonality to the rigid translation, \(\int\rho A\phi\,dx=0\). This has an immediate mechanical interpretation: the deformation mode should not translate the centre of mass of an isolated rod. The null mode and its mass-weighted constraint reappear in numerical eigenproblems built from discretised stiffness and mass matrices.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 12. Relativistic collisions, centre-of-momentum energy and thresholds

Two particles with the same total laboratory energy can have very different energy available for changing their internal state. Determine the energy and motion of the centre-of-momentum system, reconstruct two-body elastic scattering, and follow how a detector threshold transforms between frames.

Form the conserved total four-momentum

For a particle with rest mass \(m\), energy \(E\) and momentum \(\mathbf p\), write \(p^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)\) with metric signature \(+---\). The on-shell condition \(E^2-c^2p^2=m^2c^4\) holds for each freely propagating particle. In an isolated collision sum the incoming four-momenta and equate them to the outgoing sum. A frame-independent measure of the total is \(s=(E_1+E_2)^2-c^2|\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2|^2\), defined here with units of energy squared. Some books assign momentum-squared units to \(s\) instead; stating this convention fixes all factors of \(c\). The centre-of-momentum energy is \(\sqrt s\).

A boost along the total momentum brings it to zero if the total four-vector is timelike. Its velocity is \(\mathbf V_{\rm cm}=c^2(\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2)/(E_1+E_2)\). Individual particles can approach the speed of light while this frame is slow or even stationary in the laboratory when their momenta cancel. A pair of perfectly parallel massless rays gives a null total and has no rest frame. For massive particles the positive total rest energy generally ensures a timelike total. Knowing \(\sqrt s\) determines the internal energy available, while \(\mathbf V_{\rm cm}\) determines how the final directions and energies are seen by a laboratory camera.

Expand the square using the two on-shell conditions to obtain

\[s=m_1^2c^4+m_2^2c^4+2(E_1E_2-c^2\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2).\]

At fixed energies the dot product shows why head-on motion gives more centre-of-momentum energy than parallel motion. This relation also applies when one participant is a photon by setting its rest mass to zero. A familiar Newtonian analogy separates centre-of-mass translational kinetic energy from relative kinetic energy. In relativity the exact separation is carried by the invariant mass of the total four-vector and the boost of that total.

Compare a fixed target with two opposing beams

Let both incoming particles have rest energy \(m c^2=\mathcal M\). With one initially stationary and the other carrying kinetic energy \(K\), the invariant is \(s=4\mathcal M^2+2\mathcal M K\). By contrast, two identical beams carrying kinetic energy \(K_b\) each and meeting with opposite momenta give

\[\sqrt s=2(\mathcal M+K_b).\]

If a specified final channel has minimum sum of rest energies \(\mathcal W\), the massive products at threshold have zero relative momentum in their common centre-of-momentum frame. The fixed-target threshold is \(K_{\rm th}=(\mathcal W^2-4\mathcal M^2)/(2\mathcal M)\) when positive. A symmetric collider needs \(K_b=(\mathcal W-2\mathcal M)/2\) in each beam. The channel must also satisfy other conservation laws; these equations give kinematic conditions.

For an abstract excitation channel with \(\mathcal M=1.00\,{\rm GeV}\) and \(\mathcal W=3.00\,{\rm GeV}\), the fixed-target projectile needs \(K=2.50\,{\rm GeV}\), whereas each symmetric beam needs \(0.50\,{\rm GeV}\). At the fixed-target threshold the total lab energy is \(4.50\,{\rm GeV}\) and the total momentum times \(c\) is

\[\sqrt{3.50^2-1.00^2}=3.354\,{\rm GeV}.\]

Hence \(V_{\rm cm}/c=0.7454\) and \(\gamma_{\rm cm}=1.50\). The final system has centre-of-momentum energy \(3.00\,{\rm GeV}\) but still carries substantial bulk motion in the laboratory. Equating an energy deficit directly to projectile kinetic energy would ignore its conserved momentum.

Solve any two-body final state in its momentum frame

Let the two final particles have rest energies \(\mathcal M_3,\mathcal M_4\). In the centre-of-momentum frame their spatial momenta have equal magnitudes \(p_*\) and opposite directions. Energy conservation gives

\[E_3^*+E_4^*=\sqrt s.\]

Subtract their squared mass-shell relations to obtain

\[(E_3^*)^2-(E_4^*)^2=\mathcal M_3^2-\mathcal M_4^2.\]

Factoring this difference gives

\[E_3^*=\frac{s+\mathcal M_3^2-\mathcal M_4^2}{2\sqrt s},\]
\[cp_*=\frac{\sqrt{[s-(\mathcal M_3+\mathcal M_4)^2][s-(\mathcal M_3-\mathcal M_4)^2]}}{2\sqrt s}.\]

The square root exposes the threshold and is symmetric under interchanging the products. When the masses are equal, each receives exactly \(\sqrt s/2\). When they differ, equal momenta do not mean equal energies or velocities. At threshold the momentum vanishes, so an angular distribution there becomes a delicate concept; a boost can send both products in the same lab direction even when they move apart appreciably above threshold.

For \(\sqrt s=5.0\,{\rm GeV}\), \(\mathcal M_3=2.0\,{\rm GeV}\) and \(\mathcal M_4=1.0\,{\rm GeV}\), the energies are \(2.80\) and \(2.20\,{\rm GeV}\). Each gives

\[cp_*=\sqrt{2.8^2-2.0^2}=\sqrt{2.2^2-1.0^2}=1.960\,{\rm GeV}.\]

Their speed ratios \(cp_*/E^*\) are \(0.700\) and \(0.891\). Summing these two speeds would exceed \(c\) as a numerical closing rate in that frame, but the speed of either particle measured from the other's rest frame follows relativistic velocity addition and remains below \(c\).

Transform a centre-of-momentum angular pattern to a detector

Choose the laboratory boost along \(z\) with parameters \(\beta,\gamma\). A product emitted at polar angle \(\theta_*\) has \(p_{z,*}=p_*\cos\theta_*\),

\[p_{\perp,*}=p_*\sin\theta_*.\]

A Lorentz boost to the laboratory gives \(E=\gamma(E^*+\beta c p_*\cos\theta_*)\),

\[cp_z=\gamma(cp_*\cos\theta_*+\beta E^*),\]
\[p_\perp=p_{\perp,*}.\]

Use a quadrant-aware angle from the resulting longitudinal and transverse components. A tangent formula alone loses the distinction between a particle moving backward and one moving nearly forward when its denominator changes sign.

For particle 3 in the preceding example take \(\beta=0.60,\gamma=1.25\) and emission at \(90^\circ\) in the centre-of-momentum frame. The laboratory energy is \(3.50\,{\rm GeV}\), axial momentum times \(c\) is \(2.10\,{\rm GeV}\), and transverse momentum times \(c\) remains \(1.960\,{\rm GeV}\). The polar angle is \(43.0^\circ\). A detector that accepts only angles above \(60^\circ\) would miss this event despite its centre-of-momentum emission being transverse. To convert an angular density, transform the solid-angle Jacobian or count the preimage of each finite acceptance bin; matching angles by name between frames is insufficient.

If the detector instead accepts a product only above lab energy \(E_{\min}\), the inequality becomes \(\cos\theta_*\ge[E_{\min}/\gamma-E^*]/(\beta cp_*)\) for positive \(\beta,p_*\). With \(E_{\min}=4.0\,{\rm GeV}\) this gives \(\cos\theta_*\ge0.340\). For an isotropic distribution in its own momentum frame that detector accepts fraction \((1-0.340)/2\simeq0.330\), assuming full azimuth and no other losses. At \(\beta=0\) energy selection becomes independent of angle, so the divided expression must be replaced by its original inequality.

Follow an elastic collision of equal masses all the way to the laboratory

In a Newtonian elastic collision of equal masses with one initially at rest, the two nonzero outgoing velocities are perpendicular. At relativistic energies that rule changes even when the collision remains exactly elastic. For equal final masses in the centre-of-momentum frame the two outgoing energies and momentum magnitudes are equal. In the laboratory where target 2 was initially at rest, the boost speed of the centre equals the speed of each particle in the momentum frame: boosting initially stationary particle 2 into that frame supplies the equality. Write this common dimensionless speed as \(\beta\), and let particle 1 scatter through angle \(\theta_*\) to the positive boost axis. Particle 2 leaves with opposite momentum.

With \(p_*c=\beta E^*\), the laboratory longitudinal components are \(p_{1z}=\gamma p_*(1+\cos\theta_*)\) and \(p_{2z}=\gamma p_*(1-\cos\theta_*)\), and their transverse components are opposite with magnitude \(p_*\sin\theta_*\). The two lab polar angles therefore obey \(\tan\theta_1=\tan(\theta_*/2)/\gamma\) and \(\tan\theta_2=\cot(\theta_*/2)/\gamma\). Multiplying gives

\[\tan\theta_1\tan\theta_2=1/\gamma^2.\]

At low speed \(\gamma\to1\), so \(\theta_1+\theta_2\to90^\circ\). For finite boost the opening angle becomes smaller. Endpoint cases where one outgoing particle is stationary leave its direction undefined and must be handled separately.

Take rest energy \(\mathcal M\) and projectile kinetic energy \(K=2\mathcal M\) against an identical stationary target. The invariant energy is \(\sqrt8\mathcal M\) and the laboratory total energy is \(4\mathcal M\), giving \(\gamma_{\rm cm}=\sqrt2\). For a transverse centre-of-momentum collision, both products have laboratory angle \(\arctan(1/\sqrt2)=35.26^\circ\) on opposite sides of the beam, so the opening is \(70.53^\circ\). Both laboratory energies are \(2\mathcal M\); their kinetic energies are each \(\mathcal M\), sharing the incident kinetic energy equally. Angular data and energy partition together offer an overdetermined test of elastic equal-mass kinematics.

For a general \(\theta_*\), the lab kinetic-energy fractions are \([1+\cos\theta_*]/2\) and \([1-\cos\theta_*]/2\) of the incoming kinetic energy in this equal-mass geometry. This follows by inserting \(E^*=\gamma_{\rm cm}\mathcal M\) in the boost and using \(K=2\mathcal M(\gamma_{\rm cm}^2-1)\). Equal masses do not imply equal final energies in the laboratory; that occurs for the transverse centre-of-momentum geometry. A detector threshold of \(0.25K\) on both products selects \(|\cos\theta_*|\le0.50\). For an isotropic momentum-frame distribution that condition accepts half the events irrespective of the azimuth, provided the lab apparatus covers both directions.

Compare two methods of estimating missing invariant mass

Suppose a known laboratory total has energy \(5.0\,{\rm GeV}\) and axial momentum times \(c\) of \(3.0\,{\rm GeV}\). One registered product has energy \(2.0\,{\rm GeV}\), longitudinal momentum times \(c\) of \(1.0\,{\rm GeV}\), and transverse momentum times \(c\) of \(0.60\,{\rm GeV}\). Subtracting vectors gives missing energy \(3.0\,{\rm GeV}\), axial momentum times \(c\) of \(2.0\,{\rm GeV}\), and transverse momentum times \(c\) of \(-0.60\,{\rm GeV}\). Its invariant mass energy is

\[\sqrt{9-4-0.36}=2.154\,{\rm GeV}.\]

Dropping the transverse measurement would give \(2.236\,{\rm GeV}\), an error of about \(3.8\%\). Subtracting reported kinetic energies instead of total energies would introduce an uncontrolled rest-energy offset.

The same result can be reconstructed in the initial centre-of-momentum frame. The total invariant energy is \(4.0\,{\rm GeV}\), so the laboratory boost is

\[\beta=0.60,\gamma=1.25.\]

Transforming the observed product back gives \(E^*=1.25(2.0-0.60\times1.0)=1.75\,{\rm GeV}\) and \(cp_z^*=1.25(1.0-0.60\times2.0)=-0.25\,{\rm GeV}\), while its transverse component stays \(0.60\,{\rm GeV}\). Missing energy is \(2.25\,{\rm GeV}\), and missing squared momentum times \(c^2\) is \(0.25^2+0.60^2\). The invariant is again \(4.64\,{\rm GeV^2}\). Repeating the computation in two frames checks signs and coordinate components without adding independent experimental information.

To propagate uncertainty use the squared quantity before taking its square root. With \(c=1\) for numerical units, perturbations satisfy

\[\delta M_{\rm miss}^2=2E_{\rm miss}\delta E_{\rm miss}-2\mathbf p_{\rm miss}\cdot\delta\mathbf p_{\rm miss}.\]

If the energy and momentum of one detected particle come from the same calibrated tracking measurement they may be strongly correlated. Treating those uncertainties as independent can seriously misestimate the spread, especially near a missing-mass threshold where \(M^2\) is close to zero. Retain the full covariance and report a distribution in mass squared near that boundary. Mapping negative noisy values to zero before averaging biases the result upward and erases information about detector resolution.

Recover a recoil energy from photon scattering

Consider an incoming photon of energy \(E\) colliding with a free particle of rest energy \(\mathcal M\) initially at rest. The outgoing photon has energy \(E'\) and turns through angle \(\theta\) in the original laboratory. Four-momentum conservation gives the final target energy \(\mathcal M+E-E'\) and its squared momentum times \(c^2\) is \(E^2+E'^2-2EE'\cos\theta\). Imposing the final target mass-shell condition and cancelling equal squares yields

\[\mathcal M(E-E')=EE'(1-\cos\theta).\]

Therefore

\[E'=\frac{E}{1+(E/\mathcal M)(1-\cos\theta)} .\]

The scattered energy equals the incident energy for perfect forward scattering, whereas backscattering transfers most energy at fixed incident \(E\). Rearrangement gives

\[1/E'-1/E=(1-\cos\theta)/\mathcal M.\]

Multiplying by \(hc\) gives the usual wavelength shift with scale \(h/(mc)\). The argument uses only four-momentum conservation and the initial target mass to determine kinematics. It does not give the probability of any scattering angle, which requires an interaction model. Bound target motion or initially moving scatterers change the measured line shape even when the elementary free-particle kinematics is understood.

Take \(E=\mathcal M/2\). At \(\theta=90^\circ\) the outgoing photon energy is \(\mathcal M/3\); at \(\theta=\pi\) it is \(\mathcal M/4\). In the right-angle event the target kinetic energy is

\[E-E'=\mathcal M/6.\]

Its final momentum magnitude times \(c\) can be computed from its energy as \(\sqrt{(7\mathcal M/6)^2-\mathcal M^2}=\sqrt{13}\mathcal M/6\), or from the photon momentum triangle as \(\sqrt{(\mathcal M/2)^2+(\mathcal M/3)^2}\). These two values agree. A detector that measures only target kinetic energy can infer the photon angle if the initial photon energy was known and the free stationary target assumptions hold.

The maximum possible target kinetic energy is \(2E^2/(\mathcal M+2E)\). For \(E\ll\mathcal M\) this is approximately \(2E^2/\mathcal M\), much smaller than the incident energy. At \(E\gg\mathcal M\) the recoiling target can receive most of the photon energy. Scanning incident energy therefore changes both the absolute and fractional accessible recoil range. If an observed event falls beyond that kinematic edge, changing only the angular cross section cannot explain it; examine energy calibration, initial target motion, coincidence assignment or overlapping events.

A two-body decay at rest into two photons yields equal energies and an opening angle of \(180^\circ\). In any frame its invariant parent rest energy is

\[M c^2=\sqrt{2E_1E_2(1-\cos\alpha)}.\]

This formula remains valid when the parent moves and the detected photon energies differ. A common multiplicative energy calibration factor multiplies the reconstructed mass by that factor. An angular bias also changes the inferred mass, with fractional derivative \(\tfrac12\sin\alpha/(1-\cos\alpha)=\tfrac12\cot(\alpha/2)\). Nearly collinear photon pairs are therefore particularly sensitive to angular uncertainties, even when their individual energy measurements are accurate.

For \(E_1=2.0\,{\rm GeV}\), \(E_2=1.0\,{\rm GeV}\), \(\alpha=60^\circ\), the reconstructed rest energy is \(\sqrt2\,{\rm GeV}\), although the lab energy sum is \(3.0\,{\rm GeV}\). A calibration performed only on back-to-back pairs is weakly sensitive to opening-angle errors to first order.

Invariant reconstruction is especially valuable when different observers disagree on which laboratory particle appears fast or forward. Agreement requires the entire event be expressed in one frame, with coincident incoming and outgoing measurements and a consistent sign convention for momentum. A Lorentz transformation cannot repair a mispaired event. Estimate statistical backgrounds and detector resolution independently before interpreting a narrow or displaced mass feature. The invariant provides a kinematic check; it gains physical meaning from the measurement model used to construct it.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 13. Which path is shortest across the surface of a cone?

A point can move along a conical surface while its azimuth and height both change. Unroll the geometry and predict the shortest path between two marked points. The measurable minimum radius distinguishes the geodesic from a line drawn along a horizontal circle.

Turn the constraint into a distance measurement

Let \(r\) denote the slant distance from the apex and \(\alpha\) the angle between the cone's side and its central axis. A point at \(r\) lies on a horizontal circle of radius \(r\sin\alpha\). Small radial and azimuthal displacements on the surface meet at right angles. Their squared distance adds to \(ds^2=dr^2+r^2\sin^2\alpha\,d\phi^2\). The change of variable \(\psi=\phi\sin\alpha\) turns this into the polar metric of a plane. Cutting the cone along a generator and flattening it produces a sector of opening angle \(2\pi\sin\alpha\).

Without a tangential force a particle follows a straight line in the unrolled sector. Its coordinates need not evolve linearly in the original azimuth. For two positions at equal slant distance \(R\) separated by physical azimuth \(\Delta\phi\) of magnitude at most \(\pi\), the shortest branch uses a straight chord spanning \(\Delta\psi=\sin\alpha\,\Delta\phi\). Bisecting the isosceles triangle gives

\[L=2R\sin(|\Delta\psi|/2),\]
\[r_{\min}=R\cos(\Delta\psi/2).\]

The other copies of the endpoint correspond to winding around the cone on different branches. The chord formula uses a branch reached without passing through the apex. When the physical endpoints are diametrically opposite, clockwise and anticlockwise branches have equal shortest length. A laboratory string stretched across the interior would use the three-dimensional chord, which need not stay on the surface.

Detect the departure from the horizontal ring

For a cone of half-angle 30 degrees, two marks 120 degrees apart at slant distance 5 cm appear only 60 degrees apart in the plane. Their shortest surface path is 5.00 cm long and passes through radius 4.33 cm. Staying on the horizontal ring would require \(R\Delta\psi=5.24\) cm. The straight three-dimensional chord between the same marks is only 4.33 cm long but cuts through the cone. Comparing those three lengths distinguishes a free-space displacement from two distinct constrained motions.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 14. Motion in a medium and momentum carried across a boundary

Two beads can approach exactly the same terminal speed with recognisably different histories. A sand cart can slow down while the total horizontal momentum of sand and cart stays fixed. Both observations begin by specifying which material the equation of motion follows.

Infer the force law from a falling bead

Choose downward displacement as positive. A submerged bead of volume \(V\), density \(\rho_b\) and mass \(m\) displaces fluid of density \(\rho_f\). In a fluid initially at rest the static pressure gradient supplies buoyancy \(\rho_f Vg\). Combine the two constant forces into \(m g_e=(\rho_b-\rho_f)Vg\). Assume a heavy bead, negligible wall effects and sufficiently slow changes to neglect added mass and viscous memory. A drag law must reverse sign when the velocity changes sign.

\[m\dot v=mg_e-bv,\]
\[m\dot v=mg_e-c|v|v.\]

In the first model \(b\) has units kg/s, in the second \(c\) has units kg/m. Equating drag to the effective weight gives \(v_\infty=mg_e/b\) or \(v_\infty=\sqrt{mg_e/c}\). Near zero speed both models give initial acceleration \(g_e\). Equal initial slope and equal terminal speed therefore leave the force law undetermined.

Solve both initial-value problems

Choose the characteristic time \(\tau=v_\infty/g_e\) and write \(u=v/v_\infty\), \(s=t/\tau\). Starting from rest the positive-speed branches obey \(du/ds=1-u\) and \(du/ds=1-u^2\). Separation and integration from zero give \(-\ln(1-u)=s\) and \(\operatorname{artanh}u=s\). A second integration supplies displacements that a camera can measure directly.

\[u_{\rm lin}=1-e^{-s},\]
\[z_{\rm lin}=v_\infty\tau(s-1+e^{-s}).\]
\[u_{\rm quad}=\tanh s,\]
\[z_{\rm quad}=v_\infty\tau\ln(\cosh s).\]

To start a data analysis, fit the early displacement to \(g_e t^2/2\) and determine the late speed independently. After this scaling, each curve is a prediction with no free fitting amplitude. With \(g_e=9.81\) m/s² and \(v_\infty=3.0\) m/s the time scale is 0.306 s. The competing predictions already differ by about 0.39 m/s at 0.30 s.

Recall · Which drag law has a mechanical basis here?

For a rigid sphere of radius \(a\) moving slowly in a Newtonian fluid the creeping-flow result is \(b=6\pi\eta a\). Check \(\mathrm{Re}=2a\rho_f|v|/\eta\) throughout the run. When inertia matters a pressure-drag model uses \(c=\rho_f C_D A/2\) with frontal area \(A\). Treating \(C_D\) as constant requires a range in which it varies slowly.

Derive the equation for a changing collection of matter

Let a horizontal cart of instantaneous mass \(M\) and speed \(v\) collect grains at rate \(\dot M>0\). Just before collection a small parcel \(dM\) has horizontal laboratory velocity \(w\). Over \(dt\) the momentum of cart plus parcel changes by the external impulse. The incoming parcel is included on the initial side so that the comparison follows the same matter.

\[Mv+w\,dM+F_{\rm ext}\,dt=(M+dM)(v+dv).\]

Drop the product \(dM\,dv\), divide by time and keep the finite acceleration. The relative speed determines how strongly the intake changes the cart velocity.

\[M\dot v=F_{\rm ext}+(w-v)\dot M,\]
\[\frac{d(Mv)}{dt}=F_{\rm ext}+w\dot M.\]

Take a 20 kg cart rolling at 3 m/s on a level frictionless track. Grains falling vertically carry zero horizontal momentum. After collecting 10 kg, the common speed is 2 m/s. The horizontal kinetic energy falls from 90 J to 60 J. The collisions can dissipate energy even though the horizontal external impulse vanishes. The vertical momentum and gravitational energy belong to a separate balance.

Recall · Moving boundaries and signs

The formula uses laboratory-frame \(w\) immediately outside the cart and signed mass rate into the cart. For outflow use the velocity of matter as it leaves. A leaking cart with material departing at the cart's horizontal speed has no resulting horizontal acceleration.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 15. From a repeating trajectory to a chaotic attractor

A damped pendulum under a periodic drive can settle into the same orbit on each run yet visit a different point on successive drive periods. A return map supplies the right clock for distinguishing a new period from a slowly decaying transient.

Build a map from a continuous mechanical experiment

Consider a pendulum with dimensionless time in units of its small-angle period divided by \(2\pi\). Let \(q\) be the positive damping rate, \(A\) the drive amplitude and \(\omega\) the drive frequency.

\[\dot\theta=p,\]
\[\dot p=-q p-\sin\theta+A\cos(\omega t).\]

Prepare all observations at drive phase zero and evolve for \(T=2\pi/\omega\). The stroboscopic map takes \((\theta_n,p_n)\) to \((\theta_{n+1},p_{n+1})\). Complete rotations can be plotted modulo \(2\pi\) while the unwrapped angle tracks transport. An attracting period-one orbit appears as one point after transients. A period-two orbit alternates between two points and returns after \(2T\).

For a small patch in the state plane the instantaneous fractional area growth is the divergence \(\partial_\theta p+\partial_p\dot p=-q\). Integration over a period gives a Jacobian determinant \(e^{-qT}\). Phase-area contraction allows an attractor. A periodically driven Hamiltonian system at \(q=0\) instead has a unit determinant.

Recall · Why use a section at fixed drive phase?

Two trajectories with identical angle and speed can accelerate differently at different phases of an external drive. Sampling once per drive period compares equal external conditions. Recording at a nearby period can turn a perfectly regular response into an apparent cloud of points.

Calculate a period doubling in a solvable map

Near a flip bifurcation with only one slow direction, a one-dimensional map helps explain stability changes. The logistic map \(f(x)=rx(1-x)\) on \(0\leq x\leq1\) for \(0<r\leq4\) is an illustrative return law. Its \(r\) is not a calibrated pendulum torque. An interior fixed point is \(x_*=1-1/r\). For a small displacement \(\delta_{n+1}\simeq f'(x_*)\delta_n\), giving a multiplier \(2-r\). Its magnitude crosses one at \(r=3\) with negative sign.

To find the new orbit, solve \(f(f(x))-x=0\) and remove the two fixed-point factors \(x\) and \(x-x_*\). The remaining quadratic has roots

\[x_\pm=\frac{r+1\pm\sqrt{(r-3)(r+1)}}{2r}.\]

Its two-step multiplier is \(f'(x_+)f'(x_-)=-r^2+2r+4\). At \(r=3.2\) it is 0.16 and deviations fall by that factor every two observations. At \(r=1+\sqrt6\) it reaches \(-1\), so that orbit loses stability. Additional doublings create intervals of higher-period motion. Periodic windows interrupt the chaotic parameter range.

Analytical mechanics from constraints to invariants · 16. From differential acceleration to three-dimensional strain and stress

An accelerometer in a falling laboratory can read zero while a pair of falling test masses moves apart. A crystal can support different longitudinal and transverse speeds in the same direction. Both measurements require the spatial variation of a vector field, represented by a tensor.

Derive the relative acceleration in a falling frame

Let a laboratory origin fall freely at position \(\mathbf R\) in the field \(\mathbf g(\mathbf R)=-GM\mathbf R/R^3\) of a spherical source. Over a small separation \(\boldsymbol\xi\) choose parallel nonrotating axes. Subtract the origin's acceleration and expand to first order:

\[\ddot\xi_i=g_i(\mathbf R+\boldsymbol\xi)-g_i(\mathbf R)\simeq T_{ij}\xi_j,\]
\[T_{ij}=\frac{GM}{R^3}(3n_i n_j-\delta_{ij}),\]
\[\mathbf n=\mathbf R/R.\]

The radial eigenvalue is \(2GM/R^3\). Both transverse eigenvalues are \(-GM/R^3\). Outside the source the sum vanishes as required by \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf g=0\). Over a short time the fractional change of an initially stationary separation along an eigenvector is \(T_i t^2/2\). Near Earth's surface \(g/R\simeq1.54\times10^{-6}\) s⁻². Free masses initially separated by 2 m radially acquire a relative acceleration near \(6.2\) µm/s².

Recall · Which frame cancels gravity?

The origin's uniform acceleration cancels at all test masses when forming the difference. A rotating frame contributes centrifugal and Coriolis terms as well. For a small free-fall experiment state how axes are maintained and which relative velocities were initially present.

Compare nearby material points before and after a displacement

Label a reference point by \(\mathbf X\) and let its displacement be \(\mathbf u(\mathbf X)\). An initial segment \(d\mathbf X\) becomes \((I+\nabla\mathbf u)d\mathbf X\). Expand its squared length, retaining the first-order terms. The antisymmetric part drops out of the quadratic form for length change.

\[d\ell'^2-d\ell^2=2\,dX_i\,\varepsilon_{ij}\,dX_j,\]
\[\varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac12(\partial_i u_j+\partial_j u_i).\]

The trace gives fractional volume change. A rigid translation has zero displacement gradient. An infinitesimal rotation has an antisymmetric gradient, so it has zero linear strain as well. For simple shear \(u_x=\gamma y\) the component \(\varepsilon_{xy}=\gamma/2\) explains the factor of two between tensor and engineering shear strain.

Build Hooke's law from symmetries and an energy

On a cut normal to \(\mathbf n\), force per area is the traction \(t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j\). Balancing moments in a continuum without intrinsic body couples gives symmetric Cauchy stress. In an initially unstressed isotropic linear solid a quadratic elastic energy can depend only on the two independent rotationally invariant quadratic forms of the strain.

\[W=\tfrac12\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon)^2+\mu\,\varepsilon_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij},\]
\[\sigma_{ij}=\partial W/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=\lambda\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon\,\delta_{ij}+2\mu\varepsilon_{ij}.\]

Under uniform dilation \(\varepsilon=\eta I\) the bulk modulus is \(K=\lambda+2\mu/3\). Pure shear isolates \(\mu\). Stability requires \(K>0\) and \(\mu>0\). The general anisotropic relation \(\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}\) need not reduce to two constants. Crystal symmetry decides which entries can be determined by rotating a sound measurement.

Recover both moduli from two arrival times

Balance of linear momentum gives \(\rho\ddot u_i=\partial_j\sigma_{ij}\) in a homogeneous body with no body force. A plane longitudinal displacement has nonzero divergence. A transverse displacement has zero divergence. Substitution gives their phase speeds:

\[c_L^2=(K+4\mu/3)/\rho,\]
\[c_T^2=\mu/\rho.\]

For a solid of density 2500 kg/m³ with \(c_L=5000\) m/s and \(c_T=3000\) m/s the two moduli are \(\mu=22.5\) GPa and \(K=32.5\) GPa. One longitudinal speed would determine only their combination. Short wavelengths comparable with the lattice spacing, or waves in a thin guide, require dispersion or geometric corrections before using these bulk equations.
