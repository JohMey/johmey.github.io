    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Start with a system boundary. An impulse changes its momentum, a force acting through a distance changes its energy, and constraints determine which motions are possible. Linearising the constrained system near equilibrium predicts its normal modes.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Change the description while tracking the same motion. Accelerating frames add inertial forces, phase space keeps track of conjugate variables, and a central force reduces a two-body orbit or scattering encounter to radial motion.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Let orientation and driving enter the dynamics. A rigid body can exchange rotation among its principal axes. A driven or nonlinear oscillator can change its amplitude, period or even its equilibria as a control parameter varies.

    Chapters

    1. Momentum, impulse and collisions

      Use the centre-of-mass frame to predict the outcome of a collision and account for the kinetic energy that disappears from the motion of the bodies.

    2. Potential energy and turning points

      Use a potential-energy curve to distinguish allowed motion, local stability and escape over a barrier.

    3. Constraints and Lagrange’s equations

      Eliminate an unknown tension with a generalised coordinate and identify how a pulley’s rotational inertia changes an acceleration.

    4. Normal modes and energy exchange

      Decompose a pair of coupled oscillators into independent motions and predict the envelope of a beat signal.

    5. Rotating frames and the Coriolis effect

      Transform a measured trajectory between an inertial laboratory and a rotating observer, and quantify the first sideways deflection.

    6. Hamiltonian motion in phase space

      Construct a Hamiltonian, read a phase portrait and distinguish bounded motion from rotation at a separatrix.

    7. Two-body motion and binary masses

      Use the motion of both members of a binary to recover the total mass and distinguish the relative orbit from the two individual orbits.

    8. Impact parameters and scattering cross-sections

      Use flux conservation to turn a classical deflection law into an angular distribution observed by a detector.

    9. Principal axes and torque-free precession

      Use the inertia tensor to distinguish rotation about a principal axis from the precession of a freely rotating symmetric body.

    10. Forced, damped vibration

      Use amplitude, phase and power together to estimate the mechanical properties of an oscillator from a sinusoidal drive.

    11. Nonlinear stiffness and amplitude-dependent period

      Derive the leading frequency shift of a symmetric nonlinear oscillator and test whether a measured amplitude shift is consistent with a cubic spring.

    12. Bifurcation of a rotating hoop

      Use an effective potential to locate competing equilibria and predict the frequency of small oscillations on either side of a stability change.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. Changing the coordinates of a moving trap

      A translating trap looks stationary in its own coordinates. Derive the Hamiltonian measured by that observer, including the term that accounts for motion of the coordinate system.

    2. Recover a trajectory from a family of actions

      In Hamilton–Jacobi theory a partial differential equation for one function replaces several trajectory equations. A falling bead makes the different roles of position, energy and arrival time explicit.

    3. Read a period from the area enclosed by an orbit

      A track with a V-shaped potential has straight force segments but a strongly amplitude-dependent period. Its area in phase space gives a useful way to predict both the period and a slow change of the track.

    4. From a chain of springs to an elastic field

      A rod ringing after a small axial displacement can be described by one differential equation at every point. Derive that field equation, its boundary conditions and the range of wavelengths for which it represents a discrete chain.

    5. Proper time, momentum and a moving clock

      A pair of light pulses lets observers compare clocks without assuming a universal time. Build the invariant interval and use it to calculate transit times and the energy exchanged by moving material.

    6. Liouville flow and what an ensemble can remember

      A cloud of identical noninteracting beads is released with slightly different initial positions and momenta. Its position distribution widens. Does the system occupy more phase space, or has its uncertainty merely changed shape?

    7. Parametric resonance and Floquet growth

      A pendulum can gain energy when its length varies periodically even if no additive force acts on it. A modulation near twice a natural frequency amplifies one phase of the oscillation and attenuates another. The stability test concerns the growth of a perturbation over a complete modulation period.

    Applied study

    Use energy and angular momentum to find the turning points and period of an orbit.

    What fixes an orbit?

    An object at the same distance from Earth can follow a circle, a long ellipse or a path that escapes. Position sets the gravitational acceleration. The initial velocity distinguishes those trajectories. Begin in an inertial frame centred on a spherical Earth, and neglect atmospheric drag, other bodies and the motion of Earth in response to the much smaller satellite. With \(\mu=GM\), Newton’s law reads \(\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\mu\mathbf r/r^3\). Everything that follows concerns motion outside Earth.

    Crossing this equation with \(\mathbf r\) shows that the specific angular momentum \(\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}\) is constant. The orbit lies in the plane perpendicular to \(\mathbf h\). In polar coordinates \(h=r^2\dot\theta\), so the area swept per second is \(h/2\). An object must move faster around the centre when it is closer to it.

    Read the turning points from the energy

    Take the scalar product of Newton’s equation with velocity. The derivative of \(v^2/2\) equals the negative derivative of \(-\mu/r\), so the specific energy \(\mathcal E=v^2/2-\mu/r\) is constant. Substituting the angular momentum for tangential speed gives a one-dimensional radial balance

    \[\mathcal E=\frac{\dot r^2}{2}+U_{\rm eff}(r),\qquad U_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{h^2}{2r^2}-\frac{\mu}{r}.\]

    Only radii with \(\mathcal E\ge U_{\rm eff}\) can be reached. At the minimum of \(U_{\rm eff}\) a circular orbit is possible. A bound eccentric orbit has two turning radii \(r_p,r_a\). At each, \(\dot r=0\), so both are roots of \(2\mathcal E r^2+2\mu r-h^2=0\). Their sum fixes \(\mathcal E=-\mu/(2a)\), with \(a=(r_p+r_a)/2\). Substitution into the energy gives the speed at any point, \(v^2=\mu(2/r-1/a)\).

    Recover the shape and the clock

    Set \(u=1/r\) and use \(d/dt=hu^2 d/d\theta\) in the radial force equation \(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=-\mu/r^2\). This gives \(u''+u=\mu/h^2\), with primes denoting angle derivatives. Choosing \(\theta=0\) at closest approach yields \(r=(h^2/\mu)/(1+e\cos\theta)\). For \(0<e<1\) the shape is an ellipse, with \(e=(r_a-r_p)/(r_a+r_p)\) and semiminor axis \(b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\).

    An ellipse encloses area \(\pi ab\). Divide that area by \(h/2\) and use \(h^2=\mu a(1-e^2)\) to obtain \(T=2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}\). Eccentricity redistributes the time spent along the path. At a given semimajor axis it leaves the full period unchanged. Angular momentum also constrains a rotating body about its own centre. Torque-free rigid-body motion.

    From force components to Lagrange’s equation

    A constrained system often has fewer independent coordinates than Cartesian components. Consider a bead of mass \(m\) on a light rigid rod of length \(\ell\), moving in a vertical plane. The pivot fixes the radius. Its force is perpendicular to the allowed displacement, so the angle \(\theta\) measured from the downward vertical suffices. The kinetic and gravitational energies are \(K=m\ell^2\dot\theta^2/2\) and \(U=mg\ell(1-\cos\theta)\).

    For conservative forces with ideal constraints, varying the action \(\int (K-U)\,dt\) with fixed endpoints gives \(d(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q)/dt=\partial\mathcal L/\partial q\) for each independent coordinate \(q\), with \(\mathcal L=K-U\). For the rod this yields \(m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta\). Close to the stable minimum \(U\simeq mg\ell\theta^2/2\), and the motion oscillates with period \(2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\). For \(\ell=1.00\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s², the period is 2.01 s.

    The same curvature test works for a near-circular orbit. Expand the specific effective potential at \(r_c=h^2/\mu\). Its positive curvature is \(U''_{\rm eff}(r_c)=\mu/r_c^3\), so a small radial displacement oscillates at \(\sqrt{\mu/r_c^3}\). The period of this small radial motion agrees with the circular orbital period obtained from the area law. Both a pendulum and a nearly circular orbit reveal a restoring force through the curvature of an energy minimum.

    An ellipse from two distances

    Take \(\mu=3.9860\times10^{14}\) m³/s², \(r_p=6628.137\) km and \(r_a=42164\) km, all radii measured from the centre. The semimajor axis is 24396.1 km and \(e=0.7283\). The speed equation at closest approach gives 10.195 km/s. The product \(r_pv_p=r_av_a\) then gives 1.603 km/s at the far end. Using \(a\) in metres in the period formula gives 37 922 s, about 10.534 hours.

    Exercises on speed and timing

    1. Compare two bound orbits

    A circle and an ellipse have the same semimajor axis \(a\). The ellipse has \(e=0.6\). Compare their periods and find the ratio of the highest to the lowest speed in the ellipse.

    Compare the two orbits

    The periods agree because \(T\) depends only on \(a\) and \(\mu\). At the turning points the velocity is tangent. Therefore \(v_p/v_a=r_a/r_p=(1+e)/(1-e)=4\). Equal periods are compatible with very different local speeds.

    2. Find the escape threshold

    At radius \(r\), compare the circular speed with the minimum speed needed to reach arbitrarily large radius. Assume an initially tangential velocity.

    Derive the speed ratio

    For the circle, radial acceleration requires \(v_c^2/r=\mu/r^2\). Escape at threshold arrives at infinity with zero speed, so \(\mathcal E=0\) and \(v_e=\sqrt{2\mu/r}=\sqrt2\,v_c\). At a speed just below this threshold, energy is negative and there is still a finite outer turning point.
