From cut forces to local equilibrium

Engineering mechanics

A support reaction tells you the total force entering a part. To predict what an inclined internal surface carries, keep track of both the orientation of the cut and the direction of the force across it.

A force that depends on the surface you choose

Imagine a small triangular wedge cut out of a thin, slowly loaded plate. Its sloping face has unit outward normal \(\mathbf n=(n_x,n_y)\) and area \(A\). The two faces normal to the coordinate axes have areas \(A n_x\) and \(A n_y\) when both components are positive. Name the internal force per unit area on a face its traction. Opposite faces of the same cut carry equal and opposite tractions. Gravity and acceleration act on volume, which shrinks faster than area as the wedge shrinks. Dividing its force balance by \(A\) therefore gives a relation that no longer contains its size.

\[\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=n_x\mathbf t(\mathbf e_x)+n_y\mathbf t(\mathbf e_y)=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n .\]

The columns of the stress matrix are the two coordinate-face tractions. Their linear combination supplies the force on any inclined face. A small square additionally needs zero net moment. To leading order, the shear forces on opposite faces form two couples of opposite sign and equal lever arm. Their balance requires \(\sigma_{xy}=\sigma_{yx}\) for an ordinary material without internal body couples.

Why a gradient of stress carries a body force

Now use a tiny rectangular element of side lengths \(dx,dy\) and unit thickness. The right-face contribution to horizontal force is \(\sigma_{xx}(x+dx,y)dy\); the left face contributes its negative evaluated at \(x\). Their difference is \(\partial_x\sigma_{xx}\,dx\,dy\) to leading order. Resolve the upper and lower shear tractions in the same way. Add a prescribed horizontal force per volume \(b_x\) and divide by volume. Repeating in the vertical direction produces two independent equations.

\[\partial_x\sigma_{xx}+\partial_y\sigma_{xy}+b_x=0,\]
\[\partial_x\sigma_{xy}+\partial_y\sigma_{yy}+b_y=0 .\]

These equations say nothing yet about how much the material stretches. They can test an assumed stress field before the elastic modulus has even been measured. A candidate must also reproduce the applied boundary tractions. Satisfying the interior equations alone leaves many possible stresses.

Worked example · the grip on a diagonal bonding plane

At a measurement point let \(\sigma_{xx}=60,\sigma_{yy}=20,\sigma_{xy}=10\) MPa. The outward normal is \((0.6,0.8)\). Matrix multiplication gives \(\mathbf t=(44,22)\) MPa. Projection along the normal gives \(0.6(44)+0.8(22)=44.0\) MPa tension. Projection on the unit tangent \((-0.8,0.6)\) gives \(-22.0\) MPa. Reversing the tangent changes the sign of reported shear. Reversing the surface normal reverses the traction vector itself. For a uniform 100 mm² patch the force has components 4.40 and 2.20 kN. Using only normal stress would miss the tangential load that the adhesive must transmit.

Worked example · a specimen with a known stress gradient

Stand a bar upright, support it at its bottom and leave the top free. Use upward \(y\), length \(L\), constant density \(\rho\), and tension positive. The vertical equation reads \(d\sigma_{yy}/dy-\rho g=0\). Integrating from the free top gives \(\sigma_{yy}=\rho g(y-L)\). For a 3.0 m aluminium bar of density 2700 kg/m³ the base stress is −79.5 kPa. Its upward support traction is positive because the bottom face has downward outward normal. A uniform cross-sectional area of 200 mm² gives a base reaction of 15.9 N, equal to the weight. The local and global checks involve different quantities but must agree.

Find the angles where the shear traction passes through zero. Compare each with an extremum of the normal traction. Change the sign of the imposed xy shear, sketch the new angles before moving the slider, and check whether the two extreme normal stresses change.

How a continuum balance becomes a finite-element stiffness matrix

Engineering mechanics

A single bar element can predict an end displacement exactly and still miss the stress field inside. The difference follows directly from the balance it is asked to satisfy.

Turn a differential balance into work against a test displacement

Let a straight small-strain bar of length \(L\) have area \(A\), Young’s modulus \(E\), displacement \(u(x)\) and distributed axial load \(q(x)\) in N/m. Tensile axial resultant is \(N=EAu'\). Force balance on a slice gives \(N'+q=0\). Clamp the left end and prescribe a right-end force \(P\). Multiply the balance by any smooth virtual displacement \(v\) satisfying \(v(0)=0\), integrate along the bar, and integrate the derivative of \(N\) by parts.

\[\int_0^L EA\,u'v'\,dx=P\,v(L)+\int_0^L qv\,dx .\]

The boundary term at the clamp disappears because that displacement is prescribed. The unknown reaction can later be recovered by equilibrium. This formulation requires only first derivatives of trial displacements. Piecewise linear displacements are therefore admissible even though their slopes may jump at internal nodes.

Compute the stiffness rather than memorising it

On an element from \(x=0\) to \(h\), interpolate the two nodal displacements linearly: \(u=(1-x/h)u_1+(x/h)u_2\). Let the test displacement have the same two basis functions. A virtual change of the first or second node gives the corresponding row of the balance. Both derivatives are constant with opposite signs.

\[B=\begin{bmatrix}-1/h&1/h\end{bmatrix},\]
\[K_e=\int_0^h B^T EA B\,dx=\frac{EA}{h}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}.\]

For a constant distributed load, each basis function integrates to \(h/2\), so each node receives equivalent load \(qh/2\). The two rows of stiffness sum to zero. A uniform translation stores no strain energy. At a shared node the neighbouring virtual-work contributions add because both elements refer to the same unknown displacement.

Worked example · compare a nodal value with the stress in a cell

Take a 1 m bar with \(EA=2.0\) MN, uniform \(q=1200\) N/m and free right end. Exact balance integrated from \(N(L)=0\) gives \(N=q(L-x)\). Integrating strain from the clamp produces \(u=q(Lx-x^2/2)/(EA)\). The free-end displacement is 0.300 mm. With a single element the free row of the discrete system has stiffness \(EA/L\) and load \(qL/2\), giving the same displacement. Yet its piecewise constant axial force is \(EAu(L)/L=600\) N. The exact force ranges from 1200 N at the clamp to zero at the free end. The numerical force represents the mean over the cell.

Worked example · see what doubling resolution changes

Divide the bar into two equal elements. The middle node receives 600 N from both halves together, while the free node receives 300 N. The right element carries 300 N. Equilibrium of the middle node then puts 900 N in the left element. Their extensions are \(900(0.5)/(2\times10^6)=0.225\) mm and 0.075 mm. Nodal displacements agree with the exact quadratic at both nodes. The largest one-sided stress-resultant error within either element is now 300 N instead of 600 N. Comparing only tip displacements would hide this improvement.

Double the number of cells until the force error is below 100 N for the baseline load. Inspect the values on both sides of an element boundary and explain why agreement in tip displacement alone could have ended this refinement study too early.

Finding a displacement when no load acts in its direction

Engineering mechanics

A displacement gauge measures the lateral travel of a truss joint under a vertical load. A force balance determines the bar forces, but cannot directly give the sideways motion. Introduce a dummy load, differentiate the stored energy and recover the gauge reading with its sign.

Link an external force coordinate to stored strain energy

For a linear elastic pin-jointed truss, neglect support movement and temperature changes and assume small displacements. Bar i has constant axial force \(N_i\), length \(L_i\), modulus \(E_i\) and area \(A_i\). Integrating its force against its extension gives \(U_i=N_i^2L_i/(2E_iA_i)\). Superposition makes each force linear in the external loads. A small increment of load changes the equilibrium configuration. For a linear elastic conservative system the complementary energy equals U and its derivative with respect to an independent applied force gives displacement along that force.

\[U=\sum_i{N_i^2L_i\over2E_iA_i},\]
\[\delta_Q={\partial U\over\partial Q}=\sum_i {N_i n_i L_i\over E_iA_i},\]
\[n_i={\partial N_i\over\partial Q}.\]

The derivative \(n_i\) is the member force produced per unit trial load in the requested direction, with all other independent loads held fixed. Solve this trial equilibrium even if \(Q=0\) physically. Setting \(Q=0\) before differentiating erases its entire contribution. As a dimensional check, \(n_i\) is dimensionless when Q and \(N_i\) both use newtons, and the sum has dimensions of length.

Compute the two-bar flexibility matrix

A joint hangs from two overhead pins by straight bars slanting up-left and up-right, each at 45° to horizontal. Positive joint coordinates are rightward and downward. Let V be the applied downward force and H the rightward force; bars carry tension positive. Equilibrium gives \(N_L=(V+H)/\sqrt2,\ N_R=(V-H)/\sqrt2\). Define bar axial compliances \(c_i=L_i/(E_iA_i)\). Insert the forces into the energy and differentiate with respect to H and V independently:

\[\begin{bmatrix}\delta_H\\\delta_V\end{bmatrix}={1\over2}\begin{bmatrix}c_L+c_R&c_L-c_R\\c_L-c_R&c_L+c_R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}H\\V\end{bmatrix}.\]

The cross coefficients are equal because the mixed derivatives of the stored energy commute. This is Maxwell–Betti reciprocity. A cross displacement can be negative even though both direct compliances and stored energy are positive. The eigenvalues of this particular flexibility matrix are \(c_L,c_R\), corresponding to the two independent bar-extension directions.

Predict a sideways gauge reading and reverse the test

Take \(c_L=0.0020,\ c_R=0.0040\) mm/N. At \(V=100\) N and \(H=0\), both members carry 70.71 N. The vertical displacement is \(0.300\) mm, and the horizontal displacement is −0.100 mm. The more compliant right bar stretches farther; the joint moves left and down to accommodate both bar extensions. Half the vertical load times its displacement gives 15 N mm of strain energy. Summing the two bar energies independently gives the same result.

Apply instead \(H=100\) N and \(V=0\), assuming the slender right bar remains stable under compression. The vertical reading is −0.100 mm and horizontal motion is +0.300 mm. Thus the lateral response to the vertical test predicts the vertical response to a lateral test of equal magnitude. Equalizing bar areas would remove the cross response if the lengths and moduli matched. In practice a pin with clearance or a cable that goes slack can break the assumed linear bilateral response.

Exchange the left and right compliances. Before looking at the new graph, mark which readout should reverse sign and which should remain equal. Then set the two values alike and identify the symmetry that removes cross-coupling.

Coordinates, energy and equivalent inertia

Machine dynamics

Reduce a constrained drive to one coordinate and use a power balance to check its equation of motion.

From first principles

Refer moving masses to one shaft by matching kinetic energy

Choose shaft angle \(\theta\) as the independent coordinate of a rigid mechanism. Suppose the angular speeds of its rotating components and the linear speeds of its translating components are fixed multiples of \(\dot\theta\). Assume ideal constraints with no slip, so specifying \(\theta\) and \(\dot\theta\) fixes the motion. Total kinetic energy is the sum of the individual translational and rotational energies.

\[T=\sum_i\tfrac12m_i v_i^2+\sum_j\tfrac12J_j\omega_j^2=\tfrac12 J_{\rm eq}\dot\theta^2 .\]

Here rotational inertias are about the component centres or fixed rotation axes as appropriate, with no motion counted twice. Dividing by \(\dot\theta^2/2\) gives the inertia seen at the chosen shaft.

\[J_{\rm eq}=\sum_i m_i\left(\frac{v_i}{\dot\theta}\right)^2+\sum_jJ_j\left(\frac{\omega_j}{\dot\theta}\right)^2 .\]

A force on a translating part contributes the shaft torque that gives the same instantaneous power, \(T_i\dot\theta=F_iv_i\). For constant speed ratios and constant equivalent inertia, energy balance then gives \(\sum T_i=J_{\rm eq}\ddot\theta\). The square on the speed ratio in inertia reflection follows from energy and is essential when changing the reference shaft.

Derivation and applications

Derive the extra drive term for position-dependent inertia

A motor can require a torque at constant angular speed if a mechanism changes the linear speed of its payload during a revolution. Use a single angular coordinate \(\theta\), input inertia \(J_0\), and a guided mass \(m\) with position \(x=f(\theta)\). Assume a rigid lossless linkage and a horizontal guide, so gravity does no work. The kinematic constraint sets the payload velocity and fixes the instantaneous reflected inertia by equating the total kinetic energy to that of one shaft.

\[\dot x=f'(\theta)\dot\theta,\]
\[T=\tfrac12[J_0+m(f')^2]\dot\theta^2=\tfrac12 J(\theta)\dot\theta^2 .\]

Differentiate the energy with respect to time. The shaft input supplies the full change in kinetic energy through its power \(\tau\dot\theta\). Equating the two and cancelling speed gives the required drive torque. At a turning point the same equation follows directly from Lagrange's equation.

\[\tau=J(\theta)\ddot\theta+\tfrac12J'(\theta)\dot\theta^2.\]

For an ideal sinusoidal guide \(x=a\sin\theta\), substitution makes the speed-squared contribution explicit. It changes sign within each turn because the translating payload first absorbs and then releases kinetic energy.

\[J=J_0+ma^2\cos^2\theta,\]
\[\tau=J\ddot\theta-ma^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2.\]

A constant-inertia approximation omits this alternating torque even if it predicts the total energy averaged over a turn. Its integral over a full constant-speed revolution vanishes as the payload returns to its initial speed.

Worked example

Predict a constant-speed torque measurement

A 2.00 kg carriage follows \(x=0.0500\sin\theta\) metres with shaft speed 20.0 rad/s. At \(\theta=45^\circ\) its required torque is \(-2.00(0.0500)^2(0.5)(20.0)^2=-1.00\) N m. The motor absorbs 20.0 W at that instant. At 135° the required torque is \(+1.00\) N m. Both readings occur at the same shaft speed and the same reflected inertia.

Second application

Identify a payload from the torque harmonic

A separate horizontal mechanism has stroke parameter \(a=40.0\) mm and runs at 30.0 rad/s. After removing its empty-linkage trace, the measured second-harmonic torque amplitude is 0.720 N m. Since \(|\sin\theta\cos\theta|_{\max}=1/2\), the fitted mass is \(2(0.720)/[(0.0400)^2(30.0)^2]=1.00\) kg. A run at half speed should show 0.180 N m amplitude if the measured harmonic is inertial.

Follow the motion through a mechanism

A motor accelerating a load feels inertia from components that turn at different speeds. Simply adding their physical moments of inertia would treat all of those speeds as equal. Instead, choose one independent coordinate and express every kinetic energy in terms of it. No-slip belts, rigid gears and inextensible cables give useful constraints when their compliance and backlash are negligible over the motion of interest.

Consider a drum of radius \(R\) winding a cable carrying a vertically guided mass \(m\). Choose the drum angle \(\theta\) positive when the mass rises. If the effective winding radius is constant, displacement, velocity and acceleration satisfy \(x=R\theta\), \(\dot x=R\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot x=R\ddot\theta\). These equalities are kinematic. A torque relation requires force balance or work in addition. When the cable builds successive layers on a spool, the effective radius varies with angle and the constant-ratio assumption must be revisited.

Reflect mass and forces to a common shaft

Let \(J_d\) be the drum inertia. Its rotational kinetic energy and the load’s translational kinetic energy sum to \(K=(J_d+mR^2)\dot\theta^2/2\). The equivalent inertia seen at the drum is \(J_{\rm eq}=J_d+mR^2\). A small positive angle raises the mass through \(R\,d\theta\), changing its potential energy by \(mgR\,d\theta\). Thus gravity contributes a resisting torque \(mgR\). A positive applied torque \(T_d\) contributes power \(T_d\dot\theta\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12J_{\rm eq}\dot\theta^2+mgR\theta\right)=T_d\dot\theta,\qquad J_{\rm eq}\ddot\theta=T_d-mgR.\]

The second expression also holds at zero speed by force balance. It separates steady effort, needed to hold or raise the load against gravity, from the extra effort needed to accelerate the two inertias. With the motor disconnected, an ideal light cable does not make the mass accelerate quite at \(g\), since the drum must gain rotational kinetic energy too.

Calculate a motor torque

A drum with \(R=0.10\) m and \(J_d=0.15\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) lifts 25 kg. A reduction gear gives \(\dot\theta_m=4\dot\theta\). The rotor has inertia \(J_m=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Refer the train to the motor coordinate. The drum-plus-load inertia becomes \((0.15+25(0.10)^2)/4^2=0.025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), so the total at the motor is 0.045 kg·m². Gravity contributes \(25(9.81)(0.10)/4=6.13\) N·m at that shaft.

Demand upward acceleration 0.80 m/s². The drum must accelerate at 8.0 rad/s² and the motor at 32 rad/s². The motor supplies \(6.13+0.045(32)=7.57\) N·m. A separate check at load speed 1.0 m/s gives motor speed 40 rad/s. Its 303 W then divide into about 245 W raising the weight and 57.6 W increasing kinetic energy. Ideal gearing changes torque and angular speed inversely while preserving transmitted power.

Identify which approximation controls the answer

At constant lifting speed the inertial torque vanishes. At constant angular acceleration, doubling the transmission ratio decreases reflected load inertia by four but also doubles the motor acceleration for the same load motion. The rotor’s own inertia remains at the motor and eventually dominates. A gear ratio therefore cannot reduce every part of acceleration torque indefinitely. Bearings, cables and gears also dissipate energy or store it elastically. Include their effects when a measured transient shows a repeatable lag, oscillation or loss that an ideal rigid constraint cannot explain.

Is the largest reduction always preferable?

For a prescribed drum acceleration \(\alpha_d\) and reduction \(n=\omega_m/\omega_d\), collect gravity and load-side acceleration into the drum demand \(T_d=mgR+(J_d+mR^2)\alpha_d\). The motor torque becomes \(T_m=T_d/n+J_mn\alpha_d\). The first term falls as reduction rises, while rotor acceleration grows. Differentiating with respect to \(n\) gives an ideal minimum at \(n=\sqrt{T_d/(J_m\alpha_d)}\). For the stated lift \(T_d=27.73\) N m and \(\alpha_d=8\) rad/s², giving \(n=13.2\) approximately. This torque minimum assumes the motor can achieve the associated speed. At a drum speed of 10 rad/s it would turn at 132 rad/s, over three times its speed with the original reduction. Shaft-speed and voltage limits can therefore select a different practical ratio.

Derive speed ripple from a torque diagram

A crank-driven machine can require very different torque at different angles even when the average load is constant. A flywheel stores kinetic energy when driving torque exceeds resisting torque, then releases it when the load peaks. Refer all inertias and torques to the same shaft and suppose the equivalent inertia \(J\) is constant. The signed difference \(M(\theta)=T_{\rm drive}-T_{\rm load}\) gives \(J\dot\omega=M\). For continuous positive rotation write \(\dot\omega=\omega\,d\omega/d\theta\). Integration between two angular positions gives an exact work–energy relation.

\[\tfrac12 J[\omega_b^2-\omega_a^2]=\int_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}M(\theta)\,d\theta .\]

A periodic steady sequence requires zero net area under the torque-difference curve over a whole revolution. Its cumulative area nevertheless has a maximum and a minimum. Their difference \(\Delta E\) is the energy that must swing into and out of the rotating inertia. Define mean speed in the ripple specification as the arithmetic mean of the two extreme speeds \(\bar\omega=(\omega_{\max}+\omega_{\min})/2\) and fluctuation coefficient \(C_s=(\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})/\bar\omega\). Factoring the difference of squared speeds gives \(J=\Delta E/(\bar\omega^2 C_s)\). A time-average tachometer reading approximates this mean for small ripple, but the definitions are distinct for a strongly pulsating shaft.

Size a flywheel for a repeating work demand

A laboratory indexing machine runs at a nominal 300 rpm. A motor supplies approximately constant 30 N m. The resisting torque is 70 N m for the first quarter revolution and a lower constant value for the remaining three quarters. Equate total input and output work over each revolution: \(30(2\pi)=70(\pi/2)+T_l(3\pi/2)\), giving \(T_l=16.67\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). The first quarter produces a 40 N m deficit, so the cumulative energy falls by \(40(\pi/2)=62.83\,\mathrm J\). The remaining 13.33 N m surplus restores exactly that energy.

For a maximum peak-to-peak speed variation of 2% and \(\bar\omega=31.42\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), total inertia required is \(62.83/[31.42^2(0.02)]=3.18\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). If the machine already has 1.00 kg m² on this shaft, an extra 2.18 kg m² is required. A thin-ring estimate at mean radius 0.30 m gives mass \(2.18/0.30^2=24.2\,\mathrm{kg}\). A solid disc of the same radius needs twice the mass because its material lies closer to the shaft. At half speed the same torque-versus-angle work deficit requires four times the total inertia for the same fractional ripple. Startup torque or bearing load may therefore limit an inertia-based solution even when steady motor power suffices.

The torque balance also predicts where a shaft encoder should record the two speed extremes. The energy derivative with respect to angle equals the torque difference, so the maximum occurs just before the high resisting plateau begins and the minimum where that plateau ends. The maximum torque itself persists over a finite angular interval, while minimum speed occurs only at its end. This distinction matters when timing a torque pulse from a speed record: the sign of the speed derivative, rather than the absolute value of speed, identifies when the flywheel is discharging.

Energy coordinates and Lagrange’s equation

Machine dynamics

Derive a coupled translation–rotation equation with a rolling constraint and identify which reaction disappears from the energy balance.

From first principles

Derive the equation of motion using one independent coordinate

An ideal geometric constraint ties several motions together. Choose an independent coordinate \(q\) that automatically satisfies it and express kinetic energy \(T(q,\dot q)\) and potential energy \(V(q)\) in that coordinate. In the principle of virtual work, ideal constraint reactions do no work along admissible small motions. The balance of inertial, conservative and applied virtual work gives Lagrange's equation

\[\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}=Q_{\rm nc},\]
\[\delta W_{\rm nc}=Q_{\rm nc}\,\delta q .\]

For a rigid cylinder rolling on a stationary surface without slip, take centre displacement \(q=x\) and angular speed \(\dot x/R\). Its translation and rotation contribute to kinetic energy; a horizontal spring attached at the centre supplies the potential.

\[T=\tfrac12(m+I_G/R^2)\dot x^2,\]
\[V=\tfrac12kx^2,\]
\[(m+I_G/R^2)\ddot x+kx=Q_{\rm nc}.\]

Static friction supplies the contact reaction required for rolling. The contact point has zero instantaneous velocity, so that reaction contributes zero power here. The solution must still demand no more friction than the surface can supply.

Derivation and applications

Derive the effective stiffness of a pendulum on an accelerating cart

A continuously accelerating support changes both the equilibrium direction and the small-oscillation frequency of a hanging load. Model a point mass \(m\) on a massless rigid link of length \(\ell\) with an ideal hinge. Let the support move horizontally with imposed coordinate \(X(t)\) and measure angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical towards positive horizontal displacement. Ignore drag. With mass coordinates \(x=X+\ell\sin\theta\) and \(y=-\ell\cos\theta\), the kinetic and potential energies are

\[T=\tfrac12m[\dot X^2+2\dot X\ell\dot\theta\cos\theta+\ell^2\dot\theta^2],\]
\[V=-mg\ell\cos\theta .\]

Differentiate \(\partial T/\partial\dot\theta\) with respect to time and subtract \(\partial T/\partial\theta\). The velocity cross terms involving \(\dot X\dot\theta\) cancel, leaving a forcing term set by support acceleration, independent of support speed.

\[\ell\ddot\theta+g\sin\theta+\ddot X\cos\theta=0 .\]

For constant support acceleration \(a\), a stationary angle obeys \(\tan\theta_0=-a/g\). Choose the hanging equilibrium. Expanding the restoring term about \(\theta_0\) gives the effective gravitational acceleration along this new direction and the frequency of small oscillations.

\[\theta=\theta_0+\eta,\]
\[\ddot\eta+\frac{\sqrt{g^2+a^2}}{\ell}\eta=0,\]
\[\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{g^2+a^2}}{\ell}}.\]

A positive cart acceleration tips the pendulum backwards. The first correction to angle is linear in \(a/g\), while the first relative correction to frequency is quadratic. The exact equilibrium result holds at finite \(a\); the frequency expression requires small oscillations about it.

Worked example

Predict a suspended sensor's direction

A cart accelerates at 3.00 m/s² carrying a pendulum of length 0.600 m. Its steady angle is \(-\arctan(3.00/9.81)=-17.0^\circ\). The effective acceleration is 10.26 m/s², giving \(\omega_n=4.13\) rad/s and period 1.52 s. A sensor that treats the pendulum direction as vertical during this manoeuvre would carry the full angular bias.

Second application

Infer acceleration from a steady deflection

In another rig a 0.400 m pendulum settles 8.00° backwards after weak damping removes its initial swing. Constant acceleration is \(9.81\tan8.00^\circ=1.38\) m/s². The effective gravity is \(9.81/\cos8.00^\circ=9.91\) m/s², predicting a small-oscillation period \(2\pi\sqrt{0.400/9.91}=1.26\) s for weak damping. The angle is much more sensitive to modest acceleration than the period.

Let geometry remove a reaction

A cylinder rolls along a horizontal rail while a spring attached at its centre pulls it towards equilibrium. The rail friction is essential for rolling, but an ideal contact point is instantaneously at rest and the friction force does no work. One can write separate translational and rotational equations with an unknown friction force. Alternatively choose a coordinate that satisfies rolling from the beginning. The reduced energy then contains the rotational inertia without introducing a contact reaction.

Take the rightward displacement \(x\) of the cylinder centre as independent coordinate and clockwise angle as positive. On the fixed surface the no-slip condition gives \(x=R\theta\), up to a reference offset. A body of total mass \(m\) and centre inertia \(I_G\) has kinetic energy from both translation and rotation. A centre spring with stiffness \(k\) has elastic potential \(kx^2/2\).

\[T=\tfrac12(m+I_G/R^2)\dot x^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12kx^2 .\]

Connect energy to a dynamical equation

The virtual work of a horizontal force \(F\) applied at the centre is \(F\delta x\). In this coordinate its generalised force is simply \(F\). A torque \(M\) acting clockwise would do \(M\delta\theta=(M/R)\delta x\) and would enter as a force \(M/R\). With a centre damper \(c\), the dissipative force is \(-c\dot x\). Lagrange’s equation for this system becomes

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=F-c\dot x,\qquad (m+I_G/R^2)\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F .\]

For coordinates whose inertia depends on position, retain both derivatives involving \(T\). The differentiation of \(\partial T/\partial\dot x\) then generates a term proportional to \(\dot x^2\), part of which cancels \(\partial T/\partial x\). Simply multiplying a configuration-dependent effective mass by acceleration would miss the remaining term.

Predict a frequency and check rolling

A solid cylinder has \(m=8.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(R=0.10\,\mathrm m\), and \(I_G=mR^2/2=0.040\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). The effective mass seen by the spring is \(8+0.040/0.10^2=12.0\,\mathrm{kg}\). With \(k=480\,\mathrm{N/m}\) and negligible damping the natural frequency is \(\sqrt{480/12}/(2\pi)=1.007\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Release it from rest at \(x=+0.060\,\mathrm m\). Its initial acceleration is \(-480(0.060)/12=-2.40\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). Writing only the centre’s translation and ignoring rotation would overpredict the acceleration magnitude.

Reconstruct the previously eliminated contact force from translation. The spring pulls leftwards with 28.8 N and the centre accelerates leftwards with \(m\ddot x=-19.2\,\mathrm N\). Friction must therefore point rightwards with 9.6 N to supply the appropriate angular acceleration. The coefficient of static friction must be at least \(9.6/(8\times9.81)=0.122\) at release. For a smaller coefficient, the assumed rolling constraint fails and the model needs separate rotational and translational coordinates together with a slipping contact law.

Compare two cylinders with identical mass

A thin-walled hoop of the same mass and rolling radius has \(I_G=mR^2\). With the original spring its effective mass doubles the translational mass, giving natural frequency \(\sqrt{480/16}/(2\pi)=0.872\,\mathrm{Hz}\). A no-slip ring-down can therefore distinguish two bodies whose masses and outer dimensions are identical but whose internal mass distributions differ.

Use a cyclic coordinate to reduce coupled motion

Mount a pendulum on a cart that can roll horizontally on a level frictionless guide. Let cart displacement be \(x\), bob angle \(\theta\) from downward vertical, length \(l\), bob mass \(m\) and remaining cart mass \(M\). The bob position is \((x+l\sin\theta,-l\cos\theta)\). Differentiating and squaring its speed gives a cross term \(2l\dot x\dot\theta\cos\theta\) because cart and pendulum velocities need not be perpendicular. The full energy terms are

\[T=\tfrac12(M+m)\dot x^2+ml\cos\theta\,\dot x\dot\theta+\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2,\qquad V=mgl(1-\cos\theta).\]

Horizontal position itself does not occur in either expression. It is a cyclic coordinate, so \(\partial T/\partial\dot x=(M+m)\dot x+ml\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\) is conserved if no external horizontal force acts. This is horizontal momentum written in generalised coordinates. The pendulum equation from Lagrange’s equation is \(l\ddot\theta+\ddot x\cos\theta+g\sin\theta=0\). For small swings from rest, differentiate the momentum relation and linearise to obtain \(\ddot x=-ml\ddot\theta/(M+m)\). Substituting gives \([lM/(M+m)]\ddot\theta+g\theta=0\).

Free horizontal motion makes the small-swing frequency larger than for a clamped cart, by \(\sqrt{(M+m)/M}\). This result reflects motion about the system’s horizontally constrained centre of mass. The extra degree of freedom also permits uniform horizontal translation at zero frequency. Its presence does not imply an additional oscillatory mode.

Predict opposing cart travel in a release test

Let \(M=3.0\) kg, \(m=1.0\) kg and \(l=0.60\) m. Clamp the cart: the oscillation period would be \(2\pi\sqrt{0.60/9.81}=1.55\,\mathrm s\). Release its horizontal guide and the period becomes \(2\pi\sqrt{0.60(3)/(9.81\times4)}=1.35\,\mathrm s\). At release the bob is at \(\theta_0=10^\circ\) and both bodies are at rest. Because horizontal momentum remains zero, integrate the exact relation to obtain \((M+m)x+ml\sin\theta=\text{constant}\). As the bob first crosses the vertical, the cart has shifted \(ml\sin10^\circ/(M+m)=0.0260\,\mathrm m\) to the right from its starting point.

At the vertical the potential-energy decrease is \(mgl(1-\cos10^\circ)=0.0894\,\mathrm J\). Substituting \(\dot x=-ml\dot\theta/(M+m)\) into the kinetic energy at this instant gives effective angular inertia \(ml^2M/(M+m)=0.270\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Thus the angular speed magnitude is \(\sqrt{2(0.0894)/0.270}=0.814\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), and the cart speed magnitude is \(0.15(0.814)=0.122\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The frequency estimate used a small-angle approximation; the centre-of-mass and energy results at the vertical used the actual release angle. A horizontal cable pulling on the cart or measurable wheel friction would change the conserved momentum and should be measured before attributing deviations solely to pendulum nonlinearity.

The two-sensor experiment can measure conservation without estimating acceleration. Track the cart position and bob angle from video, then plot \(x+[ml/(M+m)]\sin\theta\). For the chosen masses its value should stay constant at the initial offset despite the cart and pendulum both moving. A steadily sloping line indicates nonzero initial horizontal momentum; a curved departure suggests an external force or tracking bias. This separation is useful because rolling resistance can be weak enough to leave the first measured period nearly unchanged while producing visible drift over several cycles. The conserved combination is specific to the inertial laboratory coordinate, so a camera fixed on the moving cart needs a separate record of cart displacement.

Why a radial slide presses sideways on a rotating guide

A low-friction carriage moves along a radial slot in a horizontal turntable. It can move at constant speed relative to the guide and still require a changing force. Keeping track of the moving basis exposes both acceleration components.

Differentiate the direction vectors

Let \(r(t)\) be displacement from the turntable centre along the guide and \(\theta(t)\) the angle of the guide. In fixed Cartesian components the radial unit vector is \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) and the tangential vector is \((-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\). Differentiating shows \(\dot{\mathbf e}_r=\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta\) and \(\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta=-\dot\theta\mathbf e_r\). Apply the product rule to the position \(r\mathbf e_r\) to obtain the velocity and then differentiate both terms.

\[\mathbf v=\dot r\mathbf e_r+r\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta,\]
\[\mathbf a=(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)\mathbf e_r+(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)\mathbf e_\theta .\]

One contribution to \(2\dot r\dot\theta\) comes from differentiating the radially directed velocity as its basis turns. The other comes from differentiating the magnitude of the tangential velocity \(r\dot\theta\). For positive rotation and outward travel, both point in the positive tangential direction. The expression already gives the acceleration in an inertial frame. Multiplying by mass gives the net real force required on the carriage.

Check sideways force with angular momentum and energy

Hold the angular speed at \(\Omega\), and drive the radial position as \(r=r_0+ut\) with constant u. Gravity and support cancel vertically. The net radial drive force is \(-mr\Omega^2\) and the guide must exert a side force \(2mu\Omega\). The resulting torque about the axis is \(2mru\Omega\). This equals the derivative of the angular momentum \(mr^2\Omega\), providing an independent sign and factor-of-two check.

\[P_r+P_\theta=(-mr\Omega^2)u+(2mu\Omega)(r\Omega)=mru\Omega^2={d\over dt}\left[{m\over2}(u^2+r^2\Omega^2)\right].\]

The side force does work in the laboratory since its contact point moves tangentially. Describing a smooth slot as doing zero work without specifying the frame would lose this energy transfer. The radial actuator absorbs power during outward constant-speed travel.

Setting u to zero removes the side force, while the guide still requires a radial restraint to maintain circular travel.

On an inward pass at −0.20 m/s, evaluated at the same radius, the radial force remains −2.0 N. The side force reverses and the turntable now receives angular momentum from the carriage. The magnitude of each force depends on a different combination of radius, radial speed and angular speed. Use the trace to distinguish the constant side load from the changing inward force.

When a suspended capacitor loses its stable equilibrium

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

An electrode suspended on a spring can remain motionless at a bias that makes the calculated electrostatic force grow as it moves. Static force balance alone misses the voltage at which that equilibrium disappears. Deriving the small-signal frequency gives an independent measurement of the same instability.

Start with the electrical and mechanical control variables

A movable conducting plate of area \(A\) faces a fixed electrode. Its unstressed gap is \(g\). Define positive displacement \(x\) toward the fixed plate, with spring force \(-kx\), mass \(m\) and viscous damping coefficient \(b\). For \(0\le x<g\) assume ideal parallel plates, negligible tilt and fringing, constant \(\epsilon\), and a linear elastic spring. The capacitance is \(C(x)=\epsilon A/(g-x)\). The model ends when the plates approach contact or when the suspending structure departs from its specified elastic law. It cannot by itself calculate adhesion, contact resistance or post-contact motion.

Before differentiating electrical energy specify whether the source holds voltage or charge. At fixed charge \(Q\), electrical energy is \(Q^2/[2C(x)]\), and its negative displacement derivative gives the force toward increasing \(x\). At fixed voltage \(V\), a source supplies \(VdQ\) as the plates move. The appropriate potential for mechanical equilibrium is the spring energy plus the field energy minus \(QV\), which here is \(\mathcal U_V=kx^2/2-C(x)V^2/2\). Differentiating gives the outward spring reaction and the attractive electrical force \(F_e=V^2C'(x)/2=\epsilon A V^2/[2(g-x)^2]\). An independent derivation multiplies the conductor pressure \(\epsilon E^2/2\) by area with \(E=V/(g-x)\). Both give a positive force. Replacing the source potential with the stored energy at fixed voltage would reverse its sign.

Locate all equilibria before choosing a branch

Define \(s=x/g\) and \(\Lambda=\epsilon A V^2/(2kg^3)\). The static force equation reduces to \(\Lambda=s(1-s)^2\). At a specified voltage one can find \(s\) by scanning the curve \(s\mapsto s(1-s)^2\) over the physically admissible interval \(0\le s<1\). Its derivative is \((1-s)(1-3s)\), giving an interior maximum \(4/27\) at \(s=1/3\). For \(0<\Lambda<4/27\) there are two physical roots. The smaller is a local minimum of \(\mathcal U_V\) while the larger is a local maximum. Their merger is a fold of the static equilibrium branch:

\[x_{\rm pi}=g/3,\]
\[V_{\rm pi}=\sqrt{\frac{8kg^3}{27\epsilon A}} .\]

The often-quoted one-third displacement is the result for a linear spring, ideal voltage drive and this capacitance geometry. It is not a universal travel limit determined by the gap alone. Additional mechanical restoring forces or a different electrical boundary condition change the fold. Neither positive net electrical force nor the mere existence of a real solution is evidence of stable operation. A root finder started near the almost closed gap can converge to the unstable large-displacement root with an extremely small static residual.

The two branches can be distinguished experimentally without deliberately reaching contact. At static equilibrium \(kx=F_e\). Linearise the total force about \(x_0\) while maintaining voltage at its prescribed value. The incremental restoring stiffness and corresponding weak-damping natural frequency are

\[k_{\rm eff}=k-\frac{\epsilon A V^2}{(g-x_0)^3}=k\,\frac{1-3s_0}{1-s_0},\]
\[f_{\rm small}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm eff}}m}.\]

A small push toward the fixed electrode is restored only when \(k_{\rm eff}>0\). The familiar electrostatic softening is the reason the smaller equilibrium is stable. The small-signal frequency tends to zero as the fold is approached. With appreciable damping the observed amplitude resonance and ringdown oscillation have slightly different frequencies; fit the full mechanical response rather than identifying an arbitrary peak with the undamped \(f_{\rm small}\).

Reconstruct the prepared state from a frequency measurement

Take a 100 μm square electrode in vacuum, \(g=2.00\) μm, \(k=2.00\) N/m and \(m=5.00\times10^{-10}\) kg. The predicted pull-in voltage is 7.32 V, the zero-bias capacitance 44.3 fF and the zero-bias natural frequency 10.07 kHz. On the low branch \(s_0=0.20\) gives \(x_0=0.400\) μm and voltage 6.80 V. Then \(k_{\rm eff}=1.00\) N/m and the small-signal frequency is 7.12 kHz. A measured frequency ratio \(f/f_0\) can be inverted via \(s=(1-r)/(3-r)\), where \(r=(f/f_0)^2\). The result is independent of absolute optical displacement calibration if the mass and underlying spring stiffness have not changed during the bias sweep.

At the same voltage the other physical root is \(s=0.48769\). A numerical force balance at this root would give the same supply reading but a negative tangent stiffness. The ideal voltage-controlled potential in units \(kg^2\) is \(u(s)=s^2/2-\Lambda/(1-s)\). The barrier height \(kg^2[u(0.48769)-u(0.20)]\) for this example is \(7.26\times10^{-14}\) J. A sufficiently energetic disturbance can carry the plate out of the stable basin even below static pull-in. This is different from loss of the static equilibrium itself. A quasistatic model predicts the existence and local stiffness of the equilibrium; a transient simulation must additionally specify initial displacement, velocity, drive ramp and damping.

Change the preparation to fixed charge

Disconnect a perfect isolated capacitor after placing a known charge \(Q\) on it, neglecting leakage and parasitic capacitance. Then \(F_Q=Q^2/(2\epsilon A)\) is independent of the remaining gap, because \(D=Q/A\) is fixed. Static equilibrium is \(x_0=Q^2/(2\epsilon A k)\), with incremental stiffness exactly \(k\). At the same initial voltage and gap the instantaneous force agrees with the voltage-controlled value, since \(Q=C(x_0)V\). The derivatives governing subsequent motion differ. At \(x_0=0.400\) μm the required charge in the numerical example is \(0.376\) pC. After ideal electrical isolation the small-signal frequency returns to the underlying elastic value even though the loaded static position stays the same.

Ideal charge isolation is delicate in a small device. A fixed parasitic capacitance \(C_p\) from the isolated node to a reference means the conserved charge belongs to \(C(x)+C_p\). Motion changes the fraction of total charge residing on the actuator. The electrical energy becomes \(Q_{\rm total}^2/[2(C+C_p)]\), so the force is \(Q_{\rm total}^2 C'/[2(C+C_p)^2]\), generally displacement dependent. If \(C_p\gg C\), the node voltage changes little with a small displacement and behavior is closer to voltage control. Interpreting an observed softening after disconnecting the supply as evidence against electromagnetic force balance would overlook this boundary change.

A different practical preparation puts an intentional series capacitor \(C_s\) between a fixed voltage supply and the actuator. Its series combination is \(C_{\rm ser}=\epsilon A/[g-x+\epsilon A/C_s]\). For static mechanical work at fixed supply voltage use \(F=V_s^2 C_{\rm ser}'/2\). Defining an effective electrical spacing \(g_e=g+\epsilon A/C_s\), the static fold occurs at \(x=g_e/3\) if this lies within the open physical gap. If \(\epsilon A/C_s\ge2g\), contact would occur before any such static fold in the ideal model. This calculation shows a direct connection between passive circuit design and allowed mechanical travel, while making clear that the maximum actuator voltage can differ from the voltage printed on the supply.

Why a sudden voltage change can reach contact below static pull-in

For a rapid electrical step the plate has a position and velocity inherited from the previous preparation. Suppose it starts at rest at its zero-bias position, the supply steps to a constant \(V\) much faster than a mechanical period, and mechanical damping is initially negligible. Immediately after the step, energy in the mechanical potential \(u(s)=s^2/2-\Lambda/(1-s)\) is \(u(0)=-\Lambda\) in units of \(kg^2\). Motion under this constant voltage can reach the force barrier if the initial value exceeds the potential at the unstable stationary point. This question can be solved by comparing two energies without integrating an entire time history.

Let \(s_b\) be the unstable root of \(\Lambda=s_b(1-s_b)^2\). At threshold the initial state is on the separatrix, the trajectory separating oscillations in the well from excursions to the closing gap. Set \(u(0)=u(s_b)\), subtract the two expressions and cancel \(s_b\) from the nontrivial solution. The two conditions become

\[\Lambda={s_b(1-s_b)\over2},\]
\[\Lambda=s_b(1-s_b)^2 .\]

Thus \(s_b=1/2\), \(\Lambda=1/8\), and the ideal undamped step threshold is \(V_{\rm step}/V_{\rm pi}=\sqrt{27/32}=0.919\). In our example this is 6.72 V. A slow increase to 6.80 V leads to the observed stable 0.40 μm deflection, whereas a sudden ideal voltage step to the same value from rest at the origin contains enough energy to escape the well. Starting on the biased equilibrium before applying a small perturbation is a different experiment. The direction of the threshold shift makes physical sense: damping removes mechanical energy during the first excursion, and a slow ramp allows the equilibrium to move with the plate. The undamped value specifies a particular ideal initial-value experiment, rather than a voltage rating for an unspecified drive waveform.

One can check a simulated step by monitoring \(m\dot{x}^2/2+\mathcal U_V\) after the supply settles. With viscous damping its derivative is \(-b\dot{x}^2\), so any sustained increase during a constant-voltage segment points to a numerical or modelling error. Before the step ends there is an explicit drive term \(-CV\dot V\) in this energy balance. The mechanical potential already includes the source work. Adding \(V\dot Q\) to the same balance a second time gives an artificial energy imbalance. A transient solver should stop at a physically specified minimum gap instead of marching across the singularity; a statement about contact time depends on what counts as contact.

A finite source resistance changes what a ringdown measures

A dc supply with series resistance \(R\) can maintain the static voltage accurately while failing to hold the actuator voltage fixed over a mechanical cycle. Let its steady node voltage be \(V\), with equilibrium capacitance \(C=C(x_0)\). A small sinusoidal displacement \(\delta x\) causes current \(i\omega(C\,\delta v+VC'\delta x)\) into the actuator. The small-signal supply voltage is zero, so \(\delta v=-R i\). Eliminating current gives

\[\delta v=-\frac{V C'}C\frac{i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}\delta x,\]
\[\tau=RC .\]

Linearise \(F_e=V^2 C'/2\) allowing both voltage and position to vary. The direct derivative is \(K_{e0}=V^2C''/2\), while the change from node voltage is \(VC'\,\delta v\). For an ideal parallel plate \(C''/2=(C')^2/C\), so the two terms combine to the simple complex relation \(\delta F_e=K_{e0}\delta x/(1+i\omega\tau)\). At low frequency the electrical force follows displacement with its fixed-voltage slope. At high frequency the slope vanishes, as it must for an isolated ideal plate carrying fixed charge over one oscillation. At intermediate frequency a motion-induced current dissipates heat in the resistor.

Bring \(\delta F_e\) to the left side of the mechanical equation. Its real part subtracts \(K_{e0}/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\) from the mechanical stiffness; its imaginary part adds \(K_{e0}\tau/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\) to the viscous damping coefficient. This damping is positive for a passive resistor. At \(s=0.20\) the static electrical stiffness is \(1.00\) N/m, the operating capacitance is 55.3 fF and \(R=400\) MΩ gives \(\tau=22.1\,\mu\mathrm s\). A 7.2 kHz interrogation has \(\omega\tau\simeq1\). Only about half the static electrical slope survives and the added damping is roughly \(11\,\mu\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). Treat these as frequency-dependent coefficients in the driven susceptibility. Substituting the low-frequency stiffness into a ringdown fit at this frequency would give the wrong displacement when the fitted frequency is inverted. Fitting the complex response at several frequencies avoids that ambiguity.

An experiment can separate the circuit effect from mechanical nonlinearity. Keep the same static bias and displacement, insert two known series resistances, then measure both in-phase and quadrature response to a small sinusoidal force. The displaced equilibrium should remain essentially unchanged at dc if leakage stays small. A stiffness dispersion and dissipative peak that shift in proportion to \(1/R\) support an electrical cause. Then repeat at a smaller oscillation amplitude. Amplitude-dependent frequency shift that survives the change in resistance can arise from nonlinear spring or force curvature, which the first-order susceptibility leaves out. A simple change to the external circuit has turned the slope of an energy curve into a prediction for a measured phase lag.

A moving-coil galvanometer as an electrical and mechanical system

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A scale division records an angle, yet the quantity marked on the dial is electric current. A spring, a magnetic field and the resistance of the external measuring circuit connect the two. Derive the calibration, predict how a pointer approaches its reading, and find which experiment measures the charge of a short pulse.

Convert the force on two straight sides into a torque

A light rectangular coil has \(N\) turns, active length \(l\), width \(w\), and area per turn \(A=lw\). It pivots about an axis parallel to its long sides. Shaped pole pieces confine an approximately radial magnetic field of magnitude \(B\) to an air gap. Over the working angular range the magnetic field is perpendicular to the active wire and lies approximately in the plane of the coil. An elementary length carrying conventional current \(i\) experiences \(d\mathbf F=i\,d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B\). The two long sides carry current in opposite directions and experience forces of equal magnitude \(N ilB\). Acting with lever arms \(w/2\), the forces make a couple whose torque is \(NiAB\). Their net force is approximately zero. The short ends supply negligible torque about the pivot under the ideal geometry.

Write \(K=NAB\), and choose the positive sense of current to give positive torque. Its units are \({\rm N\,m/A}\). In a uniform Cartesian field an ordinary rigid loop instead gives torque \(NiAB\sin\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the angle between its normal and the field. The radial gap maintains the factor equivalent to \(\sin\alpha\) close to unity as the pointer turns. This is a design choice that produces a nearly linear scale. If a measured calibration curve flattens at a large angle, field nonuniformity and a nonlinear torsion spring are candidate causes that can be tested separately.

For a soft torsion spring with stiffness \(\kappa\), the return torque is \(-\kappa\theta\) at small deflections from the zero-current setting. At static equilibrium \(\theta=Ki/\kappa\). With \(N=200\), \(A=1.00\,{\rm cm^2}\), \(B=0.250\,{\rm T}\) and \(\kappa=8.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm N\,m/rad}\), the torque constant is \(0.0050\,{\rm N\,m/A}\) and the current sensitivity is \(6.25\,{\rm rad/A}\). A \(5.0\,{\rm mA}\) current turns the pointer through \(0.03125\,{\rm rad}\), about \(1.79^\circ\). A mirror mounted on the same axle deflects a reflected laser through twice this angle. At a screen distance of \(0.50\,{\rm m}\) the spot shifts \(0.50\tan(2\theta)=31.3\,{\rm mm}\), making the small mechanical rotation easy to resolve.

Recover the reciprocal voltage from work

Now rotate the coil through a small positive angle while it carries positive current. The electromagnetic torque does mechanical work \(Ki\,d\theta\). The voltage associated with the motion must therefore draw electrical work \(i e_{\rm back}\,dt\) of equal magnitude when the motor torque assists that motion. This gives the back-emf relation \(e_{\rm back}=K\dot\theta\), measured as a positive drop in the motor sign convention. Its coefficient has units \({\rm V\,s/rad}\), numerically the same SI coefficient as the torque constant. One can also derive it from the motional emf on the two active sides. Each moves at speed \((w/2)\dot\theta\), crosses field \(B\), and generates a voltage \(Bl(w/2)\dot\theta\) along its wire. The two contributions add over every turn.

Let \(J\) denote the rotational inertia, \(b\) the mechanical viscous damping coefficient, \(L\) the coil inductance and \(R\) the sum of winding plus external series resistance seen by a voltage source \(v(t)\). The electromechanical equations are

\[J\ddot\theta+b\dot\theta+\kappa\theta=Ki,\]
\[L\dot i+Ri+K\dot\theta=v(t).\]

Multiplying the first equation by \(\dot\theta\) and the second by \(i\) eliminates the coupling power \(Ki\dot\theta\). The result says that source power \(vi\) equals the rate of increase of \(J\dot\theta^2/2+\kappa\theta^2/2+Li^2/2\), plus \(b\dot\theta^2+Ri^2\). This identity fixes the relative signs of the torque and back emf. If a sign mistake turns a passive closed coil into a self-accelerating pointer, the same mistake produces a residual coupling term in this power balance. Mechanical friction and winding heat represent distinct sinks even though both can damp the observed displacement.

Predict overshoot and find a critical external resistance

If the electrical relaxation time \(L/R\) is short compared with the oscillation time \(1/\sqrt{\kappa/J}\), set \(i\simeq(v-K\dot\theta)/R\). Substituting into the mechanical equation increases its effective damping to \(b_{\rm eff}=b+K^2/R\). Set \(v=0\) when discussing free relaxation with the electrical terminals connected through a resistor. A moving shorted coil generates a current that opposes its motion. Opening the circuit instead suppresses that current and leaves the mechanical damping. An ideal current source holding specified current through all pointer motions also changes the back-emf response because its output voltage adjusts to enforce that current.

The undamped angular frequency is \(\omega_0=\sqrt{\kappa/J}\) and the damping ratio is \(\zeta=b_{\rm eff}/(2\sqrt{\kappa J})\). Take \(J=2.0\times10^{-6}\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(b=1.6\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), and the preceding \(K,\kappa\). Then \(\omega_0=20.0\,{\rm rad/s}\), \(2\sqrt{\kappa J}=8.0\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), and an open circuit gives \(\zeta=0.20\). With \(R=1.00\,\Omega\) the electric contribution to damping is \(2.50\times10^{-5}\,{\rm N\,m\,s/rad}\), giving \(\zeta=0.5125\). For a voltage step the ideal fractional overshoot in the reduced model is \(\exp[-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}]=0.153\). The first peak occurs after \(\pi/(\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})=0.183\,{\rm s}\).

Critical damping occurs when \(b+K^2/R=2\sqrt{\kappa J}\), which requires a total resistance of about \(0.391\,\Omega\). A winding resistance of \(0.300\,\Omega\) therefore leaves \(0.091\,\Omega\) for external source and leads. If the winding alone exceeds the target resistance, a simple passive external series resistor cannot reach this damping in the reduced model. A finite \(L\) must also be checked: with \(L=5.0\,{\rm mH}\) and \(R=1.0\,\Omega\), \(\omega_0L/R=0.10\), so the approximation provides a useful starting estimate. Reducing \(R\) indefinitely eventually invalidates the elimination of current. The full pair of equations then predicts the damping and frequency dependence.

Distinguish a charge impulse from a slow current reading

Suppose a brief imposed current pulse ends long before the pointer has turned appreciably. Integrating the torque equation over that interval gives \(J\Delta\dot\theta\simeq K\int i\,dt=Kq\). The initial signal is now angular velocity proportional to total transmitted charge. For weak subsequent damping, the first excursion is approximately \(Kq/(J\omega_0)\). A dose of \(20\,\mu{\rm C}\) gives \(2.50\,{\rm mrad}\) with the instrument above, corresponding to about \(2.50\,{\rm mm}\) on the mirror scale. Two current pulses of identical charge but different shapes give nearly the same reading if both are short on the mechanical time scale. Their peak currents can be entirely different.

For appreciable damping, use the impulse solution \(\theta(t)=Kq\,e^{-\zeta\omega_0t}\sin(\omega_dt)/(J\omega_d)\), with \(\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) for an underdamped pointer. Its maximum obeys \(\tan(\omega_dt_p)=\omega_d/(\zeta\omega_0)\) in the first quarter cycle. Substitution corrects the ballistic calibration instead of treating the damped peak as an undamped amplitude. The state of the external circuit after the pulse matters because it determines \(\zeta\) during the throw. A calibration pulse should leave the measuring circuit connected in the same way as the unknown pulse.

Measure the torque constant without assuming the magnetic field

The preceding design calculation multiplies turn count, active area and a nominal air-gap induction. An independent calibration can measure the same constant without knowing the field distribution along every wire. Move the pointer through a small controlled angle while measuring its open-circuit voltage with a high-impedance instrument. In the same working interval the voltage is \(K(\theta)\dot\theta\) up to a polarity choice. Integrating the voltage over the sweep gives \(\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}K(\theta)\,d\theta\). If \(K\) is constant this is simply \(K\Delta\theta\), independent of sweep speed. It is often easier to integrate a recorded voltage pulse accurately than to differentiate the angle trace to obtain instantaneous speed.

For a sweep through \(0.0800\,{\rm rad}\), suppose the measured voltage integral is \(0.401\,{\rm mV\,s}\). The generator constant is \(5.01\times10^{-3}\,{\rm V\,s/rad}\). Independently add a known small torque to the spring and measure its static angle. A torque \(32.0\,\mu{\rm N\,m}\) rotates the pointer by \(0.0400\,{\rm rad}\), giving \(\kappa=8.00\times10^{-4}\,{\rm N\,m/rad}\). Combining the two measurements predicts a current sensitivity of \(6.27\,{\rm rad/A}\). Splitting the sweep into smaller equal angular intervals tests whether the measured \(K\) depends on angle. Sweeping backwards should reverse the integrated voltage. A voltage offset instead contributes its offset times duration, so repeating at two sweep speeds helps separate it.

A dynamic cross-check uses free decay under two load conditions. Measure the exponential decay rate \(\alpha_{\rm open}\) with a high-impedance voltage probe and \(\alpha_R\) with a known total resistance in the closed circuit. In the short-electrical-time limit, subtraction of the two damping laws yields \(K^2=2JR(\alpha_R-\alpha_{\rm open})\). With \(J=2.0\times10^{-6}\,{\rm kg\,m^2}\), \(R=1.00\,\Omega\), and decay rates \(4.0\) and \(10.25\,{\rm s^{-1}}\), this gives \(K=0.0050\,{\rm N\,m/A}\). The method needs two identifiable decay rates and independent inertia. If the loaded response is overdamped, the envelope of two oscillation peaks is no longer available; fit the full two-real-pole response to the measured displacement.

Keep the third dynamical variable in a frequency-response experiment

For a sinusoidal voltage at angular frequency \(\omega\) the exact linear transduction law follows by eliminating the complex current after transforming both differential equations. The mechanical impedance in angular-displacement form is \(\kappa-J\omega^2+i\omega b\); the winding impedance is \(R+i\omega L\). Using phasors with time dependence \(e^{i\omega t}\) gives

\[\frac{\Theta}{V}=\frac{K}{(R+i\omega L)(\kappa-J\omega^2+i\omega b)+i\omega K^2}.\]

At zero frequency the gain is \(K/(R\kappa)\) and the phase tends to zero, as the static calibration requires. At a sufficiently high frequency both the electrical and inertial impedances grow. For \(L>0\) the magnitude approaches \(K/(LJ\omega^3)\). The reduced model with the current eliminated algebraically falls only as \(1/\omega^2\). The change of the asymptotic slope provides an experimental way to distinguish finite inductance from an incorrectly adjusted viscous damping constant. Beyond the working band of a real instrument, elastic modes of the pointer or parasitic capacitance can enter before that asymptote is resolved.

At the nominal undamped frequency \(20\,{\rm rad/s}\), with \(R=1.0\,\Omega\) and \(L=5.0\,{\rm mH}\), the stiffness and inertia terms cancel. The denominator becomes \(-\omega^2 L b+i\omega(Rb+K^2)\), or approximately \(-0.000032+i\,0.000820\) in consistent SI units. Its magnitude gives about \(6.09\,{\rm rad/V}\). A sinusoid of \(1.0\,{\rm mV}\) peak produces about \(6.09\,{\rm mrad}\) peak, whereas the static response to \(1.0\,{\rm mV}\) is \(6.25\,{\rm mrad}\). The phases differ radically despite their similar amplitudes: near this frequency the pointer lags by about \(92.2^\circ\). A gain-only calibration at a single frequency would miss the phase lag.

Change the electrical load and compare two step models

In the live graph the source voltage is chosen anew at each resistance to give a final current of \(5.0\,{\rm mA}\) after the pointer settles. That convention keeps the final angle common to all curves and isolates the transient response of the load. The full model retains \(i,\theta,\dot\theta\); the second curve sets \(L\dot i=0\). Predict first which trace should start flatter. With finite inductance the current initially vanishes and increases at a finite rate. The initial torque and angular acceleration therefore vanish, while the reduced model applies a finite torque immediately. Increasing the inductance makes that distinction easier to see, especially when the electrical response time becomes comparable to the period of mechanical oscillation.

Velocities and accelerations in planar mechanisms

Machine dynamics

Use the rigid-body velocity and acceleration relations to connect an instantaneously known crank speed to a slider acceleration.

From first principles

Differentiate a fixed link vector in a rotating body

For two material points \(A,B\) on a rigid planar link, their separation has constant length even while its direction changes. Let \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A\) and angular velocity vector \(\boldsymbol\omega\) be perpendicular to the plane. A small rotation by \(d\theta\) sweeps a displacement perpendicular to the separation. Dividing by time gives the inertial derivative \(\dot{\mathbf r}=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\) and hence

\[\mathbf v_B=\mathbf v_A+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r .\]

Differentiate once more using the product rule. Change of angular speed provides the tangential acceleration contribution; change of the separation's direction gives the second cross product.

\[\mathbf a_B=\mathbf a_A+\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r).\]

For planar rotation the final term is \(-\omega^2\mathbf r\), directed towards \(A\). It remains even when angular speed is constant. These relations concern two fixed points on one rigid body. A slider moving along the rotating link changes the body-relative separation and contributes additional velocity and acceleration terms.

Derivation and applications

Recover planar angular acceleration from two accelerometers

Velocities identify an instantaneous rotation centre, but a time-varying rotation is better characterised by both angular speed and angular acceleration. Mount two aligned accelerometers at points \(A\) and \(B\) on one rigid planar body. Assume the measured vectors have been expressed in common inertial axes and any gravity contribution cancels in their difference. The separation vector is \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A\) with fixed length \(d\). Rigid-body kinematics gives

\[\Delta\mathbf a=\mathbf a_B-\mathbf a_A=\alpha\,\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf r-\omega^2\mathbf r .\]

The two terms point in perpendicular directions. Form the unit vector \(\mathbf e_r=\mathbf r/d\) from \(A\) to \(B\) and its counterclockwise normal \(\mathbf e_t=\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_r\). Projecting gives a radial signal proportional to squared angular speed and a tangential signal proportional to signed angular acceleration.

\[\Delta a_r=-d\omega^2,\]
\[\Delta a_t=d\alpha .\]

Thus no knowledge of the translational acceleration of the reference point is needed. An additional tangential relative-velocity measurement supplies the missing sense of rotation because the signed \(\omega\) enters linearly.

\[|\omega|=\sqrt{-\Delta a_r/d},\]
\[\alpha=\Delta a_t/d,\]
\[\omega=(\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_A)\cdot\mathbf e_t/d .\]

A positive radial acceleration difference is inconsistent with a rigid pair under the stated conventions. Flexible mounting, sensor misalignment or treating differently oriented sensor components as parallel can all spoil the projection.

Worked example

Read the motion of a rotating link

At separation 0.200 m the measured acceleration difference is \(-3.20\,\mathbf e_r+0.600\,\mathbf e_t\) m/s². The link has speed magnitude \(\sqrt{3.20/0.200}=4.00\) rad/s and counterclockwise angular acceleration \(0.600/0.200=3.00\) rad/s². A measured relative tangential velocity of \(-0.800\) m/s identifies clockwise rotation. Its speed magnitude is decreasing at this instant.

Second application

Estimate a sensor spacing from a calibrated turn

A second instrument rotates steadily at 5.00 rad/s and records radial acceleration difference \(-3.75\) m/s². Its effective sensor separation is \(3.75/25.0=0.150\) m. A later measurement with the same instrument gives tangential difference \(-0.450\) m/s², implying \(\alpha=-3.00\) rad/s². This calibration uses steady rotation for the length scale and a distinct transient to determine angular acceleration.

What can change between two points on a link?

The point at which a connecting rod drives a slider moves along a straight line although its other end moves around a crank circle. Writing every trajectory as a function of crank angle is possible, but often only one configuration matters. The rigidity constraint transfers instantaneous velocity and acceleration between points on a link. Choose a fixed inertial frame and use the positive out-of-plane direction for counterclockwise angles. Every vector component below refers to these fixed axes.

Write \(r_B=r_A+r_{B/A}\). The vector between two body points keeps its length but changes direction. Its derivative is \(\omega\times r_{B/A}\). Differentiating a second time introduces both the changing magnitude of angular velocity and the changing direction of the relative vector:

\[v_B=v_A+\omega\times r_{B/A},\qquad a_B=a_A+\alpha\times r_{B/A}+\omega\times(\omega\times r_{B/A}).\]

The last term is \(-\omega^2r_{B/A}\) for planar motion. It points along the rod towards \(A\), even when the rod has constant angular speed. Enforce constraints on the actual point \(B\); a slider constrained horizontally has zero vertical velocity and zero vertical acceleration if its guide is straight and fixed.

A crank at its uppermost position

Place crank centre \(O\) at the origin and the slider along the positive horizontal axis. The crank length is 50 mm and the connecting rod length is 130 mm. At the configuration considered its pin is \(A=(0,50)\,\mathrm{mm}\) and slider pin \(B=(120,0)\,\mathrm{mm}\), since \(120^2+50^2=130^2\). The crank rotates counterclockwise at constant 20 rad/s. Accordingly \(v_A=(-1,0)\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(a_A=(0,-20)\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

The rod vector from \(A\) to \(B\) is \((0.120,-0.050)\,\mathrm m\). Its relative velocity is \(\omega_r(0.050,0.120)\). The vertical velocity constraint gives \(0=0+0.120\omega_r\), so \(\omega_r=0\) at this instant and \(v_B=(-1,0)\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The rod is instantaneously translating. That description concerns velocity only. A moment later its two ends no longer have the same velocity.

Recover the slider force input

Since \(\omega_r=0\) here, the relative centripetal term for the rod vanishes. The angular-acceleration term gives \(0=-20+0.120\alpha_r\), hence \(\alpha_r=166.7\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}\). The slider’s horizontal acceleration is \(a_{Bx}=0+0.050(166.7)=8.33\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). At this instant the slider is moving to the left while accelerating to the right. A bare slider mass of 3 kg needs a net force of 25.0 N to the right before guide friction or process forces are considered.

A useful independent check follows from the exact geometry \(x_B=r\cos\theta+\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\theta}\). At \(\theta=\pi/2\) the first derivative with respect to angle is \(-r\); multiplication by crank speed gives −1 m/s. A numerical second derivative must resolve the curvature of this path. Differentiating noisy measured positions twice can produce a large fictitious force, so comparing observed positions with this geometric constraint is often a better starting point.

Change the drive speed

Repeating the same configuration at twice the constant crank speed doubles every point velocity. Its centripetal terms and the constraint-induced angular acceleration scale with the square of that speed, so the predicted slider acceleration becomes 33.3 m/s². This scaling is one reason mechanism inertia loads can grow rapidly with production rate.

Differentiate a coordinate measured along a rotating guide

The rigid-body formula above assumes that the position vector joining the two points is fixed in their common body. A slider moving along a rotating guide violates that assumption. Introduce a guide-fixed unit vector \(e_r\) along the slot and its positive quarter-turn \(e_\theta\). At an inertially fixed pivot the slider’s position is \(r(t)e_r\); the basis changes according to \(\dot e_r=\omega e_\theta,\ \dot e_\theta=-\omega e_r\). Differentiate both the scalar radial position and the changing basis at every step.

\[v=\dot r\,e_r+r\omega\,e_\theta,\qquad a=(\ddot r-r\omega^2)e_r+(r\dot\omega+2\dot r\omega)e_\theta .\]

The factor two in the transverse contribution has two origins. The outward relative velocity vector rotates with the guide, and the speed of the circumferential transport changes as the slider moves outwards. Each contributes \(\dot r\omega\). This Coriolis term is an actual part of the acceleration measured in an inertial frame. In a rotating-frame force balance an apparent Coriolis force of opposite sign must be introduced only if the inertial acceleration has been removed from that balance. Counting both would double the effect.

If the guide’s pivot itself translates, add its inertial acceleration to the expression. For an instantaneously stationary guide \(\omega=0\) eliminates centripetal and Coriolis terms, but a nonzero angular acceleration can still produce transverse acceleration \(r\dot\omega\). This distinction parallels the translating rod in the first worked mechanism.

Determine drive and guide reactions on a rotating table

A 2.5 kg carriage slides outwards along a straight radial slot in a horizontal table. At the given instant \(r=0.30\,\mathrm m\), \(\dot r=0.20\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ddot r=0.50\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\), \(\omega=4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) counterclockwise, and \(\dot\omega=1.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}\). The radial acceleration is \(0.50-0.30(4)^2=-4.30\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) and the transverse acceleration is \(0.30(1)+2(0.20)(4)=1.90\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). A frictionless slot supplies a 4.75 N transverse reaction in the positive rotational direction. The radial actuator must exert a net 10.75 N inward, even though the carriage is moving outwards and accelerating relative to the slot in that direction.

The transverse reaction produces a 1.425 N m moment about the centre. Independently, differentiating the angular momentum \(mr^2\omega\) gives \(m(2r\dot r\omega+r^2\dot\omega)=1.425\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). A table drive that assumes fixed radius would include only \(mr^2\dot\omega=0.225\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), missing most of the required moment in this manoeuvre. Holding angular speed constant would remove this last term but leave the 1.20 N m moment associated with increasing inertia. A torque sensor at the turntable shaft therefore detects radial carriage motion even when no angular acceleration is commanded.

Mechanical power supplies an independent sign check. The rail reaction transmits positive power \(4.75(r\omega)=5.70\) W through the transverse motion at this instant. The inward radial actuator supplies \(-10.75(0.20)=-2.15\) W. Their sum, 3.55 W, equals the instantaneous change of the point mass’s translational energy, \(m(\dot r\,a_r+r\omega a_\theta)=2.5[0.20(-4.30)+1.20(1.90)]\). The rail can supply power because it moves transversely with the table even though the contact has no work in the sliding direction.

Construct angular momentum when the rotation axis is not principal

A planar moment of inertia determines one scalar angular momentum. In three dimensions a balanced body can have angular momentum tilted relative to its angular velocity. About its centre of mass a particle at \(r\) has velocity \(\omega\times r\). Integrate \(r\times(\omega\times r)\) over mass and use the vector triple product. The result is \(H_G=I_G\omega\), with tensor \(I_G=\int[(r^Tr)\mathbf1-rr^T]\,dm\). Its \(x\)-diagonal element is \(\int(y^2+z^2)dm\) and its \(xy\) element is \(-\int xy\,dm\). Equal and opposite points cancel their first moments but add their products of inertia. Symmetry about a coordinate plane, rather than symmetry only about the centre, is sufficient to make the associated product vanish.

In a body-fixed basis rotating at \(\omega\), the external moment components obey \(M_G=I_G\dot\omega+\omega\times(I_G\omega)\). This follows from differentiating \(H_G\) in an inertial frame: the body basis vectors rotate as well as the components. Even with constant body components of angular velocity, a transverse moment can be required to keep a nonprincipal axis fixed.

Model a compact instrument carrier as two equal 0.50 kg masses at \((0.20,0.10,0)\) m and its negative, neglecting the frame mass. Its tensor in kg m² has entries \(I_{xx}=0.010,\ I_{yy}=0.040,\ I_{zz}=0.050,\ I_{xy}=-0.020\). At \(\omega=(20,0,0)\) rad/s, \(H_G=(0.20,-0.40,0)\) kg m²/s. Maintaining that rotation requires \(M_G=(0,0,-8.0)\) N m. There is no offset centre of mass to correct with a single static counterweight. Repositioning the masses to remove the product of inertia, or reorienting the shaft along a principal axis, eliminates this transverse couple in the model. A bearing reaction can reverse the sign convention for the load read by its support. Always state whether the reported moment acts on the rotor or on the frame.

Impulse, angular momentum and impact

Machine dynamics

Find the motion immediately after a short off-centre impact by combining angular impulse with a local restitution condition.

From first principles

Close a short collision with impulse and restitution

During a short collision, contact force can be large while the position changes little. Let \(g^-=v_1^--v_2^->0\) be the relative approach speed along a fixed impact normal for two freely translating masses. Ignore tangential impulse and external impulses during contact. A compressive contact impulse of magnitude \(J\) slows the first mass and speeds the second. Integrating each momentum balance gives

\[v_1^+=v_1^- - J/m_1,\]
\[v_2^+=v_2^- + J/m_2 .\]

Momentum conservation alone leaves the impulse undetermined. A measured normal coefficient of restitution \(e\) supplies the separation speed as a fraction of the approach speed. Substitution then determines the impulse.

\[v_2^+-v_1^+=eg^-,\]
\[J=\frac{(1+e)g^-}{1/m_1+1/m_2}.\]

For a rigid body struck away from its centre the impulse also changes angular momentum by \(\mathbf r\times\mathbf J\). The normal velocity used in restitution must then be evaluated at the contact point, combining centre translation and rotation. A restitution value is a contact model or measurement, whereas the impulse balances follow directly from mechanics.

Derivation and applications

Locate the impact point that leaves a pivot without an impulse

A rapid blow on a hinged body can set it rotating while producing zero instantaneous horizontal impulse at the hinge. Model a rigid pendulum initially at rest, with mass \(m\), centre of mass distance \(a\) from its frictionless pivot, and moment of inertia \(I_O\) about the pivot. Apply a short tangential impulse \(J\) at distance \(b\) along the same radial line. Neglect the impulse from gravity during contact and take positive velocity along \(J\). Angular impulse about the pivot removes the unknown hinge reaction.

\[I_O\omega^+=Jb,\]
\[v_G^+=a\omega^+ .\]

Linear impulse along the impact direction includes both \(J\) and the unknown hinge impulse \(H\). Substitution of angular speed therefore gives the signed hinge impulse as a function of the impact radius. A reaction in the opposite direction from \(J\) corresponds to \(H<0\).

\[m v_G^+=J+H,\]
\[H=J\left(\frac{mab}{I_O}-1\right).\]

Set \(H=0\) and use the parallel-axis theorem \(I_O=I_G+ma^2\). The resulting radius locates the centre of percussion; it lies beyond the centre of mass for any extended body with \(I_G>0\).

\[b_{\rm cp}=\frac{I_O}{ma}=a+\frac{I_G}{ma}.\]

This result concerns the net hinge impulse during a short tangential event. Later hinge forces as the body swings need a separate force balance. A finite-duration or oblique impact also requires examining the other force components.

Worked example

Locate the point on a laboratory pendulum

A uniform slender bar of length 0.900 m and mass 1.50 kg is hinged at one end. Its \(a=0.450\) m and \(I_O=mL^2/3=0.405\) kg m², giving \(b_{\rm cp}=0.600\) m. A tangential impulse of 0.300 N s there produces \(\omega^+=0.300(0.600)/0.405=0.444\) rad/s. Its centre momentum is \(1.50(0.450)(0.444)=0.300\) N s, accounting for all of the applied impulse.

Second application

Infer inertia from a zero-reaction test

A different hinged fixture has \(m=4.00\) kg and \(a=0.180\) m. An instrumented hinge shows vanishing tangential impulse at \(b=0.320\) m. This gives \(I_O=mab=0.2304\) kg m² and \(I_G=0.1008\) kg m². At \(b=0.160\) m, a 0.500 N s impulse should instead produce \(H=0.500(0.160/0.320-1)=-0.250\) N s, a direct sign and calibration check.

Select a time interval and a reference point

For a short collision, a finite change of velocity occurs over a time in which positions change very little. Integrate Newton’s law over that interval. A force that is harmless in an ordinary static calculation can deliver a large impulse if sustained long enough. Conversely, gravity often contributes very little over a millisecond. Compare its estimated impulse \(mg\Delta t\) with the contact impulse before neglecting it.

For planar rigid-body motion the impulse–momentum balances about the mass centre \(G\) and about an inertially fixed point \(O\) are

\[m(v_G^+-v_G^-)=\sum J,\qquad H_O^+-H_O^-=\sum r_{i/O}\times J_i .\]

The superscripts denote states just before and after contact. A hinge can deliver an unknown impulse but exerts no moment impulse about its own centre when modelled as an ideal pin. Choosing that point can eliminate unknowns when calculating angular speed. Kinetic energy is generally lost to deformation, contact vibration and other processes. It cannot supply a conservation equation without an additional elastic-impact assumption.

Apply the contact condition at the points that meet

For a smooth frictionless collision choose the normal from the incoming body towards the target. The separation speed along that normal equals the specified coefficient of restitution \(e\) times the approach speed. Evaluate velocities of the actual points in contact; the target’s mass-centre speed can be different if the force also rotates it. For a contact point at distance \(a\) along a pinned bar and normal perpendicular to the bar, the normal contact velocity immediately after impact is \(a\omega^+\).

Take a small slider of mass 1.0 kg moving at 3.0 m/s into a stationary slender uniform bar of mass 3.0 kg, length 0.60 m, pinned at one end. It strikes at \(a=0.40\,\mathrm m\), perpendicular to the bar. The bar inertia about the pin is \(I_O=m_bL^2/3=0.360\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Let \(J\) be the positive impulse delivered to the bar. The slider exits at \(3-J/1\), and the bar receives angular speed \(Ja/I_O\).

Close the collision and audit its energy

With \(e=0.50\), the restitution condition is \(a(Ja/I_O)-(3-J)=0.50(3)\), giving \(J=4.5/[1+0.4^2/0.36]=3.115\,\mathrm{N\,s}\). Thus \(\omega^+=3.46\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and the slider rebounds slowly at −0.115 m/s. The bar’s contact point recedes at 1.385 m/s, exactly 1.50 m/s faster than the slider after separation.

The initial kinetic energy was 4.50 J. The final energy is \(\tfrac12(1)(0.115)^2+\tfrac12(0.360)(3.46)^2=2.16\,\mathrm J\). The energy loss is physically consistent with the specified restitution. The pin impulse need not vanish although its moment impulse vanished. The bar’s centre moves at \(0.30(3.46)=1.04\,\mathrm{m/s}\); its linear momentum gain happens to equal about 3.12 N s for this impact position, very close to the contact impulse. Indeed \(a=I_O/(m_bL/2)=0.40\,\mathrm m\) is the centre-of-percussion location, for which the ideal transverse pin impulse vanishes exactly.

Determine whether a rough impact sticks or slides

The smooth collision model leaves tangential velocities unaffected. A rough planar impact can also exchange tangential impulse and rotational momentum. Consider a solid disc of radius \(R\) striking a fixed horizontal surface at its lowest point. Take horizontal right and vertical up as positive; angular speed is counterclockwise positive. Just before impact let centre velocities be \(u^-,v^-\) and angular speed \(\omega^-\). The horizontal speed of its contact point is \(g_t^-=u^-+R\omega^-\). A rightward tangential impulse \(J_t\) produces both a centre velocity increase \(J_t/m\) and a positive angular change \(RJ_t/I_G\).

The post-impact contact velocity is \(g_t^+=g_t^-+(1/m+R^2/I_G)J_t\). For the horizontal surface the normal impulse passes through the centre; restitution requires \(J_n=-m(1+e)v^-\) for \(v^-<0\). The tangential impulse needed to eliminate slip is

\[J_t^{\rm stick}=-g_t^-/(1/m+R^2/I_G).\]

Under a simple Coulomb impulse approximation compare this required magnitude with \(\mu J_n\). If it lies inside the bound the contact can finish without tangential slip. Otherwise take the limiting friction impulse opposing the pre-impact relative slip. This elementary model assumes no change in slip direction before the limiting impulse is reached, negligible external impulse, and no tangential elastic restitution. Using only the disc centre’s velocity to form the stick condition would miss its rotational compliance.

Compare two surfaces with the same normal restitution

A solid disc has \(m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(R=0.10\,\mathrm m\), \(I_G=mR^2/2=0.010\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Its centre approaches at \(u^-=3.0,\ v^-=-2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), with zero initial spin. For \(e=0.50\), \(J_n=6.0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and \(v^+=1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\). The inverse tangential effective mass is \(1/2+0.10^2/0.010=1.50\,\mathrm{kg^{-1}}\). Thus \(J_t^{\rm stick}=-2.0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and the friction coefficient needed is at least \(2/6=0.333\).

On a surface with \(\mu=0.20\), \(J_t=-1.2\,\mathrm{N\,s}\), giving \(u^+=2.4\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\omega^+=-12\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). The contact-point speed remains \(2.4-1.2=1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}\) to the right, consistent with the chosen sliding direction. On a surface with \(\mu=0.50\), the sticking impulse is achievable. It gives \(u^+=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\omega^+=-20\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), and zero instantaneous tangential contact speed. The disc still leaves the surface vertically because \(v^+=1.0\). Here sticking means zero relative tangential velocity at separation and does not mean a permanently attached body. The original 13 J of kinetic energy drops to 7.48 J in the sliding case and 7.00 J in the sticking case, consistent with frictional dissipation and a normal restitution below unity.

A high-speed camera that measures only the centre trajectory would determine the change in centre momentum and hence the tangential impulse, provided other horizontal impulses are negligible. Marking the disc edge permits an independent angular check: in the sliding case the ratio \(I_G|\Delta\omega|/(R m|\Delta u|)\) is \(0.010(12)/[0.10(2)(0.60)]=1\). A substantial measured deviation could indicate an additional contact couple, a second impact point or uncertainty in inertia. Measuring spin is particularly important near the transition to sticking because centre velocity by itself cannot establish that the tangential contact speed has vanished.

A marked rim therefore provides an observable that a centre-tracking experiment alone cannot supply.

Finite-amplitude oscillations and model validity

Machine dynamics

Use conservation of energy and a frequency approximation to quantify when a small-angle pendulum model loses timing accuracy.

From first principles

Derive the pendulum period and identify the small-angle assumption

A point mass \(m\) suspended on a massless rigid link of length \(\ell\) feels a gravitational torque \(-mg\ell\sin\theta\) about the hinge. Assume a stationary pivot and negligible drag. Angular momentum balance gives an exact nonlinear equation. Replacing \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) only for small angles in radians gives a linear oscillator and its predicted period.

\[m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta,\]
\[T_{\rm small}=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}.\]

To track finite amplitude, release from rest at angle \(A<\pi\) and use mechanical energy conservation. At any intermediate angle, loss of potential energy equals gained rotational kinetic energy.

\[\tfrac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2=mg\ell(\cos\theta-\cos A),\]
\[T=4\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2g}}\int_0^A\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos A}} .\]

The factor four uses the symmetry of the four quarter cycles. The mass cancels even at large amplitude. The exact restoring torque grows more slowly with deflection than its small-angle approximation, so finite-amplitude swings take longer. Measured variation of period with release angle directly tests the linearisation.

Derivation and applications

Derive the period of an oscillator with purely cubic stiffness

A spring with almost no linear stiffness near equilibrium can have a period set primarily by release amplitude. Consider mass \(m\) on a horizontal guide with restoring force \(-\beta x^3\) for \(\beta>0\). Ignore damping and assume the fitted force law holds between \(-A\) and \(+A\). Release from rest at \(x=A\). Integrating the force law gives a quartic potential, and conservation of energy provides the speed anywhere on the inward leg.

\[\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac14\beta x^4=\tfrac14\beta A^4,\]
\[|\dot x|=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2m}}\sqrt{A^4-x^4}.\]

By symmetry the time from an extreme position to the centre is one quarter of a full cycle. Write \(dt=|dx|/|\dot x|\) and substitute \(u=x/A\) in the integral. This isolates the amplitude dependence without guessing a sinusoidal shape.

\[T=\frac{4}{A}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\beta}}\int_0^1\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^4}}.\]

The dimensionless integral evaluates numerically to 1.31103. Its endpoint singularity is integrable because the speed near the turning point vanishes as the square root of the distance remaining. The resulting period and angular frequency are

\[T=\frac{7.4163}{A}\sqrt{\frac{m}{\beta}},\]
\[\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}=0.8472\,A\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{m}} .\]

The positive cubic restores the mass for displacements of either sign, but its tangent stiffness at zero is zero. The full nonlinear dynamics therefore predicts a period diverging with decreasing amplitude. Even a weak residual linear spring would eventually dominate in that limit.

Worked example

Predict the timing of two releases

Take \(m=0.500\) kg, \(\beta=2.00\times10^5\) N/m³ and \(A=20.0\) mm. The predicted period is \(7.4163\sqrt{0.500/(2.00\times10^5)}/0.0200=0.586\) s. Reducing the release to 10.0 mm doubles the predicted period to 1.17 s. At the larger amplitude the centre-crossing speed is \(A^2\sqrt{\beta/(2m)}=0.179\) m/s.

Second application

Infer stiffness from a measured period

A second fixture of mass 0.800 kg completes a cycle in 0.750 s after release at 15.0 mm. The implied coefficient is \(\beta=m[7.4163/(AT)]^2=3.48\times10^5\) N/m³. Its predicted restoring-force magnitude at release is \(\beta A^3=1.17\) N. An independent static force measurement there tests the dynamic estimate without assuming a sinusoidal motion.

Retain the actual restoring moment

A pendulum gives a practical test of a linear model because its length and release angle are easy to set while its period can be measured over many cycles. Approximate the moving body first by a point mass \(m\) at distance \(l\) from a frictionless pivot. With \(\theta\) measured from vertically downwards, gravity produces torque \(-mgl\sin\theta\). Dividing the rotational balance by \(ml^2\) leaves \(\ddot\theta+(g/l)\sin\theta=0\). The linear small-angle frequency \(\omega_0=\sqrt{g/l}\) follows only after replacing \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) in radians.

The leading correction has a definite sign: \(\sin\theta\simeq\theta-\theta^3/6\). At a large positive angle the restoring force magnitude is smaller than the linear extrapolation. The pendulum should take longer to complete its swing. A sign test like this is useful before calculating a period correction.

Derive an integral valid at finite amplitude

For release from rest at amplitude \(A<\pi\), conservation of mechanical energy gives \(\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl(1-\cos\theta)=mgl(1-\cos A)\). During one quarter of the orbit the angle travels between zero and \(A\). Substitute \(\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(A/2)\sin\psi\) into the time integral. Cancelling common square roots gives

\[T=4\sqrt{l/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\psi}},\qquad k=\sin(A/2).\]

For modest amplitudes expand the integrand as \(1+\tfrac12 k^2\sin^2\psi+\cdots\). The average of \(\sin^2\psi\) over the quarter interval is one half. Therefore \(T/T_0\simeq1+k^2/4\simeq1+A^2/16\) through second order in amplitude. The oscillation period depends on energy, while in the linear spring model all amplitudes share the same period.

Estimate a measurable timing error

Take \(l=0.80\,\mathrm m\), \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) and release at \(A=30^\circ=0.5236\,\mathrm{rad}\). The small-angle period is \(T_0=2\pi\sqrt{0.80/9.81}=1.794\,\mathrm s\). The leading correction is 1.71%, giving about 1.825 s. Over ten full oscillations the predicted delay relative to the small-angle model is roughly 0.31 s. Reducing the release angle to 15° reduces the leading relative correction by a factor of four.

A timing series from a damped experimental pendulum may show the period drifting towards \(T_0\) as its amplitude decays. Measuring only the first release angle while averaging many later periods mixes different amplitudes. To test the finite-amplitude law, use consecutive same-direction crossings to measure each local period and estimate its associated amplitude from neighbouring turning points. The correction above is an expansion around zero amplitude. As \(A\) approaches the inverted equilibrium, the exact integral grows without bound and a low-order polynomial is no longer an adequate approximation.

Check the scale of the neglected term

Retaining one further term in the integrand gives \(1+\tfrac12 k^2\sin^2\psi+\tfrac38 k^4\sin^4\psi\). The quarter-interval average of the fourth power of sine is \(3/8\), so \(T/T_0\simeq1+k^2/4+9k^4/64\). At 30° this gives 1.01738, while the leading expansion in amplitude gave 1.01713. The small difference is measurable only if the uncertainty in pendulum length, release angle and crossing times supports the comparison.

Derive an amplitude-dependent resonance relation

A pendulum softens as its amplitude grows because the sine restoring law rises more slowly than its linear tangent. A mechanical flexure can instead harden through geometry. A simple modal model is \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx+\beta x^3=F\cos\omega t\). For positive \(\beta\) the restoring stiffness increases with excursion. Assume one dominant harmonic and take \(x\approx A\cos(\omega t-\phi)\). Expanding the cubic gives \(x^3=A^3[3\cos(\omega t-\phi)+\cos(3\omega t-3\phi)]/4\). Keeping only the fundamental turns the cubic term into an amplitude-dependent in-phase force.

Balance in-phase and quadrature components against the input, square and add to eliminate \(\phi\). The first-harmonic amplitude estimate satisfies

\[F^2=A^2\left[\left(k+\tfrac34\beta A^2-m\omega^2\right)^2+(c\omega)^2\right].\]

In a lightly damped free motion the same approximation gives a backbone frequency \(\omega_b^2\approx[k+(3/4)\beta A^2]/m\). This is an amplitude-dependent estimate of conservative oscillation frequency, not a new eigenvalue of the linearised equilibrium. At very small amplitude it recovers \(\sqrt{k/m}\), while a negative cubic coefficient tilts the relation towards lower frequencies. If the cubic approximation is only a local expansion, do not extrapolate it until it predicts an unphysical loss of global stiffness.

Identify a hardening spring from two release amplitudes

A test component has moving mass 1.0 kg and small-amplitude stiffness 400 N/m. Low-level ring-down gives \(\omega_0=20\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). At measured oscillation amplitude 20 mm the dominant free frequency is approximately 22 rad/s. Under the weakly nonlinear one-harmonic model, \(\beta\simeq4m(22^2-20^2)/[3(0.020)^2]=2.80\times10^5\,\mathrm{N/m^3}\). At 10 mm the inferred backbone is \(\sqrt{400+0.75(2.8\times10^5)(0.010)^2}=20.52\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), a direct third reading against which to test the fit.

Now drive near 22 rad/s with viscous coefficient \(c=0.80\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}\). At the 20 mm backbone amplitude the spring and inertia fundamentals cancel, so maintaining this motion takes forcing \(F=c\omega A=0.352\,\mathrm N\) in quadrature with displacement. The neglected cubic third-harmonic force amplitude is \(\beta A^3/4=0.56\,\mathrm N\); its displacement effect can nevertheless be small because the oscillator is driven far from its fundamental resonance at 66 rad/s. A separate mode near that frequency would invalidate this simplification. In a forced frequency sweep the amplitude equation can admit several positive roots near resonance. Its algebra does not establish their stability; slow upward and downward sweeps, each with sufficient settling time, can reveal different stable responses and jump transitions.

An initial static measurement tests the sign of the fitted coefficient independently. At a displacement of 20 mm the linear restoring force is \(400(0.020)=8.0\,\mathrm N\); the cubic contribution is \(2.8\times10^5(0.020)^3=2.24\,\mathrm N\). The predicted static force is therefore 10.24 N, and the tangent stiffness is \(400+3(2.8\times10^5)(0.020)^2=736\,\mathrm{N/m}\). This tangent stiffness applies to small vibrations around a held biased equilibrium. Using it directly for an oscillation centred at zero would overestimate the measured amplitude-dependent frequency because the instantaneous stiffness changes during each cycle. Static bias and free oscillation amplitude are physically different tests of the same nonlinear restoring law.

Repeating a decay test around a deliberately shifted equilibrium distinguishes the two experimentally.

A spinning axisymmetric rotor supported at one point can keep a constant tilt under gravity. Predict the rate at which its axis turns, examine the fast-spin approximation and determine when slow steady precession is possible.

Identify the changing direction of angular momentum

Let the centre of mass be a distance l along the symmetry axis from a fixed pivot. The symmetry axis is tilted by \(\theta\) from the upward vertical. The moment of inertia about that axis is \(I_3\), and about either transverse axis through the pivot it is \(I_1\). Define \(\omega_3\) as the total component of angular velocity along the symmetry axis. When the spin angular momentum dominates, \(\mathbf H\simeq I_3\omega_3\mathbf e_3\). Under uniform precession at rate \(\Omega\), its tip traces a horizontal circle of radius \(I_3\omega_3\sin\theta\).

\[|\dot{\mathbf H}|\simeq\Omega I_3\omega_3\sin\theta,\]
\[|\boldsymbol\tau_g|=mgl\sin\theta,\]
\[\Omega_{\rm slow}\simeq {mgl\over I_3\omega_3}.\]

The tilt factors cancel for nonvertical orientations. Gravity changes the direction of angular momentum while doing zero work in a steady precession at fixed height. Reversing the spin changes the direction of slow precession. The support force has zero moment about the pivot.

Keep the angular momentum due to precession

For a regular precession at fixed tilt the transverse angular velocity is the part of \(\Omega\mathbf e_z\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf e_3\). Thus \(\mathbf H=I_1\Omega\mathbf e_z+(I_3\omega_3-I_1\Omega\cos\theta)\mathbf e_3\). The vertical first term is constant. The rotating second term has derivative \(\Omega\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf H\). Equating it to the gravitational torque yields

\[I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+mgl=0 .\]

For \(0<\theta<90^\circ\) and positive spin, two positive real rates exist only if \(D=(I_3\omega_3)^2-4I_1 mgl\cos\theta\ge0\). The smaller root is most accurately evaluated as \(2mgl/(I_3\omega_3+\sqrt D)\), avoiding subtraction of nearly equal numbers at high spin. The larger root corresponds to a different initial angular velocity. Releasing a rotor without providing an appropriate initial precession generally adds nutation.

Write a different equation for each direction of motion

A mass m slides horizontally against a linear spring k. During motion take friction magnitude \(F_k>0\), with force opposing velocity. At rest allow any friction up to the static limit \(F_s\ge F_k\). The moving equation is \(m\ddot x+kx=-F_k\,{\rm sgn}(\dot x)\). Release the mass at \(x=A>0\) with zero speed. If \(kA\le F_s\) static friction can hold it there. If \(kA>F_s\) it starts moving left. While it moves left, friction points right. Shift the coordinate by \(a=F_k/k\) to obtain a homogeneous oscillator:

\[m\ddot x+k(x-a)=0,\]
\[x(t)=a+(A-a)\cos(\omega_n t),\]
\[\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}.\]

At \(t=\pi/\omega_n\) the next turning point is \(x_1=2a-A\). Test static force balance there before starting another sliding segment. If the mass continues, the sign of friction changes and the next cosine is centred on \(-a\). The time for each complete sliding half-cycle is constant despite the decreasing amplitudes. An integration routine that uses only \({\rm sgn}(0)=0\) can generate spurious chatter near the final state.

Recover the amplitude drop from work done by friction

For a full crossing from a positive peak A to a negative peak \(-B\), with \(A,B>0\), friction dissipates \(F_k(A+B)\). The change in spring energy is \(\tfrac12 k(A^2-B^2)\). Cancel the travelled distance to obtain

\[A-B={2F_k\over k},\]
\[A_j-A_{j+2}={4F_k\over k}\]

for successive magnitudes on opposite sides and successive positive peaks, respectively, while sliding continues. An ordinary viscous oscillator instead has an approximately fixed ratio between successive positive peaks. A mixed friction-damping experiment can exhibit both effects. An ideal Coulomb model also permits a final equilibrium anywhere in the static-friction band \([-F_s/k,F_s/k]\).

When the third harmonic matters more than the fundamental

Machine dynamics

A machine applies a short force once per cycle. Its repetition rate looks safely below a structural resonance, but one of its harmonics can lie close to that resonance. Build the forcing spectrum from a pulse and apply a complex response coefficient to each term.

Integrate over the part of the period where force is present

Let an even rectangular force have peak P, period T and on-time dT centred on zero, where \(0<d<1\). Use an even periodic continuation. Its mean is Pd. Sine coefficients vanish by symmetry. With \(\Omega=2\pi/T\), integrate the cosine terms over the active interval and exploit periodicity:

\[F(t)=Pd+\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\cos(n\Omega t),\]
\[a_n={2\over T}\int_{-dT/2}^{dT/2}P\cos(n\Omega t)\,dt={2P\over n\pi}\sin(n\pi d).\]

The coefficients are signed. A negative coefficient adds a phase reversal; taking its magnitude before summing the time response loses that information. The mean drives a static offset. When \(nd\) is an integer, that harmonic vanishes even though the force still has abrupt edges. At a jump the Fourier series converges to the mean of the adjacent values. These details matter when reconstructing force but can have much smaller effects on the filtered displacement.

Solve each harmonic separately and keep phase

For \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F(t)\) the steady particular solution follows from \(H(i\omega)=1/(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)\). Define \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\), \(\zeta=c/(2\sqrt{km})\) and \(r_j=j\Omega/\omega_n\). The jth response contribution is the real part of \(a_jH(ij\Omega)e^{ij\Omega t}\). Thus

\[x(t)={Pd\over k}+\sum_{j=1}^\infty {a_j\over k}\,{(1-r_j^2)\cos(j\Omega t)+2\zeta r_j\sin(j\Omega t)\over(1-r_j^2)^2+(2\zeta r_j)^2}.\]

Far above resonance each force coefficient is attenuated roughly by another \(j^{-2}\) in displacement. Acceleration weights a displacement coefficient by \(j^2\) again, so an adequate displacement truncation can be inadequate for acceleration. Start-up transients are omitted because the expression is the settled periodic response for positive damping.

The first harmonic has \(r=1/3\), so its displacement amplitude is about 5.06 mm. The third is exactly resonant and gives \(1.50/(1000\cdot0.08)=18.8\) mm. A single harmonic based on the largest force coefficient therefore misses the dominant response.

The static offset rises from 2.50 to 3.33 mm, and the first two harmonics remain. Holding mean force fixed instead would require rescaling P. Proving a region of attraction with the energy of a pendulum

Control engineering

Pole locations describe a small neighbourhood of a nonlinear equilibrium. An energy argument can prove that a much larger set of initial conditions converges to the same resting point. Work out the region explicitly and test individual trajectories against its boundary.

Extract a storage function from a torque balance

Measure angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical, let \(\omega=\dot\theta\), and use pivot inertia J and gravitational torque coefficient \(K=mgl\). Add viscous damping b and a control torque \(u=-k_d\omega\) that opposes motion. The closed dynamics are \(J\dot\omega=-K\sin\theta-(b+k_d)\omega\). Define a scalar function with units of energy by adding kinetic energy to the integral of the restoring torque from zero angle:

\[V(\theta,\omega)={1\over2}J\omega^2+K(1-\cos\theta),\]
\[\dot V=J\omega\dot\omega+K\sin\theta\,\omega=-(b+k_d)\omega^2 .\]

A positive damping coefficient ensures energy cannot increase. On the branch \(-\pi<\theta<\pi\), V is positive away from the hanging rest state and zero there. Its derivative is zero whenever velocity vanishes, including ordinary turning points. To justify eventual convergence we need to establish whether any motion can remain indefinitely in this zero-derivative set.

Distinguish a turning point from an invariant state

At \(\omega=0\) a nonzero \(\sin\theta\) produces angular acceleration, taking the trajectory immediately away from zero velocity. Inside the well the only state remaining forever at zero velocity is \((0,0)\). The upright position sits at potential energy \(2K\). Each closed sublevel \(V\le c<2K\) containing the downward rest state is compact and cannot leave the well, because reaching \(\theta=\pm\pi\) would cost at least \(2K\). LaSalle's invariance principle then establishes asymptotic convergence on that component.

\[|\omega_0|<\sqrt{{2K\over J}(1+\cos\theta_0)}\]

This expresses the strict sublevel as a speed allowance at each angle. A state outside it can still settle in the same well because damping removes energy before it reaches the top. The inequality is a sufficient certificate that can be tested from an initial measurement. Exactly at the upright rest state no damping torque acts and the system can remain there.

Compare two releases at the same angle

Let \(J=0.20\) kg m², \(K=2.0\) N m and \(b+k_d=0.30\) N m s/rad. At 60° the potential energy is 1.0 J and the certificate allows speeds below \(\sqrt{30}=5.48\) rad/s. At 4 rad/s the total is 2.6 J, safely below the 4 J barrier. The turning angle cannot exceed \(\arccos(1-2.6/2)=107.5^\circ\) in magnitude. Positive damping reduces the actual maximum further.

At 6 rad/s the initial energy is 4.6 J, outside that guarantee. The instantaneous dissipation is \(0.30(6)^2=10.8\) W, so dissipation before any top crossing may be substantial. Integrate the equation with the same initial angle and compare the true energy trace with the barrier. Moving the damping slider changes the rate of energy loss, while the sublevel calculated from J and K remains fixed.

Raise the starting speed until the initial energy just exceeds the barrier. Compare a damped run with a zero-damping run. Use the two traces to distinguish a guaranteed sublevel from energy actually removed during a particular trajectory.

Two equilibria share one control value

Add a geometric condition

Correct it by solving \(F(x,p)=0\) together with \(t\cdot(y-y_*)=0\), which confines corrections to a line normal to the old tangent. At the fold the Jacobian of this pair is

\[J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\t_x&t_p\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\det J=-t_x.\]

With the tangent aligned with the displacement axis, \(t_x\) is nonzero. The pair remains locally invertible where load control alone fails. This is a change in the computation's coordinate, not removal of the physical multiple equilibria at a fixed load.

Coupled oscillators and normal coordinates

Machine dynamics

Derive and interpret the two modes of a coupled machine, then decide which mode an applied load can excite.

From first principles

Find natural modes from the free force balance

For small motions of a conservative system with several coordinates, linear inertia forces are represented by a positive-definite mass matrix \(M\) and linear restoring forces by a symmetric stiffness matrix \(K\). Collect the free balance and try a motion in which the coordinate amplitudes stay in one fixed ratio as each oscillates at frequency \(\omega\).

\[M\ddot{\mathbf x}+K\mathbf x=0,\]
\[\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\phi\cos(\omega t)\ \Longrightarrow\ (K-\omega^2 M)\boldsymbol\phi=0 .\]

A nonzero shape \(\boldsymbol\phi\) can solve these homogeneous equations only when the coefficient matrix is singular. The determinant condition gives candidate squared frequencies, and the nullspace at each frequency gives a mode shape up to scale.

\[\det(K-\omega^2M)=0,\]
\[K\boldsymbol\phi_j=\omega_j^2M\boldsymbol\phi_j .\]

For identical masses linked by a spring, a common translation leaves that link unstretched and an opposite motion stretches it from both ends. This observation identifies common and differential shapes even before evaluating the determinant. Initial displacement and velocity select their amplitudes; the eigenproblem identifies the motions the structure can support.

Derivation and applications

Quantify the splitting of two weakly coupled modes

When two machines have the same uncoupled natural frequency, adding a weak connecting spring splits that frequency into two. Consider identical sliders of mass \(m\) each attached to ground by stiffness \(k\) and linked to each other by stiffness \(k_c\). Neglect damping and choose displacements in the same direction from equilibrium. The coupling spring responds only to the displacement difference. Adding and subtracting the two force balances therefore gives separate equations for \(s=(x_1+x_2)/2\) and \(d=(x_1-x_2)/2\).

\[m\ddot s+ks=0,\]
\[m\ddot d+(k+2k_c)d=0.\]

Release slider one at \(A\) and slider two at zero, both from rest. The two modal coordinates then both start at \(A/2\). Write \(\omega_s=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\omega_d=\sqrt{(k+2k_c)/m}\) and combine their solutions.

\[x_1=\tfrac A2(\cos\omega_st+\cos\omega_dt),\]
\[x_2=\tfrac A2(\cos\omega_st-\cos\omega_dt).\]

Introduce \(\bar\omega=(\omega_s+\omega_d)/2\) and \(\Delta\omega=\omega_d-\omega_s\). The sum-to-product identities separate the fast motion from slow modulation. When the split is small, the modulation resembles a repeated transfer of motion between the sliders.

\[x_1=A\cos(\bar\omega t)\cos(\Delta\omega t/2),\]
\[x_2=A\sin(\bar\omega t)\sin(\Delta\omega t/2).\]

The first minimum of the slider-one displacement envelope occurs at \(t=\pi/\Delta\omega\). Its instantaneous velocity there need not vanish because the envelope itself is changing. The envelope timing remains identifiable from repeated peaks at weak coupling.

Worked example

Time the exchange of motion

For \(m=1.00\) kg, \(k=100\) N/m and \(k_c=10.5\) N/m, the two frequencies are 10.0 and 11.0 rad/s. The first envelope minimum appears at 3.14 s and the first full recovery of its magnitude at 6.28 s. Releasing the first slider by 8.00 mm sets both modal initial amplitudes to 4.00 mm. Releasing both sliders together instead excites only the slower common motion.

Second application

A second pair has mass 2.00 kg per slider and measured lower frequency 25.0 rad/s. Consecutive magnitude maxima of one slider's envelope occur 5.00 s apart. Thus \(\Delta\omega=2\pi/5.00=1.257\) rad/s and \(k_c=m[(25+1.257)^2-25^2]/2=64.4\) N/m. The independently inferred grounding stiffness is \(m\omega_s^2=1250\) N/m, making the connector about 5.2 percent as stiff.

Two masses allow two independent shapes

Two identical instruments sit on separate spring mounts and are connected by a flexible link. Pull both by the same amount and the link keeps its natural length. Pull them in opposite directions and the link stretches twice as much as either displacement. The two tests reveal distinct restoring mechanisms. A model with one equivalent spring and the sum of both masses would discard one of them.

Let \(x_1,x_2\) be horizontal displacements from equilibrium. Each mass is \(m\), each ground spring has stiffness \(k\), and the coupling spring has stiffness \(k_c\). With light springs, small displacements and no damping, the kinetic energy is \(m(\dot x_1^2+\dot x_2^2)/2\) and the elastic energy is \(k(x_1^2+x_2^2)/2+k_c(x_1-x_2)^2/2\). The coupling energy contains the difference of displacements because it is the extension between its ends.

Separate space from time

Taking the derivative of elastic energy with respect to each coordinate gives \(m\ddot x_1+(k+k_c)x_1-k_cx_2=0\) and the symmetric second equation. Look for \(\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\phi\cos\omega t\). Nonzero modal shapes solve \((\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)\boldsymbol\phi=0\). For this pair the determinant equation is

\[(k+k_c-m\omega^2)^2-k_c^2=0.\]

Taking both signs of the square root gives \(\omega_1^2=k/m\) and \(\omega_2^2=(k+2k_c)/m\). Substitution back yields shapes proportional to \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\). For the first, the connector transmits no incremental force. For the second, the connector acts strongly. Reversing both signs of a plotted eigenvector or multiplying it by a nonzero number describes the same physical mode.

Predict a release test at both instruments

Take \(m=5.0\) kg, \(k=2000\) N/m and \(k_c=1500\) N/m. The two angular frequencies are 20.0 and 31.62 rad/s, or 3.18 and 5.03 Hz. Pull only the first instrument 4.0 mm and release from rest. The initial displacement vector equals \(2(1,1)+2(1,-1)\) mm. Each modal amplitude starts at its corresponding value with zero initial velocity. Hence

\[x_1(t)=2\cos(20t)+2\cos(31.62t),\qquad x_2(t)=2\cos(20t)-2\cos(31.62t)\]

in millimetres. At zero time the second displacement vanishes while the connector exerts 6.0 N on its mass. Its initial acceleration is 1.2 m/s² toward the first instrument’s displacement. This apparent transfer of motion needs neither forcing nor damping: modal displacements add and cancel differently at the two observation points.

Distinguish available modes from visible modes

Define average and relative coordinates \(q_s=(x_1+x_2)/2\) and \(q_a=(x_1-x_2)/2\). Adding or subtracting the original equations decouples them. Equal external forces excite only \(q_s\); equal and opposite forces excite only \(q_a\). Taking the signal \((x_1+x_2)/2\) likewise hides the antisymmetric mode. A missing spectral peak can therefore reveal the symmetry of a load or sensor arrangement, even though the corresponding eigenmode still exists.

If the two masses differ, the simple sum and difference cease to diagonalise the equations. The general route is to form the physical mass and stiffness matrices and solve the symmetric generalised eigenproblem. Distinct modes of an undamped conservative system are orthogonal with respect to mass, \(\boldsymbol\phi_i^T\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_j=0\). This weighted orthogonality is what permits a measured motion or distributed load to be projected into separate modal coordinates.

Predict which mode responds to adding rotary inertia

For a unit mode shape \(\phi\), the quotient \(\omega^2=\phi^TK\phi/(\phi^TM\phi)\) compares stored elastic energy with inertia. Increasing inertia at a coordinate matters most when that coordinate has appreciable motion in the mode. A frame with three tip coordinates offers a way to check this reasoning beyond the symmetric two-mass illustration.

Compare two changes to the same frame: double only the tip mass from 200 to 400 kg, then restore it and double only the tip rotary inertia from 60 to 120 kg m². Predict whether either change can raise a natural frequency for the same stiffness and compute the fractional shift of the third mode in each case. Use the negligible-member-mass row throughout. Derive bounce and pitch modes of a supported machine

A long machine supported on two mounts can translate and rotate as a nearly rigid body even if there is negligible frame bending. Let the mass centre move vertically by \(z\) and rotate by small angle \(\theta\), with positive rotation raising a point to the right. Vertical spring deflections relative to ground are \(z-a\theta\) on the left and \(z+b\theta\) on the right. Measure displacements from static equilibrium. The kinetic energy is \((m\dot z^2+I_G\dot\theta^2)/2\), and the spring energy is \([k_L(z-a\theta)^2+k_R(z+b\theta)^2]/2\).

Differentiating potential energy gives stiffness terms \(K_{zz}=k_L+k_R,\ K_{\theta\theta}=k_La^2+k_Rb^2,\ K_{z\theta}=-k_La+k_Rb\). The mass matrix is diagonal because translation was measured at the centre of mass. A vertical translation produces a moment whenever the weighted mount locations fail to balance, and a pure rotation then also produces a vertical force by reciprocity. If \(k_La=k_Rb\) the modes are pure translation and pure pitch. Equal spring rates alone do not guarantee decoupling when the lever arms differ.

For free motion solve \((K-\omega^2 M)\phi=0\). In mixed coordinates, metres and radians label different displacement entries, so a numerical component ratio needs units or a stated length scale. Measured vertical motions at two actual sensor locations provide an unambiguous observable mode shape.

Separate a heaving mode from a rocking mode

Let \(m=200\) kg, \(I_G=40\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), \(a=b=0.50\) m and mount rates \(k_L=30000,\ k_R=50000\,\mathrm{N/m}\). The stiffness entries are 80000 N/m, 20000 N m/rad and 10000 N in the mixed term. Setting the determinant to zero gives \((80000-200\lambda)(20000-40\lambda)-10000^2=0\) with \(\lambda=\omega^2\). Dividing through by 8000 yields \(\lambda^2-900\lambda+187500=0\). The squared frequencies are 327.5 and 572.5 s⁻², giving 2.88 and 3.81 Hz.

For the lower mode the first row implies \(\theta/z=-(80000-200\times327.5)/10000=-1.45\,\mathrm{rad/m}\). If mass-centre motion has 1 mm amplitude, the mount-point displacements are 1.725 and 0.275 mm, in phase but strongly unequal. The higher mode gives \(\theta/z=+3.45\,\mathrm{rad/m}\), so the left point now moves oppositely to the right. An accelerometer placed close to a mode’s nodal point can scarcely detect it although that mode remains physically present. For the low mode the vertical node lies about \(1/1.45=0.69\) m to the right of the centre, beyond the right mount. Two well-separated measurement points make the rotational contribution identifiable.

A static load experiment can identify the stiffness coupling before a modal test. Apply a known vertical force \(F\) at the centre and no external moment. The pitch-equilibrium equation gives \(\theta=-K_{z\theta}z/K_{\theta\theta}=-0.50z\) rad per metre for these mounts. Substitution into the vertical balance leaves effective static stiffness \(80000-10000^2/20000=75000\,\mathrm{N/m}\). Under 150 N centre load, predicted motion is 2.00 mm and pitch −0.0010 rad, giving left and right mount readings of 2.50 and 1.50 mm. A centre gauge alone would only indicate an apparent softening, while the unequal pair exposes the rotational coupling that later appears in the two mode shapes.

Modal participation and absorber tuning

Machine dynamics

Project external forcing onto vibration modes and determine when a vibration absorber suppresses displacement at a selected frequency.

From first principles

Project a physical force onto a vibration mode

For small undamped motions a structure obeys \(M\ddot{\mathbf x}+K\mathbf x=\mathbf f(t)\) with symmetric mass and stiffness matrices. Its normal modes satisfy \(K\boldsymbol\phi_j=\omega_j^2M\boldsymbol\phi_j\). Expand displacement in these mode shapes, using time-dependent coefficients \(q_j\). Premultiply the result by the transpose of one mode.

\[\mathbf x=\sum_j\boldsymbol\phi_j q_j,\]
\[\sum_j(\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf TM\boldsymbol\phi_j\,\ddot q_j+\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf TK\boldsymbol\phi_j\,q_j)=\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf T\mathbf f .\]

For distinct frequencies, symmetry makes different modes orthogonal under both matrices. The corresponding off-diagonal products vanish, leaving a separate scalar oscillator for the selected mode.

\[m_i\ddot q_i+m_i\omega_i^2q_i=Q_i(t),\]
\[m_i=\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf TM\boldsymbol\phi_i,\]
\[Q_i=\boldsymbol\phi_i^\mathsf T\mathbf f .\]

The force projection has a direct interpretation as work per unit modal displacement. A symmetric load can have zero projection onto an antisymmetric mode despite a large total force. The displacement at a particular sensor is obtained only after adding its components from all excited modes.

Derivation and applications

Determine how a point force appears in collocated modal measurements

The displacement measured at the force location can have a zero between two structural resonances. Consider an undamped linear structure with two relevant modes, distinct natural frequencies \(\omega_1<\omega_2\) and modal masses \(m_j>0\). Apply a harmonic point force of complex amplitude \(F\) at coordinate \(a\) and measure displacement at the same coordinate. For modal shape component \(\phi_j(a)\), the generalised force is \(\phi_j(a)F\), and reconstruction at the sensor supplies a second factor of that component.

\[q_j=\frac{\phi_j(a)F}{m_j(\omega_j^2-\omega^2)},\]
\[H_{aa}=\frac{x_a}{F}=\frac{A_1}{\omega_1^2-\omega^2}+\frac{A_2}{\omega_2^2-\omega^2},\]
\[A_j=\frac{\phi_j(a)^2}{m_j}.\]

The residues \(A_j\) are positive when both modes are visible. Between the two poles the first denominator is negative and the second positive. Set the sum to zero and multiply through by both denominators to find the antiresonance.

\[A_1(\omega_2^2-\omega_z^2)+A_2(\omega_1^2-\omega_z^2)=0,\]
\[\omega_z^2=\frac{A_1\omega_2^2+A_2\omega_1^2}{A_1+A_2}.\]

A zero in the measured displacement can therefore result from cancellation of two finite modal displacements. Its position also reveals their relative participation without requiring the absolute force calibration.

\[\frac{A_1}{A_2}=\frac{\omega_z^2-\omega_1^2}{\omega_2^2-\omega_z^2}.\]

Separated force and response locations can give signed residues and change the interlacing. With damping a finite minimum commonly replaces the exact zero, so using its frequency as an exact antiresonance is an approximation.

Worked example

Predict the dip of a two-mode stage

For \(\omega_1=10.0\) rad/s, \(\omega_2=20.0\) rad/s and \(A_1=2A_2\), the squared zero frequency is \((2\times400+100)/3=300\) s⁻², hence \(\omega_z=17.3\) rad/s. At that frequency the first contribution is \(2A_2/(100-300)=-0.0100A_2\) and the second is \(A_2/(400-300)=+0.0100A_2\). The sensor is stationary while both modes respond.

Second application

Recover a relative participation from a measured minimum

A different collocated test gives resonances at 30.0 and 50.0 Hz and a negligible-damping minimum at 40.0 Hz. Ratios of squared angular frequencies can equally be expressed through squared frequencies in hertz. The residue ratio is \((40^2-30^2)/(50^2-40^2)=7/9=0.778\). With equal modal masses the first shape magnitude at the force point is \(\sqrt{7/9}=0.882\) of the second.

Recover motion from mode amplitudes

Knowing a structure’s natural frequencies does not yet tell you which frequencies a particular actuator will excite. For a linear undamped system write \(M\ddot x+Kx=f(t)\) and let \(\phi_j\) be a real mode shape solving \(K\phi_j=\omega_j^2M\phi_j\). Assume a complete set of independent modes and expand the displacement \(x=\sum_j\phi_j q_j\). Multiplying the equation by \(\phi_i^T\) uses mass and stiffness orthogonality to remove other modal coordinates.

\[m_i\ddot q_i+k_iq_i=\phi_i^Tf(t),\qquad m_i=\phi_i^TM\phi_i,\quad k_i=\omega_i^2m_i .\]

Multiplying a mode shape by a constant changes the numerical modal mass and generalised force, while the reconstructed physical motion remains invariant. Thus a tabulated mode shape needs its normalisation if it is used to compute a physical response. A forcing pattern whose projection vanishes excites none of that mode from rest, even if the excitation frequency equals its natural frequency.

Select a mode by choosing the actuator pattern

Suppose two identical masses 2 kg each have mode shapes \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\), with frequencies 10 and 20 rad/s. Both modal masses are 4 kg. A 12 N harmonic force on the first mass projects 12 N onto each mode in this normalisation. At a forcing frequency of 5 rad/s the undamped steady modal amplitudes are \(12/[4(10^2-5^2)]=0.0400\,\mathrm m\) and \(12/[4(20^2-5^2)]=0.00800\,\mathrm m\). Recombination gives first and second physical amplitudes 0.0480 and 0.0320 m.

Now distribute the same total force as 6 N on each mass in phase. The first projection stays 12 N and the second becomes \(6-6=0\). The masses now both move 0.0400 m. Relocating or phasing actuators can therefore change observed vibration without changing any mass, spring or natural frequency.

Cause the primary displacement to vanish

Attach an auxiliary mass \(m_a\) by spring \(k_a\) to a primary mass \(m_p\) grounded by spring \(k_p\). A harmonic force of amplitude \(F\) acts on the primary mass. Seek displacement amplitudes \(X_p,X_a\). The absorber equation is \(-k_aX_p+(k_a-m_a\omega^2)X_a=0\). At \(\omega^2=k_a/m_a\) this enforces \(X_p=0\). The primary equation then gives \(X_a=-F/k_a\). The absorber’s spring force balances the excitation while the primary mass is stationary in this ideal steady response.

For \(m_a=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\), a measured forcing frequency of 20 Hz requires \(k_a=m_a(2\pi20)^2=7896\,\mathrm{N/m}\). An 8 N forcing amplitude gives absorber travel amplitude 1.01 mm. The two coupled resonances bracket this tuned antiresonance; startup or a change of shaft speed can pass through them. Damping limits the resonant peaks but generally prevents the exact cancellation obtained from these undamped equations.

Choose where to observe cancellation

At the tuned absorber frequency a sensor on the primary reports zero ideal steady displacement, while a sensor on the attached mass records finite motion. A force transducer between the bodies measures the full applied excitation in the opposing phase. Moving the sensor can therefore turn the same test from an apparent null into a readily measurable response, without altering the underlying eigenfrequencies.

Project a spatially distributed load onto a flexible member

A beam or string has motion distributed along its length. Its modal forcing is determined by a spatial integral, analogous to the vector dot product for discrete coordinates. For a taut uniform string of length \(L\), linear density \(\mu\) and constant tension \(H\), small transverse displacement \(w(x,t)\) obeys \(\mu w_{tt}-H w_{xx}=p(x,t)\). The tension term follows from the difference in vertical tension components on a short segment. Assume both ends fixed in displacement and neglect bending stiffness, damping and the change of tension caused by stretching. The modes are \(\phi_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)\), with frequencies \(\omega_n=(n\pi/L)\sqrt{H/\mu}\).

Expand \(w=\sum\phi_n q_n\), multiply by \(\phi_j\) and integrate over the length. The mass integral is \(m_j=\mu\int_0^L\phi_j^2dx=\mu L/2\). Integration by parts of the tension term gives the modal stiffness \(k_j=m_j\omega_j^2\), with the fixed-end boundary terms vanishing. The modal input becomes \(Q_j=\int_0^Lp\phi_jdx\). Under a uniform harmonic load amplitude \(p_0\),

\[Q_j=\frac{p_0L}{j\pi}[1-(-1)^j].\]

Even modes have zero generalised force because their positive and negative portions cancel exactly. A point load at \(x_0\) instead yields \(Q_j=F\phi_j(x_0)\). Moving an actuator or changing a load footprint can thus suppress a resonance by symmetry without changing the material, tension or natural frequency.

Estimate a cable’s displacement from its first few modes

A laboratory cable spans 2.0 m under 200 N tension and has linear density 0.50 kg/m. Its wave speed is \(20\,\mathrm{m/s}\), so \(f_1=20/(2L)=5.0\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(m_j=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\). Apply a distributed harmonic pressure-equivalent line load \(p_0=0.40\,\mathrm{N/m}\) at 2.0 Hz. The spatial convergence pattern can be estimated before adding many terms. For odd mode number \(n\) well above the forcing frequency, the uniform-load projection decreases as \(1/n\) while modal stiffness increases as \(n^2\). Each displacement contribution therefore decreases roughly as \(1/n^3\). A point actuator at midspan instead gives modal projections of essentially alternating constant magnitude for odd modes, producing only a \(1/n^2\) displacement decay. Hence a truncation that accurately predicts the response to a distributed flow load can be less accurate for an impulsive local tap. Strain or slope predictions may require still more terms because a spatial derivative introduces an additional factor of mode number. Choose the retained modes for the actual load and observed quantity.

The uniform cable problem admits an independent harmonic calculation without modal truncation. Set \(w(x,t)=W(x)\cos\Omega t\). The governing equation gives \(W''+\kappa^2 W=-p_0/H\), \(\kappa=\Omega\sqrt{\mu/H}\). Symmetry about midspan and fixed ends select \(W(x)=[p_0/(\mu\Omega^2)]\{\cos[\kappa(x-L/2)]/\cos(\kappa L/2)-1\}\), provided the denominator is nonzero. How a longitudinal pulse reflects from a change in rod section

Machine dynamics

A tap on one end of a rod reaches a receiver after a finite delay. Two rods joined end to end can even return a pulse before the receiver sees a far-end reflection. Derive propagation speed and distinguish the displacement amplitude from the energy carried by a pulse.

From the cut force to a travelling disturbance

Measure the small axial displacement \(u(x,t)\) from a stress-free reference. In a slender homogeneous rod of area A, tensile force is \(N=EA u_x\). A slice of length dx and mass \(\rho A\,dx\) accelerates because its right and left forces differ by \(N_x dx\). For constant properties, cancelling A gives

\[\rho A u_{tt}=(EAu_x)_x,\]
\[u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx},\]
\[c=\sqrt{E/\rho}.\]

Either \(u=f(x-ct)\) or \(u=g(x+ct)\) satisfies this equation by differentiation. Curves of constant phase move right or left with speed c. Long-wavelength assumptions suppress lateral inertia and radial modes. Rod area cancels from wave speed but remains in the force carried at given particle velocity. For a right-moving pulse \(v=u_t=-cu_x\), so tensile force \(N=-Zv\), with mechanical impedance \(Z=\rho Ac=EA/c\). The sign reverses for a left-moving wave.

Match velocity and force at a bonded junction

Let amplitudes I, R and T denote incident, reflected and transmitted particle velocity at the junction. A massless, perfectly bonded interface requires equal velocities and transmitted force. Apply the preceding sign convention separately to each travelling direction. The two equations determine two unknowns.

\[I+R=T,\]
\[-Z_1 I+Z_1 R=-Z_2 T,\]
\[{R\over I}={Z_1-Z_2\over Z_1+Z_2},\]
\[{T\over I}={2Z_1\over Z_1+Z_2}.\]

The instantaneous power transported to the right is \(-Nv=Zv^2\) for a single right-moving wave. For the same pulse shape the reflected energy fraction is \((R/I)^2\) and the transmitted fraction is \((Z_2/Z_1)(T/I)^2\). They sum to one. A transmitted velocity amplitude exceeding the incident amplitude can thus be compatible with conservation of energy.

A stepped rod and a pulse-echo observation

Join two aluminium rods of areas 80 and 160 mm², \(E=70\) GPa and \(\rho=2700\) kg/m³. Both have \(c=5092\) m/s, but the second impedance is twice the first. An incident velocity of 0.30 m/s produces a reflected −0.10 m/s and a transmitted 0.20 m/s. The reflected pulse carries one ninth of the energy. Reversing the experiment makes the reflection positive and the transmitted velocity 0.40 m/s for the same incident amplitude. The two energy fractions are unchanged on reversal.

A receiver at the tap detects a reflected pulse 0.40 ms after launch, before any far-end reflection contaminates it. The return path covers the distance twice, so the inferred junction is \(c(0.00040)/2=1.02\) m away. Measure pulse velocity rather than strain when comparing the signs to the plot. Incident tensile force has sign opposite incident velocity with the convention used here.

Keep the incoming pulse fixed and change the area ratio from 2 to 0.5. Determine which velocity component reverses sign and compare reflected energy fractions. Explain the difference by writing power balance for the two experiments.

Why the frequencies of a bending beam are not equally spaced

Machine dynamics

A distributed beam can bend into infinitely many shapes. Derive the relation between wavelength and oscillation frequency, then compare the measured response of a beam with the mode family predicted by its supports.

Combine transverse force, moment and curvature

Take a straight uniform beam with small transverse deflection \(w(x,t)\), density \(\rho\), area A and second moment of area I about the bending axis. Euler–Bernoulli kinematics keeps cross-sections perpendicular to the centreline. The bending resistance is proportional to curvature \(EI w_{xx}\). For a load-free part of the beam the gradient of moment supplies shear and the gradient of shear accelerates the mass. With restoring forces opposing deflection, the resulting equation is

\[\rho A\,w_{tt}+EI\,w_{xxxx}=0 .\]

The fourth spatial derivative calls for four boundary conditions. At a simple support the displacement vanishes and the bending moment is zero, hence \(w=w_{xx}=0\). A clamp instead fixes displacement and slope. Interchanging a pin and a clamp changes the allowed shape family even if the span and material remain fixed.

Obtain the simply supported series

Try \(w=W\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)\). Two spatial derivatives bring down \(-k^2\), and four give \(+k^4\). Substitution balances the inertia and stiffness terms. The conditions at x=0 hold for every k in this sine family. At x=L the sine must vanish, yielding

\[\omega^2={EI\over\rho A}k^4,\]
\[k_n={n\pi\over L},\]
\[f_n={n^2\pi\over 2L^2}\sqrt{EI\over\rho A}.\]

A long travelling sinusoid has phase velocity \(\omega/k=k\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)}\). The group velocity \(d\omega/dk\) is twice that value in this model. A localized pulse contains many wavelengths, which can therefore separate as it propagates. Predictions at wavelengths near the thickness require accounting for rotary inertia and shear deformation.

Choose sensor positions as carefully as the frequency range

Use a 1 m steel strip 20 mm wide and 4 mm thick with \(E=200\) GPa and \(\rho=7800\) kg/m³. For bending about the thin direction \(I=bh^3/12\) and \(I/A=h^2/12\). The first three simply supported frequencies are roughly 9.18, 36.7 and 82.7 Hz. Doubling the width alone doubles stiffness and mass together and leaves these frequencies unchanged. Doubling thickness doubles each frequency. Shortening the span by 20% increases all three by \(1/0.8^2=1.56\).

At the centre a displacement pickup sees \(\sin(n\pi/2)\). It sees the first mode at its antinode and misses the second mode entirely. At the quarter point it sees that second mode strongly. An absent spectral peak can therefore result from excitation or sensing at a node. Move the measuring point before adjusting a fitted damping value to explain its absence.

Find a common node of the first and third modes and a location that suppresses the second mode alone. Change the thickness and then the span. Use the resulting frequencies to test whether each scaling is linear or quadratic.

Estimating a cantilever frequency from energy

Machine dynamics

A clamped beam carries a small sensor at its tip. Its precise mode shape is unknown. Use the static deflection shape to approximate the fundamental frequency and see why concentrating a small mass at the tip has a large effect.

Choose a displacement field that respects the support

Write \(w(x,t)=q(t)\phi(s)\) with \(s=x/L\), taking \(q\) as tip displacement. Choose the shape from a uniform cantilever under a static transverse tip force. The bending moment varies linearly from root to tip. Integrating curvature twice with \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\), then normalizing at \(s=1\), gives \(\phi=s^2(3-s)/2\). The shape satisfies both essential clamp conditions. Its free-tip shear need not match the exact unloaded vibration mode. The energy formulation can use such admissible trial shapes.

Compute effective stiffness and mass from the same field

The strain energy density of a slender beam in bending is \(\tfrac12 EI w_{xx}^2\). Its translational kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\rho A w_t^2\) per unit length. Changing variables from x to s introduces different powers of L into the two integrals. Include a point mass M at the tip, neglecting its rotary inertia.

\[U={EI q^2\over 2L^3}\int_0^1(\phi'')^2\,ds,\]
\[K={\dot q^2\over2}\left[\rho AL\int_0^1\phi^2\,ds+M\right].\]

Since \(\phi''=3(1-s)\), the stiffness integral is 3. Squaring \(s^2(3-s)/2\) and integrating \( (9s^4-6s^5+s^6)/4\) gives \(33/140\). The Euler–Lagrange equation for q is consequently a single oscillator equation with

\[\omega_R^2={3EI/L^3\over(33/140)\rho AL+M}.\]

For M=0 this gives the dimensionless coefficient \(\sqrt{140/11}=3.5675\). Solving the exact unloaded cantilever condition \(\cosh\beta\cos\beta=-1\) gives \(\beta_1^2=3.5160\). The estimate is high by about 1.5%. The Rayleigh minimum principle explains that direction of error for conservative systems with admissible trial shapes. Restricting motion to a single shape restricts the set over which the energy quotient can attain its minimum.

Choose a sensor after estimating its dynamic effect

Take \(EI=10\) N m², \(L=0.50\) m and total beam mass 0.40 kg. The tip stiffness is 240 N/m and the distributed effective mass is only 0.0943 kg. The estimate gives 8.03 Hz without a sensor and 6.49 Hz with a 0.05 kg tip sensor. The sensor weighs one eighth of the beam, yet it has over half the effective mass associated with the chosen tip coordinate.

Place the same sensor at half-span instead. There \(\phi(0.5)=0.3125\). Its kinetic energy contributes \(M\phi(0.5)^2=0.00488\) kg to the effective mass, so the estimate rises to 7.83 Hz. The sensor measures a smaller local motion as it perturbs the frequency less. Compare that trade-off at different attachment positions with the plot.

Weak forms and finite elements

Numerical partial differential equations

Derive a variational statement by integration by parts, assemble element matrices for a one-dimensional rod and explain what the Galerkin solution minimises.

From first principles

Obtain the weak diffusion problem by moving one derivative

For a stationary diffusion problem \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla u)=f\), suppose temperature is prescribed on part of the boundary and the outward flux \(q_n=-k\nabla u\cdot\mathbf n\) on the remainder. A classical second derivative may not exist across a material interface. Instead multiply the conservative equation by a smooth test function \(v\) that vanishes where \(u\) is prescribed. The divergence theorem moves one derivative from the flux onto \(v\):

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega=\int_\Omega fv\,d\Omega-\int_{\Gamma_N}vq_n\,d\Gamma .\]

Approximate \(u\) with continuous element shape functions \(N_j\) that are differentiable inside each element. Choose each allowable shape function as the test function. Linearity then yields element contributions

\[K_{ij}^{(e)}=\int_e k\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j\,d\Omega,\]
\[f_i^{(e)}=\int_e fN_i\,d\Omega .\]

Neighbouring elements contribute to the same global unknown at a shared node. Adding their contributions enforces weak equilibrium. The flux sign in the boundary term follows from an outward-positive definition; switching to prescribed inward heat supply reverses that sign in the load vector.

Derivation and applications

How a Robin boundary enters both the stiffness matrix and the load

A convecting boundary couples an unknown surface temperature to an ambient temperature. Unlike a prescribed heat source it contributes to both sides of the assembled equations. Consider steady diffusion \(-\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)=s\) in a two-dimensional region of unit thickness, with \(k>0\). On a straight boundary segment impose the empirical film law \(-k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n=h(T-T_\infty)\) using the outward normal. Multiply the field equation by a test function \(v\) vanishing on prescribed-temperature boundaries, integrate, and apply the divergence theorem:

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla v\cdot\nabla T\,dA-\int_{\partial\Omega}v k\nabla T\cdot\mathbf n\,ds=\int_\Omega vs\,dA .\]

Substitution on the film boundary \(\Gamma\) places the unknown surface term on the left and the ambient contribution on the right:

\[\int_\Omega k\nabla v\cdot\nabla T\,dA+\int_\Gamma hvT\,ds=\int_\Omega vs\,dA+\int_\Gamma hvT_\infty\,ds\]

with any other natural boundary terms added separately. To assemble one linear edge of length \(\ell\), use edge functions \(N_1=1-\xi\), \(N_2=\xi\), \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Insert the nodal interpolation for \(T\) and choose each \(N_i\) as test function. Constant \(h,T_\infty\) give

\[K_\Gamma=\frac{h\ell}{6}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\]
\[f_\Gamma=\frac{h\ell T_\infty}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The off-diagonal term is present because both nodes influence temperature across the same edge. In the limit \(h=0\) the boundary is insulated. A very large film coefficient constrains the temperature toward ambient but can introduce a large stiffness contrast.

Worked example

Assemble a convecting edge by hand

Use a one-metre out-of-plane thickness, \(\ell=0.300\) m, \(h=20.0\) W/(m² K) and \(T_\infty=290\) K. Then \(K_\Gamma\) has diagonals \(2.00\) W/K and off-diagonals \(1.00\) W/K. The load is \((870,870)\) W. At nodal temperatures \(300\) and \(310\) K the film residual \(K_\Gamma T-f_\Gamma\) is \((40,50)\) W. The sum \(90\) W agrees with \(h\ell[(300+310)/2-290]\).

Second application

Test a constant solution before using the boundary routine

Set both temperatures to \(290\) K, the ambient value. Each row has sum \(3.00\) W/K, so every residual is \(3(290)-870=0\). If the code includes the ambient load but forgets the matrix, it predicts \(1740\) W of heating at thermal equilibrium. This constant-field patch test detects sign errors and omission of the surface matrix independently of the volume mesh.

Reduce the differentiability required by equilibrium

A rod is fixed at \(x=0\), pushed by a distributed load \(f\) and by an end force \(t\) at \(x=L\). Axial displacement \(u\) satisfies \(-(EAu')'=f\), \(u(0)=0\) and \(EAu'(L)=t\). The product \(EA\) is positive. A trial profile assembled from linear pieces has jumps in its derivative, so differentiating it twice pointwise is unsuitable. Instead multiply the strong equation by a test displacement \(v\) vanishing at the fixed end and integrate by parts once.

The resulting equation is \(\int_0^L EA\,u'v'\,dx=\int_0^L fv\,dx+t v(L)\). Only first derivatives are required. The fixed displacement is imposed directly on trial and test spaces; the end traction appears in the load functional. This clarifies why prescribing both force and displacement independently at the same end can overconstrain a second-order problem. The test functions represent allowable virtual displacements, and the integral identity expresses equality of internal and external virtual work.

Assemble one element and connect it to its neighbour

On an element from \(x_i\) to \(x_{i+1}\) of length \(h\), use shape functions \(N_1=1-s,\ N_2=s\) with \(s=(x-x_i)/h\). Their physical derivatives are \(-1/h,\ 1/h\). For constant \(EA\), integrating products \(EA N'_aN'_b\) gives the matrix \((EA/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A constant distributed load contributes \(fh(1,1)^T/2\). Summing element contributions for a shared global node enforces balance there without prescribing the internal traction manually.

Take \(L=1,\ EA=1,\ f=2,\ t=0\) in consistent units and use two equal elements. After eliminating the fixed displacement, the stiffness matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&-2\\-2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and the load is \((1,0.5)^T\). Solving gives \(u(0.5)=0.75,\ u(1)=1\). The exact solution is \(2x-x^2\) and agrees at both nodes. Inside the first element at \(x=0.25\), the linear interpolant gives 0.375, whereas the exact value is 0.4375. Nodal agreement can coexist with a substantial interior error.

Assemble a physical triangle from a reference element

A line element has one length scale. A triangle can stretch, rotate and shear, so the element area alone cannot determine its diffusion matrix. Start with the reference vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\) and the linear shape functions \(1-\xi-\eta,\xi,\eta\). Send the reference triangle to a physical cell with vertices \(P_0,P_1,P_2\) by \(x=P_0+J(\xi,\eta)^T\), where the columns of \(J\) are \(P_1-P_0\) and \(P_2-P_0\). For a nondegenerate triangle, the chain rule and area transformation give

\[\nabla_x N_i=J^{-T}\nabla_{\xi,\eta}\widehat N_i,\qquad dx\,dy=|\det J|\,d\xi\,d\eta .\]

To see why the inverse transpose occurs, take an arbitrary displacement \(d\widehat x\). Both gradients must describe the same change in a shape function. Thus \((\nabla_xN_i)^TJ\,d\widehat x=(\nabla\widehat N_i)^Td\widehat x\) for every such displacement. For unit isotropic conductivity, the transformed gradients are constant and the physical stiffness entries reduce to \(K_{ij}=|T|\nabla N_i\cdot\nabla N_j\), with \(|T|=|\det J|/2\). A constant cell load \(f\) contributes \(f|T|/3\) at each vertex because each linear shape function has mean \(1/3\).

A sheared cell and an exact affine-field check

Take \(P_0=(0,0), P_1=(2,0), P_2=(1,1)\). The columns of \(J\) are \((2,0)\) and \((1,1)\), so the cell area is \(1\). Applying \(J^{-T}\) to the three reference gradients gives \((-1/2,-1/2),(1/2,-1/2),(0,1)\). Their dot products and the load \(f=3\) yield

\[K=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0&-1/2\\0&1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&-1/2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad b=(1,1,1)^T.\]

Every row sums to zero because a constant field has zero gradient. For a more discriminating test use \(u=x+2y\), with vertex values \(U=(0,2,3)^T\). Weighting the three gradients by these values gives \((1,2)\). The physical integral of its squared gradient equals \(1(1^2+2^2)=5\), and direct multiplication gives \(U^TKU=5\). A transposed mapping error can pass the constant test and fail this second one.

Identify the energy that is best approximated

Let \(a(u,v)=\int EA\,u'v'\) and denote the external work by \(\ell(v)\). The discrete solution \(u_h\) satisfies \(a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)\) for every test function from its finite-dimensional space. The continuous solution satisfies the same equation for every such test, so \(a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\). This Galerkin orthogonality means the error is perpendicular to the trial space in the energy inner product. For any other admissible discrete \(w_h\), write \(u-w_h=(u-u_h)+(u_h-w_h)\) and expand its squared energy norm. The cross term vanishes and \(\|u-w_h\|_a^2=\|u-u_h\|_a^2+\|u_h-w_h\|_a^2\).

Thus the conforming Galerkin solution is the best energy approximation in its space for this symmetric linear problem and exact integration. The statement concerns a specific norm and model. It does not guarantee pointwise optimal stresses or carry unchanged into nonsymmetric transport problems. In the rod example the computed strain is constant on each element, replacing a linearly varying exact strain. Refinement improves this derivative field even when a table of nodal displacements already looks exact.

Recover a support reaction

The reaction at a prescribed-displacement node is hidden if one inspects only the reduced interior system. Assemble all element internal forces \(Ku_h\) before discarding constrained rows, then subtract the full external load vector. The entries at free degrees of freedom should be near zero; the entry at a prescribed degree of freedom gives the reaction in the chosen sign convention.

In the uniformly loaded rod, the first element strain is \((0.75-0)/0.5=1.5\). Its internal nodal force at the left is \(-1.5\), while the first node receives an external distributed-load share of \(+0.5\). Their difference \(-2\) is the support reaction. It balances the total applied load \(2\cdot1=2\). Looking only at the derivative in the first element would have reported magnitude 1.5, missing that load share. A reaction from assembled balance and a stress recovered by pointwise differentiation are related but distinct quantities on a finite mesh.

Obtain actual norm errors from the Galerkin property

For \(-u''=f\) on the unit interval with homogeneous Dirichlet values, the elementary energy inner product is \(a(u,v)=\int u'v'\). Let \(I_hu\) be the continuous piecewise-linear interpolant of a sufficiently smooth exact solution at mesh nodes. On each element its slope is the average of \(u'\). Every linear finite-element test function \(v_h\) has constant derivative on that element. Consequently \(\int_E(u-I_hu)'v_h'=v_h'[u-I_hu]_{\partial E}=0\). Summing proves global energy orthogonality.

The Galerkin solution satisfies the same orthogonality, and positive definiteness gives uniqueness. Thus \(u_h=I_hu\) in this one-dimensional, constant-coefficient problem with exactly integrated load and matching boundary values. Nodal errors vanish, although the field and derivative between nodes can still be inaccurate. Variable coefficients inside an element generally destroy the simple telescoping argument because the factor multiplying \(v_h'\) is no longer constant. The result also should not be transferred to multidimensional triangulations without proof.

Compare nodal, field and flux accuracy for one solution

Choose \(u=x(1-x)\), so \(f=2\). On an element starting at \(a\) write \(s=x-a\in[0,h]\). Subtract the straight interpolant from the parabola to get \(u-u_h=s(h-s)\), vanishing at both nodes and attaining \(h^2/4\) at the element centre. Its derivative is \(h-2s\); the exact and numerical slopes agree at the midpoint but differ by magnitude \(h\) at each endpoint taken from that element. Testing only the midpoint slope or the nodal values would entirely conceal these other discrepancies.

Compute the squared energy error on this element: \(\int_0^h(h-2s)^2ds=h^3/3\). With \(1/h\) equal elements, the global energy error is \(h/\sqrt3\). Similarly the element squared field error is \(\int_0^hs^2(h-s)^2ds=h^5/30\), giving global \(L^2\) error \(h^2/\sqrt{30}\). For \(h=1/4\), the two errors are approximately 0.14434 and 0.01141; the maximum field error is 0.015625. Halving the mesh width halves the energy error but divides the field error by four. All these measurements concern the very same numerical solution whose nodal error is exactly zero.

For a physical diffusion flux with constant conductivity one, flux error equals minus derivative error. A cell-averaged flux test is also special here: the numerical slope equals the exact average slope on each element for the same reason used in the proof. To verify local flux resolution, compare at more than a midpoint or a cell average and retain the measure used for convergence. A reported order is a statement about an observable and a norm as well as a finite-element space.

These formulas also identify a misleading manufactured test: if an automated comparison stores only nodal differences, the estimated order is undefined because both coarse and fine errors vanish. Adding independently evaluated quadrature points inside elements exposes the field error immediately. To check a flux implementation, use a quadrature rule capable of resolving the squared linear derivative error; a one-point midpoint rule would again report zero despite the nonzero exact energy norm.

Wave speeds and numerical dispersion

Numerical partial differential equations

Derive the leapfrog dispersion relation, use the Courant condition to distinguish boundedness from accurate travel time and initialise a standing-wave calculation consistently.

From first principles

Derive the numerical wave dispersion relation by advancing a single mode

Consider a one-dimensional homogeneous wave with speed \(c\), uniform spatial grid \(h\) and time increment \(\Delta t\). Centred second differences approximate both accelerations and spatial curvatures using the values on either side of their evaluation point. The governing equation \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\) becomes

\[u_j^{n+1}-2u_j^n+u_j^{n-1}=C^2(u_{j+1}^n-2u_j^n+u_{j-1}^n),\]
\[C=c\Delta t/h .\]

To determine what the grid does to one travelling component, insert \(u_j^n=e^{i(kjh-\omega n\Delta t)}\) and divide by the value at \((j,n)\). In both directions the pair of opposite phases combines to a cosine. Using \(2-2\cos z=4\sin^2(z/2)\) then gives

\[\sin^2(\omega\Delta t/2)=C^2\sin^2(kh/2).\]

Long wavelengths recover \(\omega\simeq c|k|\). Short waves can propagate at a different phase speed even in a perfectly homogeneous physical medium. Requiring a real \(\omega\) for every resolved \(k\) gives \(C\le1\). This derivation concerns interior grid modes; domain boundaries can introduce reflected waves as a separate numerical effect.

Derivation and applications

Derive a conserved energy for the centred wave update

A time-stepping method can support a numerical travelling wave while transporting its phase at a slightly incorrect speed. For a second diagnostic, ask whether total discrete vibration energy remains bounded. Consider a uniform one-dimensional periodic grid for \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\), unit mass density and no forcing. Denote the forward spatial difference by \(D\) and the half-step velocity by \(v^{n+1/2}=(u^{n+1}-u^n)/\Delta t\). Summation by parts on this grid gives \(\langle v,D^\mathsf TD u\rangle=\langle Dv,Du\rangle\) using the grid inner product. The centred step can be written

\[v^{n+1/2}-v^{n-1/2}=-c^2\Delta t D^\mathsf TD u^n .\]

Take the inner product with \((v^{n+1/2}+v^{n-1/2})/2\). The left side becomes a difference of two kinetic energies. On the right use \(\Delta t(v^{n+1/2}+v^{n-1/2})=u^{n+1}-u^{n-1}\) and move \(D\) to the other factor:

\[E^{n+1/2}=E^{n-1/2},\]
\[E^{n+1/2}=\tfrac12\|v^{n+1/2}\|^2+\tfrac12c^2\langle Du^{n+1},Du^n\rangle.\]

The potential term couples consecutive time levels. Writing it in terms of the midpoint displacement \(\bar u=(u^{n+1}+u^n)/2\) identifies when the invariant bounds the solution:

\[E^{n+1/2}=\tfrac12\|v\|^2-\tfrac18 c^2\Delta t^2\|Dv\|^2+\tfrac12c^2\|D\bar u\|^2 .\]

Since \(\|Dv\|\le2\|v\|/\Delta x\), the kinetic part is bounded below by \((1-C^2)\|v\|^2/2\), with \(C=c\Delta t/\Delta x\). The invariant controls nonconstant waves for \(C<1\). An exact but indefinite conservation law alone would not guarantee a bounded numerical solution.

Worked example

Read a timestep from a material and grid

Take \(c=100\) m/s, \(\Delta x=0.0500\) m and \(\Delta t=0.400\) ms. Then \(C=0.800\) and the bound preserves at least \(1-C^2=0.360\) of the usual half-step kinetic term even in the worst spatial mode. The customary limiting step from this argument is \(0.500\) ms.

Second application

Show why equality of successive energies can mislead

Take the alternating grid mode for which \(\|Dv\|=2\|v\|/\Delta x\), and choose adjacent displacements whose midpoint is zero. At \(C=1.20\) the invariant for this state is \(E=(1-1.44)\|v\|^2/2=-0.220\|v\|^2\). Negative energy here is a property of the numerical quadratic form. A solver can preserve it exactly and still exhibit amplitude growth.

Distinguish transport of phase from transport of energy

A taut string with small transverse displacements and fixed tension obeys \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\). The physical speed \(c\) is independent of wavelength in this ideal one-dimensional model. A discrete solver can transport long waves at nearly the correct speed while short waves arrive late. In a pulse comprising many wavelengths, this artificial dependence on frequency distorts its shape even in a stable computation. A phase comparison at a probe provides information beyond monitoring maximum amplitude.

Use centred second differences in both time and space, with \(C=c\Delta t/h\). Solving explicitly for the new level gives \(u_i^{n+1}=2u_i^n-u_i^{n-1}+C^2(u_{i-1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i+1}^n)\). To initialise, expand \(u(\Delta t)=u(0)+\Delta t\,v(0)+(\Delta t)^2 c^2u_{xx}(0)/2+\cdots\). Replacing \(u_{xx}(0)\) by the same spatial difference gives a first level consistent with the initial velocity and acceleration. Simply copying the starting displacement to level one can introduce a spurious launch velocity.

Derive the discrete dispersion relation

Take a travelling mode \(u_i^n=\exp[\mathrm{i}(i\theta-n\omega_h\Delta t)]\) with spatial phase \(\theta=kh\). Inserting it makes the temporal second difference proportional to \(-4\sin^2(\omega_h\Delta t/2)\) and the spatial second difference proportional to \(-4\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Thus on the positive-frequency branch

\[\sin(\omega_h\Delta t/2)=C\sin(kh/2).\]

A real \(\omega_h\) exists for all resolvable real spatial phases only when \(C\leq1\). If \(C>1\), the shortest wave makes the right-hand side exceed one; a temporal amplification root then lies outside the unit circle. Even when stable, the ratio of numerical to physical phase speed is \(2\arcsin[C\sin(\theta/2)]/(C\theta)\). A fine spatial sample makes \(\theta\) small and the ratio tends to one. At \(C=1\) the ideal uniform one-dimensional interior stencil reproduces the wave phase on the resolved branch. Boundaries, material transitions and multiple dimensions require separate analysis.

Estimate a travel-time error

Take \(C=0.5\) and four points per wavelength, so \(\theta=\pi/2\). Then \(\omega_h\Delta t=2\arcsin[0.5\sin(\pi/4)]=0.72273\) rad. The physical advance is \(C\theta=\pi/4=0.78540\) rad. The numerical phase speed is about 0.920 of the true speed. After ten physical periods, its accumulated phase is about 9.20 cycles. A stable solution can therefore be nearly one oscillation behind while retaining a visually plausible amplitude.

Group velocity is \(d\omega_h/dk\), obtained by differentiating the dispersion relation. On this branch \(v_g/c=\cos(\theta/2)/\sqrt{1-C^2\sin^2(\theta/2)}\), about 0.756 in the four-point example. A narrow-band packet’s envelope and an individual crest then propagate at different numerical speeds. When interpreting a probe trace, say whether the reported arrival corresponds to a crest, an envelope or a threshold. Repeat the calculation at additional points per wavelength using the same Courant number to isolate spatial-phase improvement from changes in the time integration.

Interpret the grid’s travelling domain of dependence

In one explicit wave step, a node uses only itself and its two nearest neighbours. After \(n\) steps its numerical domain of dependence extends at most \(n\) grid cells to either side. The continuous wave at the same time can depend on initial data a distance \(cn\Delta t\) away. For convergence that physical domain must fit inside the numerical one, giving \(c\Delta t\leq h\). This reproduces the Courant condition from geometry without inserting a Fourier mode. It is a necessary information-speed test; by itself it does not establish stability or accuracy of an arbitrary scheme.

As an example, with \(c=340\) m/s and \(h=0.01\) m, the step bound is about 29.4 microseconds. A 20 microsecond step satisfies it but need not give an accurate answer for a mode represented by only three or four points per wavelength. Time-of-flight information and wavelength resolution are related to different discretisation choices. Document both when comparing two wave calculations.

Derive a reflected wave from a discrete outgoing condition

For a one-dimensional wave travelling right at speed \(c\), the outgoing continuum condition is \(u_t+cu_x=0\) at the right boundary. It annihilates an exact right-travelling waveform. A numerical boundary update using forward time and backward space gives \(u_N^{n+1}=(1-C)u_N^n+C u_{N-1}^n\), where \(C=c\Delta t/h\). A wave satisfying the interior discrete dispersion relation need not satisfy this one-sided relation. Even when the continuum boundary is perfectly absorbing, a mismatch of discrete phase relations can send energy back into the computational domain.

Let \(z=e^{-i\omega\Delta t}\), with the incident and reflected grid waves represented by \(e^{i\theta(j-N)}z^n\) and \(R e^{-i\theta(j-N)}z^n\). At the boundary their sum is \(1+R\) times \(z^n\). Substitution in the outgoing update and grouping incident and reflected terms gives

\[R=-\frac{z-1+C-Ce^{-i\theta}}{z-1+C-Ce^{i\theta}}.\]

This amplitude ratio assumes a homogeneous grid, a propagating frequency and distinct incident and reflected branches. It compares each wave at the boundary itself. Signals measured farther inward acquire additional phase and travel-time factors and can interfere if both arrive in the same observation window.

Calculate a reflection generated by numerical mismatch

Use the centred wave scheme with \(C=1/2\) and grid angle \(\theta=\pi/2\), four cells per wavelength. The interior relation gives \(\sin(\omega\Delta t/2)=(1/2)\sin(\pi/4)=1/(2\sqrt2)\), hence \(z=0.75-i\sqrt7/4\). The incident mismatch in the numerator is \(0.25-i(\sqrt7/4-0.5)\); the reflected denominator is \(0.25-i(\sqrt7/4+0.5)\). Squaring the magnitudes gives \((3-\sqrt7)/4\) and \((3+\sqrt7)/4\), respectively. Therefore \(|R|=\sqrt{(3-\sqrt7)/(3+\sqrt7)}\approx0.2505\). Roughly one quarter of the incident amplitude is reflected solely by this discrete boundary treatment.

For incident and reflected branches on the same symmetric grid, their group-speed magnitudes match. The corresponding reflected energy fraction is \(|R|^2\approx0.0627\), about six percent. That is small enough for a short experiment to appear satisfactory until the pulse returns to a sensor. Testing only the incident travel time would miss this boundary error. Compare a time window before reflection with the expected window for the return journey.

At \(C=1\) and \(0<\theta<\pi\), the right-moving interior root is \(z=e^{-i\theta}\). The numerator vanishes exactly and the boundary update transmits that branch without reflection. This exact compatibility is special to this one-dimensional grid, this step size and this outgoing condition. Varying the propagation angle in two dimensions, changing an interface spacing or using a higher-order interior stencil changes the phase relation that the boundary operator needs to match.

There is a practical way to isolate this reflection in a pulse computation. Record incident and outgoing velocity or flux at the same interior sensor, and choose the duration so that the reflected signal arrives after the incident pulse has passed. Normalise amplitudes in the same physical variable, and avoid dividing a pressure peak by a displacement peak. A broadband pulse contains many values of the grid angle, so its returned waveform can change shape even when each monochromatic component obeys the calculated reflection coefficient.

Why two finite-element wave solvers propagate the same mode at different speeds

Numerical partial differential equations

Build the element mass matrix by integrating shape functions and track the effect of diagonal lumping on the wave spectrum.

From a continuum wave to nodal masses

For \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\), multiply by a test function and integrate by parts. On a periodic line the boundary contributions cancel. A piecewise-linear element of width \(h\) uses shape functions \(1-\xi,\xi\), with \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Integrating products of shape functions gives \(M_e=(h/6)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Integrating derivative products gives \(K_e=(c^2/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Row-sum lumping replaces each mass row by its sum on the corresponding diagonal, \(h/2\). This preserves the mass associated with a uniform velocity but changes the response to a velocity that varies within an element.

Diagonalise the assembled periodic problem

A periodic nodal mode has \(u_j=e^{ij\theta}\). Each neighbour contributes \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) relative to node \(j\). Thus \(K\) multiplies by \(2c^2(1-\cos\theta)/h\), consistent \(M\) by \(h(2+\cos\theta)/3\), and lumped \(M\) by \(h\). Dividing stiffness by mass gives the discrete squared frequency. Relative to the exact frequency \(c\theta/h\),

\[\frac{\omega_L}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta},\]
\[\frac{\omega_C}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2+\cos\theta}}.\]

Both tend to one for long waves; the leading quadratic errors have opposite signs. This is a spatial effect, present even with exact time integration.

Two wavelengths in one mesh

At four cells per wavelength \(\theta=\pi/2\). The lumped ratio is \(2\sqrt2/\pi\approx0.9003\); the consistent ratio is about 1.1027. One wave solver lags by roughly ten per cent and the other leads by roughly ten per cent. At eight cells per wavelength the ratios move much closer to one, but repeated propagation over many wavelengths can still accumulate visible phase error.

At the shortest grid mode \(\theta=\pi\), the consistent frequency is \(\sqrt{12}c/h\) and the lumped frequency \(2c/h\). A central-difference time integrator stable for \(h_t\omega\le2\) therefore requires a smaller step for the consistent mass. Accuracy at one wavelength and stability at the grid scale are different design criteria.

Vibrations of monoatomic and diatomic chains

Condensed matter and band theory

Derive normal modes from a local force law and identify the physical motion behind the acoustic and optical branches.

From first principles

Turn a local spring law into a phonon dispersion relation

Consider identical atoms of mass \(m\) spaced by \(a\) along a chain. Write \(u_j\) for the displacement from the equilibrium position of atom \(j\). For small changes of bond length, expand the bond energy to second order about its minimum. Its first derivative vanishes and its positive second derivative \(K\) is the measured bond stiffness. The bond on the right pulls with force \(K(u_{j+1}-u_j)\); the left bond contributes \(K(u_{j-1}-u_j)\). Newton’s law gives

\[m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}) .\]

Translation of a normal mode by one site can change its phase but not its shape. Insert \(u_j=Ue^{i(kja-\omega t)}\), divide by the common amplitude, and simplify:

\[-m\omega^2=K(e^{ika}+e^{-ika}-2)=-4K\sin^2(ka/2),\]
\[\omega=2\sqrt{K/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.\]

For wavelengths much larger than \(a\), \(\sin(ka/2)\simeq ka/2\). The slope gives the sound speed \(a\sqrt{K/m}\). At \(k=0\) all atoms move together and no bond stretches, which explains the zero frequency. More than one atom per primitive cell permits internal relative motion even at \(k=0\), the ingredient needed for an optical branch.

Derivation and applications

Count the modes that crowd against a chain’s band edge

Why can a narrow frequency bin near the top of a vibration band contain a substantial share of all modes? Take a monoatomic chain of \(N\) equal masses \(m\) with one longitudinal displacement each. Impose periodic ends and use identical harmonic nearest-neighbour springs of stiffness \(K\) with spacing \(a\). The frequency on the positive half of the first zone rises monotonically to \(\omega_m=2\sqrt{K/m}\). Inverting that branch gives the wavevector bounding the states below a trial frequency,

\[k(\omega)=\frac{2}{a}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_m}\right),\]
\[0\leq\omega\leq\omega_m .\]

Allowed wavevectors are spaced by \(2\pi/(Na)\). Counting the interval of width \(2k(\omega)\) includes both propagation directions. In a large chain the fractional count and its derivative per atom are therefore

\[F(\omega)=\frac{2k(\omega)}{2\pi/(Na)}\frac{1}{N}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_m}\right),\]
\[g(\omega)=\frac{dF}{d\omega}=\frac{2}{\pi\omega_m\sqrt{1-(\omega/\omega_m)^2}}.\]

Here \(g\,d\omega\) is a fraction of the modes. The normalization follows from \(F(\omega_m)=1\), as required by the count of independent displacements. Near the edge the slope of the dispersion tends to zero, so evenly spaced wavevectors map into progressively smaller frequency intervals. This causes an integrable singularity in the continuum density. A finite spectrometer bin always contains a finite count. A measured spectrum can also weight modes differently through a probe’s coupling to atomic motion.

Worked example

Count modes within the upper tenth of the band

For \(N=10{,}000\), the interval between \(0.9\omega_m\) and the upper edge contains \(N[1-(2/\pi)\arcsin(0.9)]\simeq2871\) modes, allowing a mode or two for endpoints. A uniform distribution in frequency would give 1000. A nearly stationary wave packet near the upper edge and a large number of modes per unit frequency have the same origin in the small group velocity.

Second application

Use the spectral median to infer a spring stiffness

An ideal chain with atomic mass \(5.00\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}\) has half of its longitudinal modes below an ordinary frequency of \(4.00\,\mathrm{THz}\). Setting \(F=1/2\) gives \(\omega_{1/2}/\omega_m=\sin(\pi/4)\). The upper frequency is \(5.66\,\mathrm{THz}\), and \(K=m(2\pi f_m)^2/4=15.8\,\mathrm{N/m}\). The frequency ratio is unchanged by conversion to angular frequency, but the final stiffness requires that conversion.

From bond extension to a travelling displacement

Let atoms of mass \(m\) on a line be coupled by springs of stiffness \(K\), spaced \(a\) apart. The spring to the right pulls site \(n\) by \(K(u_{n+1}-u_n)\); the left contributes \(K(u_{n-1}-u_n)\). A uniform shift produces no force. Translation symmetry suggests \(u_n=u e^{i(kna-\omega t)}\). Substitution gives

\[\omega^2(k)=\frac{2K}{m}(1-\cos ka)=\frac{4K}{m}\sin^2(ka/2).\]

The sound speed at long wavelength is \(a\sqrt{K/m}\), consistent with a continuum rod whose one-dimensional stiffness is \(Ka\) and whose mass per length is \(m/a\). At \(k=\pi/a\) neighbouring atoms move in opposition and the slope of the dispersion vanishes. A wave packet made of nearby \(k\)'s moves with the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\), which can differ greatly from the phase velocity \(\omega/k\) near the boundary.

Introduce an internal coordinate

Place masses \(m,M\) alternately, with spacing \(a/2\) between neighbours and cell period \(a\). Let their displacements in cell \(n\) be \(Ue^{ikna}\) and \(Ve^{ikna}\) times the same time factor. The two force balances contain sums of neighbouring amplitudes. For example the A mass sees B in cell \(n\) and in cell \(n-1\), giving \(m\omega^2 U=2KU-K(1+e^{-ika})V\). Exchanging the masses and conjugating the phase gives the second row. Requiring a nonzero solution yields

\[mM\omega^4-2K(m+M)\omega^2+4K^2\sin^2(ka/2)=0 .\]

There are two nonnegative solutions for \(\omega^2\) per \(k\). With \(N\) cells under periodic boundary conditions this gives \(2N\) longitudinal modes, exactly matching the number of independent displacements. At \(k=0\) the lower branch has \(U=V\) and zero frequency. For the upper branch the centre of mass stays fixed, \(mU+MV=0\), with \(\omega^2=2K(1/m+1/M)\). An electric field can excite some optical modes in polar crystals; the branch name alone does not guarantee optical activity.

A gap and a sound speed answer different questions

At the zone boundary \(1+e^{ika}=0\), so the sublattices decouple. If \(M=4m\), the two edge frequencies are \(\sqrt{K/(2m)}\) and \(\sqrt{2K/m}\), a factor of two apart. When the masses become identical the two folded branches meet, recovering the original monoatomic chain in a doubled cell. An apparent branch boundary created by plotting in a smaller zone need not be a physical frequency gap.

Expand the determinant for small \(k\) on the lower branch. Since \(\omega^2\) scales as \(k^2\), discard the term of order \(k^4\). This gives \(v_s=a\sqrt{K/[2(m+M)]}\). The two springs in a cell act in series for long waves, while both masses contribute to inertia. The sound speed alone determines a stiffness-to-mass ratio; measuring the upper branch supplies independent information about the internal motion.

Convert a mode count into thermal predictions

Quantisation assigns a harmonic mode levels \(\hbar\omega(j+1/2)\). The thermal part of its mean energy is \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\). Low-temperature excitations thus select long-wavelength acoustic modes while high-frequency optical modes remain scarcely populated. Counting states in a three-dimensional sphere gives a low-frequency density proportional to \(\omega^2\) for linear branches. Rescaling the energy integral by \(\hbar\omega/k_BT\) gives thermal energy proportional to \(T^4\) and heat capacity proportional to \(T^3\). Dimensionality and linear dispersion are essential assumptions behind that power.

Count thermally generated vacancies

A mode calculation for a perfect crystal starts with one atom on every site. Heating a real sample can leave some atoms at a surface or reservoir and some lattice sites empty. How large should that defect population be even at thermal equilibrium? Consider \(N\) equivalent sites and \(n_v\) independent vacancies. At fixed temperature and pressure let \(g_f\) be the Gibbs cost of making one vacancy and transferring its atom to the reservoir. It may contain local vibrational contributions. We count the separate entropy of choosing the missing sites explicitly.

There are \(W=N!/[n_v!(N-n_v)!]\) such arrangements. With \(x=n_v/N\) and macroscopic occupations, Stirling’s formula gives

\[\frac{G-G_0}{N}=xg_f+k_BT\left[x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right].\]

Minimizing with respect to \(x\) gives \(g_f+k_BT\ln[x/(1-x)]=0\). The second derivative is positive. Thus \(x=1/(1+e^{g_f/(k_BT)})\). For positive \(g_f\gg k_BT\) there are few vacancies and \(x\simeq e^{-g_f/(k_BT)}\). Each vacancy costs energy, but at tiny \(x\) the number of possible positions grows fast enough to favour a nonzero population. A finite microscopic specimen can still contain zero vacancies at a given instant.

Take \(g_f=0.800\,\mathrm{eV}\) as constant over the chosen range and \(k_B=8.6173\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV/K}\). At \(900\,\mathrm K\), \(k_BT=0.07756\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(x=3.31\times10^{-5}\). A region with \(10^{15}\) sites contains an expected \(3.31\times10^{10}\) empty sites. At \(600\,\mathrm K\) the equilibrium fraction drops to \(1.91\times10^{-7}\). Rapid cooling may leave an excess because vacancies must migrate to sinks before the concentration relaxes. The formation free energy in this calculation sets an equilibrium population. A migration barrier would enter the time to attain it.

A light substitution can localize a vibration

An infinite monatomic chain has a continuous vibration band, while a single altered atom can support a vibration spatially concentrated near one site. Let identical springs of stiffness \(K\) connect masses \(m\) on a chain of spacing \(a\), except the central mass at site zero is \(M\). Seek a symmetric localized solution with alternating neighbouring amplitudes \(u_n=A(-q)^{|n|}e^{-i\omega t}\) for \(0<q<1\). Alternation is motivated by the top of the perfect-chain band where adjacent atoms oscillate out of phase. Exponential decay supplies the localization.

At any ordinary site away from the impurity, the equation \(-m\omega^2u_n=K(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_n)\) gives \(m\omega^2=K(2+q+q^{-1})\). Since \(q+q^{-1}>2\), this frequency lies above the perfect-chain band edge \(\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{K/m}\). At site zero both neighbours have displacement \(-q u_0\), giving \(M\omega^2=2K(1+q)\). Eliminating \(\omega\) and cancelling \(1+q\) yields

\[q=\frac{M}{2m-M},\qquad \omega^2=\frac Km(2+q+q^{-1}).\]

The physical condition \(0<q<1\) requires a lighter central mass \(0<M<m\). A heavy isotope can still scatter travelling waves or produce resonant response within the band. This ansatz predicts no exponentially localized symmetric mode above the band for the heavier substitution. Its absence follows from the admissible decay factor rather than an assumption that a heavy impurity cannot move.

Predict a localized spectral peak

Take host atomic mass \(m=4.00\times10^{-26}\) kg, spring stiffness \(K=40.0\) N/m, lattice spacing \(a=0.300\) nm and impurity \(M=m/2\). The host upper angular frequency is \(2\sqrt{40.0/(4.00\times10^{-26})}=6.32\times10^{13}\) rad/s. For the impurity \(q=1/3\), so \(\omega^2=(16/3)K/m\), giving \(\omega=7.30\times10^{13}\) rad/s or \(f=11.6\) THz. The ordinary chain ends near \(10.1\) THz.

The neighbour amplitude is minus one third of the central amplitude, and the second neighbour has plus one ninth. Expressing the absolute envelope as \(e^{-|n|a/\xi}\) gives localization length \(\xi=a/\ln3=0.273\) nm. As \(M\) approaches \(m\) from below, \(q\) approaches one, localization length diverges and the extra frequency merges with the band edge. A finite-chain calculation should therefore vary chain length before claiming that a very weak impurity mode is spatially localized: a box shorter than its decay length cannot separate that broad tail from a standing host mode.

Spatially resolved displacement data can provide two independent estimates of impurity mass. Invert the decay-factor relation to obtain \(M/m=2q/(1+q)\) from neighbouring displacement amplitudes. Separately insert the measured localized frequency and the same host force constant in the central equation of motion. Agreement tests the assumption that the impurity changes only inertia. A substituted atom may also change the two neighbouring springs: in that case the observed frequency and decay factor can no longer be fitted by altering mass alone. This inversion distinguishes an isotope-like substitution from a stronger local bonding perturbation.

A weak second impurity far away perturbs the localized mode only through its exponentially small tail. Bringing two such impurities within a few decay lengths allows the modes to mix and split in frequency, offering another test of their spatial localization.

Energy and geometric integration

Numerical ordinary differential equations

Compare three time updates on a conservative oscillator and explain why a method can have small short-term state error yet distort an orbit over many periods.

From first principles

Build an oscillator update out of two area-preserving shears

Take one undamped oscillator of mass \(m>0\) and stiffness \(k>0\). Its canonical position \(q\) and momentum \(p\) satisfy \(\dot q=p/m\) and \(\dot p=-kq\). Exact trajectories run along energy ellipses and preserve phase-plane area. If both slopes are taken at the left endpoint, the update and its area factor are

\[\begin{pmatrix}q_{n+1}\\p_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&h/m\\-hk&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}q_n\\p_n\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\det=1+h^2k/m .\]

A positive factor greater than one at each step makes the numerical orbit spiral outward. Instead update the momentum at fixed position, then move the position using that new momentum. Both operations are shears:

\[p_{n+1}=p_n-hkq_n,\]
\[q_{n+1}=q_n+h p_{n+1}/m.\]

The product matrix has diagonal entries \(1-h^2k/m\) and \(1\), with off-diagonal product \(-h^2k/m\), giving determinant one. Its eigenvalues form a conjugate pair on the unit circle when \(|2-h^2k/m|<2\). This calculation explains the bounded oscillatory behaviour for \(0<h\sqrt{k/m}<2\). Preserving area alone would also allow one real mode to grow while another shrinks. It does not establish either stability or exact conservation of the original energy.

Derivation and applications

Which quadratic energy does symplectic Euler preserve?

An undamped linear oscillator conserves the physical sum of kinetic and potential energy. A geometric time integrator may conserve a nearby quadratic form instead. Take \(q''=-\omega^2q\) with constant \(\omega\) and unit mass. Scale velocity by setting \(u=\dot q/\omega\) so that both variables have units of length, and write \(z=h\omega\). Use the kick-then-drift version of symplectic Euler. Its step is two triangular shears:

\[u'=u-zq,\]
\[q'=q+zu'=(1-z^2)q+zu .\]

To find a preserved quantity, test a quadratic form \(I=q^2+u^2+\alpha qu\). The changes of the ordinary sum and the product follow directly by multiplication and collection:

\[q'^2+u'^2-q^2-u^2=z(q'u'-qu),\]
\[I'-I=(z+\alpha)(q'u'-qu).\]

Choosing \(\alpha=-z\) cancels the entire change at every step. Completing the square identifies the range where the invariant confines trajectories to closed ellipses:

\[I=q^2+u^2-zqu=(u-zq/2)^2+(1-z^2/4)q^2.\]

For \(|z|<2\) the form is positive definite. The physical energy is proportional to \(q^2+u^2\) and can oscillate, but the invariant bounds it. As \(h\to0\) the tilted ellipse approaches the exact energy circle. At the endpoint \(|z|=2\) the quadratic form ceases to bound both variables; preservation of an indefinite form alone does not guarantee stable trajectories.

Worked example

Follow a moderate step exactly

Take \(\omega=2.00\) s⁻¹, \(h=0.250\) s, \(q=1.00\) m and \(u=0\). Then \(z=0.500\), \(u'=-0.500\) m and \(q'=0.750\) m. The ordinary sum falls from \(1.00\) to \(0.8125\) m². But \(I'=0.8125-0.500(0.750)(-0.500)=1.00\) m². A plot restricted to physical energy at one time could mistake the discrepancy for damping.

Second application

Diagnose a seemingly exact conservation test outside stability

Choose \(z=2.50\) and the same initial \((q,u)=(1,0)\). One step gives \((-5.25,-2.50)\). The ordinary sum is \(33.8125\), whereas the cross term is \(2.50(13.125)=32.8125\), so \(I'=1\). The invariant remains exact while the amplitude grows. Testing positivity of the conserved quadratic form therefore supplies information absent from a conservation-only check.

Choose the diagnostic before choosing the method

A mass on an ideal spring with unit mass and stiffness obeys \(\dot q=p,\ \dot p=-q\). The state moves on circles with conserved energy \(H=(q^2+p^2)/2\). At short times a state norm measures trajectory error, but over many periods there are independent questions about amplitude, phase and energy. A time-stepping method that systematically increases the orbit radius can produce an artificial runaway even on this simplest stable physical system.

Forward Euler updates \(q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_n,\ p_{n+1}=p_n-hq_n\). Square and add: the cross terms cancel, leaving \(H_{n+1}=(1+h^2)H_n\). The phase space area factor of the update matrix is its determinant \(1+h^2\), also greater than one. Backward Euler instead contracts energy by \(1/(1+h^2)\). The second method is stable in the sense of bounded states, but it removes mechanical energy from an undamped model. The sign of drift explains much more here than a solitary endpoint comparison.

Update momentum before position

The momentum-first symplectic Euler scheme sets \(p_{n+1}=p_n-hq_n\) and \(q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_{n+1}\). Its update matrix in \((q,p)\) has rows \((1-h^2,h)\) and \((-h,1)\). Its determinant is one and trace is \(2-h^2\). For \(0<h<2\), the two eigenvalues are a complex conjugate pair of unit magnitude because their product is one and the trace lies strictly between −2 and 2. The resulting numerical frequency satisfies \(2\cos\theta=2-h^2\). Thus phase error still accumulates even if the amplitudes remain bounded.

Direct substitution also verifies an exact discrete invariant \(\tilde H=(q^2+p^2-hqp)/2\). For \(h<2\) the corresponding quadratic form is positive definite, so constant \(\tilde H\) confines the state to an ellipse. The exact physical energy can fluctuate around its initial level as the point moves around that ellipse. Area preservation alone would not guarantee this behaviour: for \(h>2\) the determinant remains one but real reciprocal eigenvalues permit growth. The eigenvalue and invariant calculations supply the missing qualification.

Check a long-time forecast with numbers

Start at \((q,p)=(1,0)\) with \(h=0.1\). Forward Euler gives \((1,-0.1)\) and \(H=0.505\) after one step. After 100 steps the exact multiplicative law predicts \(H=0.5(1.01)^{100}=1.3524\). Symplectic Euler instead gives \((0.99,-0.1)\), \(H=0.49505\), but \(\tilde H=0.5\) again. The first step alone might suggest that the second scheme dissipates energy. The invariant predicts that the decrease cannot continue monotonically over many periods.

Implicit midpoint, \((y_{n+1}-y_n)/h=J(y_n+y_{n+1})/2\) with the skew-symmetric oscillator matrix \(J\), preserves the exact quadratic energy. Dot both sides with the midpoint state. The left side is proportional to \(H_{n+1}-H_n\) and the right side is zero by skew symmetry. Its rotations need not accumulate the exact physical phase, since the rotation angle per step is \(2\arctan(h/2)\). A meaningful long-time study therefore records measured period, energy range and reversibility alongside any state error after a specified number of cycles.

Test an algorithm by reversing the clock

A method is time symmetric if taking a step of length \(h\) and then a step of length \(-h\) restores the starting state, up to arithmetic and solve tolerance. For the midpoint update, exchanging the old and new values leaves their average unchanged and changes both sides of the difference equation consistently. This gives exact reversibility for an accurately solved implicit step. For forward Euler on the oscillator, the product of the forward and backward matrices is \((1+h^2)I\), so reversal actually scales the state.

At \(h=0.1\), the simple reversal from \((1,0)\) therefore returns \((1.01,0)\) for forward Euler, making the failure visible in one pair of steps. This diagnostic is useful only for a reversible underlying evolution paired with its time-reversed integration law. A dissipative or dissipatively constrained physical system has different requirements. Reversibility, energy preservation and phase accuracy probe separate mathematical properties, each with a different operational test.

Test a flow map on a small cell of phase space

For canonical coordinates \(z=(q,p)\), a differentiable Hamiltonian update \(z_+=\Psi(z)\) is symplectic when its Jacobian \(D\Psi\) preserves the matrix \(J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) through \(D\Psi^T J D\Psi=J\). Direct multiplication shows that for any real two-by-two matrix \(M\), \(M^TJM=(\det M)J\). Thus in one degree of freedom symplecticity is equivalent to preserving oriented area. A small rectangle of nearby initial conditions should map to a region of the same signed area even when its sides stretch or tilt.

For more than one degree of freedom, preserving ordinary volume is necessary but no longer sufficient. A map may stretch one canonical \((q,p)\) plane and compress another by the reciprocal factor. Its full determinant remains one while the area pairing in each plane changes. Energy is a further, independent diagnostic: an area-preserving map can move individual points among nearby energy contours. The determinant calculation tests a geometric property of the map across neighbouring trajectories, rather than just the energy at one chosen state.

Compare the phase volume of an oscillator ensemble

For \(q'=p,\ p'=-q\) with unit frequency, forward Euler gives \(M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&h\\-h&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), of determinant \(1+h^2\). Backward Euler gives \((I-hA)^{-1}\), where \(A\) has off-diagonal entries \(1,-1\), so its determinant is \(1/(1+h^2)\). A kick followed by a drift gives the symplectic Euler matrix with rows \((1-h^2,h)\) and \((-h,1)\), whose determinant is exactly one. These facts hold for every input rectangle, so they can be checked by integrating its corners rather than relying on a single solution.

Choose \(h=0.2\) and an initial ensemble occupying a rectangle of area 0.01 in normalised phase coordinates. After 25 steps forward Euler predicts area \(0.01(1.04)^{25}\approx0.02666\). Backward Euler gives \(0.01/(1.04)^{25}\approx0.00375\). Symplectic Euler preserves 0.01. Repeated steps compound even a four percent per-step area change into a substantial distortion. For the oscillator all three approximate individual paths can initially look plausible, so this ensemble diagnostic reveals information inaccessible in a short plot of one coordinate.

As a counterexample in four dimensions, scale the first pair of coordinates by \((2,1)\) and the second pair by \((1/2,1)\). The volume multiplier is one, but the first canonical area doubles while the second halves. A full symplecticity test must check \(M^TJM-J\) using the correctly ordered canonical coordinate pairs. An algorithm preserving volume in an arbitrary collection of engineering state units is not automatically qualified for long-time Hamiltonian integration.

Area preservation also survives composing individual canonical shears: the determinants multiply and every shear has determinant one. This explains why reversing the order of a kick and a drift changes the phase error but preserves the two-dimensional area test. The two compositions may therefore produce different numeric positions while agreeing precisely on one geometric invariant. A useful verification suite includes both ensemble area and a trajectory observable with a known time dependence.

Störmer–Verlet and a discrete invariant

Numerical ordinary differential equations

Derive the leapfrog update, identify its oscillatory stability interval and quantify the difference between bounded energy and correct phase.

From first principles

Derive a reversible position update from Newton’s law

For a force that depends only on position, \(m\ddot q=F(q)\), the acceleration can be evaluated at a stored position without an implicit solve. Expand the exact position one step forward and one step backward about the same time \(t_n\). The odd derivatives reverse sign and cancel when the expansions are added:

\[q(t_n+h)+q(t_n-h)=2q(t_n)+h^2\ddot q(t_n)+O(h^4).\]

Replace acceleration by force divided by mass and solve for the forward position. The resulting relation is unchanged if the labels \(n+1\) and \(n-1\) are exchanged:

\[q_{n+1}=2q_n-q_{n-1}+\frac{h^2}{m}F(q_n).\]

Introduce the staggered momentum \(p_{n-1/2}=m(q_n-q_{n-1})/h\). The same algebra becomes an impulse followed by a drift,

\[p_{n+1/2}=p_{n-1/2}+hF(q_n),\]
\[q_{n+1}=q_n+h p_{n+1/2}/m .\]

A specified momentum at \(t=0\) starts the first half step with \(p_{1/2}=p_0+h F(q_0)/2\). For smooth forces the method has second-order position accuracy. It is important to distinguish the staggered momentum from momentum at the stored position time when checking energy.

Derivation and applications

Why a stable Verlet trajectory can arrive at the wrong time

Long-term energy behaviour is important in reversible mechanics, but phase error can dominate a timing measurement. Consider the velocity-independent linear oscillator \(q''=-\omega^2q\). Use a uniform step \(h\), an accurately initialized first point and the position recurrence obtained by eliminating the half-step momenta in Verlet. Write \(z=h\omega\):

\[q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-z^2q_n .\]

Assume a discrete mode \(q_n=e^{in\vartheta}\). Divide by the mode value at \(n\) and combine the two exponentials. The angle advanced per step obeys

\[2\cos\vartheta-2=-z^2,\]
\[\sin(\vartheta/2)=z/2 .\]

For \(0<z<2\) choose the branch continuous with the exact oscillation at small \(h\). The discrete angular frequency and its expansion are

\[\widetilde\omega=\frac2h\arcsin(z/2)=\omega\left[1+\frac{z^2}{24}+O(z^4)\right].\]

The numerical oscillator runs slightly fast even though its amplitude can remain bounded indefinitely. Its phase lead grows approximately linearly with elapsed time at fixed step. Above \(z=2\) the dispersion relation permits no real phase angle, signaling a separate stability failure. The formula concerns time discretization alone; incorrect initial half-step velocities can add an initial phase offset.

Worked example

Estimate a ten-period timing error

For \(\omega=5.00\) rad/s and \(h=0.100\) s, \(z=0.500\) and \(\widetilde\omega=20\arcsin(0.250)=5.054\) rad/s. Ten true periods last \(12.57\) s. Over that duration the numerical phase gains about \((5.054-5.00)(12.57)\simeq0.674\) rad. The estimated time at which the numerical oscillator completes its tenth period is \(20\pi/5.054=12.43\) s, about \(0.13\) s too soon.

Second application

Select a step from a phase requirement

Suppose 100 periods should accumulate less than \(0.100\) rad of phase error. The leading estimate gives \(2\pi(100)z^2/24<0.100\), so \(z<0.0618\). For \(\omega=5.00\) rad/s this means \(h<0.0124\) s. A choice based only on the stability requirement \(h<0.400\) s would be far too coarse. Halving a small step should reduce the accumulated phase error by approximately four at fixed physical duration.

Position and momentum at staggered times

For unit mass and a time-independent potential \(V(q)\), the equations are \(q'=p,\ p'=-V'(q)\). Treat the potential force over a half step, drift with the updated momentum for a full step, then use the new position for the last half kick. This yields

\[p_{n+1/2}=p_n-\tfrac h2V'(q_n),\quad q_{n+1}=q_n+hp_{n+1/2},\quad p_{n+1}=p_{n+1/2}-\tfrac h2V'(q_{n+1}).\]

Successive half kicks at the same position share a force evaluation. Reversing the sign of \(h\) exactly undoes the steps in reverse order, in exact arithmetic. Subtracting consecutive position increments gives \(q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-h^2V'(q_n)\). The starting position and velocity must initialise the very first half step correctly; using the full velocity as the half-step velocity injects an initial phase error.

The oscillator has an exactly computable modified energy

For \(V=\omega^2q^2/2\) define \(z=h\omega\). The position coefficient of \(q_n\) after one step is \(a=1-z^2/2\); the momentum coefficient of \(q_n\) is \(-h\omega^2(1-z^2/4)\). The full map has equal diagonal entries \(a\) and off-diagonal entries \(h,-hc\) with \(c=\omega^2(1-z^2/4)\). Since \(a^2+h^2c=1\), expanding squares shows \(p_{n+1}^2+cq_{n+1}^2=p_n^2+cq_n^2\).

When \(0<z<2\), \(c>0\) and level sets of this invariant are bounded ellipses. The eigenvalues are \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) with \(\cos\theta=a\). Beyond two, one real eigenvalue has magnitude greater than one and trajectories generically grow. At exactly two the invariant loses control of position and a repeated eigenvalue can cause linear growth. An apparently bounded run starting from specially chosen data at the threshold cannot certify stability for arbitrary perturbations.

Follow a step and its timing

For \(\omega=1,h=1,q_0=1,p_0=0\), the half kick gives \(-0.5\), the drift gives \(q_1=0.5\), and the final half kick gives \(p_1=-0.75\). The physical energy decreases from 0.5 to 0.40625 in this first step. The modified energy \((p^2+0.75q^2)/2\) equals 0.375 at both points. The discrete phase is \(\theta=\pi/3\), so the computed trajectory returns after six steps. The true oscillator returns after \(2\pi\approx6.283\) seconds. A stable trajectory with a bounded energy error can therefore accumulate a large timing error over many cycles.

For small \(z\), expanding \(\theta=2\arcsin(z/2)\) gives \(\theta=z+z^3/24+O(z^5)\). At a fixed final time the accumulated phase error is therefore second order in \(h\). A period estimate and an energy trace probe different aspects of long-time integration and should be reported separately.

Why the initial velocity test matters

A test with only the initial position nonzero may conceal the borderline instability at \(h\omega=2\). At that boundary the update is \(q_{n+1}=-q_n+hp_n,\ p_{n+1}=-p_n\). For \(p_0=0\) the positions merely alternate sign forever. With \(q_0=0\) and \(p_0=1\), however, explicit iteration gives \(p_n=(-1)^n\) and \(q_n=(-1)^{n-1}nh\). The position magnitude grows linearly. A stability test must account for all independent starting directions or small perturbations of the chosen one, especially when an eigenvalue of modulus one is repeated.

Prove conservation of angular momentum by individual substeps

For a particle of constant mass in a radial potential \(V(\|q\|)\), the force is parallel to the position vector. Its angular momentum is \(L=q\times p\). During a kick of duration \(\tau\), momentum changes by \(\tau F(q)\) at fixed position, so the change in \(L\) is \(\tau q\times F(q)=0\). During a drift, position changes by \(\tau p/m\) at fixed momentum, giving change \((\tau/m)p\times p=0\). The full kick–drift–kick map therefore preserves angular momentum exactly in arithmetic without rounding, for any step length for which these evaluations are defined.

The proof uses radial force and the relationship between velocity and momentum. An external noncentral force exerts a physical torque, while a coordinate-dependent mass matrix changes the drift subflow. Under either modification the two zero cross products need rechecking. The argument also gives no bound on energy or phase. Exact conservation of angular momentum constrains orbital geometry but leaves freedom to move from the correct energy level onto a nearby one or to return to a point on the orbit at the wrong time.

Compare three diagnostics on an elliptical oscillator trajectory

Use unit mass and two-dimensional potential \(V=(x^2+y^2)/2\). Start at \(q=(1,0)\), \(p=(0,2)\), so \(L=2\) and physical energy is \(E=(1+4)/2=2.5\). At step \(h=0.5\), the first half kick gives \(p_{1/2}=(-0.25,2)\). The drift leads to \(q_1=(0.875,1)\). The last half kick subtracts \(0.25q_1\), giving \(p_1=(-0.46875,1.75)\). The resulting planar angular momentum is \(0.875(1.75)-1(-0.46875)=2\), exactly as proved.

Calculate energy separately. The squared position is \(0.875^2+1=1.765625\), and the squared momentum is \(0.46875^2+1.75^2=3.2822265625\). Hence the physical energy after this step is 2.52392578125, about 0.96 percent above the initial value. The exact continuous position at this time is \((\cos0.5,2\sin0.5)\approx(0.87758,0.95885)\), so the position error is concentrated in the second coordinate. Angular momentum, physical energy and the individual trajectory coordinates expose three distinct properties of the same update.

Because the harmonic force is separable by coordinate, the modified invariant from the scalar oscillator adds across the two directions: \([\|p\|^2+(1-h^2/4)\|q\|^2]/2\) is constant. Substitution gives 2.46875 at both steps, independently checking the arithmetic. For a general radial potential this particular quadratic modified invariant no longer follows, while the angular momentum proof remains valid. A conservation claim should identify the symmetry responsible rather than assuming every observed invariant survives a change of potential.

If there were a small constant transverse force, the physical change in angular momentum would be the time integral of \(q\times F_{\rm ext}\). A better diagnostic would subtract the accumulated applied torque from the measured change in \(L\), rather than demand that \(L\) remain fixed. This distinction also guards against a subtle implementation error: zeroing a real torque to improve a conservation plot would change the physical problem and could hide a significant trajectory bias.

Measuring the signed torque makes its direction as well as its magnitude available for this balance check.

Implicit midpoint, reversibility and the phase of an oscillator

Numerical ordinary differential equations

Imagine integrating a mechanical trajectory forward, saving only its end state and then running the same method backward. Recovering the start is a stronger demand than obtaining a plausibly small one-step error. Even exact recovery does not guarantee that an oscillation peaked at the correct time.

Build a method from a centred difference

Integrate \(y'=f(t,y)\) over an interval of length \(h\). Midpoint quadrature approximates the integral by \(h\) times its integrand at \(t_n+h/2\). The arithmetic mean of the unknown endpoint states approximates the exact midpoint state up to order \(h^2\) on a smooth trajectory. Using this mean as the derivative's state argument gives

\[y_{n+1}-y_n=h f\!\left(t_n+h/2,(y_n+y_{n+1})/2\right).\]

Errors from midpoint quadrature and from evaluating \(f\) at the approximate midpoint both contribute \(O(h^3)\) locally, giving global order two on a fixed interval under the usual regularity and stability conditions. In general the unknown appears inside \(f\), so an implicit equation must be solved. If \(f\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L_f\), direct fixed-point iteration contracts when \(|h|L_f/2<1\). Newton iteration can converge over a wider range. Neither a failed nonlinear solve nor loose tolerances inherit the exact algebraic properties of the method.

Exchange \(y_n,y_{n+1}\) and replace \(h\) by \(-h\). The left side changes sign, the arithmetic midpoint is identical and the midpoint time is identical if the reverse step starts at \(t_{n+1}\). Thus, on the same locally unique solution branch, \(\Phi_{-h}=\Phi_h^{-1}\). A round trip using variable steps must repeat the accepted steps in reverse order. Independently choosing a new adaptive mesh on the way back need not retrace the states.

Solve the step for a harmonic oscillator

Write \(q'=\omega p,\ p'=-\omega q\), with scaled momentum \(p\) in the same units as \(q\), and \(z=q+ip\). The ODE becomes \(z'=-i\omega z\). Solving the linear midpoint equation gives the scalar multiplier

\[M(u)=\frac{1-iu/2}{1+iu/2}=\exp[-2i\arctan(u/2)],\]
\[u=\omega h.\]

Its numerator and denominator are complex conjugates. Hence it preserves \(q^2+p^2\), proportional to the oscillator energy, exactly in real arithmetic. For any radius or step size \(M(-u)M(u)=1\). The rotation per step is \(\theta=2\arctan(u/2)=u-u^3/12+O(u^5)\). The numerical phase lags by \(u-\theta\) per step. After \(N\) steps the accumulated lag is \(N(u-\theta)\), increasing linearly in elapsed time even while the energy remains exactly constant.

Compare explicit midpoint Runge–Kutta. A half Euler predictor leads to \(M_{\rm RK2}=1-iu-u^2/2\). It also has order two, but \(|M_{\rm RK2}|^2=1+u^4/4\). The amplitude grows slowly at every nonzero step. A forward and reverse pair multiplies the starting amplitude by \(1+u^4/4\). In a conservative problem these two second-order procedures can produce visibly different long records.

What different observers measure

Waves, radiation and relativity

A moving observer carries clocks, rulers and a calibrated test charge. A statement about the electric field must specify which observer held that test charge at rest. In this chapter we derive how different measurements can agree on the force law and yet assign different electric fields, magnetic fields and charge densities to the same physical apparatus. A current-carrying wire and a light pulse will provide independent checks.

Events, synchronisation and directed boosts

Use two inertial coordinate systems with aligned axes. The primed origin moves at speed \(v\) in the positive laboratory \(x\) direction. Choose coincident origins when both clocks read zero and synchronise distant clocks in each frame by light signals. Homogeneity makes the transformation linear. The worldline of the primed origin requires \(x'=A(x-vt)\). A light ray moving along either axial direction must still obey \(x'=\pm ct'\). Substituting its two possible worldlines into the linear map fixes the relative coefficients of \(x\) and \(t\) in \(t'\). Reversing the transformation by changing the sign of \(v\) fixes the remaining scale.

\[x'=\gamma(x-vt),\]
\[t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2),\]
\[y'=y,\]
\[z'=z,\]
\[\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.\]

The squared interval \(c^2\Delta t^2-|\Delta\mathbf r|^2\) is unchanged, a direct algebra check that detects a surprising number of sign choices. Two sparks separated by 3.0 m along a laboratory bench and simultaneous on laboratory clocks have \(\Delta t'=-7.51\) ns for \(v=0.60c\), if the second event is at larger \(x\). Their ordering in this frame is consistent with their spacelike separation. Events joined by a light signal are null-separated and retain their order under this boost.

Length measurements need two simultaneous endpoint readings in the chosen frame. A bar at rest in the lab and parallel to the axis has length \(L/\gamma\) in the primed frame. Substituting the simultaneous laboratory endpoint events into the boost instead gives \(\gamma L\), but their primed times differ. This distinction reappears when we count charge per unit length in a moving wire. The result of that count depends on the spacetime slice used to perform it.

A current is a rate of charge crossing a surface

Begin with a single population of particles, each carrying charge \(q\), whose proper number density is \(n_0\). Their counting volume is Lorentz-contracted only along their motion. The lab assigns density \(n=\gamma_u n_0\) and current density \(q n\mathbf u\). Both are components of \(j^\mu=q n_0 u^\mu\), where \(u^\mu=\gamma_u(c,\mathbf u)\) is the four-velocity. Independent species can be superposed. A total four-current can consequently be spacelike even though every material particle moves more slowly than light.

\[\rho'=\gamma(\rho-vJ_x/c^2),\]
\[J'_x=\gamma(J_x-v\rho),\]
\[J'_y=J_y,\]
\[J'_z=J_z .\]

The usual continuity law becomes \(\partial_\mu j^\mu=0\) with \(\partial_0=c^{-1}\partial_t\). Transformation of the derivative and of the current cancels exactly in this contraction. An isolated finite charge distribution has an observer-independent total charge if each observer integrates through a complete spacelike slice. One way to see this is to connect the two slices by a large timelike enclosing wall. Four-dimensional flux conservation sets the difference between the two charge integrals equal to the current crossing the wall. For a wall outside all the charge trajectories that last contribution vanishes.

For a charge-neutral straight wire carrying conventional current \(I=2.0\) A along \(+x\), a frame moving parallel to the wire at \(0.20c\) assigns line density \(\lambda'=-\gamma vI/c^2=-1.36\) nC/m. Transverse cross-section is unchanged. There is no requirement that electrons themselves travel at \(0.20c\). Both the mobile and immobile species contribute to the transformed density. The ideal wire is infinitely long, so one cannot integrate its transformed charge density out to infinity and apply the finite isolated system argument.

Obtaining field components from measured forces

Place a test charge at rest at an event in the primed frame. In the lab it has velocity \(v\mathbf e_x\) and force \(q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\). To transform the force, transform the corresponding momentum change. For a small transverse kick, \(dp'_y=dp_y\) while \(dt'=dt/\gamma\) on the test charge's path. Thus \(F'_y=\gamma F_y\) and similarly for \(z\). For the parallel component use \(dp'_x=\gamma(dp_x-v\,d\mathcal E/c^2)\) and \(d\mathcal E/dt=vF_x\). The result is \(F'_x=F_x\). Dividing by \(q\) gives the electric field. Requiring the same force law for charges moving transversely, or transforming the magnetic flux measured by a moving loop, gives the magnetic field.

\[\mathbf E'_\parallel=\mathbf E_\parallel,\]
\[\mathbf B'_\parallel=\mathbf B_\parallel,\]
\[\mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)_\perp,\]
\[\mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf B-\mathbf v\times\mathbf E/c^2)_\perp .\]

Imagine a uniform \(+y\) electric field of 90.0 kV/m and a \(+z\) magnetic field with \(cB=150\) kV/m. For a boost along \(+x\), the \(y\) component of \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\) is negative. At \(\beta=0.60\) the electric field vanishes. The remaining magnetic field has \(cB'=1.25(150-54)=120\) kV/m or 0.400 mT. A particle comoving with that frame feels no force at that event. A different particle moving across the magnetic field can still curve.

Vary the observer speed and compare the electric and magnetic field invariants

Compare two force measurements near an ideal wire

Return to the 2.0 A wire and put the observation point 1.0 cm along \(+y\). Ampère's law gives \(B_z=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)=40.0\) microtesla. The lab radial electric field is zero in this idealised symmetric model. A positive test charge moving along \(+x\) at \(0.20c\) experiences an inward magnetic force per unit charge of 2.40 kV/m. In its momentary rest frame the wire's negative line charge produces an inward radial electric field. The field from Gauss's law is \(|\lambda'|/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)=2.45\) kV/m. The difference in the reported transverse forces is precisely the factor \(\gamma=1.0206\) derived from the momentum kick.

There is no contradiction between a field with zero laboratory divergence outside the wire and a different electric field in the primed frame. The charge density at the wire itself also transforms. If the laboratory wire carries a real driving surface charge and a return conductor, add their fields and charges before transforming. A claimed measurement of this ideal result without specifying the return path and electrostatic shielding needs a separate model for those additional fields.

When can a field be made purely electric or magnetic?

Direct substitution in the transverse transformation leaves the scalars \(B^2-E^2/c^2\) and \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) unchanged. The first is a difference, so retain its sign. If the second is nonzero, no observer sees either field vanish. If the fields are orthogonal and \(E<cB\), choose \(\mathbf v=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). The vector identity for \((\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B\) cancels \(\mathbf E\), and the required speed is below \(c\). With \(E>cB\), choosing \(c^2\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/E^2\) instead cancels \(\mathbf B\).

When both invariants vanish one must still distinguish a field identically zero from a nonzero null field. A vacuum travelling plane wave satisfies \(E=cB\) and orthogonality everywhere. The advertised boost would then require speed \(c\). An observer can lower its measured frequency and amplitude continuously by following the wave at increasing speed but can never transform away its nonzero field. A superposition of waves need not be null. A standing wave has positions and times where only one of the fields is present.

Phase matching across inertial frames

Every observer agrees on whether a particular spacetime event is a crest. The phase \(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t\) therefore labels the same event in each frame. Substituting the inverse coordinate transformation gives the transformation of frequency and wavevector. For vacuum propagation at laboratory angle \(\theta\) to the boost, divide the transformed longitudinal wavenumber by the transformed magnitude.

\[\omega'=\gamma\omega(1-\beta\cos\theta),\]
\[\cos\theta'=\frac{\cos\theta-\beta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}.\]

An observer chasing a collinear 600 nm light wave at \(0.60c\) sees half its frequency and a wavelength of 1200 nm. A beam entering at \(90^\circ\) to the boost appears at \(\cos\theta'=-0.60\) and frequency \(1.25\omega\). Asking for a transverse Doppler shift without saying in whose frame the angle is a right angle leaves the problem under-specified. A detector counts phase fronts crossing its own trajectory and its own clock sets the elapsed time.

A boosted light beam also transports momentum

Frequency alone does not specify the energy measured by a moving detector. Its collecting surface and clock must be assigned to an observer. For a monochromatic collimated beam in vacuum, one route is to transform both fields and calculate their new Poynting vector. Another route is to transform the stress-energy tensor, whose energy density, momentum density and momentum flux describe the same field together. This second route shows explicitly which measured quantity is being transformed and extends to superposed beams with zero mean momentum in one frame.

For a right-moving plane wave along \(x\), the energy density is \(u=S_x/c\), momentum density is \(u/c\) and normal momentum flux is \(u\). In coordinates with \(x^0=ct\), the relevant block has \(T^{00}=T^{01}=T^{10}=T^{11}=u\). Boost both indices using the same Lorentz matrix as for coordinates. The transformed energy density follows directly:

\[T'^{00}=\gamma^2(T^{00}-2\beta T^{01}+\beta^2T^{11})=\gamma^2(1-\beta)^2u .\]

The frequency Doppler factor enters twice. One factor corresponds to lower energy per photon and one to the changed number per unit volume. For a finite pulse, its length at a common primed time grows for an observer following it, so the total energy changes by only one Doppler factor. Volumes measured between equal-time spatial endpoints must be computed on the appropriate observer's simultaneity slice, just as for a moving rod.

Now superpose two equal-intensity mutually incoherent plane waves in opposite axial directions. In the laboratory \(T^{01}=0\), while \(T^{11}=T^{00}=u\) for the combined radiation. An observer moving at \(0.60c\) obtains \(u'=\gamma^2(1+\beta^2)u=2.125u\), with net flux \(S'_x=-2\gamma^2\beta\,cu=-1.875\,cu\). The counterpropagating beam that the observer meets head-on is blueshifted, while the other is redshifted. Energy density remains greater than the magnitude of momentum density times \(c\), as required for a superposition of distinct propagation directions. This check fails if one transforms a zero Poynting vector as though it were the spatial component of a four-vector with no stress information.

The direction-independent radiation field in an ideal cavity has \(T^{ii}=u/3\) for each spatial axis in its rest frame rather than \(T^{11}=u\). The one-third comes from averaging \(n_i^2\) over a sphere. The moving observer then sees \(u'=\gamma^2u(1+\beta^2/3)\) and a net longitudinal flux. At \(\beta=0.60\) the energy-density factor is 1.75. An isotropic thermal field becomes directionally distorted; assigning one Doppler factor to the whole sky would give neither the correct integrated energy density nor its directional spectrum.

A moving parallel-plate capacitor tests surface sources

Choose large parallel conducting plates at \(z=\pm d/2\) carrying opposite uniform surface densities \(\pm\sigma\). Ignore edge effects in the central region. In the lab the field between them has magnitude \(E_0=\sigma/\epsilon_0\) and there is no magnetic field. A frame boosted along \(+x\) sees both plates move at \(-v\). Their areas contract along \(x\), so their surface charge magnitudes become \(\gamma\sigma\); their equal and opposite surface currents point in opposite senses. Use Gauss's law and the magnetic jump condition on each sheet to reconstruct the transformed fields. The current sheets reinforce magnetically between the plates and cancel outside, just as electric sheets of opposite charge do electrostatically.

For laboratory electric field along \(+z\), the moving observer has \(E'_z=\gamma E_0\) and \(B'_y=+\gamma vE_0/c^2\). Cross the primed plate velocity \(-v\mathbf e_x\) with this magnetic field to find a force along \(-z\) on a positive surface charge, partly cancelling the enhanced electric attraction towards the opposite plate. When finding the force on a sheet, use the fields of the other sheet or the arithmetic mean of the two limiting fields. Substituting the total interior field without removing the self contribution would double the result.

At \(E_0=2.00\) MV/m and \(\beta=0.60\), the primed interior field is 2.50 MV/m and its magnetic field 5.00 mT. Per positive surface charge the transverse attraction from the opposite sheet has magnitude \(\gamma E_0(1-\beta^2)/2=E_0/(2\gamma)\). This is 0.800 MV/m, compared with 1.00 MV/m in the laboratory. The transformation factor is inverse \(\gamma\) because the charge in this example is at rest in the original laboratory; the earlier force derivation placed the test charge at rest in the primed frame. Interchanging those initial conditions without changing the time conversion reverses the factor.

Multiplying the primed force per charge by the larger surface density \(\gamma\sigma\) restores the same force per transverse plate area in this tangential boost. The cancellation supplies a local cross-check among length contraction, field transformation and mechanical force. A complete calculation of the total energy or momentum of an accelerated finite capacitor must also account for stresses in its supports and for the fields near its edges. Taking the uniform central field times a transformed volume is adequate for a local comparison, but does not by itself describe that whole closed mechanical system.

Worked observer problem · a null of one component does not imply a vanishing field

At one event a laboratory finds \(\mathbf E=(30,40,0)\) kV/m and \(c\mathbf B=(0,0,50)\) kV/m. Another laboratory reports that it can remove the magnetic field by boosting along the direction of \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\). Test the claim and predict the fields for a boost of speed \(0.80c\) along this direction. Does the local measurement establish that this is an infinite plane wave?

Evaluate the invariants and transform the transverse field

The electric and scaled magnetic fields both have magnitude 50 kV/m and are perpendicular. Both quadratic Lorentz invariants vanish, while the fields themselves are nonzero. With \(\mathbf v\) along \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\), both field vectors are perpendicular to the boost. The cross terms reduce their magnitudes by a common factor \(\gamma(1-\beta)=1/3\) for this speed, so \(E'=cB'=16.7\) kV/m. Their directions in this transverse configuration are unchanged. No subluminal boost removes either nonzero field, as follows also by applying the inverse transformation to an alleged zero field.

A pulse at one point can have these local field values, as can the field near a suitable superposition at a particular event. A local tensor and its invariants contain no information about all the field derivatives elsewhere. To establish a plane-wave model measure spatial phase across transverse probes, verify the propagation relation at separated stations and bound amplitude variation over the required observation region. Infinity is an idealisation established only to a scale specified by the experiment.

As an error check, compare the measured difference \(E^2-c^2B^2\) with the propagated uncertainties of both meters. Small nonzero estimated differences near a null may be statistically insignificant, while a systematic alignment error in \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) can obscure orthogonality. Classifying a field as electric-dominated or magnetic-dominated from a difference of nearly equal large squares requires an uncertainty budget. The exact mathematical classification and the experimental confidence in assigning it answer different questions.

Recover a source direction from two clocks and a known speed

Two perfectly synchronised receivers travel with equal speed in opposite directions along the laboratory axis. They sample the same monochromatic plane wave. Their measured frequencies are proportional to \(1-\beta\cos\theta\) and \(1+\beta\cos\theta\), with a common Lorentz factor. Taking their difference divided by their sum determines \(\beta\cos\theta\) without prior knowledge of emitted frequency. Their sum then fixes that frequency once the speed is calibrated. The construction combines two observer assignments rather than treating a transverse Doppler correction as an unknown empirical shift.

For speeds of half the speed of light and a measured frequency ratio of three to two, with the receiver travelling toward negative \(x\) recording the higher value, the inferred cosine is 0.40. If their average measured frequency is 600 THz, the emitted laboratory frequency is about 520 THz. A second direction on the opposite side of the boost axis has the same cosine, so the two receivers alone cannot determine the full incoming unit vector.

A third receiver with a nonparallel velocity supplies another directional projection. Detector clocks must be calibrated in their respective rest frames and each must see the same stable source frequency. If the source itself chirps between noncoincident measurements, that chirp can imitate an angular shift. Using overlapping signals and comparing phase histories addresses this limitation more directly than quoting only two peak frequencies.

Cambridge Electromagnetism · electromagnetism and relativity

Relativistic particle dynamics and the field tensor

Waves, radiation and relativity

A circular track measures momentum per charge. A clock following the particle registers proper time, and a detector records how much kinetic energy it has gained. We will put these observations in one dynamical equation, solve that equation in uniform fields and exhibit a numerical pusher that can get the energy right while its orbit phase slowly slips.

What the track, clock and calorimeter measure

Consider a particle of invariant mass \(m\), signed charge \(q\) and ordinary velocity \(\mathbf v\). Inertial observers agree on the proper time between neighbouring events on its timelike worldline, \(d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}\). Adopt coordinates \(x^\mu=(ct,x,y,z)\) and metric \(g=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)\). Dividing the displacement by proper time produces a vector of fixed Minkowski norm. Multiplying it by invariant mass gives four-momentum.

\[u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v),\]
\[p^\mu=mu^\mu=(\mathcal E/c,\mathbf p),\]
\[\mathcal E^2-c^2|\mathbf p|^2=m^2c^4 .\]

Energy includes the rest term. Momentum is \(\gamma m\mathbf v\). Differentiating the last equation on a trajectory gives \(d\mathcal E/dt=\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf p/dt\). This identity replaces the nonrelativistic step of dotting \(m\mathbf a\) with velocity. A magnetic field can rotate momentum with fixed magnitude and energy, whereas an electric field can change energy by doing work.

Suppose an electron begins at rest and gains 10.0 keV of kinetic energy across an electrostatic potential difference in vacuum. Its rest energy is 511 keV, giving \(\gamma=1.01957\) and \(v/c=\sqrt{1-1/\gamma^2}=0.1950\). The nonrelativistic estimate is 0.1978. In a modest energy measurement the discrepancy could lie below the experimental spread. For a precise orbit calculation the 1.96 percent change in relativistic cyclotron period is measurable independently of that discrepancy in speed.

A linear map that produces the Lorentz force

Seek a local equation linear in field and four-velocity and covariant under boosts. For the energy component to give \(q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\), and for the spatial components to give the electric force and the oriented cross product, construct the antisymmetric field tensor. With the stated metric and \(\epsilon^{123}=1\), set

\[F^{0i}=-E_i/c,\]
\[F^{i0}=E_i/c,\]
\[F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B_k,\]
\[\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu .\]

Lowering the index on \(u\) reverses the signs of its spatial components. The spatial product becomes \(q\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\), and division by \(\gamma\) converts the proper-time derivative to \(d\mathbf p/dt\). The time component similarly yields \(d\mathcal E/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\). Contract the force with \(u_\mu\). The symmetric product \(u_\mu u_\nu\) annihilates the antisymmetric tensor, which proves that the evolution preserves the mass shell.

To recover Maxwell's kinematic constraints from potentials, put \(A^\mu=(\phi/c,\mathbf A)\) and \(F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu\). Then \(F^{0i}=(\partial_t A_i+\partial_i\phi)/c=-E_i/c\), while the spatial-spatial components are the magnetic curl. Adding a four-gradient to \(A^\mu\) leaves the tensor unchanged because ordinary derivatives commute. The particle action \(S=-mc^2\int d\tau-q\int A_\mu dx^\mu\) gives the same force after integrating variations by parts with fixed endpoints. Gauge changes add only an endpoint term to this action. A trajectory measured with local forces cannot distinguish such potential representatives.

Constant force, variable coordinate acceleration

Put a particle initially at rest in a uniform static electric field along \(x\). Let \(a_0=qE/m\) be signed. Integration of \(dp_x/dt=qE\) gives \(p_x=ma_0t\), so the speed tends to \(c\) even while momentum increases linearly. Proper-time evolution is simplest in terms of rapidity \(\eta\), where \(u^0=c\cosh\eta\), \(u^x=c\sinh\eta\). The two tensor equations imply \(d\eta/d\tau=a_0/c\).

\[t=\frac{c}{a_0}\sinh(a_0\tau/c),\]
\[x=\frac{c^2}{a_0}\{\cosh(a_0\tau/c)-1\},\]
\[\frac{dv_x}{dt}=\frac{a_0}{\gamma^3}.\]

The initial time and position are zero. The work \(qEx=mc^2(\cosh\eta-1)\) equals kinetic energy, which checks both the integration constant and the dimensional factor in the displacement. For an electron use the direction of the electric force as positive to simplify signs. At 20.0 kV/m it gains 1.0 keV in 5.0 cm. The energy gain follows from the static potential even though the speed does not grow exactly as the square root of distance. The field region must last long enough for the particle to cross it and fringe fields can alter the entry trajectory.

Helical motion, radius and period

A uniform magnetic field along \(z\) keeps \(p_z\), energy and \(|\mathbf p_\perp|\) fixed. Write \(p_x+ip_y\) as a complex number. The Lorentz force reduces to a first-order rotation equation and a single time integration gives the corresponding position. With positive \(B\), positive charges starting along \(+x\) initially turn towards \(-y\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}(p_x+ip_y)=-i\frac{qB}{\gamma m}(p_x+ip_y),\]
\[R=\frac{p_\perp}{|q|B},\]
\[T=\frac{2\pi\gamma m}{|q|B}.\]

The helical pitch is \(v_zT\). Radius gives transverse momentum and a calibrated period gives total energy through \(\gamma\). A track seen only in projection along the field gives no direct evidence that longitudinal momentum vanishes. A second view or a timing measurement can resolve this ambiguity.

At 10.0 keV the electron has \(pc=\sqrt{K(K+2mc^2)}=101.6\) keV. With entry perpendicular to a 2.0 mT field its radius is 0.169 m and its period 18.2 ns. Doubling the field halves both radius and period at fixed injection energy. Doubling momentum at fixed field doubles radius but changes period by the ratio of Lorentz factors. These are different tests of a track calibration. A beam with a spread of energies will dephase relative to a narrow timing gate even when every single particle remains in a perfectly uniform field.

A drift frame gives an exact crossed-field solution

For constant crossed fields with \(E<cB\), transform to the frame moving at \(\mathbf v_d=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). There \(E'=0\) and \(B'=\sqrt{B^2-E^2/c^2}\); calculate the transformed initial momentum and integrate a helix. Transform two successive equal-phase events back to the lab. Their perpendicular displacement in the electric-free frame is zero. If the motion has no component parallel to \(\mathbf B\), the entire displacement there is zero, and the average lab cycle velocity is exactly \(\mathbf v_d\). The detailed lab trace need not be circular and the instantaneous lab energy may oscillate.

Take \(E=30.0\) kV/m along \(y\) and \(B=0.50\) mT along \(z\). The drift is along \(+x\) at \(6.00\times10^7\) m/s. A particle injected at that velocity crosses the uniform fields on a straight line because the forces cancel. An initially stationary positive particle instead begins by accelerating along \(+y\). These observations can use the same field with different injected particles. If \(E\) exceeds \(cB\), this electric-free frame is unavailable. Transform to a magnetic-free frame and integrate electric acceleration instead.

Continuous rotation and a discrete experiment

A naive forward step \(\mathbf p_{n+1}=\mathbf p_n+q\Delta t\,\mathbf v_n\times\mathbf B\) adds a vector perpendicular to the previous momentum. Its squared norm increases by the squared magnitude of that kick. The apparent heating will persist in perfectly static uniform magnetic fields. For a purely magnetic update, evaluate the direction using the mean of old and new momenta. At fixed \(\gamma\), the resulting implicit two-dimensional linear map can be solved algebraically and is an exact rotation by a modified angle.

\[\delta=2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega_c\Delta t}{2}\right),\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{qB}{\gamma m}.\]

The sign of rotation follows the earlier complex equation. Dotting the update with the mean momentum gives zero change in squared norm, which verifies the energy property even for relatively large time steps. A common electromagnetic pusher surrounds the magnetic rotation by two electric half-kicks. In spatially varying fields, specify at what time and position each field is sampled, and check spatial interpolation and time convergence. For ultrarelativistic nearly cancelling electric and magnetic fields, preserving rotation in a pure field alone does not ensure cancellation is represented accurately.

Solve the constant tensor without transforming every time step

When uniform electric and magnetic fields are constant in spacetime, the Lorentz equation is a linear constant-coefficient equation for four-velocity as a function of proper time. Nonlinearity in ordinary time hides that simple structure, because the rate at which proper time accumulates changes along the orbit. Define the mixed tensor \(M^\mu{}_\nu=(q/m)F^{\mu\alpha}g_{\alpha\nu}\). In a uniform field it does not vary along the trajectory. A formal solution is then

\[u(\tau)=e^{M\tau}u(0),\]
\[x(\tau)-x(0)=\int_0^\tau e^{Ms}u(0)\,ds .\]

The coordinate formula is written as an integral since \(M\) can be singular, as it is in a purely magnetic field. Blindly replacing this integral by \(M^{-1}(e^{M\tau}-I)\) fails in exactly such simple cases. A power series for the integral or a block matrix exponential treats zero eigenvalues without an artificial inverse. Antisymmetry of the tensor implies \(M^{\mathsf T}g+gM=0\). Differentiating \(e^{M^{\mathsf T}\tau}ge^{M\tau}\) shows that it stays equal to \(g\), making preservation of \(u^\mu u_\mu=c^2\) a structural property of the exact mapping.

For parallel constant fields along \(z\), the matrix decomposes into a boost in the \(ct,z\) subspace and a rotation in \(x,y\). Write \(\alpha=qE/(mc)\), \(\Omega=qB/m\), and denote the initial four-velocity components by \(u_0^\mu\). The proper-time solution is

\[u^0=u_0^0\cosh\alpha\tau+u_0^z\sinh\alpha\tau,\]
\[u^z=u_0^z\cosh\alpha\tau+u_0^0\sinh\alpha\tau,\]
\[u^x+i u^y=(u_0^x+i u_0^y)e^{-i\Omega\tau}.\]

The longitudinal and transverse operations commute because they act in different subspaces. In lab time the transverse rotation slows as the electric field increases energy, although the proper-time rotation frequency stays constant. A particle therefore traces a helix whose transverse radius \(p_\perp/(|q|B)\) is fixed while the pitch evolves. A single measured turn time without a tagged position in the accelerating region is insufficient for an energy reconstruction.

As a numerical test use an electron, quoting magnitudes, with \(B=1.0\) mT, \(E=10.0\) kV/m and initial purely transverse speed \(0.10c\). One magnetic turn takes 35.72 ns in proper time and accumulates electric rapidity magnitude \(|\alpha|\tau\simeq0.2096\). The final Lorentz factor is \(\gamma_i\cosh(0.2096)=1.0272\), giving final kinetic energy about 13.9 keV, compared with 2.57 keV initially. The lab-time turn duration is \(\gamma_i\sinh(|\alpha|\tau)/|\alpha|\simeq36.2\) ns. Reversing the electric field changes the direction of the axial displacement but leaves final energy and turn duration unchanged for this initially transverse particle. Reversing the initial longitudinal velocity would generally break that energy symmetry.

Infer uncertainty and test units in a momentum reconstruction

A track fitter commonly reports field, radius, pitch, time and a fitted covariance matrix. The dynamical relation turns these observed quantities into momentum. Let \(L_z\) denote a signed pitch per turn. With known signed direction of circulation and calibrated absolute field magnitude, \(|\mathbf p|=|q|B\sqrt{R^2+(L_z/2\pi)^2}\). For small errors, logarithmic differentiation gives weights for fractional radius and pitch errors:

\[d\log p=d\log B+w_R\,d\log R+w_L\,d\log |L_z|,\]
\[w_R=\frac{R^2}{R^2+(L_z/2\pi)^2},\]
\[w_L=1-w_R .\]

For \(R=0.20\) m and \(L_z=0.30\) m the radius accounts for 94.6 percent of the squared transverse-plus-longitudinal geometric factor. Independent relative uncertainties of 0.20 percent in field, 0.50 percent in radius and 2.0 percent in pitch yield about 0.525 percent uncertainty in total momentum. A common detector scale controlling both radius and pitch would enter with correlated signs and should not be counted as two independent errors. When the pitch tends to zero, use the original derivatives with respect to \(L_z\); a reported fractional error on a quantity consistent with zero is ill defined.

Differentiating \(K=\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}-mc^2\) gives \(d\log K=(\gamma+1)d\log p/\gamma\). The coefficient approaches two at low energy and one at very high energy. At the approximately 3.70 keV energy of the tutorial the above momentum error would imply about a 1.05 percent energy error. A kinetic-energy estimate from a small difference of nearly equal numbers can also lose numerical precision at much lower energies. Rationalise it as \(K=p^2c^2/(\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}+mc^2)\) when working near rest.

Several independent checks use this reconstruction without assuming the track fitter is correct. The turn time inferred from the fitted \(\gamma\) can be compared with a separate timing channel. Reverse the magnetic field to reverse the direction of curvature while keeping the speed and energy approximately fixed. Translate the beam within a mapped uniform field and look for changing fitted radius that correlates with position, an indication of geometric or field-map bias. Finally plot the residual of the magnetic force law separately for transverse magnitude and angle. A time integrator may conserve momentum magnitude for symmetry reasons even when its field interpolation biases the phase or shifts the guiding centre systematically.

Worked timing problem · infer an accelerating voltage without a radius

A tagged electron bunch completes ten nearly circular turns in a calibrated 5.0 mT magnetic field. Two time stamps indicate an elapsed 73.0 ns. The field is perpendicular to the orbit. Use the relativistic period sensitivity rather than the energy fraction directly

The nonrelativistic turn period is \(2\pi m/(eB)=7.145\) ns. The measured period is 7.30 ns, giving \(\gamma\simeq1.0217\) and \(K=(\gamma-1)mc^2\simeq11.1\) keV. The relative energy sensitivity is much larger than the relative period change when \(\gamma\) is close to one. A one-percent change in \(K\) corresponds to \(\Delta\gamma\simeq2.17\times10^{-4}\), or relative period change \(2.12\times10^{-4}\). Over the ten turns this gives approximately 15.5 ps.

To resolve that change spectroscopically, uncertainties in the elapsed time and field scale must together be significantly smaller than the equivalent change. A constant pickup cable delay cancels if the same electronics register both ends of the interval, while a drift of its timebase scale does not. Timing over more turns improves the total accumulated signal but also increases exposure to field inhomogeneity, energy loss and dephasing among particles with different injection energies. A narrower timing histogram does not automatically eliminate a systematic field calibration.

The measured wave packet may broaden so strongly that later crossings overlap. Recording the full pickup waveform and tracking ordered crossings, along with an independent rough radius estimate, can constrain the turn assignment before fitting the small relativistic shift. A perfect circular image with no time labels cannot supply that missing count.

Why momentum and kinetic energy do not gain fixed fractions together

In an electrostatic accelerating gap, the kinetic energy increment for a particle passing between two equipotential surfaces is its charge times the potential drop, regardless of injection energy. This statement follows by integrating the Lorentz power law over the actual path. A magnetic guiding field can alter the path and transit time while doing no direct work. A time-dependent electric field, a missed equipotential endpoint or appreciable radiation changes the simple inference and must be addressed separately.

For an electron entering with 10 keV and acquiring an additional 10 keV, kinetic energy doubles. Its initial momentum times \(c\) is about 101.6 keV and its final value about 144.4 keV. The radius in the same subsequent perpendicular magnetic field increases by a factor approximately 1.42. Doubling the radius would instead require doubling momentum, which demands a substantially greater energy increment at this low initial energy. Comparing radii before and after a known voltage supplies an independent check on the sign and magnitude of a particle's charge-to-mass model.

Semiclassical quantization

Quantum mechanics

Use a local wavelength to construct the quantization rule for a smooth well and recognize where that construction breaks down.

From first principles

Build the semiclassical phase and the turning-point condition

In a classically allowed interval define \(p(x)=\sqrt{2m[E-V(x)]}>0\) and seek a one-dimensional stationary wave \(\psi=A(x)e^{iS(x)/\hbar}\). Assume \(p\) varies little over a local wavelength. Substitute into the Schrödinger equation and separate terms by powers of \(\hbar\). The leading real and next imaginary balances give

\[(S')^2=p^2,\]
\[2A'S'+AS''=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^2 S'=\text{constant}.\]

Thus the phase increment is \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\) and the travelling-wave amplitude varies as \(p^{-1/2}\). This approximation fails where \(p=0\). Near each smooth simple turning point the potential is locally linear; the decaying exterior Airy solution matches an interior sine with phase shift \(\pi/4\). The waves entering from the left and right must describe the same standing wave. Combining the two end phases gives

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\]
\[n=0,1,\ldots .\]

The offset comes from the two soft turning points. A hard wall imposes its own node condition and changes the quantization offset. The construction becomes more reliable when the state samples many local wavelengths inside the well.

Derivation and applications

Read a quantum level spacing from a classical clock

The semiclassical quantization rule uses the area swept in phase space. Its change with energy also carries information about time. Consider a smooth one-dimensional binding potential with two simple turning points \(x_\pm(E)\), mass \(m\), and many accessible levels. Write the half-cycle action using the positive momentum branch \(p(x,E)=\sqrt{2m[E-V(x)]}\):

\[I(E)=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x,E)\,dx,\]
\[I(E_n)\simeq\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12).\]

The endpoints move with energy, but their boundary contributions when differentiating the integral contain \(p(x_\pm)=0\). Inside the well \(\partial_Ep=m/p\). Its divergence near a simple turning point behaves as an inverse square root and is integrable. Using \(p/m=\dot x\) on the forward leg now gives

\[\frac{dI}{dE}=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{m}{p(x,E)}\,dx=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{dx}{\dot x}=\frac{T_{\rm cl}(E)}{2}.\]

Two neighbouring quantized actions differ by \(\pi\hbar\). If the classical period varies little over a level spacing, linearizing the action about one level leads to

\[E_{n+1}-E_n\simeq\frac{2\pi\hbar}{T_{\rm cl}(E_n)}=\frac{h}{T_{\rm cl}(E_n)} .\]

Thus a densely spaced part of the spectrum is associated with a long classical excursion. For a harmonic well the period is constant and the result is exactly the known spacing; near a separatrix the rapidly varying period makes a one-step linearization less reliable. Only the period of a complete return to the starting phase-space point belongs in this relation.

Worked example

Estimate a frequency from adjacent lines

Consider an approximately harmonic vibrational well with nearby levels separated by \(2.50\) meV. With \(h=4.136\) meV ps, the inferred classical period is \(1.65\) ps and the ordinary oscillation frequency is \(0.604\) THz. A transit from the left turning point to the right should take about \(0.827\) ps. Equating this one-way transit with the period would overestimate the energy spacing by two.

Second application

Predict the scaling change for a quartic well

In \(V(x)=\lambda x^4\) the turning point is \(x_t=(E/\lambda)^{1/4}\). Substituting \(x=x_tu\) into \(T_{\rm cl}=2\int_{-x_t}^{x_t}m\,dx/p\) leaves an energy-independent integral and gives \(T_{\rm cl}\propto E^{-1/4}\). Therefore local level spacing grows as \(E^{1/4}\). At sixteen times the energy a spacing of \(0.180\) meV should become about \(0.360\) meV, assuming both regions contain enough levels for semiclassical counting. The quadratic trap would show no comparable growth.

A locally oscillating solution

Consider one-dimensional bound motion of mass \(m\) in a smoothly varying potential. In allowed regions the stationary equation is \(\psi''+p(x)^2\psi/\hbar^2=0\) with \(p=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}\). If the potential changes little over a local wavelength, a small interval resembles a free-particle region. Accumulating its local phase produces the action integral \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\). Yet a wave with changing local momentum must also change amplitude in order to conserve current.

Substitute \(\psi=A(x)\exp[iS(x)/\hbar]\). The leading real terms give \(S'^2=p^2\). The next imaginary terms give \(2A'S'+AS''=0\), or \((A^2S')'=0\). Therefore each travelling contribution has amplitude proportional to \(1/\sqrt p\). Equivalently a classical particle dwells longer where it is slow, and the smoothed semiclassical probability mirrors this dwell time. The neglected derivative terms are small away from turning points when \(\hbar|p'|\ll p^2\).

Match through a turning point

At an ordinary turning point \(V(x_t)=E\) with nonzero slope, the local wavelength diverges and the previous criterion fails. Linearizing \(V\) there gives the Airy equation. Its decaying forbidden-side solution continues to an oscillatory allowed-side standing wave with a phase offset of \(\pi/4\). Matching decaying exterior tails on both sides of a well bounded by two such turning points imposes

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots .\]

The offset encodes the two smooth reflections. An infinite hard wall instead imposes a node directly and has a different reflection phase. Applying this rule unchanged to a hard box would count its levels incorrectly. For slowly varying barriers the forbidden action also controls exponential attenuation, but the two-turning-point integral above determines the energies of a classically allowed well.

A trap with measurable spacings

For \(V=m\omega^2x^2/2\) let \(x_t=\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}\). Put \(x=x_t\sin u\) in the action integral. The integrand becomes \((2E/\omega)\cos^2u\,du\) between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), yielding \(\pi E/\omega\). The rule gives \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)\), exceptionally exact even for the oscillator’s lowest levels.

For a mechanical trap at frequency 100 kHz, \(E_4=4.5hf=2.98\times10^{-28}\) J, equivalent to \(21.6\) μK on division by \(k_B\). The spacing is \(hf\) or \(4.80\) μK. These temperatures express energy units; no equilibrium ensemble was assumed. As a transfer test consider \(V=gx^4\). Rescaling \(x=(E/g)^{1/4}y\) makes the action proportional to \(E^{3/4}\). Quantization gives \(E_n\propto(n+1/2)^{4/3}\), so successive spacings grow with excitation. At sufficiently high level number the action changes slowly over a wavelength, making the predicted trend more trustworthy than a low-level numerical prefactor.

An independent check on the energy spacing follows by differentiating the action with respect to energy. The integrand vanishes at the turning points, so their moving endpoints add no first-order term. Inside the integral \(\partial p/\partial E=m/p=1/v\). Consequently the derivative of the one-way action is the time for the classical particle to travel from one turning point to the other. Adjacent quantum levels at high excitation then have separation approximately \(h\) divided by the full classical period. Measuring a variation of level spacing with excitation is therefore also probing the dependence of the classical orbit period on energy.

Estimate tunnelling between two bound wells

A nearly symmetric double well can have pairs of quantum states concentrated mainly in classically separate regions. For one such pair take approximately orthogonal localized states \(|L\rangle,|R\rangle\) with equal isolated energy \(E_0\) and a small real tunnelling matrix element \(-J\). The sign of either localized basis state can be chosen so \(J>0\). The two-level Hamiltonian has symmetric and antisymmetric eigenstates with energies \(E_0-J\) and \(E_0+J\), respectively. A local preparation \(|L\rangle\) is a superposition of both and acquires a relative phase at rate \(2J/\hbar\).

On evolution the probability to be in the other well is \(P_R(t)=\sin^2(Jt/\hbar)\). The first complete transfer occurs at \(t_{\rm tr}=\pi\hbar/(2J)=h/(2\Delta E)\) with \(\Delta E=2J\). In a high smooth barrier the localized wave tails overlap weakly. A semiclassical estimate gives \(J\simeq J_0 e^{-\mathcal S/\hbar}\), with \(\mathcal S=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m[V(x)-E_0]}\,dx\) between inner turning points. This is an amplitude action. The through-barrier transmission probability of a scattering experiment instead contains twice that exponent. Confusing the two quantities gives an incorrect predicted change in the oscillation time.

Predict how a gate changes a tunnelling period

Suppose a symmetric electron double well shows a spectroscopic splitting \(\Delta E=40.0\) μeV. Its predicted first full transfer time is \(h/(2\Delta E)=51.7\) ps. Use the measured splitting to eliminate the unknown semiclassical prefactor. A gate modification adds a nearly flat forbidden segment of width \(0.300\) nm where \(V-E_0=0.150\) eV and otherwise changes the wells negligibly. Using \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV nm² gives decay wavenumber \(\kappa=\sqrt{0.150/0.0381}=1.98\) nm⁻¹. The added dimensionless action is 0.595.

If the prefactor and individual well frequencies remain similar, the new splitting is \(40.0e^{-0.595}=22.1\) μeV, and transfer time rises to \(93.6\) ps. Doubling the width change multiplies the splitting by the square of the original ratio, not by twice the original ratio. These estimates concern coherent dynamics before energy relaxation or phase noise destroys the relative phase.

A gate can also shift the two well bottoms differently. With detuning \(\delta\) the splitting is \(\sqrt{\delta^2+4J^2}\), while maximum transfer falls to \(4J^2/(\delta^2+4J^2)\). A larger measured splitting can therefore accompany poorer population transfer if symmetry is lost. Comparing spectroscopy and time-resolved populations distinguishes a changed tunnel coupling from an unintended potential offset.

The small tunnel coupling also sets a time criterion for disorder: a fluctuating left–right offset comparable with the splitting can produce substantial changes in both transfer amplitude and oscillation frequency. A static distribution of offsets across repeated preparations can wash out ensemble oscillations even when each individual preparation evolves coherently. Repeating the experiment after compensating the measured detuning separates this ensemble averaging from intrinsic energy relaxation. The semiclassical action comparison is most reliable when the two localized levels remain nearly resonant throughout the modification and no higher intrawell level enters the same small energy window.

Independent measurements of the individual well minima supply a practical detuning calibration.

A phase accumulated by slowly turning a magnetic field

Quantum mechanics

A spin aligned with a slowly rotating field returns to its original measurement probabilities after one loop. Derive the additional phase that becomes observable when that path interferes with a reference path.

Separate following the state from acquiring a phase

Let a spin one-half have \(H=-(\hbar\omega_0/2)\,\mathbf n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), with \(\mathbf n\) a unit vector and constant \(\omega_0>0\). The two instantaneous energies are separated by \(\hbar\omega_0\). Prepare the lower-energy state and rotate \(\mathbf n\) on a smooth path. If the rate of change of its eigenvector is small compared with the gap in angular-frequency units, transitions to the upper state are small. Following one eigenstate still allows a time-dependent complex phase.

Write \(|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\alpha(t)}|n(t)\rangle\), substitute in the time-dependent Schrödinger equation and project onto \(\langle n|\). Normalization makes \(\langle n|\dot n\rangle\) imaginary. Consequently

\[\dot\alpha=-E_n/\hbar+i\langle n|\dot n\rangle .\]

The first contribution accumulates in proportion to elapsed time. The integral of the second depends on the path of the instantaneous eigenvector. The splitting into pieces changes with the arbitrary phase used to define \(|n(t)\rangle\); after a closed loop the geometric phase in a single-valued gauge is invariant modulo \(2\pi\).

Evaluate a circle on the sphere of directions

Represent the aligned spin by the column \((\cos(\theta/2),e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2))^T\). Its mean spin points along the polar angles \(\theta,\phi\). Hold \(\theta\) fixed and increase \(\phi\) by one revolution. Only the lower component contributes to the derivative. Its coefficient is its occupation weight:

\[i\langle n|\partial_\phi n\rangle=-\sin^2(\theta/2),\]
\[\gamma=-2\pi\sin^2(\theta/2)=-\pi(1-\cos\theta).\]

The solid angle enclosed by the latitude is \(2\pi(1-\cos\theta)\), found by integrating \(\sin\theta'\,d\theta'\,d\phi\) over the cap. The aligned spin accumulates minus half this angle. Reversing traversal changes its sign. As the cone closes to a point, the result vanishes continuously. Around the equator the result is \(-\pi\).

Predict an interference fringe

Split a coherent preparation into two equal-amplitude paths and arrange the reference so the dynamical phases match. If only the signal path gains \(\gamma\), an ideal balanced recombiner gives the bright-port probability \(P=(1+\cos\gamma)/2\) for zero additional reference phase. The overall phase of one isolated spin would never produce this signal. The reference path supplies a phase comparison.

For \(\theta=60^\circ\), the solid angle is \(\pi\), the geometric phase is \(-\pi/2\), and \(P=1/2\). For a circuit of the equator the same port becomes dark. Reversing the \(60^\circ\) loop still gives one-half at zero reference phase. Introduce a known reference shift \(\chi\): the probability becomes \([1+\cos(\chi+\gamma)]/2\) in the chosen convention. Choosing \(\chi=\pi/2\) changes the two directions of traversal into full and zero probability, revealing the sign.

Scan the reference phase

Set the cone angle, then vary the reference phase. The horizontal axis scans the cone angle and the marker value is returned in the readout. A source with finite path coherence would reduce the modulation depth, whereas unmatched dynamical phases would shift the fringe.

A magnetic orbit that spirals outward in a perfect vacuum

Numerical field laboratory

Track a charged particle in a prescribed uniform field, derive a rotation update and separate numerical energy growth from a real magnetic force.

Use the work law before solving the trajectory

A nonrelativistic charged particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) moves perpendicular to a uniform, time-independent magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\). There is no electric field and the dilute test particles do not affect the applied field. The Lorentz force gives \(\dot v_x=\omega v_y,\ \dot v_y=-\omega v_x\) for \(\omega=qB/m\). Dotting with \(\mathbf v\) gives zero, so the speed should stay constant. This property holds for either sign of the charge; the direction of rotation changes. It is a better initial test of a trajectory solver than how circular a short segment looks on screen.

Define \(J(v_x,v_y)=(v_y,-v_x)\), so \(J^T=-J\) and \(J^2=-I\). The exact velocity over a time step \(h\) is \([\cos(\omega h)I+\sin(\omega h)J]\mathbf v\). Integration in time gives a circular orbit of radius \(v_\perp/|\omega|\) once the guiding centre is chosen. A magnetic field alone bends a trajectory without changing its kinetic energy; a slowly growing orbit radius at fixed \(B\) and fixed initial speed demands an explanation.

Why forward Euler adds energy at every step

Euler gives \(\mathbf v_+=(I+dJ)\mathbf v\) with \(d=\omega h\). Squaring the norm eliminates the cross term because \(v^TJv=0\), leaving \(\|\mathbf v_+\|^2=(1+d^2)\|\mathbf v\|^2\). No nonzero step has exact unit gain. A geometric picture makes the bias tangible: a tangent step leaves the initial velocity circle on its outside, whatever the point of departure.

After \(N\) steps the speed factor is \((1+d^2)^{N/2}\). With 24 steps per physical orbit, \(d=2\pi/24\simeq0.2618\), so a full orbit multiplies speed by roughly 2.2. At shorter times or smaller steps this can resemble weak physical acceleration. Reducing \(h\) at fixed physical duration reduces the growth. Testing one step at fixed count, rather than comparing over a fixed physical interval, underestimates the cumulative error.

Solve a centred magnetic step exactly

Use the time-centred velocity in the Lorentz force: \(\mathbf v_+-\mathbf v=d J(\mathbf v_++\mathbf v)/2\). Premultiply by the transpose of \(\mathbf v_++\mathbf v\). The left side is the difference of squared speeds and the right side vanishes, so the update preserves speed for every \(d\) in exact arithmetic. Rearranging yields a two-dimensional linear solve; using \(J^2=-I\) turns it into a rotation,

\[\mathbf v_+=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\mathbf v+\frac{2t}{1+t^2}J\mathbf v,\]
\[t=d/2.\]

These coefficients are \(\cos\alpha\) and \(\sin\alpha\) for \(\alpha=2\arctan(d/2)\). This is the magnetic rotation used in the nonrelativistic Boris integrator. For simultaneous electric fields, the usual split step adds two half electric impulses around the magnetic rotation. The current experiment isolates the magnetic part; it makes no claim about a nonuniform electric field or the accuracy of interpolation from a field mesh to particles.
