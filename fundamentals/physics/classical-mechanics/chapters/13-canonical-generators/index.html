What does a change of coordinates have to preserve?

A counter moving along a rail reports the bead position relative to the counter. Its reading alone does not transform the bead momentum or tell us how energy changes in the moving frame. Let the laboratory Hamiltonian be \(H(q,p,t)\). The phase-space action integrates \(p\dot q-H\) between specified configurations. Adding a total time derivative changes that integral only at the ends. It therefore provides a way to construct new coordinates in which the same interior trajectories make the action stationary.

Demand that the old and new action forms differ by an exact differential. A convenient function uses the old position and the new momentum as arguments. Subtract \(QP\) from it to convert the independent end variables. The product rule gives

\[p\,dq-H\,dt=P\,dQ-K\,dt+d(F_2-QP)=dF_2-Q\,dP-K\,dt .\]

Now compare the coefficients of the independent differentials separately. The new energy has an extra term whenever the coordinate map depends explicitly on time.

\[p=\partial_q F_2,\]
\[Q=\partial_P F_2,\]
\[K=H+\partial_t F_2 .\]

For a simple test take \(F_2=\lambda qP\) with constant positive \(\lambda\). Then \(Q=\lambda q\) and \(P=p/\lambda\). A position interval stretches while the conjugate momentum interval shrinks by the reciprocal factor. The product \(dq\,dp\) stays fixed. This checks the derivative rules without using a particular force law.

Move the origin and then move the momentum

Take the trap centre to be \(a(t)\) and the laboratory potential to be \(k(q-a)^2/2\). Choosing \(F_2=(q-a)P\) gives \(Q=q-a,\ p=P\). Thus the coordinate is relative position, but its conjugate momentum still equals the laboratory momentum. Hamilton's velocity equation reads \(\dot Q=P/m-\dot a\), exactly the velocity seen by the moving counter.

To obtain a momentum equal to \(m\dot Q\), choose \(F_2=(q-a)(P+m\dot a)+g(t)\). This produces \(p=P+m\dot a\). Substitute it into the kinetic energy and the explicit time derivative of the generator. The cross terms \(\dot a P\) cancel. The remaining terms depending on time alone can be removed by choosing \(\dot g=m\dot a^2/2\). The result is

\[K=\frac{P^2}{2m}+\frac{kQ^2}{2}+m\ddot a\,Q,\]
\[m\ddot Q=-kQ-m\ddot a .\]

The apparent force comes from the acceleration of the reference point. A constant translation speed drops out of this final equation. A time-dependent acceleration can do work on the relative motion, so the bead's relative mechanical energy need not be conserved.

Predict a displaced equilibrium

Use a 0.50 kg bead and a spring with \(k=8.0\) N/m. Accelerate the support uniformly at \(1.6\) m/s² in the positive direction. A stationary bead in the accelerating frame has \(Q_*=-m\ddot a/k=-0.100\) m. Relative oscillations about this new centre have the original angular frequency \(4.0\) rad/s. The curvature of the potential sets the frequency and the added linear term changes its minimum.

If the bead is initially at \(Q=0\) with zero relative velocity when acceleration starts, its subsequent displacement is \(Q=Q_*[1-\cos(\omega t)]\). At \(t=\pi/\omega=0.7854\) s it reaches \(-0.200\) m, twice the static shift. It then returns. An overdamped measuring device would instead approach the static reading without that overshoot. Damping has been omitted here.

Tutorial on reconstructing the motion

A bead released from the old equilibrium reaches its first relative extremum at −0.060 m after 0.50 s. Find its angular frequency and the support acceleration.

Work through the solution

The first extremum occurs after half a cycle, giving \(\omega=\pi/0.50=6.283\) rad/s. The equilibrium shift is half the extremum, \(Q_*=-0.030\) m. The equation for that shift gives \(\ddot a=-\omega^2Q_*=1.18\) m/s². Both quantities follow from the trajectory without a separate mass measurement.

Check the momentum transformation directly by measuring kinetic energy. In a frame attached to a support moving at constant \(0.30\,\mathrm{m/s}\), a \(0.50\) kg bead has \(P=0.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\) along the support velocity. What laboratory momentum and kinetic energy should an observer assign?

Work through the solution

The moving-frame kinetic momentum is \(m(\dot q-\dot a)\). Thus \(p=P+m\dot a=0.35\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\), the relative kinetic energy is \(P^2/(2m)=0.0400\,\mathrm J\) and the laboratory kinetic energy is \(p^2/(2m)=0.1225\,\mathrm J\). Expanding the square gives the missing cross term \(P\dot a=0.0600\,\mathrm J\) and offset \(m\dot a^2/2=0.0225\,\mathrm J\).

A support acceleration varies with time. How could you reconstruct it from a measured relative trajectory, and which measurements become unreliable first in the presence of position noise?

Work through the solution

Rearranging the equation gives \(\ddot a=-\ddot Q-kQ/m\). A static deflection measures only constant acceleration after transients are controlled. In a time-dependent experiment a second time derivative is unavoidable for this inversion. It amplifies short-time position noise, so fitted trajectories or independent acceleration data can test the reconstruction.
