From first principles

Find the instability threshold of the rotating hoop

Assume a frictionless bead constrained to a vertical circular hoop of radius \(R\). The hoop rotates at prescribed constant angular speed \(\Omega\) about its vertical diameter. With angle \(\theta\) measured upward from the bottom, the bead moves along the hoop at speed \(R\dot\theta\) and around the axis at speed \(\Omega R\sin\theta\). These directions are perpendicular, and its gravitational height above the bottom is \(R(1-\cos\theta)\). Thus its Lagrangian is

\[L=\tfrac12mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)-mgR(1-\cos\theta).\]

Apply the one-coordinate Lagrange equation. The rotation term tends to move the bead outward while gravity acts toward the bottom:

\[\ddot\theta=\sin\theta(\Omega^2\cos\theta-g/R).\]

Linearizing about the bottom gives

\[\ddot\theta\simeq(\Omega^2-g/R)\theta.\]

For \(\Omega^2<g/R\) the coefficient restores a small displacement; above threshold the bottom has growing small disturbances. Setting the exact right side to zero gives the additional equilibrium and its range:

\[\Omega_c=\sqrt{g/R},\]
\[\cos\theta_*=g/(R\Omega^2)\quad(\Omega\ge\Omega_c).\]

The two nonzero angles are related by reflection. Their existence follows from the prescribed rotation, so a freely slowing hoop would require an additional dynamical coordinate.

Derivation and applications

How high is the barrier between the two tilted equilibria?

In the rotating hoop, a new pair of stable angles appears where the bottom loses stability. Its curvature predicts a small-oscillation frequency; its depth determines whether a larger disturbance can carry the bead across the bottom. Consider a bead of mass \(m\) on a circular hoop of radius \(R\) spinning at prescribed constant angular speed \(\Omega\) about its vertical diameter. Ignore friction. Measure \(\theta\) upward along the hoop from the bottom. The vertical height change is \(R(1-\cos\theta)\) and the distance to the rotation axis is \(R\sin\theta\).

The constrained bead has kinetic energy \(mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)/2\). Collect the terms independent of \(\dot\theta\) as the potential governing motion in the rotating coordinate:

\[U_{\rm eff}(\theta)=mgR(1-\cos\theta)-\tfrac12mR^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta .\]

Write \(s=\Omega^2R/g\) and differentiate. A tilted equilibrium exists only for \(s>1\). At either stable angle \(\theta_*\) substitute \(\cos\theta_*=1/s\) into the potential and factor the result:

\[U'_{\rm eff}=mgR\sin\theta(1-s\cos\theta),\]
\[U_{\rm eff}(\theta_*)=-mgR\,\frac{(s-1)^2}{2s}.\]

The bottom has \(U_{\rm eff}(0)=0\). Subtracting the energies gives the barrier and the threshold angular speed for a bead launched from a tilted minimum toward the bottom:

\[\Delta U=mgR\,\frac{(s-1)^2}{2s},\]
\[|\dot\theta|_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2\Delta U/(mR^2)}.\]

The velocity here is angular speed relative to the hoop. At threshold the trajectory approaches the unstable bottom asymptotically; crossing takes slightly more initial energy. Near \(s=1\) the barrier collapses quadratically even though two distinct equilibria already exist. The externally maintained rotation can exchange ordinary laboratory energy with the bead, while the effective one-coordinate energy is conserved.

Worked example

Estimate the speed needed to change branches

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

Take \(m=0.0500\) kg, \(R=0.200\) m and choose \(\Omega\) so that \(s=2.00\). The two minima then occur at \(\pm60.0^\circ\) and the barrier is

\[mgR/4=0.0245\,\mathrm{J}.\]

The tangential threshold speed is \(\sqrt{2\Delta U/m}=0.990\) m/s, or \(4.95\) rad/s along the hoop. A launch at \(0.800\) m/s has only \(0.0160\) J and returns to its original branch.

Second application

Use two measured angles to compare barrier depths

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

On the same hoop reduce the rotation until the two minima are at \(\pm36.9^\circ\). Their cosine is about \(0.800\), giving \(s=1.25\). The new barrier is \(mgR(0.25)^2/2.50=0.00245\) J, a tenth of the previous value. The new threshold tangential speed is \(0.313\) m/s. A smaller change in angle than this tenfold energy reduction might suggest is a measurable feature of a shallow double well.

Locate the bifurcation by measuring curvature

A vertical hoop rotates about its vertical diameter at a prescribed angular speed \(\Omega\). A frictionless bead can move along the hoop. Measure \(\theta\) from the bottom. The laboratory kinetic energy is \(mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)/2\). In the equation for the bead the effective potential per unit \(mgR\) is \(-\cos\theta-(\Omega^2R/2g)\sin^2\theta\). Its derivative factors as \(\sin\theta[1-(\Omega^2R/g)\cos\theta]\).

Open this experiment online

Plot the potential before and after crossing \(\Omega_c=\sqrt{g/R}\). At a stable equilibrium the slope vanishes and the curvature is positive. Above the critical speed the bottom loses positive curvature and two stable angles appear. The motor holding \(\Omega\) fixed exchanges angular momentum with the system. A freely rotating hoop with fixed total angular momentum describes a different experiment.

Reduce the constraint to one coordinate

A bead slides on a vertical circular hoop of radius \(R\). An external motor rotates the hoop at prescribed constant angular velocity \(\Omega\) about its vertical diameter. Let \(\theta\) be the angle from the lowest point. The bead’s height relative to that point is \(R(1-\cos\theta)\) and its distance from the rotation axis is \(R\sin\theta\). Assume negligible friction and a rigid constraint. The bead moves both along the hoop and azimuthally with it, even if \(\theta\) is momentarily stationary.

In laboratory coordinates its speed squared is \(R^2\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta\). Subtract gravitational potential energy from this kinetic energy to construct a one-coordinate Lagrangian. Lagrange’s equation gives

\[\ddot\theta=\sin\theta(\Omega^2\cos\theta-g/R).\]

Although motion in the laboratory is constrained by a driven apparatus, the reduced equation is autonomous for constant \(\Omega\).

Track equilibria as the rotation rate changes

The torque vanishes at the bottom and the top because \(\sin\theta=0\). Off-axis equilibria also appear when \(\cos\theta=g/(\Omega^2R)\). They are possible only if the rotation exceeds \(\Omega_c=\sqrt{g/R}\). Each positive-angle equilibrium then has a negative-angle partner. The two have equal energies because the apparatus is symmetric.

For small displacement from the bottom,

\[\ddot\theta\simeq-(g/R-\Omega^2)\theta.\]

Below threshold the bottom is stable with angular frequency \(\sqrt{g/R-\Omega^2}\). As the threshold is approached this frequency tends to zero; the potential flattens. Above threshold the sign reverses and a small perturbation grows. Linearising about either displaced equilibrium instead gives a positive frequency squared \(\Omega^2\sin^2\theta_{\rm eq}\). Measuring this small-amplitude response distinguishes two predictions: where the bead rests and how steeply it is restored when disturbed.

Interpret the effective energy

The reduced motion admits the effective potential \(U_{\rm eff}=mgR(1-\cos\theta)-m\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta/2\). Its derivative predicts the reduced equation. Its negative centrifugal term differs in sign from the positive laboratory azimuthal kinetic energy. The motor supplies or absorbs work as the bead’s distance from the rotation axis changes. Treating the bead’s laboratory mechanical energy alone as conserved would therefore lead to the wrong equilibrium.

At exactly the critical rate, the quadratic terms in a small-angle expansion cancel and the first restoring term in \(U_{\rm eff}\) is positive and quartic. A finite disturbance can still oscillate there, but its period depends on its amplitude. An arbitrarily small oscillation cannot be assigned a finite harmonic frequency at that point.

Estimate the transition in a real apparatus

With \(R=0.250\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s² the critical rate is 6.26 rad/s, or about 59.8 rpm. At 5.0 rad/s the bead returns to the bottom with small-oscillation frequency \(\sqrt{39.24-25}=3.77\) rad/s. At 8.0 rad/s the two new equilibria satisfy \(\cos\theta=39.24/64=0.613\), or \(\theta=\pm52.2°\). Small oscillations about either have angular frequency \(8\sqrt{1-0.613^2}=6.32\) rad/s.

When observing the transition, tilt of the axis or a small asymmetry of the hoop can favour one side. Hysteresis-like delays during a fast sweep can also arise from finite settling time in weak damping. Recording both equilibrium angles on slow sweeps and the independently measured oscillation frequency supplies a more discriminating test of the symmetric bifurcation than simply noting which side the bead occupies.

Break the symmetry with a weak bias

The two off-centre positions of the ideal rotating bead have equal energy. A small constant generalized torque \(\tau_b\) favouring positive \(\theta\) breaks that symmetry. Over a small working angular range it adds a term \(-\tau_b\theta\) to the effective potential. Write the original potential relative to its value at the bottom as \(mgR(1-\cos\theta)-m\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta/2\). Expanding near the critical rate \(\Omega_c^2=g/R\) gives quadratic coefficient \(mR^2(\Omega_c^2-\Omega^2)/2\). The quartic coefficient evaluated at threshold is \(mR^2\Omega_c^2/8\).

On the high-speed side define \(a=\Omega^2-\Omega_c^2>0\), \(b=\Omega_c^2/2\), and \(h=\tau_b/(mR^2)\). To leading order stationarity and local stability become

\[b\theta^3-a\theta-h=0,\qquad 3b\theta^2-a>0 .\]

With zero bias there are two symmetric minima and an unstable central position. Increasing positive bias deepens the positive minimum and eventually destroys the negative one. At the point where the negative minimum meets the adjacent maximum, both the stationarity equation and its derivative in \(\theta\) vanish. Thus

\[\theta_*=-\sqrt{a/(3b)},\]
\[h_*=(2a/3)\sqrt{a/(3b)}.\]

The critical bias scales as \(a^{3/2}\). Near the threshold a surprisingly small imperfection can remove one of the two resting positions entirely.

Estimate the bias needed to select a branch

Use a bead of mass \(0.0500\) kg on a hoop of radius \(0.250\) m. Then \(\Omega_c^2=39.24\) s⁻². Set \(\Omega^2=\Omega_c^2+0.600\) s⁻², making \(b=19.62\) s⁻². The unbiased displaced equilibrium angles are approximately \(\pm\sqrt{0.600/19.62}=\pm0.175\) rad. As positive bias is increased, the negative branch disappears near \(\theta_*=-0.101\) rad. The corresponding generalized acceleration is \(h_*=0.0404\) s⁻², giving \(\tau_b=mR^2h_*=1.26\times10^{-4}\) N m. A tangential force with moment arm \(R\) would supply about \(0.505\) mN.

This estimate uses both small angles and operation close enough to the critical speed that quartic truncation is accurate. An exact calculation differentiates the full trigonometric potential twice and solves the two equations simultaneously. In a slow sweep with weak damping, a bead initially on the unfavoured branch can remain there until its minimum disappears. Vibration or thermal agitation may make it switch earlier by crossing the remaining barrier. Repeating the sweep in both directions and at several bias amplitudes helps distinguish an imperfect constraint from random switching in a nearly symmetric system.

The same model predicts increasing sensitivity before an actual switch. Differentiate the cubic stationarity condition with respect to bias while following a particular equilibrium branch:

\[d\theta/dh=1/(3b\theta^2-a).\]

Its denominator is the local dimensionless restoring curvature. Thus a branch nearing disappearance both moves a large angular distance under a small bias increment and shows a very low small-oscillation frequency. Measuring those two effects on the same branch tests the local shape of the potential without requiring a precise measurement of the noisy switching moment. Away from a minimum the static slope can instead be negative, describing an unstable mathematical solution.

The sharp loss of a branch is best resolved with bias steps small compared with the measured interval between its creation and disappearance.

Where will a bead settle on a rotating hoop?

Choose zero potential at the bottom. Gravity contributes \(mgR(1-\cos\theta)\); the rotating-frame centrifugal term contributes \(-m\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta/2\). Inspect both the slope and the curvature at the bottom while increasing speed.
