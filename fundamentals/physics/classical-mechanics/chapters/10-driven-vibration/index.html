Forced, damped vibration

Use amplitude, phase and power together to estimate the mechanical properties of an oscillator from a sinusoidal drive.

Separate the transient from the driven motion

A shaker applies a sinusoidal force to a mass on a spring and viscous damper. Its displacement can lag the force and can remain large even when the applied force is small. Assume motion about a stable equilibrium, constant stiffness \(k\), positive damping coefficient \(c\) and drive \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Newton’s equation is \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t\). The solution contains a homogeneous contribution set by preparation and a particular response set by the continuing drive.

Positive damping makes the homogeneous part decay. To estimate the steady frequency response, measurements must last several decay times at each frequency. Measurements taken immediately after the drive changes include residual free motion. Close to weakly damped resonance that settling time can span many oscillations, so sweeping too rapidly can shift the apparent location of a peak.

Solve for amplitude and lag

Write the steady displacement as the real part of \(Xe^{i\omega t}\) and take \(F_0\) real. Substituting gives \(X=F_0/(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)\). The denominator groups the elastic and inertial contributions along the real axis and viscous loading in quadrature. Its magnitude gives

\[|X|=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2}}.\]

The lag angle is the argument of the denominator, evaluated in the appropriate quadrant. At low frequency \(X\simeq F_0/k\), in phase with force. At high frequency \(X\simeq-F_0/(m\omega^2)\), nearly opposite in phase. At \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) the reactive terms cancel and the displacement lags by a quarter cycle. This phase condition identifies the undamped natural frequency even when the amplitude maximum lies slightly lower.

Connect absorbed power to damping

Multiply the equation by \(\dot x\) and average over one settled cycle. Both stored kinetic and elastic energies return to their initial values, so the mean input power is dissipated by the damper. For peak displacement, \(\langle P\rangle=c\omega^2|X|^2/2\). At \(\omega_n\), the velocity is in phase with force, giving \(\langle P\rangle=F_0^2/(2c)\). Minimising the denominator of the displacement response gives a different frequency, \(\omega_{\rm peak}^2=k/m-c^2/(2m^2)\), if this is positive. A reported “resonance frequency” should identify the measured observable.

A power scan near a lightly damped resonance has a narrow peak whose half-power bandwidth is approximately \(c/m\) in rad/s. The damping estimate obtained from that width can be compared with an independent ring-down estimate. Significant disagreement could indicate an amplitude-dependent damper or loss in the drive system.

Read a frequency-response experiment

Take \(m=2.0\) kg, \(k=800\) N/m, \(c=8.0\) N·s/m and \(F_0=4.0\) N. The low-frequency displacement is 5.0 mm. At 20 rad/s the displacement is 25 mm, the velocity amplitude is 0.50 m/s and the mean power is 1.0 W. The displacement maximum occurs near \(\sqrt{400-8}=19.8\) rad/s. A run at a single frequency near the peak cannot identify all three parameters. A low-frequency measurement constrains stiffness, the quarter-cycle lag constrains mass relative to stiffness and the absorbed power at that frequency constrains damping independently.

Motion imposed at the support

A vibration mount is often driven through a moving base rather than by a calibrated force on the mass. Let \(y(t)\) be the prescribed absolute displacement of the support and \(x(t)\) the absolute displacement of a supported mass \(m\). A spring and a viscous damper both connect mass to base, so they respond to \(x-y\) and \(\dot x-\dot y\). Newton’s equation is \(m\ddot x+c(\dot x-\dot y)+k(x-y)=0\). The same mass and stiffness as in a force-driven oscillator can therefore produce a different transfer function because the input enters both elastic and dissipative forces.

For harmonic motion proportional to \(e^{i\omega t}\) rearrange the amplitude equation to give \((k-m\omega^2+i c\omega)X=(k+i c\omega)Y\). Set \(r=\omega/\omega_n\), \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n)\). The ratio of absolute displacement amplitudes is

\[\left|\frac XY\right|^2=\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}.\]

At very low frequencies the mass follows the support almost rigidly. To have \(|X|<|Y|\) requires \((1-r^2)^2>1\), which for positive frequency means \(r>\sqrt2\). Increasing damping reduces the resonant peak but increases the high-frequency path through which the moving support can exert a force. Damping is therefore a design variable with different consequences in different frequency bands.

Check both isolation and suspension travel

A 5.00 kg optical instrument sits on springs with total stiffness \(2000\) N/m and damping ratio \(0.10\). Its natural angular frequency is \(20.0\) rad/s and viscous coefficient \(c=2\zeta m\omega_n=20.0\) N s/m. A nearby machine imposes a sinusoidal base motion of amplitude \(0.800\) mm at \(60.0\) rad/s. Here \(r=3.00\) and \(2\zeta r=0.600\), giving \(|X/Y|=\sqrt{1.36/64.36}=0.145\). The instrument moves through an absolute amplitude of \(0.116\) mm.

The suspension itself accommodates relative displacement. Subtract \(Y\) from \(X\) in the complex equation before taking a magnitude. This gives \(|(X-Y)/Y|=r^2/\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}=1.12\). The spring travel is therefore about \(0.898\) mm, exceeding the base amplitude despite successful instrument isolation. Subtracting the two absolute amplitudes directly would ignore phase and underpredict travel. At twice the damping ratio the same calculation gives \(|X/Y|=0.193\), so high-frequency isolation degrades even as resonance becomes less dangerous. Two simultaneous position sensors, on base and instrument, allow both absolute response and relative travel to be assessed.

A common practical constraint is allowable acceleration rather than displacement. In a sinusoidal test absolute acceleration has amplitude \(\omega^2|X|\). For the stated 60 rad/s motion the mount gives an instrument acceleration of \(3600\times0.000116=0.418\) m/s². The bare base acceleration is \(2.88\) m/s², so the ratio is the same as the displacement ratio at that particular frequency. Across a sweep the factor \(\omega^2\) changes. A decreasing displacement curve therefore does not guarantee a decreasing absolute acceleration, especially if the operational environment has a roughly constant displacement excitation over its upper frequency band.

Mechanical stops add a further check on the model. Once the relative travel reaches a stop, the constraint changes within each cycle, producing harmonics absent from the linear solution. A distorted accelerometer trace can therefore identify a travel problem before mean isolation performance is assessed.

Which frequency does a phase measurement identify?

Drive a viscously damped oscillator with a sinusoidal force, measure displacement and retain its phase relative to the force. The complex compliance is proportional to \(1/(1-r^2+2i\zeta r)\). Use the quadrant-aware argument near and above resonance.
