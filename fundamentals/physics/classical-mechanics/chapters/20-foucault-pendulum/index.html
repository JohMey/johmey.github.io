A free pendulum supplies two clocks. The bob returns in seconds, while the axis traced on the floor takes hours to rotate. Derive both rates from the tension and gravity, then work out what a camera actually measures and how a slightly imperfect suspension can imitate part of the signal.

A clock in a room that is itself rotating

Hang a bob from a long, flexible thread. Mark an east–west line on the floor, pull the bob a little way east of its resting point and release it without a push. Watching a few passages through the centre usually suggests a fixed swing plane. Compare photographs taken hours apart, however, and the line traced in the room may point somewhere else. To describe that observation we need the acceleration relative to the room. A distant inertial observer and a camera attached to the building do not use the same time derivative of a rotating vector.

For a vector expressed in axes rotating with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), its inertial derivative is its rotating-frame derivative plus \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A\). Apply this rule twice to position. For steady rotation the acceleration contains the local acceleration, twice the cross product of rotation rate with relative velocity, and the centrifugal contribution with the opposite apparent force when moved to the equation for relative acceleration. The motion of the support and the steady centrifugal force are already included when a plumb line defines local vertical and a gravimeter supplies \(g\). Using that measured \(g\) a second time as a purely gravitational inertial acceleration would double-count part of the effect.

Choose \(x\) east, \(y\) north and \(z\) up. At latitude \(\lambda\) the vertical component of Earth's angular velocity is \(f=\Omega\sin\lambda\). For a nearly horizontal bob velocity the product of the horizontal rotation component with that velocity is vertical. It therefore changes the tension to first order but makes no leading horizontal Coriolis force. Small horizontal displacements tilt the thread by approximately \(x/L\) and \(y/L\); multiplying by the equilibrium tension \(mg\) gives the linear restoring components. Ignoring damping and spatial changes of effective gravity across the apparatus, the result is

\[\ddot x=-\omega_0^2 x+2f\dot y,\]
\[\ddot y=-\omega_0^2 y-2f\dot x,\]
\[\omega_0^2=g/L .\]

A positive eastward velocity thus produces a southward sideways acceleration at positive latitude. Checking this sign using an actual right-handed east–north–up triad prevents a correct rate from being reported in the wrong direction. The Coriolis force has zero dot product with the horizontal velocity. It can change the trace while doing no work, so constant mechanical energy is consistent with a slowly turning pattern.

How two nearby circular modes make a turning line

Put \(u=x+iy\). Multiplication by \(i\) turns a horizontal vector to the left through a right angle. The coupled real equations become

\[\ddot u+2if\dot u+\omega_0^2u=0.\]

Seek a rotating coordinate \(u=e^{-ift}v\). The two terms proportional to \(\dot v\) cancel and the equation for \(v\) is a harmonic oscillator with \(\nu^2=\omega_0^2+f^2\). This is an exact solution of the linear model with the stated effective local gravity. Terms of order \(\Omega^2\) omitted in constructing an ideal local apparatus would matter if one tried to measure the tiny quadratic correction to the swing period itself.

The release specifies both a position and a velocity in the room. For \(u(0)=A\) and \(\dot u(0)=0\) the initial conditions for \(v\) are \(A\) and \(ifA\). Hence

\[u(t)=A e^{-ift}\left[\cos(\nu t)+i\frac{f}{\nu}\sin(\nu t)\right].\]

In the second set of axes the point traces an ellipse with semiaxis ratio \(|f|/\nu\). Its long axis rotates in the floor frame at signed rate \(-f\). Omitting the small sine term gives an attractive sketch, but that approximation gives the bob the initial sideways velocity \(-ifA\). The experiment genuinely released from rest discriminates between these initial conditions even though the ellipse width is too small for many classroom measurements.

Equivalently expand the sine and cosine into circular exponentials. The two counter-rotating circular motions have positive angular-speed magnitudes \(\nu-f\) and \(\nu+f\). Their splitting is \(2f\), whereas the line azimuth drifts at one half of that splitting with the sign set by the floor axes. A frequency spectrum with inadequate resolution may see a single broad swing peak. The orientation of many short traces measures the beat without requiring a direct observation of two separate spectral peaks.

A numerical prediction for a camera in the hall

Take \(L=10.0\) m, \(g=9.81\) m/s² and \(\lambda=48^\circ\). Earth turns through \(2\pi\) radians in a sidereal day, approximately 86,164 s. This gives \(f=5.42\times 10^{-5}\) rad/s. The swing period is 6.34 s; the clockwise azimuth change is about \(11.18^\circ\) in one hour. A measurement three hours after release should therefore rotate the trace through \(33.5^\circ\). At a 15 cm release amplitude the off-axis semiaxis predicted in rotating swing axes is only about eight micrometres. A visually broad ellipse has another cause worth investigating.

There are two meanings of a return. A directed arrow attached to the long positive axis turns through \(360^\circ\) in about 32.2 hours here. A painted line on the floor has no arrow. Its geometrical orientation repeats after \(180^\circ\), or around 16.1 hours. Neither interval promises that the bob will occupy exactly its starting position with exactly its original velocity then. That stronger recurrence would require the fast and slow phases to line up simultaneously.

Extract azimuth from positions with no marked turning point

A practical measurement records pairs of east and north coordinates at evenly spaced times. Subtract the local mean to remove the camera origin and form the two-by-two covariance matrix of positions in a time window. For a fixed straight swing along a unit vector \(\mathbf e\), the position is \(A\mathbf e\cos\nu t\). Averaging over complete cycles gives \(A^2\mathbf e\mathbf e^\mathsf T/2\); its larger-eigenvalue eigenvector therefore estimates the swing direction. The smaller eigenvalue measures transverse spread, which can include noise, optical distortion or a real elliptical orbit. Fitting a slope \(y/x\) would instead be singular just when the line passes the north axis.

The principal direction is defined modulo \(\pi\). A robust way to unwrap it is to double its angle, unwrap the result as a usual full-circle angle, and then divide by two. Choose a window containing many fast cycles but little slow rotation. If consecutive windows are separated by a day, the smaller principal-angle change can have the wrong sign after unwrapping. Predict the largest plausible rotation before choosing a sampling interval. A useful comparison is to fit the same drift with several window lengths and check whether the result stays within the uncertainty due to noisy coordinates.

A suspension with unequal horizontal stiffnesses produces different natural frequencies even in a laboratory with \(f=0\). Release away from its principal axes and the two linear oscillations accumulate relative phase, turning an initially straight trace into an appreciably open ellipse. At the north pole an ideal device has maximal Coriolis rotation; at the equator the linear vertical-component model has none. A nonzero equatorial drift is thus a reason to inspect the suspension, ellipticity, air currents or camera geometry. Rotating the release direction relative to the support is a further discrimination because a true latitude dependence does not select a preferred horizontal spring direction.

Tutorial on phase, latitude and identifiable quantities

A camera sees the line turn clockwise by 42.0 degrees in four solar hours. Estimate the latitude. How does a two-degree uncertainty in the measured angle propagate?

Work through the solution

The rate is 10.5 degrees per hour, while Earth's sidereal angular speed is 15.041 degrees per solar hour. The sine of latitude is their ratio, approximately 0.698, giving about 44.3 degrees north. A two-degree angle uncertainty means 0.5 degree per hour in the rate. Dividing by the equatorial rate and by the cosine of 44.3 degrees gives about 0.046 rad, or 2.7 degrees, of local latitude uncertainty. The same inference becomes ill-conditioned close to a pole.

Double the thread length but keep the release angle small. Which observable changes by a factor of the square root of two and which one stays fixed?

Work through the solution

The fast period grows approximately as the square root of length, so a 10 m bob's 6.34 s becomes about 8.97 s at 20 m. The slow angular rate still equals minus the local vertical component of Earth's rotation. At fixed linear amplitude the fractional transverse width grows because the fast angular frequency drops. The dependence of the width does not imply that the slow rotation accelerates.

A 120 s camera window is used at 48 degrees north. Estimate its number of fast cycles and drift within the window. Would a 3 h window make a better single direction estimate?

Work through the solution

At 10 m there are about 18.9 cycles in 120 s, and the swing plane turns only 0.37 degree. Endpoint weighting or an integer number of cycles can improve the covariance estimate. Over three hours the true azimuth changes about 33.5 degrees. Averaging all of those samples gives an orientation intermediate between the endpoints and appreciable transverse variance even for a perfect apparatus. Several shorter windows resolve the secular rotation.
