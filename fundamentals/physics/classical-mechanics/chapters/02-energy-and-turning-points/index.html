From first principles

Obtain the energy curve from the measured force

Consider a point mass constrained to a fixed one-dimensional track with no dissipation. Measure the signed force \(F(x)\) along the track and suppose it depends only on position. Newton's second law supplies the time evolution. Multiplying by displacement converts it into a statement that can be integrated with respect to position:

\[m\ddot x\,dx=m\dot x\,d\dot x=d(\tfrac12m\dot x^2)=F(x)\,dx .\]

Define potential energy relative to an arbitrary reference position \(x_r\) by integrating the negative force. The arbitrary additive constant cannot affect any measured speed:

\[U(x)-U(x_r)=-\int_{x_r}^x F(s)\,ds,\]
\[\tfrac12m\dot x^2+U(x)=E .\]

Thus accessible positions satisfy \(U(x)\le E\) and a position reached with zero speed has \(U(x)=E\). Whether it is an ordinary turning point or an equilibrium also depends on the slope. Near an equilibrium \(x_*\) the linearized force is \(-U''(x_*)(x-x_*)\), giving

\[\delta\ddot x+\frac{U''(x_*)}{m}\delta x=0 .\]

Positive curvature creates a restoring force and local oscillations; a vanishing slope alone does not establish stability.

Derivation and applications

Why does a trajectory linger near the top of a barrier?

A turning point where the slope of the potential is finite is reached in finite time. A smooth barrier summit presents a different case. Near a nondegenerate maximum at \(x=0\) write \(U(x)=U_b-\kappa x^2/2\), with curvature \(\kappa>0\). Take a particle of mass \(m\) crossing from \(-L\) to \(+L\) at energy \(E=U_b+\epsilon\). Use a short interval on which the quadratic fit is accurate, and neglect friction. For \(\epsilon>0\) the positive-velocity branch of energy conservation gives

\[\dot x=\sqrt{(2\epsilon+\kappa x^2)/m},\]
\[t=\sqrt{m}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\epsilon+\kappa x^2}} .\]

The scale \(x_0=\sqrt{2\epsilon/\kappa}\) separates a slow central region from faster motion on the sides. To integrate across both without approximating the centre, set \(x=x_0\sinh u\). The factor \(\cosh u\) from \(dx\) cancels the one from the square root:

\[t=2\sqrt{m/\kappa}\int_0^{\operatorname{arsinh}(L/x_0)}du=2\sqrt{m/\kappa}\operatorname{arsinh}(L/x_0).\]

For an energy excess much smaller than \(\kappa L^2/2\), use \(\operatorname{arsinh}y=\ln(y+\sqrt{1+y^2})\). This gives the near-threshold dependence

\[t\simeq \sqrt{m/\kappa}\ln\!\left(\frac{2\kappa L^2}{\epsilon}\right).\]

Higher-order curvature corrections away from the summit can change a finite offset. The logarithmic growth comes from the increasingly slow passage next to the stationary point. At exactly the barrier energy a trajectory arriving from the left approaches the summit asymptotically.

Worked example

Time a crossing on a calibrated track

Let \(m=0.200\) kg, \(\kappa=5.00\) N/m, \(L=0.0400\) m and \(\epsilon=0.100\) mJ. The central speed is

\[\sqrt{2\epsilon/m}=0.0316\,\mathrm{m/s}.\]

The length \(x_0=6.32\) mm gives \(L/x_0=6.32\), so the exact transit time is \(0.400\,\operatorname{arsinh}(6.32)=1.02\) s. Dividing the entire distance by the central speed would give \(2.53\) s, since it treats the slowest point as representative of the entire track.

Second application

Infer a curvature from progressively slower crossings

On another track reducing a sufficiently small energy excess by a factor of four adds \(0.416\) s to the transit time. Subtracting two logarithms removes the unknown finite offset. Thus

\[\sqrt{m/\kappa}=0.416/\ln4=0.300\,\mathrm{s}.\]

For a \(0.180\) kg cart the inferred curvature is \(2.00\) N/m. As a check, another fourfold reduction should add about the same time, even though its absolute energy change is smaller.

Problem sheet 4 · Reconstruct an orbit from its clock

Read the model setup and preparatory problems

Replace time with position

A small cart travels through a shaped track or along a nonlinear spring. Its acceleration changes continuously with position, yet one can often predict where it will reverse direction before solving for its position as a function of time. Consider a single coordinate \(x\) in an inertial frame, a constant mass and a force that depends on position alone. Then \(F=m\ddot x\) times \(\dot x\) gives

\[F(x)\dot x=d(m\dot x^2/2)/dt.\]

Define the potential difference by \(U(x)-U(x_0)=-\int_{x_0}^xF(s)\,ds\). It follows that \(E=m\dot x^2/2+U(x)\) is constant.

The zero of \(U\) is conventional. Turning points and speed depend on differences, so adding the same constant to \(E\) and \(U\) has no observable consequence. Allowed positions satisfy

\[U(x)\le E.\]

Where the inequality is strict, two velocities have the same energy, one in each direction. Specifying a position and energy therefore leaves a choice of initial direction.

Examine a pair of wells

Take an illustrative bistable spring with potential \(U(x)=A(x^2-b^2)^2\), where \(A>0\) has units J/m⁴ and \(b\) is a length. Differentiation gives

\[F=-4Ax(x^2-b^2).\]

The equilibrium positions are \(0\) and \(\pm b\). At either outer equilibrium the curvature \(U''=4A(3x^2-b^2)\) is \(8Ab^2>0\). At the origin the curvature is negative. A small displacement from the central equilibrium grows because the force points away from it.

An energy below \(Ab^4\) confines motion to one well, assuming a start there. At higher energy the cart can cross the centre. At exactly the barrier energy, a cart approaching the top from the side slows asymptotically in this frictionless model. Near the top \(E-U(x)\) is proportional to \(x^2\), so the time integral \(\int dx/\sqrt{2(E-U)/m}\) diverges logarithmically as \(x\) approaches zero. A test that checks only \(U=E\) misses this distinction between an ordinary reversal and an unstable equilibrium.

Extract the local frequency

Write \(x=b+\xi\) and expand the potential about the right-hand minimum. Its constant and linear contributions vanish, leaving \(U\simeq (8Ab^2)\xi^2/2\). The equation of motion is harmonic to this order, with \(\omega=\sqrt{8Ab^2/m}\). This curvature estimate uses small displacements compared with \(b\). For excursions sampling a significant fraction of the well, the oscillation need not be sinusoidal and its period can differ substantially.

At an arbitrary energy with adjacent turning points \(x_-,x_+\), a full oscillation takes twice the outward transit time. Thus \(T=2\int_{x_-}^{x_+}dx/\sqrt{2(E-U(x))/m}\). For an ordinary turning point with nonzero slope of \(U\), the denominator vanishes as the square root of distance and the integral remains finite. This expression supplies a direct route from a measured force curve to the period without measuring acceleration.

Interpret a threshold experiment

Let \(m=0.200\) kg, \(A=4000\) J/m⁴ and \(b=0.0500\) m. The barrier height is 0.0250 J and the effective spring stiffness at either minimum is 80.0 N/m. A weak disturbance oscillates at 20.0 rad/s, with period 0.314 s. From the minimum an initial speed of 0.400 m/s supplies 0.0160 J, below the crossing threshold. Solving \(x^2=b^2\pm\sqrt{E/A}\) gives right-well turning points 0.0224 and 0.0671 m. The displacement is asymmetric about the minimum even though the local harmonic approximation is symmetric.

A launch exactly at \(\sqrt{2(0.0250)/0.200}=0.500\) m/s is marginal. Crossing the barrier in finite time requires greater speed in this idealisation. In an experiment, dissipation raises the energy the cart needs at launch. Measuring the speed at several known positions tests conservation along the path and helps distinguish a wrong potential shape from an actual energy loss.

Time measurements give a second access to the shape of the potential. Between ordinary turning points the speed is \(\sqrt{2[E-U(x)]/m}\). The half-period is therefore the integral of its reciprocal over the allowed interval. The integrand diverges at a simple turning point, but only as the inverse square root of distance, giving a finite travel time. At an energy that just approaches a smooth unstable maximum, the divergence becomes logarithmic. Extremely slow passage near the barrier can easily be mistaken for a stopped cart.

Design a track whose period is independent of amplitude

The local curvature of a smooth potential fixes a small-oscillation period. An extended track can also be shaped to give exactly harmonic motion over a finite range. Use signed arc length \(s\) from the lowest point as coordinate. On a frictionless fixed track kinetic energy is \(m\dot s^2/2\). In a uniform gravitational field the only configuration-dependent energy is \(mgy(s)\). To obtain \(\ddot s=-\omega^2s\), require

\[g\,dy/ds=\omega^2s.\]

Integration sets the necessary height profile \(y(s)=\omega^2s^2/(2g)\), with height zero at the bottom.

A cycloidal arc realizes this condition. With scale \(a\) take \(x=a(\phi+\sin\phi)\) and \(y=a(1-\cos\phi)\) for \(-\pi<\phi<\pi\). Differentiate both coordinates. The speed along the parametric curve is

\[ds/d\phi=a\sqrt{(1+\cos\phi)^2+\sin^2\phi}=2a\cos(\phi/2).\]

Integrating from the bottom gives \(s=4a\sin(\phi/2)\), retaining the sign of \(\phi\). Substituting in the height expression eliminates the parameter:

\[y=\frac{s^2}{8a},\]
\[\ddot s+\frac{g}{4a}s=0.\]

The coordinate that executes sinusoidal motion is the path length. Its horizontal projection generally does something different because the track slope changes. A bead released at rest reaches the bottom after one quarter of the period for every nonzero admissible release amplitude. The cancellation between a longer path and a higher typical speed is encoded in the exactly quadratic gravitational potential in \(s\).

Time two different releases

Set \(a=0.250\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s². The angular frequency is \(\sqrt{9.81/(4\times0.250)}=3.13\) rad/s and the predicted time to the bottom is

\[\pi/(2\omega)=0.502\,\mathrm{s}.\]

Release a bead first from \(s=0.400\) m and then from \(s=0.800\) m. Their initial heights are respectively \(0.0800\) m and \(0.320\) m. At the bottom their speeds are \(\omega |s_0|=1.25\) and \(2.51\) m/s. Doubling arc length quadruples starting potential energy, providing just the required increase in speed to preserve arrival time.

A timing gate near the bottom and an electronic release can measure both quantities independently. A rolling ball has additional rotational inertia and a centre following a curve offset from the track. Treating it as the sliding point mass can bias the comparison even if rolling dissipation is small. Near the bottom the cycloid agrees with a circular arc of its local curvature. Farther away the circular arc develops anharmonic corrections, so comparing equal-time releases at several amplitudes discriminates between two tracks that look almost identical near their lowest point.

There is a useful inverse interpretation of a release series. Suppose the measured bottom speed doubles on doubling signed release distance, but the measured time to the lowest point changes. Correct speed scaling tests only the endpoint height relation. Equal travel times require the integrated speed to have the required dependence throughout the intervening path, and a contact force doing work can spoil that comparison. Measuring several intermediate heights as a function of arc length checks the quadratic law directly. Taking horizontal distance as the release coordinate introduces a purely geometric nonlinearity even for a perfectly machined cycloidal track.

Locate the turning points of a bound orbit
