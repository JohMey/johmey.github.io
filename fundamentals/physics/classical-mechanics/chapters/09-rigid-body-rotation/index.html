From first principles

Build the inertia tensor from the momenta of particles in a rigid body

Choose the centre of mass as origin and consider a rigid body of fixed mass distribution. Its instantaneous internal velocity field is \(\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\). Starting from angular momentum as the moment of linear momentum, integrate over small masses. The vector triple-product identity separates the part parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\) from a direction-dependent subtraction:

\[\mathbf L=\int\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dm=\int[r^2\boldsymbol\omega-(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)\mathbf r]\,dm .\]

Reading off coefficients of the components of \(\boldsymbol\omega\) defines the symmetric inertia tensor. It is diagonal only in appropriately chosen principal axes:

\[L_i=\sum_j I_{ij}\omega_j,\]
\[I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_i r_j)\,dm .\]

Torque gives the laboratory time derivative of angular momentum. In a body-fixed principal basis the tensor components are constant but the basis rotates. Applying the rotating-vector derivative yields, for the first component,

\[\tau_1=I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3 .\]

Cyclic permutation gives the others. Even zero torque can change the body-frame components of angular velocity. Alignment of angular velocity and angular momentum holds about a principal direction, or about every direction for spherical inertia.

Derivation and applications

Find a pivot that gives a specified physical-pendulum period

An extended body hanging from a pivot rarely oscillates like a point at its centre of mass. Take a rigid body of mass \(M\) with centre of mass a distance \(d\) below a horizontal, frictionless axis. Let \(I_C\) be its moment of inertia about the parallel axis through the centre of mass. Measure angle \(\theta\) from vertically downward. For each mass element, decompose its perpendicular displacement from the pivot into the centre-of-mass offset and a displacement relative to the centre. The cross term integrates to zero by definition of the centre:

\[I_O=\int r_\perp^2\,dm=I_C+Md^2 .\]

Gravity is equivalent to a force \(Mg\) at the centre, so its torque depends only on \(d\). Angular momentum about the fixed pivot is \(I_O\dot\theta\) along the rotation axis. The angular equation, followed by its small-angle limit, is

\[(I_C+Md^2)\ddot\theta=-Mgd\sin\theta,\]
\[\omega^2=\frac{Mgd}{I_C+Md^2}.\]

Define the radius of gyration \(k_C=\sqrt{I_C/M}\). A simple pendulum with the same period has effective length \(\ell_{\rm eff}=g/\omega^2\). Unlike the actual distance \(d\), this length includes the cost of rotating the mass distribution about its centre:

\[T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{\rm eff}}g},\]
\[\ell_{\rm eff}=d+\frac{k_C^2}{d}.\]

Moving the pivot toward the centre eventually increases the period because the torque vanishes faster than the inertia. Differentiating the effective length gives \(d\ell_{\rm eff}/dd=1-k_C^2/d^2\), so the shortest period occurs at \(d=k_C\) if the apparatus admits that pivot.

Worked example

Compare two pivot positions on a uniform bar

A thin \(0.900\) m uniform bar oscillates in a vertical plane. Integration of \(x^2\,dm\) along its length gives \(k_C^2=L^2/12=0.0675\) m². Pivoting at the end gives \(d=0.450\) m and \(\ell_{\rm eff}=0.600\) m, hence \(T=1.55\) s for \(g=9.81\) m/s². Pivoting \(0.150\) m from the centre also gives \(0.150+0.0675/0.150=0.600\) m. The two periods agree even though the gravitational torque coefficients differ by a factor of three.

Second application

Infer an unknown inertia from equal periods

Two distinct pivot distances \(d_1=0.0800\) m and \(d_2=0.180\) m give the same measured period for a \(0.500\) kg body. Equating the effective lengths and canceling \(d_1-d_2\) yields \(k_C^2=d_1d_2=0.0144\) m². Thus \(I_C=0.00720\) kg m², and the common effective length is \(d_1+d_2=0.260\) m. A predicted period of \(1.02\) s checks the inference independently of the inertia measurement.

Distinguish loss of orientation from loss of angular momentum

Release a body spinning almost about its intermediate principal axis, with moments \(0.030,\ 0.040,\ 0.050\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Describe rotation around a moving centre

Take a rigid body with a freely moving centre of mass and no applied torque about that centre. Remove the uniform translation and label material positions \(\mathbf r\) relative to the centre. Each material point has velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\). Integrating \(\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dm\) gives \(\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega\), where \(I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_i r_j)dm\). The inertia tensor is symmetric, so a set of orthogonal body axes diagonalises it.

In its principal axes the rotational kinetic energy is \(\sum_i I_i\omega_i^2/2\). This expression follows by integrating the kinetic energy of individual mass elements. A body containing mass far from an axis usually has large moment of inertia about that axis. Unless the components seeing different moments vanish, angular momentum and angular velocity point in different directions. A body can therefore rotate freely even when the angular velocity changes direction relative to painted marks on its surface.

Apply the moving-frame derivative

The laboratory angular momentum is constant in the absence of torque. Its components in the body basis satisfy \((d\mathbf L/dt)_{\rm body}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L=0\). For axial symmetry put \(I_1=I_2=I_\perp\) and call \(I_3\) the moment about the symmetry axis. Then \(\dot\omega_3=0\), \(\dot\omega_1=-(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_2\omega_3/I_\perp\) and \(\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_1\omega_3/I_\perp\). These last two equations describe uniform rotation of the pair of transverse components in body coordinates.

The signed angular rate of that rotation is \(\Omega_b=(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_3/I_\perp\). A body-mounted sensor sees its transverse spin vector execute a circle, even though a laboratory observer sees a constant angular-momentum vector. For a spherical inertia tensor the transverse components also become constant. The possible motion then simplifies because \(\mathbf L\) is parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\) for any spin direction.

Predict what two observers see

The symmetry-axis unit vector \(\mathbf e_3\) has laboratory derivative \(\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_3\). Express the angular velocity as \(\mathbf L/I_\perp\) plus a term parallel to \(\mathbf e_3\), which drops out of the cross product. The result is \(\dot{\mathbf e}_3=(\mathbf L/I_\perp)\times\mathbf e_3\). Thus in the laboratory the symmetry axis sweeps around fixed \(\mathbf L\) at rate \(L/I_\perp\) and constant tilt. This rate differs from the spin-vector rotation observed in body coordinates because the two measurements track different vectors relative to different frames.

Let \(I_\perp=0.020\) kg·m², \(I_3=0.030\) kg·m², \(\omega_3=40\) rad/s and initial transverse angular speed 6.0 rad/s. The body-frame rate is 20 rad/s. The magnitude of momentum is \(\sqrt{(0.020\times6)^2+(0.030\times40)^2}=1.206\) kg·m²/s. Laboratory precession is about 60.3 rad/s. The tilt obeys \(\tan\theta=0.120/1.20=0.10\), giving 5.71°. Two cameras recording these distinct angular rates need not disagree on the mechanics.

For three distinct moments the two transverse equations no longer form this simple uniform circle. The stability of individual principal-axis spins then becomes an additional question. Recording both energy and angular momentum helps discriminate conservative tumbling from external bearing drag.

One can also infer a moment of inertia without opening the rotor. Measure angular momentum by applying a calibrated torque for a known duration and integrating the torque impulse. During free motion, track the body axis with an external camera and find its precession frequency about the fixed angular momentum. For the symmetric rotor their ratio estimates the transverse moment \(I_1\). A second measurement of the axial component of angular momentum and spin gives \(I_3\).

Balance torque and angular momentum in a heavy top

Support a symmetric top at one fixed point on its symmetry axis, a distance \(\ell\) below its centre of mass when upright. Unlike free rotation about the centre, gravity now exerts a torque about the pivot. Use principal moments about the pivot, \(I_1=I_2\) and \(I_3\), and let \(\theta\) be the tilt from upward vertical. In steady precession \(\theta\) is constant, the azimuth increases at rate \(\Omega\), and the body’s total angular-velocity component along its symmetry axis is \(\omega_3=\dot\psi+\Omega\cos\theta\).

The rotational kinetic energy in Euler angles is \(I_1(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)/2+I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2/2\). Insert \(U=mg\ell\cos\theta\) into the \(\theta\) equation, differentiating before imposing constant tilt. For \(\sin\theta\ne0\) this yields

\[I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+mg\ell=0.\]

The slower root for rapid spin tends to \(mg\ell/(I_3\omega_3)\). This is the rate required for gravity to turn the nearly axial angular momentum. The full quadratic distinguishes that rate from a second, fast precession and gives the spin threshold for a tilted steady solution when \(\cos\theta>0\). Initial conditions outside such a solution produce a varying tilt, called nutation.

For \(m=0.200\) kg, \(\ell=0.0450\) m, \(I_1=0.00180\) kg m², \(I_3=0.000800\) kg m², \(\omega_3=200\) rad/s and \(\theta=30^\circ\), the slow-root approximation is \(0.0883/0.160=0.552\) rad/s. Substituting the quadratic coefficients gives \(0.555\) rad/s, a precession period of about 11.3 s. The other root is about 102 rad/s. A camera measuring only rotation of a mark painted on the body cannot generally separate axial spin, precession and nutation without also tracking the axis.

Stability of rotation about a principal axis

A symmetric rotor can precess steadily. A body with three different principal moments has a striking additional possibility. Suppose \(I_1<I_2<I_3\) and no torque acts about the centre of mass. In body coordinates Euler’s equations can be written \(I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3\), with cyclic permutations for the other components. A pure rotation about any one principal axis is an exact solution. Stability asks what happens to a small transverse disturbance of that solution, a question about neighbouring trajectories rather than the exact rotation itself.

Consider nominal rotation about axis 2 at rate \(\Omega\). Write \(\omega_1=u\) and \(\omega_3=w\), both initially small. The change of \(\omega_2\) is a product of two small quantities and can be discarded to first order. The first and third equations give \(\dot u=-(I_3-I_2)\Omega w/I_1\) and \(\dot w=-(I_2-I_1)\Omega u/I_3\). Differentiating the first and substituting the second produces

\[\ddot u=\Omega^2\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}\,u .\]

A positive coefficient gives hyperbolic solutions with an exponentially growing component for a generic disturbance. Repeating the calculation about the smallest or the largest moment reverses the sign of the product, giving bounded oscillatory disturbances. Energy and angular momentum remain conserved throughout. A nearby angular-velocity direction can change substantially while moving along the intersection of the two invariant surfaces.

Estimate the onset of a visible tumble

Take moments \(I_1=0.020\), \(I_2=0.030\) and \(I_3=0.040\) kg m² and a nominal intermediate-axis rotation \(\Omega=20.0\) rad/s. The small-perturbation growth rate is \(20.0\sqrt{0.010\times0.010/(0.020\times0.040)}=7.07\) s⁻¹. Prepare an initial transverse component of \(0.020\) rad/s in the growing eigenmode. Reaching \(0.200\) rad/s then takes \(\ln(10)/7.07=0.326\) s, still within the small-angle range. A perturbation prepared with zero initial derivative instead contains both growing and decaying contributions and follows a hyperbolic cosine initially.

These estimates predict the early departure, not the duration of a full flip. When transverse components approach the nominal spin, the nonlinear terms that preserved the dominant spin to first order can no longer be dropped. As two adjacent moments approach equality the growth rate tends to zero, recovering the absence of this isolated intermediate-axis instability in a symmetric rotor. A motion-capture experiment that records all three body components can test the exponential interval and independently check whether external torques or aerodynamic drag have changed the conserved angular momentum.

There is also a camera-independent prediction for the orientation of the early disturbance in body coordinates. A growing solution has \(\dot u=\lambda u\). From the first linearized equation this requires \(w/u=-I_1\lambda/[(I_3-I_2)\Omega]\). In the numerical example that ratio is \(-0.707\): the small components point in opposite signed directions. A disturbance with the reverse sign projects partly onto the decaying solution. The eventual growth rate can be the same while the visibility of the first few cycles differs greatly. Recording both components avoids assigning a disagreement in initial conditions to the underlying instability rate.
