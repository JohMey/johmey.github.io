From first principles

Turn an incident annulus into a measured angular distribution

Assume a uniform incident number flux \(\mathcal F\) on one central scatterer. The trajectories vary only by azimuth and by their initial perpendicular offset \(b\). A ring of incoming impact parameters between \(b\) and \(b+db\) has area \(2\pi b\,db\). Thus the number of events entering that interval per time is

\[d\dot N=\mathcal F\,2\pi b\,|db| .\]

For an axisymmetric interaction the corresponding polar exit angles \(\theta\) to \(\theta+d\theta\) fill an angular band of solid angle \(d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,|d\theta|\). Equate the rate from the entrance to the rate reported by a detector normalized to flux and solid angle:

\[\mathcal F\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}2\pi\sin\theta\,|d\theta|=\mathcal F\,2\pi b\,|db|,\]
\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|.\]

This is a change of variables based on flux conservation, not a force law. The actual interaction supplies \(b(\theta)\). If multiple impact parameters map into one angular band, add their positive contributions. A finite detector averages this distribution over its acceptance, which matters near a sharp angular feature.

Background · Differential cross-section
\[d\sigma/d\Omega=(b/\sin\theta)|db/d\theta|\]

For an axially symmetric single-valued scattering branch this converts incoming area to outgoing solid angle. Sum contributions if more than one impact parameter gives the same angle.

Derivation and applications

Which scattering cross section measures a loss of directed momentum?

A detector counting encounters adds one event in every accepted direction. A force measurement weights the same encounters according to how much directed momentum each removes. Consider a dilute parallel stream of identical particles of speed \(v\) and mass \(m\) scattered elastically by isolated, stationary, much heavier centres. The incoming direction defines \(z\). The outgoing speed is unchanged, and assume axial symmetry so that transverse recoils cancel in the ensemble. Let \(\mathcal F\) be the incident number per area per time.

The outgoing momentum projection is \(mv\cos\theta\) for deflection angle \(\theta\). Multiply the lost projection by the differential encounter rate in the detector’s solid angle:

\[\Delta p_z=mv(1-\cos\theta),\]
\[d\dot N=\mathcal F\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega .\]

Summing over all azimuths, with \(d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\), defines the momentum-transfer cross section \(\sigma_{\rm tr}\) and the longitudinal force on one centre:

\[F_z=\mathcal F\,mv\,\sigma_{\rm tr},\]
\[\sigma_{\rm tr}=\int(1-\cos\theta)\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega .\]

For a smooth monotone deflection function \(\theta(b)\), the annular area of incident trajectories is \(2\pi b\,db\). Changing variables back to impact parameter \(b\) shows exactly which trajectories contribute:

\[\sigma_{\rm tr}=2\pi\int b\,[1-\cos\theta(b)]\,db.\]

Use the sum of all annuli if the same angle is reached from several impact parameters. Forward scattering transfers almost no longitudinal momentum. Exact backscattering transfers twice the incident longitudinal momentum, even though it still counts as a single encounter.

Worked example

Compute the momentum flux for elastic spheres

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

For a hard sphere of effective contact radius \(R\), specular reflection gives \(\cos\theta=2(b/R)^2-1\) for \(0\le b\le R\). The integral is

\[4\pi\int_0^R[b-b^3/R^2]db=\pi R^2.\]

For \(R=1.00\) mm, \(\mathcal F=2.00\times10^7\) m⁻²s⁻¹, \(m=1.00\times10^{-9}\) kg and \(v=2.00\) m/s, the encounter rate is \(62.8\) s⁻¹ and the mean force is \(1.26\times10^{-7}\) N.

Second application

Compare two angular distributions with identical event counts

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

Suppose an angular histogram is fitted by \(d\sigma/d\Omega=C(1+\alpha\cos\theta)\), with \(|\alpha|\le1\). Its integrated count is \(4\pi C\) for every \(\alpha\). Averaging the momentum factor uses \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\) on a sphere, giving \(\sigma_{\rm tr}=4\pi C(1-\alpha/3)\). For \(\alpha=0.900\), the same count produces only \(0.700\) of the force for an isotropic distribution. An encounter count alone cannot determine a transport coefficient.

What does a detector with equal angular bins count?

A stream of dilute neutral beads meets a much heavier fixed hard sphere with effective contact radius \(R\). For specular elastic reflection the impact parameter and polar deflection angle satisfy

\[b=R\cos(\theta/2).\]

The incoming annulus has area \(2\pi b\,|db|\), while an outgoing ring subtends \(2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\). Dividing gives the constant differential cross-section \(R^2/4\) per steradian. A detector with equal polar-angle bins sees a changing count because those bins have unequal solid angle.

Specify an ensemble of trajectories

A beam of dilute particles approaches a stationary scattering centre. Particles enter parallel to one another with the same speed, but at different perpendicular offsets from the centre. This offset \(b\), measured on the incoming asymptotic straight line, is the impact parameter. For a central potential each trajectory remains in one plane; axial symmetry makes its polar deflection angle depend on \(b\) rather than on the azimuth of the initial offset. Neglect collisions between incoming particles and assume a sufficiently heavy centre that recoil can be ignored.

The cross-section has dimensions of area. It quantifies how a known incident flux becomes counts in a detector. If an incoming beam supplies \(j\) particles per unit area per second, an annulus of impact parameters from \(b\) to \(b+db\) contributes \(j\,2\pi b\,db\) particles per second to the corresponding outgoing angles. A large response can arise either from a large interval of impact parameters or from trajectories compressed into a narrow angular interval.

Derive the transformation to detector angles

For a single monotonic deflection branch, a ring of outgoing directions between \(\theta\) and \(\theta+d\theta\) subtends \(d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\). Equating the incoming count and the outgoing count yields

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|.\]

The absolute value matters because stronger deflections often correspond to smaller impact parameters. If several distinct impact parameters produce the same angle, their contributions must be added. Multiplying by a detector’s solid angle is justified when its angular aperture is small enough that the differential cross-section is nearly constant across it.

Test the construction on a hard sphere

Take an impenetrable smooth sphere of radius \(R\) that reflects incident point particles elastically. At a surface hit the normal component of velocity reverses while the tangential component stays fixed. A central hit returns straight back and a grazing hit barely deflects. The geometry of the contact normal gives

\[b=R\cos(\theta/2).\]

Differentiation and \(\sin\theta=2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)\) give

\[d\sigma/d\Omega=R^2/4.\]

Integrating over all \(4\pi\) steradians recovers \(\pi R^2\), the area presented to the incoming parallel beam.

Although the angular density is isotropic in this classical example, the trajectories are deterministic. Isotropy results from the area-to-angle map. A detector covering a fixed span of polar angle but all azimuths receives different counts near the pole and near the equator because its solid angle changes.

Calculate detector counts

Let \(R=2.0\) mm, \(j=1.0\times10^9\) m⁻²s⁻¹ and a small detector subtend 0.020 sr in a clear outgoing direction. The differential cross-section is \(1.0\times10^{-6}\) m²/sr. The expected rate is \(j(d\sigma/d\Omega)\Delta\Omega=20\) s⁻¹. Integrating over angles beyond 90° gives half the total count, or \(j\pi R^2/2=6.28\times10^3\) s⁻¹. Only incoming offsets below \(R/\sqrt2\) enter this backward hemisphere.

Soft interactions can produce very different distributions. In particular, a force reaching far outside any material body can scatter particles arriving at offsets larger than the body radius. The hard-sphere area no longer supplies a universal total cross-section. Detector acceptance and the minimum observable angle then become part of the measurement definition.

Attraction enlarges the area that meets a body

An astronomical body can collect small incoming particles from a larger area than its apparent disk. Consider a stationary spherical body of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\) with vacuum outside. Small particles arrive from far away with common speed \(v_\infty\) and an approximately uniform distribution across their beam. Assume negligible particle mass, no drag, no interactions among particles and an absorbing surface. Gravity changes which incident trajectories reach the surface while conserving each particle’s energy and angular momentum until its first contact.

At infinity the specific angular momentum is \(h=bv_\infty\) for impact parameter \(b\). The trajectory that just grazes the surface is tangent there, so \(h=Rv_p\), where \(v_p\) is its speed at closest approach. Energy conservation between infinity and the grazing point gives

\[v_p^2=v_\infty^2+2GM/R.\]

Eliminating \(v_p\) supplies the critical impact parameter and capture cross section

\[b_{\max}^2=R^2\left(1+\frac{v_{\rm esc}^2}{v_\infty^2}\right),\]
\[\sigma_{\rm hit}=\pi b_{\max}^2,\]
\[v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}.\]

The incident area element is measured in the undisturbed beam at infinity. Particles with \(b<R\) obviously hit; some initially farther from the centre do as well. Very fast particles hardly change direction before passing the body, giving the geometric area in that limit. Very slow incident particles experience strong focusing, and the idealized cross section grows as \(v_\infty^{-2}\). For extremely small speeds the assumed uniform, isolated far-field beam can cease to be physically meaningful because other gravitating masses perturb it.

Compare two incoming populations

A spherical minor body has radius \(R=100\) km and escape speed \(v_{\rm esc}=100\) m/s. Its implied gravitational parameter is \(GM=v_{\rm esc}^2R/2=5.00\times10^8\) m³/s². A population approaching at 50.0 m/s has \(b_{\max}=R\sqrt{1+4}=224\) km. The collection area is \(1.57\times10^{11}\) m², five times the projected solid disk. The speed at grazing contact is \(\sqrt{50^2+100^2}=112\) m/s, and the angular momenta on the two sides agree.

For a second population at 200 m/s the enhancement is only \(1.25\), giving \(3.93\times10^{10}\) m². At equal incident particle flux per square metre the slower population therefore supplies four times as many impacts. At equal far-field number density, however, flux itself is proportional to speed: multiplying each cross section by its own speed makes the two collection rates equal for these particular values. This distinction matters when comparing an instrument that measures incident counts per unit area with a model that reports only a space density.

This collection calculation can be recast as a minimum-angle criterion in a numerical trajectory experiment. Launch particles far upstream at specified offsets and find which trajectories reach the radius of the body before turning away. To estimate a cross section with equally weighted trajectories, sample radius with cumulative probability proportional to its square. Uniform sampling of the impact parameter instead overrepresents rays near the axis. If that sampling is used, weight each ray by the area of its annulus. Convergence of the measured grazing offset and convergence of the weighted hit fraction test distinct aspects of the calculation.
