Proper time, momentum and a moving clock

A pair of light pulses lets observers compare clocks without assuming a universal time. Build the invariant interval and use it to calculate transit times and the energy exchanged by moving material.

Identify what every inertial observer can agree on

Assign coordinates \(x,t\) to events using rulers and synchronized clocks in an inertial laboratory. A light ray moving along the positive or negative axis obeys \(x=\pm ct\). A linear transformation between two inertial laboratories must take each family of light rays into itself if both measure the same \(c\). Introduce \(u=ct-x\) and \(w=ct+x\), coordinates constant on the two families. A boost rescales them. Reciprocity between inertial frames and spatial reflection give opposite rescalings,

\[u'=e^\eta u,\ w'=e^{-\eta}w.\]

Here the additive parameter \(\eta\) is called rapidity.

\[c^2t'^2-x'^2=u'w'=uw=c^2t^2-x^2.\]

Adding and subtracting the transformed coordinates gives

\[ct'=ct\cosh\eta-x\sinh\eta,\]
\[x'=x\cosh\eta-ct\sinh\eta.\]

The moving origin obeys \(x'=0\), so

\[v/c=\tanh\eta,\]
\[\cosh\eta=\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}.\]

These statements concern pairs of events. An experiment involving a rod must separately specify which endpoint events are simultaneous in the observer's frame.

Follow one clock along its trajectory

Events on the same material clock have \(dx'=0\) instantaneously in its rest frame. The invariant interval then gives

\[d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-v(t)^2/c^2}.\]

Over a curved path integrate this local relation. Two synchronized clocks reunited at the same event can have accumulated different proper times because their intervening paths differ.

Consider an inertially moving clock passing two fixed laboratory gates 12 light-microseconds apart. With \(v=0.80c\) the lab crossing interval is 15 µs. The travelling clock accumulates \(15/1.6667=9.0\) µs. It measures the simultaneous distance between gates in its own frame as 7.2 light-microseconds. Dividing that distance by \(0.80c\) gives the same 9.0 µs. Combining a laboratory distance with a clock time from another frame would obscure this check.

From a clock to energy and momentum

Differentiate the spacetime position with respect to the invariant proper time. The resulting four-velocity transforms as a four-vector. For a particle of constant rest mass \(m\), multiply by that mass to obtain the four-momentum \(p^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)=m\gamma(c,\mathbf v)\). The invariant norm is obtained in the rest frame and gives

\[E^2-p^2c^2=m^2c^4,\]
\[K=E-mc^2 .\]

For low speed, expanding \(\gamma\) returns \(K=mv^2/2\) to lowest order. For light, the massless relation \(E=pc\) holds without defining a rest frame. These elementary cases are useful checks when adding the four-momenta of a system.

Imagine two identical material samples with equal and opposite speeds \(0.60c\) close into a sealed calorimeter at rest in their centre-of-momentum frame. Ignoring the original container mass and any energy escaping, the total momentum vanishes and the invariant mass of the combined contents is \(2\gamma m=2.50m\). The excess \(0.50mc^2\) over the original rest energies is internal energy in this frame. Energy conservation applies throughout the process.

Tutorial on identifying the frame

A laboratory separates two gates by 24 light-microseconds. A travelling clock measures 18 µs between crossings. Determine the speed.

Work through the solution

In units with distance in light-microseconds, the lab time is \(24/\beta\) µs. The interval gives \(18^2=(24/\beta)^2-24^2\), so

\[\beta=24/30=0.800.\]

The laboratory time is 30 µs. This inversion uses one distance and two events without assuming the clocks display the same elapsed time.

An emission and reception have laboratory separation \(6\) light-microseconds and time difference \(10\,\mu\mathrm s\). Could one inertial clock record both, and which speed would it need?

Work through the solution

The interval is timelike because \(10^2-6^2=64>0\). The unique inertial path between the two events has speed \(0.6c\). Its measured time is \(8\,\mu\mathrm s\), consistent with \(10/\gamma\) and \(\gamma=1.25\). A laboratory separation of 12 light-microseconds at the same time difference would be spacelike and could not be joined by such a clock.

In the laboratory a single light pulse has energy \(E\) and momentum \(E/c\). Compare its invariant mass with that of two pulses of energy \(E\) travelling in opposite directions together as a system.

Work through the solution

For one pulse \(E^2-p^2c^2=0\). For the pair the energies add to \(2E\) and the vector momenta cancel. The system therefore has invariant mass \(2E/c^2\). The invariant mass of a composite system depends on total four-momentum. Individual invariant masses alone do not specify it.
