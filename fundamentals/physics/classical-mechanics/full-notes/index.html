Chapter 1 · Momentum, impulse and collisions

Use the centre-of-mass frame to predict the outcome of a collision and account for the kinetic energy that disappears from the motion of the bodies.

From first principles

Construct the centre-of-mass and relative kinetic energies

Take two bodies of constant masses \(m_1,m_2\) moving along one axis. During their brief contact suppose the external impulse is negligible. The mechanical inputs are Newton's momentum balance and equal, opposite contact forces. Add the two balances, then divide the resulting conserved total momentum \(P\) by total mass \(M\):

\[\dot P=\dot p_1+\dot p_2=0,\]
\[V=P/M,\]
\[M=m_1+m_2 .\]

Let \(u=v_1-v_2\) be the signed relative speed. Solving this definition together with \(m_1v_1+m_2v_2=MV\) recovers each velocity. These expressions distinguish the common motion shared by both bodies from their approach:

\[v_1=V+(m_2/M)u,\]
\[v_2=V-(m_1/M)u.\]

Insert both in the kinetic-energy sum. The terms proportional to \(Vu\) cancel, while those in \(u^2\) combine into the reduced mass \(\mu=m_1m_2/M\):

\[K=\tfrac12m_1v_1^2+\tfrac12m_2v_2^2=\tfrac12MV^2+\tfrac12\mu u^2 .\]

No constitutive law for the contact has entered this identity. Since \(V\) is fixed, only the second energy term can change during an isolated collision. Restitution or another contact measurement is needed to predict the final relative motion.

Derivation and applications

Where does kinetic energy go during a finite contact?

A collision record often contains a force trace but no direct measurement of stored deformation energy. Consider two carts moving along the same straight track. Neglect the external impulse during contact, assume that the bodies do not rotate, and define the initial closing speed as \(u=v_1-v_2>0\). The common centre-of-mass speed is constant, but the relative speed changes continuously as the contact force grows and subsides.

Let \(J(t)\) be the positive impulse applied to cart 2 up to time \(t\). Cart 1 receives the opposite impulse. Subtract the resulting velocities and collect the two inverse masses into the reduced mass \(\mu\):

\[v_1(J)=v_1-J/m_1,\]
\[v_2(J)=v_2+J/m_2,\]
\[u(J)=u-J/\mu,\]
\[\mu^{-1}=m_1^{-1}+m_2^{-1}.\]

For each cart the instantaneous kinetic-energy change is velocity times momentum change. Consequently the sum of both changes measures the relative velocity during contact. Integrating over the measured impulse gives

\[dK=-u(J)\,dJ,\]
\[K(J)-K(0)=-uJ+\frac{J^2}{2\mu}.\]

Maximum compression occurs when the relative velocity first vanishes, at \(J_c=\mu u\). At separation let the measured opening speed be \(eu\), where \(e\) is the coefficient of restitution. Solving \(u(J_f)=-eu\) and inserting the resulting final impulse gives

\[J_f=(1+e)\mu u,\]
\[K(0)-K(J_f)=\tfrac12\mu u^2(1-e^2).\]

The two stages need not have the same force histories. The loss is fixed by endpoint data because total momentum was conserved. An elastic contact temporarily removes relative kinetic energy and returns it all. A sticking contact returns none.

Worked example

Reconstruct the two stages of a cart encounter

A \(0.600\) kg cart catches a \(0.400\) kg cart with \(u=1.50\) m/s. Their force sensor measures \(J_f=0.576\) N s. Here \(\mu=0.240\) kg, so \(J_c=0.360\) N s and \(e=0.576/0.360-1=0.600\). Compression removes \(0.270\) J of relative motion. Separation returns \(0.270(0.600)^2=0.0972\) J; the bodies leave contact with \(0.173\) J less kinetic energy.

Second application

Repeat in a moving laboratory

Suppose the initial cart velocities were \(2.00\) and \(0.500\) m/s. The impulses predict final velocities \(1.04\) and \(1.94\) m/s. An observer travelling at \(1.00\) m/s instead obtains \(0.040\) and \(0.940\) m/s after contact. In that frame the initial kinetic energy is \(0.350\) J and the final energy \(0.177\) J. Their difference again gives \(0.173\) J: changing the observer changes the centre-of-mass contribution by the same amount at both endpoints.

Select a system and a clock

Two gliders collide on a level air track. A force sensor can display a sharp pulse, while video resolves only the velocities before and after contact. Can the two instruments describe the same event? Begin with Newton’s law for a constant-mass particle. Integrating over the contact time gives \(\mathbf p_f-\mathbf p_i=\int\mathbf F\,dt\). The right-hand side is an impulse. It has units of N·s, equal to kg·m/s. A large force acting briefly can produce the same impulse as a smaller force acting for longer.

Draw a boundary around both gliders. The contact forces contribute equal and opposite impulses within that boundary. The change in their total momentum equals the external impulse. Gravity and the track reaction cancel vertically. Provided that horizontal friction supplies little impulse during contact, the horizontal momentum is conserved to the accuracy of that approximation. This reasoning also tells us which claim a measurement can test. Momentum conservation constrains the two final velocities together, but supplies only one scalar equation in a one-dimensional experiment.

Separate translation and approach

Write \(M=m_1+m_2\), \(V=(m_1v_1+m_2v_2)/M\), \(u=v_1-v_2\) and \(\mu=m_1m_2/M\). Solving for the laboratory velocities gives \(v_1=V+m_2u/M\) and \(v_2=V-m_1u/M\). Substitute both expressions in the kinetic energy. The terms linear in \(Vu\) cancel and the squared terms give

\[K=\tfrac12 MV^2+\tfrac12\mu u^2.\]

The first term translates the centre of mass. A collision with negligible external impulse leaves it fixed. The second term measures the energy available for deformation, heating and relative motion after impact. All observers moving uniformly along the track agree on the relative speed. They assign different values to the first term while agreeing on the kinetic energy lost during this isolated collision.

Supply a material observation

Suppose the gliders approach with \(u_i>0\) and separate afterwards. Define the restitution coefficient along the line of impact as \(e=-u_f/u_i\). Elastic rebound gives \(e=1\), and two bodies sticking together have \(e=0\). Substituting the definition into the energy decomposition gives \(K_i-K_f=\mu(1-e^2)u_i^2/2\). A smaller restitution coefficient reduces the energy available for separation. A spring mechanism initially storing energy can instead produce a relative speed larger than its approach speed.

Restitution is an empirical closure. Inferring it from a pair of outgoing velocities is an experimental result. In an oblique impact the normal relative velocity supplies the restitution condition. Tangential motion requires a separate physical assumption about friction and rotation. Applying the one-dimensional formula to the full speed would implicitly impose a tangential contact law as well.

Reconstruct an air-track event

A 0.60 kg glider moving right at 1.20 m/s strikes a 0.40 kg glider initially at rest. High-speed video gives \(e=0.50\). The centre of mass travels right at \(0.72\) m/s. The final relative velocity is \(-0.60\) m/s, hence the first glider travels at \(0.72+0.40(-0.60)=0.48\) m/s and the second at \(0.72-0.60(-0.60)=1.08\) m/s. Both move right, but the smaller glider pulls away.

The impulse on the smaller glider is \(0.40(1.08)=0.432\) N·s. A 12 ms contact would therefore give a mean force of 36 N. The kinetic energy lost is \(0.5(0.24)(1-0.25)(1.20)^2=0.130\) J. Momentum and energy provide independent checks on an instrument record. If both measured impulses agree while the kinetic energy falls by this amount, the missing mechanical energy can reside in the contact material and the sound it emitted.

A different choice of frame changes each cart’s measured kinetic energy but leaves the loss in a collision unchanged if total momentum is conserved. To verify this, subtract a constant velocity \(U\) from each velocity before forming the initial and final kinetic sums. In each sum the additional terms are \(-UP+MU^2/2\). The two corrections cancel in the energy difference. This is why a collision film taken by an observer riding beside the track can give the same inferred internal-energy gain.

Resolve an oblique impact along the contact normal

A single restitution coefficient describes the part of relative velocity along the line of impact. A two-dimensional collision needs a geometric measurement as well. Take two smooth disks gliding on a horizontal air table, with negligible external impulse during contact. At first touch draw a unit normal \(\mathbf n\) from the centre of disk 1 toward disk 2. Let \(\mathbf t\) be a perpendicular direction in the table. Assume the contact exerts no tangential impulse. The force then passes through the centres and leaves the two spins unchanged, even when the centres approach on intersecting paths.

Call the positive magnitude of contact impulse \(J\). Disk 1 receives \(-J\mathbf n\) and disk 2 receives \(+J\mathbf n\). Subtract the individual impulse–momentum equations; if \(\mathbf g=\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2\), the relative normal velocity after impact is \(g'_n=g_n-J(1/m_1+1/m_2)\). Approaching disks have \(g_n>0\) with this choice of normal. Applying \(g'_n=-e g_n\) gives

\[J=(1+e)\mu g_n,\qquad \mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.\]

Both tangential velocities remain individually unchanged. The energy lost is \(\mu(1-e^2)g_n^2/2\), even when tangential motion carries most of the initial relative kinetic energy. A grazing trajectory with \(g_n\) tending to zero therefore transfers very little momentum and loses even less energy. If the centres are already moving apart, this instantaneous collision model applies no impulse.

Predict both tracks after an off-centre hit

Take \(m_1=0.20\) kg, \(m_2=0.30\) kg, \(\mathbf v_1=(1.00,0)\) m/s and disk 2 initially at rest. A frame taken at contact places \(\mathbf n=(0.80,0.60)\), and the normal restitution coefficient is \(0.75\). Projecting the incoming velocity gives \(g_n=0.80\) m/s. The reduced mass is \(0.120\) kg, so the impulse magnitude is \((1.75)(0.120)(0.80)=0.168\) N s. Resolving the changes in the laboratory axes yields

\[\mathbf v'_1=(1,0)-(0.168/0.20)(0.80,0.60)=(0.328,-0.504)\ {\rm m/s},\]
\[\mathbf v'_2=(0.168/0.30)(0.80,0.60)=(0.448,0.336)\ {\rm m/s}.\]

The vertical momenta are equal and opposite. Their horizontal sum is \(0.200\) kg m/s as before. The final normal relative velocity is \(-0.600\) m/s, and the energy loss is \(0.0168\) J. Using the full relative speed in the restitution loss formula would give a different result. In the laboratory, fit straight lines to both tracks on either side of contact and compare the inferred impulse vector with the measured centre line. A significant tangential component would motivate adding friction or rotational degrees of freedom to the contact law.

The two-dimensional data also permit a consistency check even when the contact frame is slightly blurred. Subtract disk 2’s incident velocity from its outgoing velocity and normalize that vector. The result estimates the centre line from the observed impulse, given the smooth-disk assumption. Reconstruct the restitution coefficient using this direction and repeat with the direction recorded by the camera. If the inferred coefficient changes significantly between the two choices, possible explanations include a rotational friction impulse or an error in locating the instant of contact. Spin markings on the disks supply an independent discriminator because an off-centre tangential impulse changes their rotation.

How should two colliding carts be mass-matched?

Launch a cart at a stationary target on a horizontal track. Assume a one-dimensional elastic impact. Momentum and relative-speed reversal give \(v_2=2u_1/(1+r)\) for mass ratio \(r=m_2/m_1\). Squaring this speed alone misses the mass factor in the transferred energy.

Chapter 2 · Potential energy and turning points

Use a potential-energy curve to distinguish allowed motion, local stability and escape over a barrier.

From first principles

Obtain the energy curve from the measured force

Consider a point mass constrained to a fixed one-dimensional track with no dissipation. Measure the signed force \(F(x)\) along the track and suppose it depends only on position. Newton's second law supplies the time evolution. Multiplying by displacement converts it into a statement that can be integrated with respect to position:

\[m\ddot x\,dx=m\dot x\,d\dot x=d(\tfrac12m\dot x^2)=F(x)\,dx .\]

Define potential energy relative to an arbitrary reference position \(x_r\) by integrating the negative force. The arbitrary additive constant cannot affect any measured speed:

\[U(x)-U(x_r)=-\int_{x_r}^x F(s)\,ds,\]
\[\tfrac12m\dot x^2+U(x)=E .\]

Thus accessible positions satisfy \(U(x)\le E\) and a position reached with zero speed has \(U(x)=E\). Whether it is an ordinary turning point or an equilibrium also depends on the slope. Near an equilibrium \(x_*\) the linearized force is \(-U''(x_*)(x-x_*)\), giving

\[\delta\ddot x+\frac{U''(x_*)}{m}\delta x=0 .\]

Positive curvature creates a restoring force and local oscillations; a vanishing slope alone does not establish stability.

Derivation and applications

Why does a trajectory linger near the top of a barrier?

A turning point where the slope of the potential is finite is reached in finite time. A smooth barrier summit presents a different case. Near a nondegenerate maximum at \(x=0\) write \(U(x)=U_b-\kappa x^2/2\), with curvature \(\kappa>0\). Take a particle of mass \(m\) crossing from \(-L\) to \(+L\) at energy \(E=U_b+\epsilon\). Use a short interval on which the quadratic fit is accurate, and neglect friction. For \(\epsilon>0\) the positive-velocity branch of energy conservation gives

\[\dot x=\sqrt{(2\epsilon+\kappa x^2)/m},\]
\[t=\sqrt{m}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\epsilon+\kappa x^2}} .\]

The scale \(x_0=\sqrt{2\epsilon/\kappa}\) separates a slow central region from faster motion on the sides. To integrate across both without approximating the centre, set \(x=x_0\sinh u\). The factor \(\cosh u\) from \(dx\) cancels the one from the square root:

\[t=2\sqrt{m/\kappa}\int_0^{\operatorname{arsinh}(L/x_0)}du=2\sqrt{m/\kappa}\operatorname{arsinh}(L/x_0).\]

For an energy excess much smaller than \(\kappa L^2/2\), use \(\operatorname{arsinh}y=\ln(y+\sqrt{1+y^2})\). This gives the near-threshold dependence

\[t\simeq \sqrt{m/\kappa}\ln\!\left(\frac{2\kappa L^2}{\epsilon}\right).\]

Higher-order curvature corrections away from the summit can change a finite offset. The logarithmic growth comes from the increasingly slow passage next to the stationary point. At exactly the barrier energy a trajectory arriving from the left approaches the summit asymptotically.

Worked example

Time a crossing on a calibrated track

Let \(m=0.200\) kg, \(\kappa=5.00\) N/m, \(L=0.0400\) m and \(\epsilon=0.100\) mJ. The central speed is \(\sqrt{2\epsilon/m}=0.0316\) m/s. The length \(x_0=6.32\) mm gives \(L/x_0=6.32\), so the exact transit time is \(0.400\,\operatorname{arsinh}(6.32)=1.02\) s. Dividing the entire distance by the central speed would give \(2.53\) s, since it treats the slowest point as representative of the entire track.

Second application

Infer a curvature from progressively slower crossings

On another track reducing a sufficiently small energy excess by a factor of four adds \(0.416\) s to the transit time. Subtracting two logarithms removes the unknown finite offset. Thus \(\sqrt{m/\kappa}=0.416/\ln4=0.300\) s. For a \(0.180\) kg cart the inferred curvature is \(2.00\) N/m. As a check, another fourfold reduction should add about the same time, even though its absolute energy change is smaller.

OneSimulation laboratory · Measure a period from an orbit

Replace time with position

A small cart travels through a shaped track or along a nonlinear spring. Its acceleration changes continuously with position, yet one can often predict where it will reverse direction before solving for its position as a function of time. Consider a single coordinate \(x\) in an inertial frame, a constant mass and a force that depends on position alone. Then \(F=m\ddot x\) times \(\dot x\) gives \(F(x)\dot x=d(m\dot x^2/2)/dt\). Define the potential difference by \(U(x)-U(x_0)=-\int_{x_0}^xF(s)\,ds\). It follows that \(E=m\dot x^2/2+U(x)\) is constant.

The zero of \(U\) is conventional. Turning points and speed depend on differences, so adding the same constant to \(E\) and \(U\) has no observable consequence. Allowed positions satisfy \(U(x)\le E\). Where the inequality is strict, two velocities have the same energy, one in each direction. Specifying a position and energy therefore leaves a choice of initial direction.

Examine a pair of wells

Take an illustrative bistable spring with potential \(U(x)=A(x^2-b^2)^2\), where \(A>0\) has units J/m⁴ and \(b\) is a length. Differentiation gives \(F=-4Ax(x^2-b^2)\). The equilibrium positions are \(0\) and \(\pm b\). At either outer equilibrium the curvature \(U''=4A(3x^2-b^2)\) is \(8Ab^2>0\). At the origin the curvature is negative. A small displacement from the central equilibrium grows because the force points away from it.

An energy below \(Ab^4\) confines motion to one well, assuming a start there. At higher energy the cart can cross the centre. At exactly the barrier energy, a cart approaching the top from the side slows asymptotically in this frictionless model. Near the top \(E-U(x)\) is proportional to \(x^2\), so the time integral \(\int dx/\sqrt{2(E-U)/m}\) diverges logarithmically as \(x\) approaches zero. A test that checks only \(U=E\) misses this distinction between an ordinary reversal and an unstable equilibrium.

Extract the local frequency

Write \(x=b+\xi\) and expand the potential about the right-hand minimum. Its constant and linear contributions vanish, leaving \(U\simeq (8Ab^2)\xi^2/2\). The equation of motion is harmonic to this order, with \(\omega=\sqrt{8Ab^2/m}\). This curvature estimate uses small displacements compared with \(b\). For excursions sampling a significant fraction of the well, the oscillation need not be sinusoidal and its period can differ substantially.

At an arbitrary energy with adjacent turning points \(x_-,x_+\), a full oscillation takes twice the outward transit time. Thus \(T=2\int_{x_-}^{x_+}dx/\sqrt{2(E-U(x))/m}\). For an ordinary turning point with nonzero slope of \(U\), the denominator vanishes as the square root of distance and the integral remains finite. This expression supplies a direct route from a measured force curve to the period without measuring acceleration.

Interpret a threshold experiment

Let \(m=0.200\) kg, \(A=4000\) J/m⁴ and \(b=0.0500\) m. The barrier height is 0.0250 J and the effective spring stiffness at either minimum is 80.0 N/m. A weak disturbance oscillates at 20.0 rad/s, with period 0.314 s. From the minimum an initial speed of 0.400 m/s supplies 0.0160 J, below the crossing threshold. Solving \(x^2=b^2\pm\sqrt{E/A}\) gives right-well turning points 0.0224 and 0.0671 m. The displacement is asymmetric about the minimum even though the local harmonic approximation is symmetric.

A launch exactly at \(\sqrt{2(0.0250)/0.200}=0.500\) m/s is marginal. Crossing the barrier in finite time requires greater speed in this idealisation. In an experiment, dissipation raises the energy the cart needs at launch. Measuring the speed at several known positions tests conservation along the path and helps distinguish a wrong potential shape from an actual energy loss.

Time measurements give a second access to the shape of the potential. Between ordinary turning points the speed is \(\sqrt{2[E-U(x)]/m}\). The half-period is therefore the integral of its reciprocal over the allowed interval. The integrand diverges at a simple turning point, but only as the inverse square root of distance, giving a finite travel time. At an energy that just approaches a smooth unstable maximum, the divergence becomes logarithmic. Extremely slow passage near the barrier can easily be mistaken for a stopped cart.

Design a track whose period is independent of amplitude

The local curvature of a smooth potential fixes a small-oscillation period. An extended track can also be shaped to give exactly harmonic motion over a finite range. Use signed arc length \(s\) from the lowest point as coordinate. On a frictionless fixed track kinetic energy is \(m\dot s^2/2\). In a uniform gravitational field the only configuration-dependent energy is \(mgy(s)\). To obtain \(\ddot s=-\omega^2s\), require \(g\,dy/ds=\omega^2s\). Integration sets the necessary height profile \(y(s)=\omega^2s^2/(2g)\), with height zero at the bottom.

A cycloidal arc realizes this condition. With scale \(a\) take \(x=a(\phi+\sin\phi)\) and \(y=a(1-\cos\phi)\) for \(-\pi<\phi<\pi\). Differentiate both coordinates. The speed along the parametric curve is \(ds/d\phi=a\sqrt{(1+\cos\phi)^2+\sin^2\phi}=2a\cos(\phi/2)\). Integrating from the bottom gives \(s=4a\sin(\phi/2)\), retaining the sign of \(\phi\). Substituting in the height expression eliminates the parameter:

\[y=\frac{s^2}{8a},\qquad \ddot s+\frac{g}{4a}s=0.\]

The coordinate that executes sinusoidal motion is the path length. Its horizontal projection generally does something different because the track slope changes. A bead released at rest reaches the bottom after one quarter of the period for every nonzero admissible release amplitude. The cancellation between a longer path and a higher typical speed is encoded in the exactly quadratic gravitational potential in \(s\).

Time two different releases

Set \(a=0.250\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s². The angular frequency is \(\sqrt{9.81/(4\times0.250)}=3.13\) rad/s and the predicted time to the bottom is \(\pi/(2\omega)=0.502\) s. Release a bead first from \(s=0.400\) m and then from \(s=0.800\) m. Their initial heights are respectively \(0.0800\) m and \(0.320\) m. At the bottom their speeds are \(\omega |s_0|=1.25\) and \(2.51\) m/s. Doubling arc length quadruples starting potential energy, providing just the required increase in speed to preserve arrival time.

A timing gate near the bottom and an electronic release can measure both quantities independently. A rolling ball has additional rotational inertia and a centre following a curve offset from the track. Treating it as the sliding point mass can bias the comparison even if rolling dissipation is small. Near the bottom the cycloid agrees with a circular arc of its local curvature. Farther away the circular arc develops anharmonic corrections, so comparing equal-time releases at several amplitudes discriminates between two tracks that look almost identical near their lowest point.

There is a useful inverse interpretation of a release series. Suppose the measured bottom speed doubles on doubling signed release distance, but the measured time to the lowest point changes. Correct speed scaling tests only the endpoint height relation. Equal travel times require the integrated speed to have the required dependence throughout the intervening path, and a contact force doing work can spoil that comparison. Measuring several intermediate heights as a function of arc length checks the quadratic law directly. Taking horizontal distance as the release coordinate introduces a purely geometric nonlinearity even for a perfectly machined cycloidal track.

Locate the turning points of a bound orbit

Hold the angular momentum per unit mass fixed at \(h^2=GM r_0\). The radial kinetic energy is the vertical distance from \(V_{\rm eff}=h^2/(2r^2)-GM/r\) up to the total energy. Find both intersections for \(E=-3GM/(8r_0)\) before inspecting the curve.

Problem sheet 1 · Impulses and energy landscapes

Connect the collision measurements of Lecture 1 with the allowed-motion diagrams of Lecture 2.

A triangular force pulse

A 0.40 kg glider travelling right at 1.0 m/s strikes a fixed instrumented wall. The force on the glider points left, rises linearly to a peak of 120 N and falls linearly to zero within 6.0 ms. Calculate the impulse, outgoing velocity and kinetic energy change. Explain which of those results would survive a change in pulse shape at fixed area.

Integrate the measured force

The signed area is \(-(120)(0.0060)/2=-0.360\) N·s. The final momentum is \(0.400-0.360=0.040\) kg·m/s and the final velocity is still \(+0.100\) m/s. The kinetic energy changes from 0.200 to 0.0020 J, a decrease of 0.198 J. For the stated fixed wall, this time interval cannot describe a completed rebound. The glider is still moving towards it, so the record may cover only part of the contact. For the same initial state any pulse of the same signed area gives the same final velocity and kinetic energy. Its peak force and the maximum deformation can differ.

Compare two frames

A 1.0 kg cart travelling at 3.0 m/s catches a 2.0 kg cart travelling in the same direction at 0.60 m/s. They lock. Calculate the final speed and energy lost in the lab. Repeat using the centre-of-mass frame. Which quantity differs between frames?

Transform the individual speeds

Total momentum is \(4.2\) kg·m/s, total mass is 3.0 kg, and the coupled speed is 1.4 m/s. The energy falls from \(4.5+0.36=4.86\) J to \(0.5(3)(1.4)^2=2.94\) J, a loss of 1.92 J. Subtracting the centre-of-mass speed from both initial velocities gives \(+1.6\) and \(-0.8\) m/s. Their initial energies are then 1.28 and 0.64 J and the final energy is zero. The initial energy differs by the energy of uniform translation. The energy lost and the carts’ relative approach speed agree. Momentum has different numerical values in the two frames but is conserved in each.

A vertical spring releases its load

A cart of mass 0.50 kg moves vertically on a linear spring of stiffness 50 N/m, attached at both ends so that compression and extension are possible. Measure \(y\) downward from the spring’s unstretched position. Write the gravitational and spring potential together, find the stable equilibrium and determine the turning points and period if released from rest at \(y=0\).

Complete the square

The potential is \(U=ky^2/2-mgy\). Its derivative vanishes at \(y_*=mg/k=0.0981\) m and its curvature is \(k>0\). Completing the square gives \(U=k(y-y_*)^2/2-ky_*^2/2\). Release gives total energy zero in the chosen reference. Solving \(U=0\) gives \(y=0\) and \(y=2y_*=0.1962\) m. Measured from equilibrium the amplitude is 0.0981 m and \(T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}=0.628\) s. Gravity shifts the centre of motion and the turning points but leaves the local curvature unchanged.

Chapter 3 · Constraints and Lagrange’s equations

Eliminate an unknown tension with a generalised coordinate and identify how a pulley’s rotational inertia changes an acceleration.

From first principles

Derive the one-coordinate Lagrange equation from virtual work

For point masses with fixed, smooth ideal constraints, describe all positions as \(\mathbf r_i(q)\). An allowed instantaneous virtual displacement is \(\delta\mathbf r_i=(\partial_q\mathbf r_i)\delta q\). Assume constraint reactions do zero total virtual work. Newton's equations projected along this common displacement therefore eliminate the unmeasured reactions:

\[\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i\cdot\partial_q\mathbf r_i=\sum_i\mathbf F_i^{\rm applied}\cdot\partial_q\mathbf r_i=Q .\]

Use \(K=\sum_i m_i\mathbf v_i^2/2\) and \(\mathbf v_i=(\partial_q\mathbf r_i)\dot q\). Since \(\partial_{\dot q}\mathbf v_i=\partial_q\mathbf r_i\), differentiate \(\partial_{\dot q}K\) in time. The product term involving \(d(\partial_q\mathbf r_i)/dt\) equals \(\partial_q K\). Subtracting it leaves

\[\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i\cdot\partial_q\mathbf r_i=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial K}{\partial q}.\]

If the applied forces derive from a potential \(U(q)\) then \(Q=-\partial_qU\). Moving the potential term gives the equation for \(L=K-U\):

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0 .\]

The coordinate choice can eliminate a tension without assuming that tension vanishes. A rolling constraint requires the virtual displacements to obey its no-slip condition.

Derivation and applications

How does a moving pulley change the inertia seen by a load?

A string constraint fixes acceleration ratios before tensions are known. Consider a mass \(M\) carried by a light movable pulley. One end of a single inextensible string is fixed to the ceiling. It passes around the movable pulley, over a fixed pulley of radius \(R\) and moment of inertia \(I\), and down to a free counterweight \(m\). Use downward coordinate \(q\) for the movable assembly and downward coordinate \(y\) for the counterweight. The fixed pulley rotates freely about its axle. Assume a massless string, no slip, and constant lengths of the wrapped arcs.

The moving pulley changes the lengths of two vertical segments. Differentiate the length constraint to determine both the counterweight speed and the rim speed of the fixed pulley:

\[2q+y=\text{constant},\]
\[\dot y=-2\dot q,\]
\[|\omega|=2|\dot q|/R .\]

Write all kinetic and potential energies in terms of the single coordinate. Downward displacement reduces gravitational potential, and raising the counterweight contributes with the opposite sign:

\[K=\tfrac12\!\left(M+4m+4I/R^2\right)\dot q^2,\]
\[U=(-M+2m)gq+\text{constant}.\]

The Lagrange equation equates the rate of change of generalized momentum to the generalized force. The velocity ratios enter the inertia squared, whereas the displacement ratios enter gravitational work linearly:

\[(M+4m+4I/R^2)\ddot q=(M-2m)g.\]

All terms multiplying acceleration have units of mass. An ideal fixed pulley with a heavy rim still slows the motion, even though its centre does not translate. At \(M=2m\) the gravitational driving force vanishes for every value of \(I\).

Worked example

Predict a descending load and an ascending counterweight

Set \(M=1.20\) kg, \(m=0.400\) kg, \(R=0.0500\) m and \(I=2.50\times10^{-4}\) kg m². The rotational contribution is \(0.400\) kg and the effective mass is \(3.20\) kg. The driving force is \(0.400g=3.924\) N, giving \(\ddot q=1.23\) m/s² downward and counterweight acceleration \(2.45\) m/s² upward. A release from rest moves the load \(0.153\) m in \(0.500\) s if sufficient travel is available.

Second application

Measure a pulley inertia from a reversed acceleration

With the same \(M\) and \(R\), change the counterweight to \(m=0.800\) kg. A camera records upward load acceleration \(0.8175\) m/s². The signed numerator is now \(-3.924\) N, so the measured effective mass is \(4.80\) kg. Subtracting \(M+4m=4.40\) kg leaves \(4I/R^2=0.400\) kg, recovering \(I=2.50\times10^{-4}\) kg m² to measurement precision. The reversal tests both the sign of gravitational work and the factor of two in the length constraint.

Count independent motions

Two hanging masses and a massive pulley appear to introduce two translations, a rotation and several forces. Yet an inextensible taut string and rolling contact without slip leave just one independent motion. Let mass \(m_2\) descend a distance \(q\) while mass \(m_1\) rises by the same distance. For a fixed axle and pulley radius \(R\), the rotation is \(\theta=q/R\) relative to its initial value. Assume the masses stay clear of the pulley, the string is light, the axle friction is negligible and the pulley’s moment of inertia about the axle is \(I\).

A constraint relates permitted displacements before any force balance is written. The fact that both ends move the same distance follows from length conservation. It does not require equal tensions. A massive accelerating pulley needs a net torque, so replacing the tensions by a common value would remove its angular acceleration from the model. This example is a useful test of when a constraint allows us to remove coordinates and when it also allows us to remove forces.

Construct the stationarity equation

For ideal time-independent constraints the reaction forces do no total work on allowed virtual displacements. Form the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L=K-U\) using the independent coordinates. Consider two paths with the same initial and final values of \(q\), differing by a small variation \(\delta q(t)\). The first variation of the action is \(\int(\partial_q\mathcal L\,\delta q+\partial_{\dot q}\mathcal L\,\delta\dot q)dt\). Integrate the second term by parts. The endpoint term vanishes and stationarity for arbitrary interior variations requires

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q}=0.\]

This equation supplies one independent balance for each independent coordinate. If an applied nonconservative force does work \(\delta W=Q\,\delta q\), its generalised force \(Q\) enters the right-hand side. For an angular coordinate \(Q\) has units of torque; for this translational coordinate it has units of force. It must be calculated using the same sign choice as the coordinate.

Find a conserved quantity from a continuous symmetry

Constraints identify which coordinates can vary independently. A symmetry asks a different question: which small change of those coordinates leaves the action unchanged? Let \(q_i\mapsto q_i+\varepsilon\xi_i(q,t)\) at fixed time and \(p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i\). Along a solution the Euler–Lagrange equations turn the variation into a total derivative,

\[\frac{\delta L}{\varepsilon}=\sum_i\left(\dot p_i\xi_i+p_i\dot\xi_i\right)=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i p_i\xi_i .\]

If the left side vanishes for every path, \(Q=\sum_i p_i\xi_i\) is conserved on solutions. More generally, a change \(\delta L=\varepsilon\,dB/dt\) changes only endpoint terms in the action, and \(Q=\sum_i p_i\xi_i-B\) is conserved. This is Noether's theorem for this fixed-time transformation. Separate conservation of mechanical energy requires time-translation symmetry.

Consider a bead of mass \(m\) on a smooth horizontal table. A light string attached to the bead passes through a central hole. Pulling the string prescribes the radius \(r(t)\). Its tension has no azimuthal component. Rotation of the entire trajectory through a constant azimuth leaves \(L=m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)/2\) unchanged. Therefore \(mr^2\dot\phi\) is constant even when a pulling device supplies work. For \(m=0.250\) kg, \(r_i=0.400\) m and \(\dot\phi_i=3.00\) rad/s, \(Q=0.120\) kg m²/s. Halving the radius raises angular speed to \(12.0\) rad/s. With zero radial speed at each endpoint, kinetic energy rises from \(0.180\) to \(0.720\) J. A central force can do work while exerting no torque about the centre.

Add every energy contribution

Both hanging masses move at speed \(|\dot q|\). The pulley contributes rotational kinetic energy \(I(\dot q/R)^2/2\). Raising the first mass increases its gravitational potential, while lowering the second decreases it. Omitting an arbitrary constant gives \(U=(m_1-m_2)gq\). The full kinetic energy is \(K=(m_1+m_2+I/R^2)\dot q^2/2\). Substitution yields

\[(m_1+m_2+I/R^2)\ddot q=(m_2-m_1)g.\]

The quantity \(I/R^2\) enters like an additional translational mass. It stores kinetic energy without changing the gravitational driving force. A constant opposing axle torque of magnitude \(\tau_f\) would give a generalised resisting force \(\tau_f/R\) while the second mass descends. Applying a single constant sign across a reversal would incorrectly model dry friction.

Verify the reactions by restoring the constraints

Take \(m_1=1.00\) kg, \(m_2=1.50\) kg, \(R=0.100\) m and \(I=0.0050\) kg·m². The effective mass is 3.00 kg and the unbalanced weight is 4.905 N. Thus \(\ddot q=1.635\) m/s². From rest, a descent of 0.300 m produces a speed \(\sqrt{2(1.635)(0.300)}=0.990\) m/s. The drop in potential energy is 1.472 J, equal to the gain in translational and rotational energy together.

Now isolate each hanging body. Upward acceleration on the lighter side requires \(T_1=m_1(g+\ddot q)=11.45\) N. The heavier side gives \(T_2=m_2(g-\ddot q)=12.26\) N. The tension difference times radius is about 0.082 N·m. The pulley independently requires \(I\ddot q/R=0.0818\) N·m. Agreement checks both the direction convention and the contribution of pulley inertia. The energy description solved for observable motion first. The separate body balances recovered forces that a mount or a string must actually withstand.

A useful check is to let one inertia become dominant. If the pulley’s moment of inertia is made very large while its radius and both hanging masses stay fixed, the effective inertia in the denominator grows and acceleration approaches zero. The two string tensions then approach their respective hanging weights, which can remain unequal despite the tiny acceleration. The finite difference supplies torque to a very large rotational inertia. Equality of tension throughout a string that wraps around a massive pulley is therefore an additional physical assumption that must be justified.

Rolling without slipping and the force enforcing it

A constraint can remove an unknown contact force from the equation of motion while leaving a definite physical condition for its realization. Consider a cylinder of mass \(m\), radius \(R\) and moment of inertia \(I\) about its centre, rolling straight down a plane inclined at angle \(\alpha\). Let \(x\) increase down the plane, and take the spin angle \(\theta\) positive for forward rolling. A stationary contact point on the plane requires \(\dot x=R\dot\theta\). In this straight-line geometry the velocity constraint integrates directly to \(x-R\theta=\mathrm{constant}\). General rolling on a surface with changing direction requires more care because the analogous constraints need not integrate to coordinate relations.

Use \(x\) as the sole coordinate. The centre falls through \(x\sin\alpha\) and kinetic energy contains both translation and rotation. The reduced Lagrangian is

\[L=\tfrac12(m+I/R^2)\dot x^2+mgx\sin\alpha .\]

The Euler–Lagrange equation gives \(\ddot x=g\sin\alpha/(1+I/(mR^2))\). For a body released from rest, the speed follows by integrating \(\dot x\,d\dot x/dx=\ddot x\). Different mass distributions at fixed radius and mass therefore arrive at different times even if their initial gravitational energies are equal. Some of that energy appears in spin, whose contribution grows with \(I\).

To determine the unknown friction, return to the original translational and rotational balances. If \(f\) points up the plane, \(m\ddot x=mg\sin\alpha-f\) and \(I\ddot\theta=fR\). Combining with the rolling constraint gives \(f=(I/R^2)\ddot x\). The absence of \(f\) from the reduced Lagrangian came from zero virtual work under an admissible rolling displacement. It did not imply zero force.

Decide whether a solid cylinder will actually roll

A uniform solid cylinder has \(I=mR^2/2\). Let \(m=2.00\) kg, \(R=0.100\) m and \(\alpha=30.0^\circ\). Predicted acceleration is \(9.81(0.5)/(1.5)=3.27\) m/s². The required upward friction is \((m/2)\ddot x=3.27\) N, while the normal force is \(mg\cos30^\circ=17.0\) N. Static friction can maintain rolling only for \(\mu_s\ge f/N=0.192\). This comparison tests an assumption that could not be checked from the acceleration equation alone.

After travelling 1.00 m from rest a rolling cylinder reaches \(2.56\) m/s and spins at \(25.6\) rad/s. Translational energy is \(6.54\) J and rotational energy is \(3.27\) J, accounting for the \(9.81\) J released by gravity. In the limit \(I/(mR^2)\to0\), nearly all energy goes into translation, acceleration tends to \(g\sin\alpha\) and the required friction tends to zero. A surface with a measured coefficient below the threshold instead permits slipping. The translation and rotation must then be solved independently using the sliding contact law.

Rolling tests can be run without knowing the cylinder’s mass distribution in advance. Obtain acceleration from two light gates at known positions and use \(I/(mR^2)=g\sin\alpha/\ddot x-1\) to infer a dimensionless inertia. Repeat at a shallower inclination. A genuine moment of inertia has the same inferred value in both runs, whereas slipping can appear only above a limiting angle. The most direct test tracks a painted mark on the rim as well as the centre. Admissible rolling requires the increase in centre displacement to equal radius times accumulated spin angle in each time interval.

Chapter 4 · Normal modes and energy exchange

Decompose a pair of coupled oscillators into independent motions and predict the envelope of a beat signal.

From first principles

Derive the coupled oscillator equations from individual spring extensions

Take identical carts of mass \(m\) with outer springs of stiffness \(k\) and an interconnecting spring of stiffness \(k_c\). Measure displacements \(x_1,x_2\) rightward from equilibrium. For small motions Hooke's law makes elastic energy quadratic in the extension of each individual spring. The middle one extends only when the positions differ:

\[U=\tfrac12k x_1^2+\tfrac12k x_2^2+\tfrac12k_c(x_2-x_1)^2 .\]

The forces are minus the corresponding derivatives of \(U\). Add the two Newton equations to isolate motion that does not stretch the coupling. Subtract them to isolate opposing motion that stretches it twice:

\[m(\ddot x_1+\ddot x_2)=-k(x_1+x_2),\]
\[m(\ddot x_1-\ddot x_2)=-(k+2k_c)(x_1-x_2).\]

Each new coordinate is a single unforced oscillator; comparing its acceleration with \(-\omega^2\) times displacement fixes the two frequencies:

\[\omega_s^2=k/m,\]
\[\omega_a^2=(k+2k_c)/m .\]

For positive coupling the antisymmetric frequency is higher. Releasing only one cart excites both coordinates, so neither measured cart trajectory is generally a single sinusoid. At \(k_c=0\) the frequencies coincide and energy cannot pass through the absent coupling spring.

Derivation and applications

What replaces symmetry coordinates when the masses differ?

The symmetric and antisymmetric combinations for identical carts cease to decouple when one cart is loaded. Still, a normal mode has a definite frequency and a fixed ratio of displacements. Take carts of masses \(m\) and \(2m\), each attached to an outer wall by a spring of stiffness \(k\). Connect them with a third spring, also of stiffness \(k\). Measure both small displacements rightward from equilibrium. The coupling spring extends by \(x_2-x_1\); this determines the signs of its two forces without assuming a mode in advance.

\[m\ddot x_1=-2kx_1+kx_2,\]
\[2m\ddot x_2=kx_1-2kx_2.\]

Seek a sinusoidal solution \(x_j=A_j\cos(\omega t+\phi)\). A nonzero pair of amplitudes must solve two homogeneous equations simultaneously. Eliminating \(A_2\) and writing \(s=m\omega^2/k\) gives

\[(2-s)A_1=A_2,\]
\[(2-2s)A_2=A_1,\]
\[(2-s)(2-2s)=1 .\]

The last equation is quadratic in squared frequency. Its roots and their displacement ratios are

\[s_\pm=(3\pm\sqrt3)/2,\]
\[\omega_\pm=\sqrt{ks_\pm/m},\]
\[(A_2/A_1)_\pm=2-s_\pm .\]

The low mode has the carts moving in the same direction, while the high mode stretches their connecting spring by moving them oppositely. The amplitude ratios no longer equal one in magnitude. Ordinary dot products do not describe the kinetic energy of unequal masses. Weighting the second component by two gives \(1+2(2-s_+)(2-s_-)=0\), the mass-weighted orthogonality that allows arbitrary initial conditions to be decomposed.

Worked example

Separate measured spectral peaks

For \(m=0.250\) kg and \(k=25.0\) N/m, \(k/m=100\) s⁻². The predicted modal frequencies are \(7.96\) and \(15.38\) rad/s, or \(1.27\) and \(2.45\) Hz. The ratios \(A_2/A_1\) are \(1.366\) and \(-0.366\). A measured \(3.00\) mm amplitude of the lighter cart in the high mode therefore accompanies a \(1.10\) mm amplitude of the heavier cart in the opposite phase.

Second application

Prepare motion in only one mode

Displace the lighter cart \(4.00\) mm rightward. To excite the low mode alone, move the heavier cart \(5.46\) mm rightward and release both from rest. If instead the heavy cart starts at zero, write the initial light-cart displacement as \(a_-+a_+=4.00\) mm. The second component requires \(1.366a_--0.366a_+=0\). Solving gives \(a_-=0.845\) mm and \(a_+=3.15\) mm. Both frequencies now appear even though the same lighter cart was released from the same place.

Start with two measurable coordinates

Fix two identical carts of mass \(m\) to opposite ends of a track using identical springs of stiffness \(k\), and join the carts to each other with a third spring of stiffness \(k_c\). The equilibrium lengths have already been set. Let \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) be small displacements from equilibrium, both positive to the right. The coupling spring extends by \(x_2-x_1\). Both coordinates contribute independently to the kinetic energy, but their potential energies are linked through this difference.

From \(U=k(x_1^2+x_2^2)/2+k_c(x_2-x_1)^2/2\), the equations are \(m\ddot x_1=-(k+k_c)x_1+k_cx_2\) and \(m\ddot x_2=k_cx_1-(k+k_c)x_2\). One moving cart thus exerts a force on its neighbour. Release a single displaced cart and both generally move even though only one carried potential energy in its end spring at the start. The task is to predict that exchange without assuming that either cart alone executes a single harmonic oscillation.

Use symmetry to choose coordinates

Interchanging the two labels leaves the equations invariant. Their sum contains only \(x_1+x_2\) and their difference contains only \(x_1-x_2\). Define \(q_s=(x_1+x_2)/2\) and \(q_a=(x_1-x_2)/2\). Adding then subtracting the equations yields

\[\ddot q_s+(k/m)q_s=0,\qquad \ddot q_a+[(k+2k_c)/m]q_a=0.\]

The symmetric mode moves both carts together so that the central spring keeps its length. The antisymmetric mode moves them oppositely and stretches that spring by twice either displacement. These patterns determine the frequencies before an amplitude is chosen. Initial conditions select the weights and phases of the modes. For unequal masses symmetry is lost, but substituting \(\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\phi\cos\omega t\) in \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf x}+\mathbf K\mathbf x=0\) still gives the generalised eigenproblem \(\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi=\omega^2\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi\).

Follow a release from one side

Displace cart 1 by \(A\) and leave cart 2 at its equilibrium position, both initially at rest. Each normal coordinate starts at \(A/2\) with zero velocity. Recombining gives \(x_1=A(\cos\omega_s t+\cos\omega_a t)/2\) and \(x_2=A(\cos\omega_s t-\cos\omega_a t)/2\). With \(\bar\omega=(\omega_s+\omega_a)/2\) and \(\Delta\omega=\omega_a-\omega_s\), the first expression is \(A\cos(\bar\omega t)\cos(\Delta\omega t/2)\). The rapid motion and the slow envelope arise from the same two undamped modes. A fading displacement over a few cycles need not indicate dissipated energy.

The second cart has a complementary envelope. When the cosine envelope of cart 1 first vanishes, the sine envelope of cart 2 has unit magnitude. Its instantaneous displacement depends on the fast phase. The coupling spring also stores energy, so attributing all system energy to individual carts from their instantaneous displacements can misidentify the time of energy transfer. Modal energies remain constant for a linear undamped system.

Interpret a pair of peaks

Use \(m=1.00\) kg, \(k=100\) N/m, \(k_c=10.5\) N/m and \(A=0.030\) m. The angular frequencies are exactly 10.0 and 11.0 rad/s. A spectrum of the motion of cart 1 contains peaks at 1.592 and 1.751 Hz. The envelope first vanishes after \(\pi/\Delta\omega=3.142\) s and first regains unit magnitude after \(2\pi/\Delta\omega=6.283\) s. This is about ten rapid cycles. From the two measured frequencies and the known cart mass one can estimate the coupling because \(k_c=m(\omega_a^2-\omega_s^2)/2\).

Repeat the release with both carts displaced by the same amount. Only the lower-frequency mode should survive. A remaining upper peak would be evidence to look for unequal initial positions, mass mismatch or spring asymmetry. Comparing the peaks under two physically different preparations gives a sharper model check than a single time trace.

Symmetry gives the shapes directly for identical carts. With unequal carts, multiply the eigenvalue equation for mode \(i\) from the left by the transpose of mode \(j\), then subtract the reverse relation. Symmetry of the mass and stiffness matrices leaves \((\omega_i^2-\omega_j^2)\boldsymbol\phi_j^{T}\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi_i=0\). Distinct modes are orthogonal under the mass-weighted product. This states exactly how to project a measured initial displacement onto unequal-mass modes. An ordinary unweighted average of the two cart displacements would generally mix them. Simultaneous recordings then test predicted mode shapes as well as their frequencies.

A tuned auxiliary mass can suppress the driven motion

Normal modes can be used to alter which coordinate moves under a repeated force. Attach a primary mass \(m\) to ground with a spring \(k\) and connect an auxiliary mass \(m_a\) to the primary with another spring \(k_a\). Both move along one line. Ignore damping for the ideal design and drive only the primary mass with \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Let \(X,Y\) denote signed steady harmonic displacement amplitudes. The equilibrium equations at the drive frequency are

\[(k+k_a-m\omega^2)X-k_aY=F_0,\qquad -k_a X+(k_a-m_a\omega^2)Y=0.\]

At \(\omega^2=k_a/m_a\), the coefficient multiplying \(Y\) in the second equation vanishes. It then requires \(X=0\). The primary equation fixes \(Y=-F_0/k_a\): the auxiliary spring force cancels the external drive on the primary. This is an antiresonance of the primary coordinate. A system with more than one coordinate can have a deep minimum between two resonance peaks because the measured coordinate combines both modal responses with opposing signs.

To distinguish this cancellation from a normal mode, set the right-hand side to zero. Nonzero free motion demands a zero determinant, \((k+k_a-m\omega^2)(k_a-m_a\omega^2)-k_a^2=0\). At the proposed cancellation frequency that determinant is \(-k_a^2\), strictly nonzero, so the forced solution there is unique. On initial start-up a real experiment also excites transients at both natural frequencies. Weak damping or carefully chosen initial conditions is needed before the pure harmonic response can be observed.

Tune an instrument support

A 2.00 kg instrument sits on a 200 N/m mount and experiences a 1.00 N harmonic force at \(10.0\) rad/s, exactly its original undamped natural frequency. Add \(m_a=0.200\) kg and choose \(k_a=m_a\omega^2=20.0\) N/m. The ideal primary coordinate remains stationary at the drive frequency, while the auxiliary mass requires amplitude \(1.00/20.0=0.0500\) m. A design can therefore predict excellent isolation and still fail a travel constraint.

Set \(s=\omega^2\) in the free-motion determinant. With these values it becomes \(0.4s^2-84s+4000=0\), with roots near \(73.0\) and \(137.0\) s⁻². The new natural frequencies are \(8.54\) and \(11.7\) rad/s, bracketing the designed minimum. The primary response is therefore vulnerable to detuning in either direction. As auxiliary mass tends to zero at fixed tuning, its spring stiffness tends to zero as well and the required auxiliary travel diverges. Monitoring auxiliary displacement while sweeping the forcing frequency gives a stronger design test than looking only for a minimum in the instrument record.

It is worth distinguishing force cancellation from motion cancellation when interpreting a measured antiresonance. At the designed frequency the primary mass has negligible absolute acceleration, so the summed spring forces and external force must cancel on it. The attachment still experiences a finite cyclic force, equal to its stiffness times relative motion. A load cell in that small spring can therefore record a pronounced signal while the primary displacement sensor records its minimum. The two readings together check that suppression comes from the auxiliary degree of freedom, rather than from a drop in the drive force at that frequency.

Find how much mistuning the absorber tolerates

Return to the mounted instrument and suppose the operating frequency drifts. Eliminate the auxiliary displacement from the two harmonic equations without setting it to resonance first. Cramer's rule gives primary compliance

\[\frac{X}{F_0}=\frac{k_a-m_a\omega^2}{(k+k_a-m\omega^2)(k_a-m_a\omega^2)-k_a^2}.\]

The numerator determines a zero and the denominator determines the two poles. At zero frequency the ratio is \(1/k\), since the free auxiliary mass offers no static restoring force. At very high frequency the ratio tends to \(-1/(m\omega^2)\) because the primary inertia dominates. Both limits are useful for checking the absolute phase and calibration of a measured response.

Set the drive to \(9.80\) rad/s, only two percent below design. The auxiliary dynamic stiffness is \(20.0-0.200(9.80)^2=0.792\) N/m. The primary diagonal term is \(220-2.00(9.80)^2=27.9\) N/m. With the original 1.00 N drive the predicted primary amplitude is \(0.792/|27.9(0.792)-400|=2.10\) mm. The auxiliary amplitude from the second equation is about 53.0 mm. At \(10.2\) rad/s the corresponding primary amplitude is about 1.98 mm and changes sign relative to the force. Thus a two-percent frequency drift already matters if the instrument’s allowed motion is one millimetre.

A small damper between the two masses limits the two resonant peaks but removes exact cancellation at the tuned frequency. The spring's dynamic stiffness is then replaced by \(k_a+i\omega c_a\) in both coupling terms. With real drive frequency and positive damping the numerator no longer vanishes exactly. The engineering choice can now be stated quantitatively: hold zero response at one precisely controlled frequency, or allow a finite minimum in order to improve nearby peak response when operating frequency varies.

Measure the inertia of a pulley

With string radius 80 mm, hanging masses of 1.20 kg and 0.80 kg accelerate at 1.40 m/s², heavier side down. Neglect friction. Infer the pulley inertia, calculate the tension difference and predict the acceleration if the same inertia is used at a winding radius of 40 mm.

Recover the hidden rotational energy

The mass difference supplies 3.924 N, requiring an effective total mass \(3.924/1.40=2.803\) kg. Subtract the hanging mass sum of 2.00 kg to get \(I/R^2=0.803\) kg, hence \(I=0.00514\) kg·m². Torque balance gives \(T_h-T_l=I\ddot q/R^2=1.124\) N. Halving the radius quadruples the effective rotational mass. The new acceleration is \(3.924/(2.00+4\times0.803)=0.753\) m/s². This test could help separate a radius-dependent inertia effect from an incorrectly weighed hanging mass.

Identify the springs from soundings

Two identical carts of mass 0.50 kg have measured angular frequencies 8.0 and 12.0 rad/s. Identical end springs and a connecting spring are assumed. Determine both stiffnesses. Prescribe a release that isolates the higher mode and predict the time of the first return to the initial configuration.

Invert the two modal equations

The symmetric motion uses only the end springs, so \(k=m\omega_s^2=32\) N/m. The antisymmetric equation gives \(k_c=m(\omega_a^2-\omega_s^2)/2=20\) N/m. Displace the carts equally in opposite directions and release together from rest. Their centre stays fixed and the first full return occurs after \(2\pi/12=0.524\) s. After half that interval the displacements are interchanged, which is distinguishable if the carts are marked.

Find a frequency from a bead’s constraint

A bead of mass \(m\) slides without friction in a vertical plane on a wire of shape \(z=\alpha x^2\) with \(\alpha=2.0\) m⁻¹, where \(z\) increases upwards. Derive the one-coordinate Lagrangian. Find the small-amplitude frequency and estimate how good the kinetic-energy approximation is at \(x=0.10\) m.

Include the vertical velocity

The constraint gives \(\dot z=2\alpha x\dot x\). Thus \(K=m(1+4\alpha^2x^2)\dot x^2/2\) and \(U=mg\alpha x^2\). The exact Euler–Lagrange equation is \((1+4\alpha^2x^2)\ddot x+4\alpha^2x\dot x^2+2g\alpha x=0\). Keeping terms first order in the small motion gives \(\omega=\sqrt{2g\alpha}=6.26\) rad/s or 0.997 Hz. At 0.10 m the kinetic-energy factor is 1.16. Treating the bead as moving horizontally undercounts the exact kinetic energy at the same horizontal speed by \(0.16/1.16=13.8\%\). The small-amplitude frequency is a local prediction near the bottom.

Two free carts of masses 0.40 and 0.60 kg interact through a spring whose relaxed length is controlled as a function of time. Initially their speeds are +0.90 and −0.10 m/s. Derive the conserved quantity from a simultaneous translation and find the second cart's speed when the first stops. Can total kinetic plus spring energy change?

Derive the invariant and use it

The potential depends on \(x_2-x_1-\ell(t)\), so equal translations give \(\xi_1=\xi_2=1\) and \(\delta L=0\). Noether's charge is \(m_1\dot x_1+m_2\dot x_2=0.300\) kg m/s. When \(\dot x_1=0\), \(\dot x_2=0.300/0.60=0.500\) m/s. Chapter 5 · Rotating frames and the Coriolis effect

Transform a measured trajectory between an inertial laboratory and a rotating observer, and quantify the first sideways deflection.

From first principles

Differentiate a vector whose coordinate axes are turning

Let a moving observer have origin \(\mathbf R_O\) and an orthonormal basis \(\mathbf e_i(t)\) rotating with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). Write relative position as \(\mathbf r=\sum_i r_i\mathbf e_i\). A small rigid rotation \(d\boldsymbol\theta\) moves a unit vector perpendicular to itself through \(d\mathbf e_i=d\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf e_i\). Hence differentiating both coordinates and basis gives the transport identity

\[\left(\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\right)_{\rm lab}=\left(\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\right)_{\rm rot}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r .\]

The absolute velocity is therefore the sum of origin motion, observed relative motion and transport around the origin. Differentiate those three contributions again. In particular, differentiating relative velocity in the lab adds one \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot}\), and differentiating the transport velocity adds a second:

\[\mathbf a_{\rm lab}=\mathbf a_O+\mathbf a_{\rm rot}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot}+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).\]

Newton’s law applies with the laboratory acceleration if that frame is inertial. Moving the extra accelerations to the force side predicts the rotating observer’s trajectory. A marker fixed to the rotating frame has zero relative velocity, yet its laboratory acceleration can remain nonzero.

Derivation and applications

Why does a dropped object land east of the plumb line?

A release from rest beside a tower initially shares the ground's rotation. It starts with zero velocity in the local rotating frame, yet acquires a horizontal deflection as it falls. Use local axes east, north and up, and let \(\varphi\) denote latitude. Over a short fall treat effective gravitational acceleration \(g\) and Earth's angular velocity \(\Omega\) as constant. The plumb line defines local vertical and already incorporates the static centrifugal acceleration. Neglect air resistance, gravity gradients and corrections of second order in \(\Omega t\).

The zeroth-order motion is vertical. Express the rotation vector in the local axes and place that vertical velocity in the Coriolis acceleration:

\[\boldsymbol\Omega=(0,\Omega\cos\varphi,\Omega\sin\varphi),\]
\[\mathbf v_0=(0,0,-gt),\]
\[\mathbf a_C=-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_0 .\]

The east component is positive and grows linearly in time because the fall speed is increasing. Integrating once from zero horizontal velocity gives

\[a_E(t)=2\Omega g t\cos\varphi,\]
\[v_E(t)=\Omega g t^2\cos\varphi.\]

Integrate again and eliminate the flight time using the vertical drop \(h\). The horizontal displacement at first contact with the ground is

\[x_E=\tfrac13\Omega g\cos\varphi\,(2h/g)^{3/2}.\]

Using the final acceleration for the entire fall would overestimate the result. Only the horizontal component of Earth’s rotation couples directly to vertical velocity. At the poles this contribution vanishes. Changing from northern to southern latitudes of equal magnitude leaves the eastward sign unchanged.

Worked example

Estimate the offset below a tower

Drop a compact test mass through \(h=45.0\) m in an evacuated shaft at latitude \(48.0^\circ\). With \(g=9.81\) m/s² and \(\Omega=7.292\times10^{-5}\) rad/s, flight time is \(3.03\) s. Substitution gives \(x_E=4.43\) mm. The final eastward velocity is \(4.39\) mm/s. Millimetre accuracy makes control of sideways launch velocities essential: an initial eastward drift of \(1.00\) mm/s would add another \(3.03\) mm to the measured offset.

Second application

Compare two release heights without knowing latitude

At one location, two careful drops from \(20.0\) and \(80.0\) m give eastward residuals after a constant launch drift has been removed. The formula predicts \(x_E(80)/x_E(20)=4^{3/2}=8\). A \(1.40\) mm residual in the shorter drop predicts \(11.2\) mm in the longer drop. A residual caused by a fixed unremoved sideways speed would instead scale with flight time, giving a factor of two. Varying height thus separates two effects that can look identical in one photograph.

The observer changes the acceleration

Imagine a puck launched directly outwards from the centre of a horizontal rotating table. The nearly frictionless puck moves in a straight line in the laboratory after release. To a camera fixed to the table, it moves sideways. The two descriptions refer to the same sequence of points in space and time, but their coordinate axes evolve differently. A discrepancy in reported acceleration alone does not imply that a new physical interaction acted on the puck.

Let the table rotate with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). During a small time \(dt\), a vector fixed to the table changes by \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A\,dt\) as seen from the laboratory. If a vector also changes its components relative to the table, the two contributions add. Thus \((d\mathbf A/dt)_{\rm lab}=(d\mathbf A/dt)_{\rm rot}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A\). The rule applies to any vector, including the velocity measured with the rotating basis.

Differentiate twice

Permit the rotating origin to translate with acceleration \(\mathbf a_O\). Write \(\mathbf r\) for the position relative to that origin. Differentiating the position once adds the frame velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r\). Differentiating a second time and collecting the contributions yields

\[\mathbf a_{\rm lab}=\mathbf a_O+\mathbf a_{\rm rot}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot}+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).\]

The two equal contributions to the velocity-dependent term arise from differentiating the moving basis of \(\mathbf v_{\rm rot}\) and from the rotating-frame derivative of \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r\). Dropping either loses a factor of two. To solve Newton’s equation for the rotating acceleration, transfer all frame terms to the force side with their signs reversed. For steady rotation at a fixed origin the remaining extra terms are the Coriolis and centrifugal forces.

Distinguish velocity-dependent deflection from a static load

For vertical rotation, the centrifugal force points horizontally away from the axis and has magnitude \(m\Omega^2r_\perp\). An object at rest on the rotating table experiences this term in its rotating-frame balance. In the laboratory a force directed towards the axis supplies its centripetal acceleration. Both descriptions predict the same required friction or tension. By contrast, the Coriolis force vanishes when the rotating-frame velocity is zero. When present it is perpendicular to that velocity, so its instantaneous power in the rotating frame is zero.

A static rotating liquid displays the centrifugal term directly. With gravity downwards and angular speed constant, equilibrium gives \(\partial_r p=\rho\Omega^2r\) and \(\partial_zp=-\rho g\). On the free surface the pressure is constant, hence \(dz/dr=\Omega^2r/g\). Integration gives a parabolic surface with rise \(\Omega^2R^2/(2g)\) from centre to container radius \(R\). With \(\Omega=3.0\) rad/s and \(R=0.20\) m the rise is 18.3 mm. If the container stops suddenly, the preceding static argument ceases to describe the transient flow.

Reconstruct the camera image

Let the puck leave the common origin at speed \(u=0.80\) m/s along the positive laboratory \(x\) axis when the two frames coincide. If the table turns counterclockwise at \(\Omega=0.50\) rad/s, transforming the laboratory position \((ut,0)\) gives \(x'=ut\cos(\Omega t)\) and \(y'=-ut\sin(\Omega t)\). After 0.40 s the rotating camera sees \(x'=0.314\) m and \(y'=-0.0636\) m. The puck lags behind the rotating radial line.

For \(\Omega t\ll1\) the transverse displacement is \(y'\simeq-u\Omega t^2\). Its initial acceleration is therefore \(-2u\Omega=-0.80\) m/s², just the Coriolis term. At launch the centrifugal term is zero because the puck sits at the axis. Later both frame terms contribute. Extrapolating the initial acceleration indefinitely would give a parabola, whereas the exact coordinate transformation yields the rotating trace for any elapsed time that the puck stays on the table.

A further check follows from work. The Coriolis term is perpendicular to instantaneous relative velocity, since \((\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf v')\cdot\mathbf v'=0\). It can turn a track without directly changing speed in the rotating frame. For constant angular velocity the centrifugal term can be derived from the potential \(-m|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2\). A time-dependent rotation also introduces the Euler term, whose work generally cannot be folded into that same static potential. This explains why an experiment on a spinning-up table differs from one performed at the same instantaneous angular speed after settling.

Let a pendulum carry a direction across a rotating Earth

A laboratory pendulum can reveal a slow rotation too small to see during one swing. Consider a long pendulum of small amplitude at latitude \(\varphi\). Use laboratory axes east \(x\), north \(y\) and vertically upward. The vertical component of Earth's angular velocity is \(\Omega_v=\Omega_\oplus\sin\varphi\). To first order in rotation, the horizontal Coriolis acceleration from horizontal motion involves only this component. The neglected vertical response contributes at higher order to the horizontal swing. Include the nearly constant local centrifugal contribution in the measured gravitational acceleration \(g\).

For length \(\ell\), ordinary horizontal restoring acceleration is \(-\omega_0^2(x,y)\) with \(\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}\). Resolving \(-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}\) yields \(\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=2\Omega_v\dot y\) and \(\ddot y+\omega_0^2y=-2\Omega_v\dot x\). The coupling turns velocity through a right angle. Combining the coordinates into \(z=x+iy\) converts the pair into one equation,

\[\ddot z+2i\Omega_v\dot z+\omega_0^2z=0 .\]

Substitute \(z=w e^{-i\Omega_v t}\). The terms proportional to \(\dot w\) cancel, leaving \(\ddot w+(\omega_0^2+\Omega_v^2)w=0\) within the horizontal model. To first order the motion in \(w\) is the ordinary swing. The prefactor rotates its axes in the laboratory at \(-\Omega_v\). This predicts clockwise precession when viewed from above in the northern hemisphere. The small second-order shift of swing frequency should not be extracted from this reduced model without restoring the other omitted effects of comparable order.

Plan an observation at midlatitudes

Set \(\ell=10.0\) m and \(\varphi=48.0^\circ\). A swing period is approximately \(2\pi\sqrt{10.0/9.81}=6.34\) s. In the same interval Earth turns only a small fraction of a degree. Using \(\Omega_\oplus=7.292\times10^{-5}\) rad/s gives \(\Omega_v=5.42\times10^{-5}\) rad/s. The swing axis rotates by \(0.195\) rad, or \(11.2^\circ\), in one hour. A complete precession takes about 32.2 hours. Changing the pendulum length changes the number of swings counted during that hour while leaving the ideal precession angle unchanged.

At the equator the vertical component vanishes and the first-order horizontal precession disappears. At the pole its period is one sidereal day. These limits help separate the effect from anisotropic support stiffness, which can also rotate or distort an observed swing ellipse yet has no required latitude dependence. A reproducible procedure fits the major axis over successive groups of several swings, records its slow orientation change and repeats the launch along another initial azimuth to expose a fixed preferred direction in the mount.

The sign prediction can also be checked geometrically. At the north pole the suspension point rotates with Earth beneath an almost fixed inertial oscillation axis. An observer attached to the floor sees the opposite rotation. At the south pole the local upward direction reverses relative to Earth's rotation vector, so the observed precession reverses. At a midlatitude a camera pointed down from above reads the sign used in the equations; a camera looking upward through a transparent floor records the opposite visual sense. Stating the viewing direction prevents a purely optical reversal from appearing to contradict the mechanics.

Chapter 6 · Hamiltonian motion in phase space

Construct a Hamiltonian, read a phase portrait and distinguish bounded motion from rotation at a separatrix.

From first principles

Construct Hamilton’s equations by exchanging velocity for momentum

Begin with a smooth Lagrangian \(L(q,\dot q,t)\) for a conservative mechanical system. Assume the momentum definition \(p=\partial_{\dot q}L\) can locally be inverted to give \(\dot q\) as a function of \((q,p,t)\). This regularity is what lets position and momentum serve as independent state coordinates. Define the Hamiltonian and differentiate the product before substituting any equations of motion:

\[H=p\dot q-L,\]
\[dH=\dot q\,dp+p\,d\dot q-L_q\,dq-L_{\dot q}\,d\dot q-L_t\,dt .\]

The two \(d\dot q\) terms cancel by the momentum definition. Comparing the coefficients of the independent differentials gives \(H_p=\dot q\) and \(H_q=-L_q\). Hamilton's principle previously gave the Lagrange equation \(\dot p=L_q\), so

\[\dot q=\partial_pH,\]
\[\dot p=-\partial_qH,\]
\[\partial_tH=-\partial_tL .\]

Along a solution the \(q\) and \(p\) contributions to \(dH/dt\) cancel. For a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H\) is constant, so planar trajectories lie on its level sets. In the elementary case \(L=m\dot q^2/2-U(q)\), the transformation yields \(H=p^2/(2m)+U(q)\).

Derivation and applications

How Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space area

An energy contour shows where a single trajectory may lie. To learn what the dynamics does to nearby initial conditions, follow a small patch of the canonical \((q,p)\) plane. Consider one degree of freedom and a twice continuously differentiable Hamiltonian. Let the system advance by a short time \(\delta t\). To first order the coordinate map is a displacement along the Hamiltonian velocity field:

\[q'=q+H_p\,\delta t,\]
\[p'=p-H_q\,\delta t .\]

The area of a sufficiently small parallelogram changes by the determinant of this map's derivative. Differentiate with respect to both initial coordinates. The product of off-diagonal elements enters only at second order:

\[\det\frac{\partial(q',p')}{\partial(q,p)}=(1+H_{pq}\delta t)(1-H_{qp}\delta t)+H_{pp}H_{qq}\delta t^2=1+O(\delta t^2).\]

For the exact continuous map its area rate is the divergence of the phase velocity. Equality of the mixed second derivatives gives

\[\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=\partial_q\dot q+\partial_p\dot p=H_{pq}-H_{qp}=0\]

for an infinitesimal material area \(A\). Integrating adjacent patches gives the same conclusion for a finite region. The outline can stretch and rotate. Even explicit time dependence of \(H\) does not change this volume result. A friction force imposed independently of the Hamiltonian can change it. The second-order remainder above belongs to a short expansion; repeatedly taking finite steps with an approximate rule can accumulate a spurious change.

Worked example

Track a cloud in a harmonic trap

Prepare a small rectangular set of initial states, \(2.00\) mm wide in position and \(0.0100\) kg m/s wide in momentum. Its area is \(2.00\times10^{-5}\) J s. In a trap of mass \(0.500\) kg and frequency \(\omega=4.00\) rad/s, the exact quarter-period map is \(q'=p/(m\omega)\), \(p'=-m\omega q\). The new widths are \(5.00\) mm and \(0.00400\) kg m/s. Their product retains the original area even though neither width is conserved.

Second application

Diagnose a numerical phase portrait

An explicit Euler step in the same trap uses \(q'=q+p\delta t/m\) and \(p'=p-m\omega^2q\delta t\). Its determinant is exactly \(1+\omega^2\delta t^2\). At \(\delta t=0.0500\) s it expands area by \(1.04\) per step, giving \(1.04^{50}=7.11\) times the initial area after 50 steps. A graph of the growing cloud could look like physical heating. The determinant exposes a discretization error without requiring an exact trajectory for each point.

Position needs a conjugate momentum

The position of a pendulum at one instant does not say which way it will swing. A state needs both a configuration coordinate and a momentum. In Lagrangian mechanics define the canonical momentum by \(p=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q\). It can have different physical dimensions depending on the coordinate. For a particle on a line it has units of linear momentum. For a pendulum angle it has units of angular momentum. The pair \((q,p)\) forms a two-dimensional phase space for one degree of freedom.

Provided the relation between \(p\) and \(\dot q\) can be inverted, define \(H(q,p,t)=p\dot q-\mathcal L(q,\dot q,t)\) with the velocity expressed in terms of \(q,p\). Take a differential. The terms proportional to \(d\dot q\) cancel by the definition of \(p\). Using Lagrange’s equation \(\partial_q\mathcal L=\dot p\) gives \(dH=\dot q\,dp-\dot p\,dq-\partial_t\mathcal L\,dt\). Matching the coefficients produces Hamilton’s equations

\[\dot q=\partial_p H,\qquad \dot p=-\partial_q H.\]

For a system with ordinary quadratic kinetic energy, a velocity-independent potential and fixed constraints, \(H=K+U\). Its change along a solution is \(dH/dt=\partial_tH\) because the two terms containing the derivatives with respect to \(q,p\) cancel. A time-independent Hamiltonian confines the trajectory to a level set.

Use Poisson brackets to test conservation

Hamilton's two equations specify how \(q_i\) and \(p_i\) change. To follow an observable \(A(q,p,t)\), apply the chain rule and substitute \(\dot q_i=\partial H/\partial p_i\) and \(\dot p_i=-\partial H/\partial q_i\). This defines the Poisson bracket,

\[\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+\{A,H\},\qquad \{A,B\}=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial B}{\partial q_i}\right).\]

Since \(\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}\), a candidate change of phase-space coordinates must preserve these brackets to be canonical. A vanishing bracket with \(H\) certifies conservation for an observable having no explicit time dependence. It is a local calculation and does not require solving an orbit.

For a particle in a two-dimensional trap set \(H=(p_x^2+p_y^2)/(2m)+(k_xx^2+k_yy^2)/2\) and \(L_z=xp_y-yp_x\). The momentum derivatives in the bracket cancel the two kinetic contributions. The remaining coordinate terms give \(\dot L_z=(k_x-k_y)xy\), exactly the torque \(xF_y-yF_x\). With \(k_x=4.00\) N/m, \(k_y=6.00\) N/m and instantaneous position \((0.030,0.040)\) m, angular momentum decreases at \(2.40\times10^{-3}\) N m. At a point on either axis the instantaneous derivative vanishes even for the anisotropic trap. For a conservation law holding on every trajectory the expression must vanish throughout phase space, as it does when \(k_x=k_y\).

Draw energy contours before integrating

A rigid pendulum of mass \(m\) and length \(\ell\) has \(p=m\ell^2\dot q\) and \(H=p^2/(2m\ell^2)+mg\ell(1-\cos q)\). At the bottom \(q=0,p=0\) is a stable equilibrium. Low-energy contours are ellipses to leading order because the cosine potential is quadratic there. Each closed contour returns to its original phase point after one full oscillation. Points of greatest \(|p|\) are fastest and occur at the bottom.

The top \(q=\pi,p=0\) has energy \(2mg\ell\). Its contour separates swinging trajectories from trajectories that keep rotating in one direction. This separating curve is the separatrix. Since angles differing by \(2\pi\) label the same physical position, the full portrait lives on a cylinder. A rotation winds around that cylinder. As a trajectory approaches the separatrix, it spends a long time near the unstable top, so the small-angle period no longer estimates its return time accurately.

Compare thresholds and phase velocities

For \(m=0.50\) kg, \(\ell=0.80\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s², the potential barrier is 7.85 J. A start from the bottom at 5.0 rad/s supplies kinetic energy \(0.5(0.50)(0.80)^2(5.0)^2=4.00\) J. At the turning point \(\cos q=1-4.00/(0.50\times9.81\times0.80)=-0.0194\). The maximum angle is about 91.1°. Energy classifies this as a swing even though a small-angle expansion would be inaccurate at the turning point.

Continuous rotation requires bottom speed above \(\sqrt{4g/\ell}=7.00\) rad/s for this ideal pendulum. At precisely that value the system approaches the upper equilibrium asymptotically. This statement assumes a rigid link that can sustain compression. A pendulum made from a flexible string may lose tension before reaching the top, in which case the assumed circular constraint fails.

Check a numerical phase portrait

For any smooth one-degree Hamiltonian the divergence of the phase velocity is \(\partial_q\dot q+\partial_p\dot p=H_{pq}-H_{qp}=0\). A small patch of initial states can shear and stretch while preserving its phase-space area. Viscous damping would instead contract the patch. Numerical time integration can also produce apparent contraction or growth, so refining the step is essential before interpreting a spiral as evidence of friction.

For the harmonic oscillator, ordinary forward Euler gives \(q'=q+\Delta t\,p/m\) and \(p'=p-\Delta t\,kq\). The determinant of this linear update is \(1+\Delta t^2 k/m\), larger than one at every step. The spurious outward spiral follows from the numerical map alone. Updating momentum first and then using the new momentum for the position gives determinant one. That area property preserves the geometric structure, though it does not by itself guarantee accurate phase for a coarse step. Keep both geometry and timing in view when judging numerical trajectories.

The area inside a closed harmonic-oscillator contour provides another numerical test. Its semiaxes are \(\sqrt{2E/k}\) in position and \(\sqrt{2mE}\) in momentum. Their product times \(\pi\) gives \(2\pi E/\omega\). Increasing oscillator energy therefore expands the area in direct proportion. A phase portrait plotted using velocity replaces every momentum value by its value divided by mass, changing numerical areas and their units. Specify the vertical coordinate before comparing this diagnostic between models with different masses.

A slowly changing spring transports a phase-space area

Suppose an oscillator's spring stiffness can be varied externally during the motion. Its Hamiltonian \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega(t)^2q^2/2\) now depends explicitly on time, so ordinary energy conservation does not apply. At an instantaneous point on a trajectory Hamilton's equations make the terms involving \(\dot p\) and \(\dot q\) cancel in \(dH/dt\). The remaining change is \(dE/dt=\partial H/\partial t=m\omega\dot\omega q^2\). Energy is supplied when the imposed parameter raises the potential at the current displacement.

Take \(|\dot\omega|\ll\omega^2\) so that many cycles occur while the frequency changes by an appreciable fraction. Over one cycle \(\omega\) and \(E\) may then be treated as nearly fixed. Because average potential and kinetic energies of a harmonic oscillator are equal, \(\langle q^2\rangle=E/(m\omega^2)\). Inserting this mean into the work rate gives the averaged relation

\[\frac{dE}{dt}\simeq\frac{E}{\omega}\dot\omega,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac E\omega\right)\simeq0 .\]

The quantity \(J=E/\omega\) is the area enclosed by the instantaneous constant-energy ellipse divided by \(2\pi\). Calling it an adiabatic invariant states both the approximation and the observable. A slowly changing system migrates between contours of different energies in a way that preserves this action to leading order. This mechanical meaning of adiabatic concerns a timescale ratio. The derivation does not assume heat exchange, dissipation or a thermodynamic temperature.

Compare a slow compression with a sudden change

A \(0.500\) kg oscillator has initial angular frequency \(20.0\) rad/s and amplitude \(0.0400\) m. Its energy is \(m\omega_i^2 A_i^2/2=0.160\) J and initial action is \(0.00800\) J s. Raise the frequency smoothly to \(40.0\) rad/s over many periods. Action conservation predicts \(E_f=0.320\) J and final amplitude \(\sqrt{2E_f/(m\omega_f^2)}=0.0283\) m. The external control supplies work even as the maximum displacement decreases.

For comparison, change the stiffness instantaneously at a turning point, retaining the same initial and final frequencies. Position and momentum are continuous, but the potential energy at that position quadruples immediately. The new energy is \(0.640\) J and the amplitude remains \(0.0400\) m. Make the same switch at the zero crossing instead and the energy initially remains \(0.160\) J because the potential vanishes there. These two outcomes distinguish a rapid change tied to an oscillator phase from the nearly phase-independent slow ramp. Recording both position and velocity after the ramp allows energy, action and phase to be assessed separately.

A time-dependent Hamiltonian still carries each small phase-space area element into one of equal area, even during a sudden change of curvature. This exact conservation for an ensemble is distinct from slow conservation of the contour area assigned to a single orbit. A group of uniformly distributed initial phases illustrates the difference: immediately after a sudden spring switch all points occupy the same locations and enclose their original region, but their new energies depend on phase. The switch maps the original single-energy contour onto points that subsequently occupy many different energy contours of the new Hamiltonian.

Chapter 7 · Two-body motion and binary masses

Use the motion of both members of a binary to recover the total mass and distinguish the relative orbit from the two individual orbits.

From first principles

Reduce two gravitational accelerations to one observed orbit

Consider two point masses that interact only through Newtonian gravity. The measured relative separation vector is \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1\) with magnitude \(r\). Newton’s gravitational force law is the model input; division by each body's own inertial mass gives opposite accelerations of generally different magnitudes:

\[\ddot{\mathbf r}_1=Gm_2\mathbf r/r^3,\]
\[\ddot{\mathbf r}_2=-Gm_1\mathbf r/r^3 .\]

Subtract these equations to find the relative acceleration and add the mass-weighted equations to establish uniform centre-of-mass motion:

\[\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{G(m_1+m_2)}{r^3}\mathbf r,\]
\[(m_1+m_2)\ddot{\mathbf R}_{\rm cm}=0 .\]

The axial torque about the focus is zero, so \(\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}\) is constant. For a circular relative orbit of radius \(a\), the inward acceleration has magnitude \(a(2\pi/P)^2\). Equality with gravity yields

\[P^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{G(m_1+m_2)} .\]

For elliptic motion the same result uses the relative semimajor axis; it follows by combining constant areal speed with the ellipse’s area and angular momentum. Observing only one component's orbit gives its distance from the centre of mass, which is generally smaller than the required relative orbit.

Derivation and applications

Convert an orbital position into a clock reading

The conic equation tells us where the companion can be seen, but not when it passes a particular position. Work in the relative orbit of an isolated Newtonian binary. Let \(a\) denote its semimajor axis, \(e<1\) its eccentricity, and \(b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\) its semiminor axis. Fix the origin at the occupied focus and the positive \(x\) axis toward pericentre. Parametrize the ellipse by eccentric anomaly \(u\) using a circle stretched along one axis:

\[x=a(\cos u-e),\]
\[y=b\sin u,\]
\[r=a(1-e\cos u).\]

The parameter \(u\) is measured on the auxiliary circle; it usually differs from the observed direction \(\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\). A short sector about the focus has oriented area \((x\,dy-y\,dx)/2\). Differentiating the parametrization gives the area swept since pericentre:

\[dA=\tfrac12ab(1-e\cos u)\,du,\]
\[A(u)=\tfrac12ab(u-e\sin u).\]

The central gravitational force produces zero torque, so area is swept at a constant rate. A full period \(P\) sweeps the entire ellipse \(\pi ab\). The fraction of a period elapsed since pericentre is therefore \(A(u)/(\pi ab)\):

\[\frac{2\pi(t-t_p)}{P}=u-e\sin u .\]

This is Kepler’s equation, where \(t_p\) is the time of pericentre. Its derivative with respect to \(u\) is \(1-e\cos u>0\), so there is a unique position within one revolution for every time. The circular limit \(e=0\) makes anomaly grow uniformly; the distinction between an auxiliary-circle angle and the observed bearing then disappears.

Worked example

Time the crossing of the semiminor-axis endpoint

Take a visual binary with \(P=8.00\) yr and \(e=0.600\). At the upper endpoint of the minor axis, \(u=\pi/2\), and \(t-t_p=8(\pi/2-0.600)/(2\pi)=1.24\) yr. Exactly one quarter of the geometric parameter range has passed, but the time is considerably shorter than two years. Relative to the focus this endpoint has coordinates \((-0.600a,0.800a)\) and distance \(a\).

Second application

Recover an orbital eccentricity from timed astrometry

A second binary reaches the same geometric point \(2.10\) yr after pericentre in a \(10.0\) yr orbit. Using \(u=\pi/2\) gives \(e=\pi/2-2\pi(2.10/10.0)=0.251\). The implied ratio of apocentre distance to pericentre distance is \((1+e)/(1-e)=1.67\). A resolved relative orbit with that ratio gives an independent geometric test of the timing determination.

Remove the motion of the whole system

Two stars pull on each other. Fixing one star at the origin would be a poor approximation for comparable masses, but the central-force calculation can still be made exact within Newtonian gravity. Take masses \(m_1,m_2\), inertial positions \(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2\) and relative vector \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1\). Assume spherical bodies or sufficiently large separation that departures from point-mass gravity can be neglected. Internal forces cancel when the equations of motion are added. With no external forces, the centre of mass moves uniformly.

Subtract the two accelerations instead. The relative coordinate obeys \(\ddot{\mathbf r}=-G(m_1+m_2)\mathbf r/r^3\). The familiar one-centre orbit therefore applies to the separation vector if the gravitational parameter uses the total mass. In the centre-of-mass frame the individual positions are \(\mathbf r_1=-(m_2/M)\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf r_2=(m_1/M)\mathbf r\), with \(M=m_1+m_2\). The bodies reach closest approach together while each describes its own scaled ellipse. Confusing either individual semimajor axis with the relative semimajor axis changes the mass inferred from timing.

Account for the relative energy

Write each velocity in terms of the centre-of-mass velocity and \(\dot{\mathbf r}\). Their kinetic energies separate into \(M V^2/2+\mu_r|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2\), where \(\mu_r=m_1m_2/M\) and the squared speed is the full squared relative velocity. The interaction energy is \(-Gm_1m_2/r\). Dividing the relative energy by \(\mu_r\) recovers the specific potential \(-GM/r\). For a bound relative ellipse, its semimajor axis \(a\) fixes the relative orbital energy at \(-Gm_1m_2/(2a)\). The energy of the centre of mass can be changed by a uniform frame change without changing any internal separation or phase.

A complete relative orbit sweeps area \(\pi a^2\sqrt{1-e^2}\). With constant areal rate and the inverse-square conic relation this gives \(P^2=4\pi^2a^3/(GM)\). Two independent measurements, physical semimajor axis and period, determine the sum of the masses. A third measurement, such as the two individual orbit sizes or speeds in the centre-of-mass frame, determines their ratio.

Read a visual binary

Suppose the relative orbit is circular with radius 2.00 astronomical units and period 2.00 years. In units where a one-year circular orbit at one astronomical unit corresponds approximately to one solar mass, the total mass is \(2^3/2^2=2.00\) solar masses. If the more massive component moves on a circle of radius 0.50 astronomical units, the other radius is 1.50 astronomical units. The mass ratio is inverse to the radii, so the components have masses 1.50 and 0.50 solar masses.

A telescope measures an angular separation on the sky; obtaining lengths requires a distance and an orbital inclination model. In a spectroscopic binary each Doppler amplitude measures a projected speed, so their ratio can reveal the mass ratio even before the inclination is known. An independent inclination is needed to turn those projected speeds into absolute masses. This illustrates why a clean period estimate can coexist with a substantial mass uncertainty. For a nearly negligible secondary mass the same relative equation underlies the orbit experiment.

Derive the shape from angular momentum and the force

The period–mass law uses a bound orbit's overall dimensions. A central-force observation can also recover the shape locally. In the relative two-body problem write \(h=r^2\dot\theta\) for the specific angular momentum and \(\mu_g=G(m_1+m_2)\). The radial equation is \(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=-\mu_g/r^2\). Introduce the reciprocal radius \(u=1/r\) and differentiate with respect to the polar angle instead of time. Because \(\dot\theta=h u^2\), the radial velocity becomes \(\dot r=-h\,du/d\theta\). Differentiating once more gives \(\ddot r=-h^2u^2d^2u/d\theta^2\). The geometric acceleration term is \(r\dot\theta^2=h^2u^3\). Combining the terms leaves a linear equation despite the nonlinear time evolution,

\[\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}+u=\frac{\mu_g}{h^2},\qquad r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos(\theta-\theta_p)},\qquad p=\frac{h^2}{\mu_g}.\]

Two integration constants determine the eccentricity and the direction of closest approach. Choose that direction as zero angle. In a bound ellipse the minimum and maximum radii are \(r_p=p/(1+e)\) and \(r_a=p/(1-e)\). Solving these two geometric equations gives \(e=(r_a-r_p)/(r_a+r_p)\) and \(p=2r_ar_p/(r_a+r_p)\). Their arithmetic mean is the semimajor axis. This construction separates what can be inferred from images alone from what requires a gravitational mass or a time measurement.

Reconstruct speed away from either turning point

Suppose a resolved relative orbit has nearest separation 2.00 AU and farthest 6.00 AU. Use an independently known \(\mu_g=4\pi^2\) AU³/yr². The semimajor axis is 4.00 AU, eccentricity \(0.500\) and semilatus rectum 3.00 AU. Specific angular momentum is \(\sqrt{\mu_gp}=10.88\) AU²/yr, so at right angles to periapsis the separation is 3.00 AU and transverse velocity is \(h/r=3.63\) AU/yr.

The orbit is still moving outward there. Differentiating the reciprocal-radius solution gives \(\dot r=(\mu_g/h)e\sin\theta=1.81\) AU/yr. Its total relative speed is \(\sqrt{3.63^2+1.81^2}=4.06\) AU/yr, about 19.2 km/s. Independently the energy relation predicts \(v^2=\mu_g(2/r-1/a)=16.45\) AU²/yr², consistent to rounding. An image sequence can test the transverse speed and the outward sign separately. If the two measured turning radii coincide the eccentricity vanishes, the reconstructed radial velocity is zero at every angle and the orientation \(\theta_p\) becomes unobservable. Nearly circular orbit fits therefore determine a radius more reliably than a direction of periapsis.

The time information missing from a still image can be recovered by integrating the areal law. An area element swept from the focus is \(dA=r^2d\theta/2\); its rate is therefore \(h/2\). The total ellipse area is \(\pi ab\), with semiminor axis \(b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\). For the reconstructed orbit \(b=3.46\) AU and the swept area is \(43.5\) AU² each revolution. Dividing by \(h/2=5.44\) AU²/yr yields a period of \(8.00\) years, independently matching the orbital-period relation from the semimajor axis. Equal angle intervals around the orbit generally correspond to very unequal time intervals because swept areas also depend on separation.

Chapter 8 · Impact parameters and scattering cross-sections

Use flux conservation to turn a classical deflection law into an angular distribution observed by a detector.

From first principles

Turn an incident annulus into a measured angular distribution

Assume a uniform incident number flux \(\mathcal F\) on one central scatterer. The trajectories vary only by azimuth and by their initial perpendicular offset \(b\). A ring of incoming impact parameters between \(b\) and \(b+db\) has area \(2\pi b\,db\). Thus the number of events entering that interval per time is

\[d\dot N=\mathcal F\,2\pi b\,|db| .\]

For an axisymmetric interaction the corresponding polar exit angles \(\theta\) to \(\theta+d\theta\) fill an angular band of solid angle \(d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,|d\theta|\). Equate the rate from the entrance to the rate reported by a detector normalized to flux and solid angle:

\[\mathcal F\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}2\pi\sin\theta\,|d\theta|=\mathcal F\,2\pi b\,|db|,\]
\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|.\]

This is a change of variables based on flux conservation, not a force law. The actual interaction supplies \(b(\theta)\). If multiple impact parameters map into one angular band, add their positive contributions. A finite detector averages this distribution over its acceptance, which matters near a sharp angular feature.

Derivation and applications

Which scattering cross section measures a loss of directed momentum?

A detector counting encounters adds one event in every accepted direction. A force measurement weights the same encounters according to how much directed momentum each removes. Consider a dilute parallel stream of identical particles of speed \(v\) and mass \(m\) scattered elastically by isolated, stationary, much heavier centres. The incoming direction defines \(z\). The outgoing speed is unchanged, and assume axial symmetry so that transverse recoils cancel in the ensemble. Let \(\mathcal F\) be the incident number per area per time.

The outgoing momentum projection is \(mv\cos\theta\) for deflection angle \(\theta\). Multiply the lost projection by the differential encounter rate in the detector’s solid angle:

\[\Delta p_z=mv(1-\cos\theta),\]
\[d\dot N=\mathcal F\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega .\]

Summing over all azimuths, with \(d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\), defines the momentum-transfer cross section \(\sigma_{\rm tr}\) and the longitudinal force on one centre:

\[F_z=\mathcal F\,mv\,\sigma_{\rm tr},\]
\[\sigma_{\rm tr}=\int(1-\cos\theta)\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega .\]

For a smooth monotone deflection function \(\theta(b)\), the annular area of incident trajectories is \(2\pi b\,db\). Changing variables back to impact parameter \(b\) shows exactly which trajectories contribute:

\[\sigma_{\rm tr}=2\pi\int b\,[1-\cos\theta(b)]\,db.\]

Use the sum of all annuli if the same angle is reached from several impact parameters. Forward scattering transfers almost no longitudinal momentum. Exact backscattering transfers twice the incident longitudinal momentum, even though it still counts as a single encounter.

Worked example

Compute the momentum flux for elastic spheres

For a hard sphere of effective contact radius \(R\), specular reflection gives \(\cos\theta=2(b/R)^2-1\) for \(0\le b\le R\). The integral is \(4\pi\int_0^R[b-b^3/R^2]db=\pi R^2\). For \(R=1.00\) mm, \(\mathcal F=2.00\times10^7\) m⁻²s⁻¹, \(m=1.00\times10^{-9}\) kg and \(v=2.00\) m/s, the encounter rate is \(62.8\) s⁻¹ and the mean force is \(1.26\times10^{-7}\) N.

Second application

Compare two angular distributions with identical event counts

Suppose an angular histogram is fitted by \(d\sigma/d\Omega=C(1+\alpha\cos\theta)\), with \(|\alpha|\le1\). Its integrated count is \(4\pi C\) for every \(\alpha\). Averaging the momentum factor uses \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\) on a sphere, giving \(\sigma_{\rm tr}=4\pi C(1-\alpha/3)\). For \(\alpha=0.900\), the same count produces only \(0.700\) of the force for an isotropic distribution. An encounter count alone cannot determine a transport coefficient.

Specify an ensemble of trajectories

A beam of dilute particles approaches a stationary scattering centre. Particles enter parallel to one another with the same speed, but at different perpendicular offsets from the centre. This offset \(b\), measured on the incoming asymptotic straight line, is the impact parameter. For a central potential each trajectory remains in one plane; axial symmetry makes its polar deflection angle depend on \(b\) rather than on the azimuth of the initial offset. Neglect collisions between incoming particles and assume a sufficiently heavy centre that recoil can be ignored.

The cross-section has dimensions of area. It quantifies how a known incident flux becomes counts in a detector. If an incoming beam supplies \(j\) particles per unit area per second, an annulus of impact parameters from \(b\) to \(b+db\) contributes \(j\,2\pi b\,db\) particles per second to the corresponding outgoing angles. A large response can arise either from a large interval of impact parameters or from trajectories compressed into a narrow angular interval.

Derive the transformation to detector angles

For a single monotonic deflection branch, a ring of outgoing directions between \(\theta\) and \(\theta+d\theta\) subtends \(d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\). Equating the incoming count and the outgoing count yields

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|.\]

The absolute value matters because stronger deflections often correspond to smaller impact parameters. If several distinct impact parameters produce the same angle, their contributions must be added. Multiplying by a detector’s solid angle is justified when its angular aperture is small enough that the differential cross-section is nearly constant across it.

Test the construction on a hard sphere

Take an impenetrable smooth sphere of radius \(R\) that reflects incident point particles elastically. At a surface hit the normal component of velocity reverses while the tangential component stays fixed. A central hit returns straight back and a grazing hit barely deflects. The geometry of the contact normal gives \(b=R\cos(\theta/2)\). Differentiation and \(\sin\theta=2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)\) give \(d\sigma/d\Omega=R^2/4\). Integrating over all \(4\pi\) steradians recovers \(\pi R^2\), the area presented to the incoming parallel beam.

Although the angular density is isotropic in this classical example, the trajectories are deterministic. Isotropy results from the area-to-angle map. A detector covering a fixed span of polar angle but all azimuths receives different counts near the pole and near the equator because its solid angle changes.

Calculate detector counts

Let \(R=2.0\) mm, \(j=1.0\times10^9\) m⁻²s⁻¹ and a small detector subtend 0.020 sr in a clear outgoing direction. The differential cross-section is \(1.0\times10^{-6}\) m²/sr. The expected rate is \(j(d\sigma/d\Omega)\Delta\Omega=20\) s⁻¹. Integrating over angles beyond 90° gives half the total count, or \(j\pi R^2/2=6.28\times10^3\) s⁻¹. Only incoming offsets below \(R/\sqrt2\) enter this backward hemisphere.

Soft interactions can produce very different distributions. In particular, a force reaching far outside any material body can scatter particles arriving at offsets larger than the body radius. The hard-sphere area no longer supplies a universal total cross-section. Detector acceptance and the minimum observable angle then become part of the measurement definition.

Attraction enlarges the area that meets a body

An astronomical body can collect small incoming particles from a larger area than its apparent disk. Consider a stationary spherical body of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\) with vacuum outside. Small particles arrive from far away with common speed \(v_\infty\) and an approximately uniform distribution across their beam. Assume negligible particle mass, no drag, no interactions among particles and an absorbing surface. Gravity changes which incident trajectories reach the surface while conserving each particle’s energy and angular momentum until its first contact.

At infinity the specific angular momentum is \(h=bv_\infty\) for impact parameter \(b\). The trajectory that just grazes the surface is tangent there, so \(h=Rv_p\), where \(v_p\) is its speed at closest approach. Energy conservation between infinity and the grazing point gives \(v_p^2=v_\infty^2+2GM/R\). Eliminating \(v_p\) supplies the critical impact parameter and capture cross section

\[b_{\max}^2=R^2\left(1+\frac{v_{\rm esc}^2}{v_\infty^2}\right),\qquad \sigma_{\rm hit}=\pi b_{\max}^2,\qquad v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}.\]

The incident area element is measured in the undisturbed beam at infinity. Particles with \(b<R\) obviously hit; some initially farther from the centre do as well. Very fast particles hardly change direction before passing the body, giving the geometric area in that limit. Very slow incident particles experience strong focusing, and the idealized cross section grows as \(v_\infty^{-2}\). For extremely small speeds the assumed uniform, isolated far-field beam can cease to be physically meaningful because other gravitating masses perturb it.

Compare two incoming populations

A spherical minor body has radius \(R=100\) km and escape speed \(v_{\rm esc}=100\) m/s. Its implied gravitational parameter is \(GM=v_{\rm esc}^2R/2=5.00\times10^8\) m³/s². A population approaching at 50.0 m/s has \(b_{\max}=R\sqrt{1+4}=224\) km. The collection area is \(1.57\times10^{11}\) m², five times the projected solid disk. The speed at grazing contact is \(\sqrt{50^2+100^2}=112\) m/s, and the angular momenta on the two sides agree.

For a second population at 200 m/s the enhancement is only \(1.25\), giving \(3.93\times10^{10}\) m². At equal incident particle flux per square metre the slower population therefore supplies four times as many impacts. At equal far-field number density, however, flux itself is proportional to speed: multiplying each cross section by its own speed makes the two collection rates equal for these particular values. This distinction matters when comparing an instrument that measures incident counts per unit area with a model that reports only a space density.

This collection calculation can be recast as a minimum-angle criterion in a numerical trajectory experiment. Launch particles far upstream at specified offsets and find which trajectories reach the radius of the body before turning away. To estimate a cross section with equally weighted trajectories, sample radius with cumulative probability proportional to its square. Uniform sampling of the impact parameter instead overrepresents rays near the axis. If that sampling is used, weight each ray by the area of its annulus. Convergence of the measured grazing offset and convergence of the weighted hit fraction test distinct aspects of the calculation.

Chapter 9 · Principal axes and torque-free precession

Use the inertia tensor to distinguish rotation about a principal axis from the precession of a freely rotating symmetric body.

From first principles

Build the inertia tensor from the momenta of particles in a rigid body

Choose the centre of mass as origin and consider a rigid body of fixed mass distribution. Its instantaneous internal velocity field is \(\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\). Starting from angular momentum as the moment of linear momentum, integrate over small masses. The vector triple-product identity separates the part parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\) from a direction-dependent subtraction:

\[\mathbf L=\int\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dm=\int[r^2\boldsymbol\omega-(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)\mathbf r]\,dm .\]

Reading off coefficients of the components of \(\boldsymbol\omega\) defines the symmetric inertia tensor. It is diagonal only in appropriately chosen principal axes:

\[L_i=\sum_j I_{ij}\omega_j,\]
\[I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_i r_j)\,dm .\]

Torque gives the laboratory time derivative of angular momentum. In a body-fixed principal basis the tensor components are constant but the basis rotates. Applying the rotating-vector derivative yields, for the first component,

\[\tau_1=I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3 .\]

Cyclic permutation gives the others. Even zero torque can change the body-frame components of angular velocity. Alignment of angular velocity and angular momentum holds about a principal direction, or about every direction for spherical inertia.

Derivation and applications

Find a pivot that gives a specified physical-pendulum period

An extended body hanging from a pivot rarely oscillates like a point at its centre of mass. Take a rigid body of mass \(M\) with centre of mass a distance \(d\) below a horizontal, frictionless axis. Let \(I_C\) be its moment of inertia about the parallel axis through the centre of mass. Measure angle \(\theta\) from vertically downward. For each mass element, decompose its perpendicular displacement from the pivot into the centre-of-mass offset and a displacement relative to the centre. The cross term integrates to zero by definition of the centre:

\[I_O=\int r_\perp^2\,dm=I_C+Md^2 .\]

Gravity is equivalent to a force \(Mg\) at the centre, so its torque depends only on \(d\). Angular momentum about the fixed pivot is \(I_O\dot\theta\) along the rotation axis. The angular equation, followed by its small-angle limit, is

\[(I_C+Md^2)\ddot\theta=-Mgd\sin\theta,\]
\[\omega^2=\frac{Mgd}{I_C+Md^2}.\]

Define the radius of gyration \(k_C=\sqrt{I_C/M}\). A simple pendulum with the same period has effective length \(\ell_{\rm eff}=g/\omega^2\). Unlike the actual distance \(d\), this length includes the cost of rotating the mass distribution about its centre:

\[T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{\rm eff}}g},\]
\[\ell_{\rm eff}=d+\frac{k_C^2}{d}.\]

Moving the pivot toward the centre eventually increases the period because the torque vanishes faster than the inertia. Differentiating the effective length gives \(d\ell_{\rm eff}/dd=1-k_C^2/d^2\), so the shortest period occurs at \(d=k_C\) if the apparatus admits that pivot.

Worked example

Compare two pivot positions on a uniform bar

A thin \(0.900\) m uniform bar oscillates in a vertical plane. Integration of \(x^2\,dm\) along its length gives \(k_C^2=L^2/12=0.0675\) m². Pivoting at the end gives \(d=0.450\) m and \(\ell_{\rm eff}=0.600\) m, hence \(T=1.55\) s for \(g=9.81\) m/s². Pivoting \(0.150\) m from the centre also gives \(0.150+0.0675/0.150=0.600\) m. The two periods agree even though the gravitational torque coefficients differ by a factor of three.

Second application

Infer an unknown inertia from equal periods

Two distinct pivot distances \(d_1=0.0800\) m and \(d_2=0.180\) m give the same measured period for a \(0.500\) kg body. Equating the effective lengths and canceling \(d_1-d_2\) yields \(k_C^2=d_1d_2=0.0144\) m². Thus \(I_C=0.00720\) kg m², and the common effective length is \(d_1+d_2=0.260\) m. A predicted period of \(1.02\) s checks the inference independently of the inertia measurement.

Describe rotation around a moving centre

Take a rigid body with a freely moving centre of mass and no applied torque about that centre. Remove the uniform translation and label material positions \(\mathbf r\) relative to the centre. Each material point has velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\). Integrating \(\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dm\) gives \(\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega\), where \(I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_i r_j)dm\). The inertia tensor is symmetric, so a set of orthogonal body axes diagonalises it.

In its principal axes the rotational kinetic energy is \(\sum_i I_i\omega_i^2/2\). This expression follows by integrating the kinetic energy of individual mass elements. A body containing mass far from an axis usually has large moment of inertia about that axis. Unless the components seeing different moments vanish, angular momentum and angular velocity point in different directions. A body can therefore rotate freely even when the angular velocity changes direction relative to painted marks on its surface.

Apply the moving-frame derivative

The laboratory angular momentum is constant in the absence of torque. Its components in the body basis satisfy \((d\mathbf L/dt)_{\rm body}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L=0\). For axial symmetry put \(I_1=I_2=I_\perp\) and call \(I_3\) the moment about the symmetry axis. Then \(\dot\omega_3=0\), \(\dot\omega_1=-(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_2\omega_3/I_\perp\) and \(\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_1\omega_3/I_\perp\). These last two equations describe uniform rotation of the pair of transverse components in body coordinates.

The signed angular rate of that rotation is \(\Omega_b=(I_3-I_\perp)\omega_3/I_\perp\). A body-mounted sensor sees its transverse spin vector execute a circle, even though a laboratory observer sees a constant angular-momentum vector. For a spherical inertia tensor the transverse components also become constant. The possible motion then simplifies because \(\mathbf L\) is parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\) for any spin direction.

Predict what two observers see

The symmetry-axis unit vector \(\mathbf e_3\) has laboratory derivative \(\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_3\). Express the angular velocity as \(\mathbf L/I_\perp\) plus a term parallel to \(\mathbf e_3\), which drops out of the cross product. The result is \(\dot{\mathbf e}_3=(\mathbf L/I_\perp)\times\mathbf e_3\). Thus in the laboratory the symmetry axis sweeps around fixed \(\mathbf L\) at rate \(L/I_\perp\) and constant tilt. This rate differs from the spin-vector rotation observed in body coordinates because the two measurements track different vectors relative to different frames.

Let \(I_\perp=0.020\) kg·m², \(I_3=0.030\) kg·m², \(\omega_3=40\) rad/s and initial transverse angular speed 6.0 rad/s. The body-frame rate is 20 rad/s. The magnitude of momentum is \(\sqrt{(0.020\times6)^2+(0.030\times40)^2}=1.206\) kg·m²/s. Laboratory precession is about 60.3 rad/s. The tilt obeys \(\tan\theta=0.120/1.20=0.10\), giving 5.71°. Two cameras recording these distinct angular rates need not disagree on the mechanics.

For three distinct moments the two transverse equations no longer form this simple uniform circle. The stability of individual principal-axis spins then becomes an additional question. Recording both energy and angular momentum helps discriminate conservative tumbling from external bearing drag.

One can also infer a moment of inertia without opening the rotor. Measure angular momentum by applying a calibrated torque for a known duration and integrating the torque impulse. During free motion, track the body axis with an external camera and find its precession frequency about the fixed angular momentum. For the symmetric rotor their ratio estimates the transverse moment \(I_1\). A second measurement of the axial component of angular momentum and spin gives \(I_3\).

Balance torque and angular momentum in a heavy top

Support a symmetric top at one fixed point on its symmetry axis, a distance \(\ell\) below its centre of mass when upright. Unlike free rotation about the centre, gravity now exerts a torque about the pivot. Use principal moments about the pivot, \(I_1=I_2\) and \(I_3\), and let \(\theta\) be the tilt from upward vertical. In steady precession \(\theta\) is constant, the azimuth increases at rate \(\Omega\), and the body’s total angular-velocity component along its symmetry axis is \(\omega_3=\dot\psi+\Omega\cos\theta\).

The rotational kinetic energy in Euler angles is \(I_1(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)/2+I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2/2\). Insert \(U=mg\ell\cos\theta\) into the \(\theta\) equation, differentiating before imposing constant tilt. For \(\sin\theta\ne0\) this yields

\[I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+mg\ell=0.\]

The slower root for rapid spin tends to \(mg\ell/(I_3\omega_3)\). This is the rate required for gravity to turn the nearly axial angular momentum. The full quadratic distinguishes that rate from a second, fast precession and gives the spin threshold for a tilted steady solution when \(\cos\theta>0\). Initial conditions outside such a solution produce a varying tilt, called nutation.

For \(m=0.200\) kg, \(\ell=0.0450\) m, \(I_1=0.00180\) kg m², \(I_3=0.000800\) kg m², \(\omega_3=200\) rad/s and \(\theta=30^\circ\), the slow-root approximation is \(0.0883/0.160=0.552\) rad/s. Substituting the quadratic coefficients gives \(0.555\) rad/s, a precession period of about 11.3 s. The other root is about 102 rad/s. A camera measuring only rotation of a mark painted on the body cannot generally separate axial spin, precession and nutation without also tracking the axis.

Stability of rotation about a principal axis

A symmetric rotor can precess steadily. A body with three different principal moments has a striking additional possibility. Suppose \(I_1<I_2<I_3\) and no torque acts about the centre of mass. In body coordinates Euler’s equations can be written \(I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3\), with cyclic permutations for the other components. A pure rotation about any one principal axis is an exact solution. Stability asks what happens to a small transverse disturbance of that solution, a question about neighbouring trajectories rather than the exact rotation itself.

Consider nominal rotation about axis 2 at rate \(\Omega\). Write \(\omega_1=u\) and \(\omega_3=w\), both initially small. The change of \(\omega_2\) is a product of two small quantities and can be discarded to first order. The first and third equations give \(\dot u=-(I_3-I_2)\Omega w/I_1\) and \(\dot w=-(I_2-I_1)\Omega u/I_3\). Differentiating the first and substituting the second produces

\[\ddot u=\Omega^2\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}\,u .\]

A positive coefficient gives hyperbolic solutions with an exponentially growing component for a generic disturbance. Repeating the calculation about the smallest or the largest moment reverses the sign of the product, giving bounded oscillatory disturbances. Energy and angular momentum remain conserved throughout. A nearby angular-velocity direction can change substantially while moving along the intersection of the two invariant surfaces.

Estimate the onset of a visible tumble

Take moments \(I_1=0.020\), \(I_2=0.030\) and \(I_3=0.040\) kg m² and a nominal intermediate-axis rotation \(\Omega=20.0\) rad/s. The small-perturbation growth rate is \(20.0\sqrt{0.010\times0.010/(0.020\times0.040)}=7.07\) s⁻¹. Prepare an initial transverse component of \(0.020\) rad/s in the growing eigenmode. Reaching \(0.200\) rad/s then takes \(\ln(10)/7.07=0.326\) s, still within the small-angle range. A perturbation prepared with zero initial derivative instead contains both growing and decaying contributions and follows a hyperbolic cosine initially.

These estimates predict the early departure, not the duration of a full flip. When transverse components approach the nominal spin, the nonlinear terms that preserved the dominant spin to first order can no longer be dropped. As two adjacent moments approach equality the growth rate tends to zero, recovering the absence of this isolated intermediate-axis instability in a symmetric rotor. A motion-capture experiment that records all three body components can test the exponential interval and independently check whether external torques or aerodynamic drag have changed the conserved angular momentum.

There is also a camera-independent prediction for the orientation of the early disturbance in body coordinates. A growing solution has \(\dot u=\lambda u\). From the first linearized equation this requires \(w/u=-I_1\lambda/[(I_3-I_2)\Omega]\). In the numerical example that ratio is \(-0.707\): the small components point in opposite signed directions. A disturbance with the reverse sign projects partly onto the decaying solution. The eventual growth rate can be the same while the visibility of the first few cycles differs greatly. Recording both components avoids assigning a disagreement in initial conditions to the underlying instability rate.

Chapter 10 · Forced, damped vibration

Use amplitude, phase and power together to estimate the mechanical properties of an oscillator from a sinusoidal drive.

From first principles

Derive the steady amplitude and its phase lag

The force laws for a simple vibration test are an elastic restoring force \(-kx\), a viscous force \(-c\dot x\) and a prescribed drive \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Newton's law gives \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t\). Assume linear coefficients, positive damping and enough time for the free transient to decay. Seek the response at the measured drive frequency by setting \(x=\operatorname{Re}(Xe^{i\omega t})\). Each differentiation multiplies the complex amplitude by \(i\omega\), so

\[(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)X=F_0 .\]

The magnitude of the complex denominator fixes the measured displacement amplitude; its argument gives the lag of the response relative to the applied force:

\[|X|=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2}},\]
\[\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(c\omega,k-m\omega^2).\]

At low frequency the spring supports nearly the entire force and the displacement approaches \(F_0/k\). At \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\) the spring and inertia terms cancel, leaving a quarter-period displacement lag. Multiplying velocity by force and averaging distinguishes this condition of maximum absorbed power from the maximum displacement amplitude.

Derivation and applications

Read the exact mechanical bandwidth from absorbed power

A measured displacement peak need not occur at the frequency of maximum energy absorption. Consider a linear oscillator of mass \(m\), stiffness \(k\) and viscous damping coefficient \(c>0\), driven steadily by \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Let \(X\) denote the complex displacement amplitude. Work over a complete period supplies the energy dissipated by the damper, whose instantaneous loss is \(c\dot x^2\). Since the velocity amplitude is \(\omega|X|\), averaging its square gives

\[\overline P=\tfrac12 c\omega^2 |X|^2=\frac{c F_0^2\omega^2/2}{(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2}.\]

Divide numerator and denominator by \(\omega^2\). The frequency-dependent term in the denominator becomes \((k/\omega-m\omega)^2\), which vanishes exactly at \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\). Hence \(\overline P_{\max}=F_0^2/(2c)\). The half-power condition is particularly simple:

\[|k-m\omega^2|=c\omega .\]

There are two positive roots, one on each side of \(\omega_0\). Solving the two quadratics separately yields

\[\omega_\pm=\frac{\sqrt{c^2+4mk}\pm c}{2m},\]
\[\omega_+-\omega_-=\frac cm,\]
\[\omega_+\omega_-=\frac km .\]

The half-power bandwidth in angular-frequency units is exactly \(c/m\) for this viscously damped, constant-force experiment. For weak damping the frequencies become nearly symmetric around \(\omega_0\). In stronger damping the geometric mean still gives \(\omega_0\). The frequencies correspond to half the peak power, equivalently to the velocity amplitude falling to \(1/\sqrt2\) of its maximum.

Separate the transient from the driven motion

A shaker applies a sinusoidal force to a mass on a spring and viscous damper. Its displacement can lag the force and can remain large even when the applied force is small. Assume motion about a stable equilibrium, constant stiffness \(k\), positive damping coefficient \(c\) and drive \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Newton’s equation is \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t\). The solution contains a homogeneous contribution set by preparation and a particular response set by the continuing drive.

Positive damping makes the homogeneous part decay. To estimate the steady frequency response, measurements must last several decay times at each frequency. Measurements taken immediately after the drive changes include residual free motion. Close to weakly damped resonance that settling time can span many oscillations, so sweeping too rapidly can shift the apparent location of a peak.

Solve for amplitude and lag

Write the steady displacement as the real part of \(Xe^{i\omega t}\) and take \(F_0\) real. Substituting gives \(X=F_0/(k-m\omega^2+ic\omega)\). The denominator groups the elastic and inertial contributions along the real axis and viscous loading in quadrature. Its magnitude gives

\[|X|=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2}}.\]

The lag angle is the argument of the denominator, evaluated in the appropriate quadrant. At low frequency \(X\simeq F_0/k\), in phase with force. At high frequency \(X\simeq-F_0/(m\omega^2)\), nearly opposite in phase. At \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) the reactive terms cancel and the displacement lags by a quarter cycle. This phase condition identifies the undamped natural frequency even when the amplitude maximum lies slightly lower.

Connect absorbed power to damping

Multiply the equation by \(\dot x\) and average over one settled cycle. Both stored kinetic and elastic energies return to their initial values, so the mean input power is dissipated by the damper. For peak displacement, \(\langle P\rangle=c\omega^2|X|^2/2\). At \(\omega_n\), the velocity is in phase with force, giving \(\langle P\rangle=F_0^2/(2c)\). Minimising the denominator of the displacement response gives a different frequency, \(\omega_{\rm peak}^2=k/m-c^2/(2m^2)\), if this is positive. A reported “resonance frequency” should identify the measured observable.

A power scan near a lightly damped resonance has a narrow peak whose half-power bandwidth is approximately \(c/m\) in rad/s. The damping estimate obtained from that width can be compared with an independent ring-down estimate. Significant disagreement could indicate an amplitude-dependent damper or loss in the drive system.

Read a frequency-response experiment

Take \(m=2.0\) kg, \(k=800\) N/m, \(c=8.0\) N·s/m and \(F_0=4.0\) N. The low-frequency displacement is 5.0 mm. At 20 rad/s the displacement is 25 mm, the velocity amplitude is 0.50 m/s and the mean power is 1.0 W. The displacement maximum occurs near \(\sqrt{400-8}=19.8\) rad/s. A run at a single frequency near the peak cannot identify all three parameters. A low-frequency measurement constrains stiffness, the quarter-cycle lag constrains mass relative to stiffness and the absorbed power at that frequency constrains damping independently.

Motion imposed at the support

A vibration mount is often driven through a moving base rather than by a calibrated force on the mass. Let \(y(t)\) be the prescribed absolute displacement of the support and \(x(t)\) the absolute displacement of a supported mass \(m\). A spring and a viscous damper both connect mass to base, so they respond to \(x-y\) and \(\dot x-\dot y\). Newton’s equation is \(m\ddot x+c(\dot x-\dot y)+k(x-y)=0\). The same mass and stiffness as in a force-driven oscillator can therefore produce a different transfer function because the input enters both elastic and dissipative forces.

For harmonic motion proportional to \(e^{i\omega t}\) rearrange the amplitude equation to give \((k-m\omega^2+i c\omega)X=(k+i c\omega)Y\). Set \(r=\omega/\omega_n\), \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n)\). The ratio of absolute displacement amplitudes is

\[\left|\frac XY\right|^2=\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}.\]

At very low frequencies the mass follows the support almost rigidly. To have \(|X|<|Y|\) requires \((1-r^2)^2>1\), which for positive frequency means \(r>\sqrt2\). Increasing damping reduces the resonant peak but increases the high-frequency path through which the moving support can exert a force. Damping is therefore a design variable with different consequences in different frequency bands.

Chapter 11 · Nonlinear stiffness and amplitude-dependent period

Derive the leading frequency shift of a symmetric nonlinear oscillator and test whether a measured amplitude shift is consistent with a cubic spring.

From first principles

Derive the first amplitude correction to a spring’s frequency

A symmetric elastic restoring force has no quadratic displacement term; the first correction to a linear spring can therefore be cubic. Suppose a conservative oscillator satisfies \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\) with \(k>0\) and \(|\alpha A^2/k|\ll1\) at measured peak amplitude \(A\). Leading motion is approximately \(A\cos\Omega t\). Cube this motion to find which frequencies enter the perturbing force:

\[x^3\simeq A^3[\tfrac34\cos\Omega t+\tfrac14\cos3\Omega t] .\]

To maintain a bounded periodic solution, its fundamental component must balance at first order. Otherwise the remaining fundamental force would resonantly grow the correction. Combine the inertia and restoring-force coefficients at \(\cos\Omega t\):

\[-m\Omega^2A+kA+\tfrac34\alpha A^3\simeq0,\]
\[\Omega\simeq\sqrt{k/m}\left(1+\frac{3\alpha A^2}{8k}\right).\]

The third-harmonic component also distorts the waveform, but reinserting that order-\(\alpha\) distortion into the cubic force affects the fundamental only at the next perturbative order. Positive \(\alpha\) raises frequency with amplitude; negative \(\alpha\) lowers it within the local range where the stable weakly nonlinear model applies.

Derivation and applications

Find the third harmonic generated by a weak cubic spring

A change in oscillation period is one signature of a nonlinear restoring force. The same nonlinearity also changes the shape of the recorded motion. Let an undamped mass obey \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\), with \(k>0\). Release it from rest at the measured peak displacement \(A\). Define \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\), the small signed parameter \(\epsilon=\alpha A^2/k\), and a stretched time \(\tau=\Omega t\). Write \(x/A=\cos\tau+\epsilon u(\tau)\) with \(u(0)=u'(0)=0\) so that \(A\) remains the physical turning point.

Allow the frequency to shift as \(\Omega=\omega_0(1+\epsilon\nu)\). Substitute the expansion into the equation and retain terms only to first order in \(\epsilon\). Primes below denote differentiation with respect to \(\tau\):

\[u''+u=2\nu\cos\tau-\cos^3\tau=(2\nu-3/4)\cos\tau-\tfrac14\cos3\tau .\]

A term driven at the unit frequency would grow proportionally to \(\tau\sin\tau\), contradicting a bounded, periodic correction. Choose \(\nu=3/8\) to remove it. The remaining forcing is at three times the fundamental. A trial \(C\cos3\tau\) has \(u''+u=-8C\cos3\tau\). Add the required homogeneous term to satisfy the turning-point conditions:

\[u(\tau)=\frac{\cos3\tau-\cos\tau}{32}.\]

The predicted displacement therefore contains two lines with linked amplitudes and a shifted fundamental:

\[x(t)\simeq A\!\left[(1-\epsilon/32)\cos\Omega t+(\epsilon/32)\cos3\Omega t\right],\]
\[\Omega/\omega_0\simeq1+3\epsilon/8 .\]

The coefficient sign gives the third harmonic relative to the phase of a release at positive displacement. Squaring an amplitude to obtain a power spectrum erases that sign. The approximation assumes \(|\epsilon|\ll1\) and excludes appreciable dissipation during the observation.

Worked example

Estimate a micrometre-scale distortion

Take \(A=12.0\) mm, \(k=20.0\) N/m and \(\alpha=1.00\times10^4\) N/m³. Then \(\epsilon=0.0720\), so the third-harmonic displacement amplitude is \(12.0(0.0720)/32=0.0270\) mm. The fundamental frequency should increase by \(2.70\%\). With \(\omega_0=10.0\) rad/s, the third-harmonic line lies close to \(30.8\) rad/s.

Second application

Test whether a measured line comes from the spring

At \(A=6.00\) mm on the same apparatus, \(\epsilon\) falls by four and the third-harmonic amplitude falls by eight to \(3.38\) micrometres. The fractional frequency shift becomes \(0.675\%\). A third-harmonic signal that instead halves with amplitude could arise from an already distorted input or readout. The two amplitude scalings plus an independent frequency measurement test the cubic model more strongly than observing a line at three times the frequency.

Start from a reversible force law

A spring that looks linear under small deflection can stiffen as it is displaced further. For an oscillator symmetric under \(x\mapsto-x\), the restoring force expanded about equilibrium has no even powers. Keep the leading correction and write \(m\ddot x+kx+\alpha x^3=0\), with \(k>0\) and \(\alpha\) measured in N/m³. Assume the displacement is small enough that neglected fifth-order terms remain unimportant. Positive \(\alpha\) gives a hardening spring. A negative cubic coefficient describes local softening and may need higher-order terms to represent large displacements safely within the model.

The potential is \(kx^2/2+\alpha x^4/4\). Without damping the total energy is conserved and a release at amplitude \(A\) fixes that energy. Unlike a harmonic oscillator, the quadratic curvature at the origin no longer fixes the period for every possible energy. We want a quantitative connection between the amplitude of the motion and its zero-crossing interval.

Obtain the first frequency correction

Try \(x\simeq A\cos\Omega t\) and substitute it into the cubic equation. The identity \(\cos^3 u=(3\cos u+\cos3u)/4\) shows that the nonlinearity introduces a component at the original frequency and a third harmonic. Balance the fundamental coefficient to get \(-m\Omega^2+k+3\alpha A^2/4\simeq0\). Expanding the square root gives

\[\Omega\simeq\omega_0\left(1+\frac{3\alpha A^2}{8k}\right),\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}.\]

The control parameter is \(|\alpha|A^2/k\), the ratio of cubic to linear force at maximum displacement. The small additional third harmonic changes the waveform at first order but only feeds back into this leading frequency result at higher order. Fitting a perfect sinusoid to a high-precision trace may therefore show a residual even when the measured average period follows this formula.

Check the result using the conserved energy

The quarter-period is the time to move from equilibrium to \(A\). Substitution of the energy at the turning point gives an integral over \(dx/\sqrt{2[E-U(x)]/m}\). Let \(x=A\sin u\) to remove the square-root endpoint. The remaining integrand is proportional to \(\{1+[\alpha A^2/(2k)](1+\sin^2u)\}^{-1/2}\). Expanding and averaging over the interval from zero to \(\pi/2\) uses mean \(\sin^2 u=1/2\), yielding \(T\simeq(2\pi/\omega_0)[1-3\alpha A^2/(8k)]\). This agrees with the reciprocal of the harmonic-balance result.

The integral approach generalises to experimentally tabulated conservative forces by integrating them first to obtain a potential. It would need modification for strong damping, where energy varies significantly between the two turning points of one cycle.

Infer stiffness from period data

For \(m=0.50\) kg, \(k=200\) N/m and \(\alpha=4.0\times10^4\) N/m³, \(\omega_0=20.0\) rad/s. At \(A=0.020\) m the force ratio is 0.080, giving a leading frequency increase of 3.0% to 20.6 rad/s. At half this amplitude the predicted increase is 0.75%. Measure the period over several cycles at each amplitude, keeping the amplitude change due to loss small. A plot of frequency against the square of amplitude should be approximately straight near the origin. A nonzero intercept shift under a change of temperature could indicate a change in ordinary linear stiffness instead of a new nonlinear coefficient.

Compute the entire amplitude dependence of a pendulum

A cubic expansion is useful near equilibrium. For a pendulum it is possible to carry the energy calculation beyond every finite Taylor approximation. Take a point mass with fixed length \(\ell\), no friction and a release from rest at angle \(0<\theta_0<\pi\). Its conserved energy gives \(\dot\theta^2=(2g/\ell)(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)\). Use \(\cos\theta=1-2\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Defining \(k=\sin(\theta_0/2)\) reduces the speed magnitude to \(2\sqrt{g/\ell}\sqrt{k^2-\sin^2(\theta/2)}\).

One quarter period is the time to travel between zero angle and the turning point. The reciprocal speed appears to diverge at the latter, but it has an integrable square-root singularity for every ordinary turning angle. To evaluate it stably, set \(\sin(\theta/2)=k\sin u\). The factor \(\cos u\) from differentiating cancels the same factor inside the square root. Four equivalent quarters give

\[T=4\sqrt{\ell/g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}}=4\sqrt{\ell/g}\,K(k).\]

Here \(K\) denotes the complete elliptic integral with modulus \(k\). Expanding the integrand at small \(k\) and integrating the first correction gives \(T/T_0=1+k^2/4+\cdots=1+\theta_0^2/16+\cdots\), where \(T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\). The positive correction is the familiar softening: increasing amplitude lengthens the period because the restoring torque grows more slowly than its linear extrapolation.

Decide when a small-angle clock loses accuracy

A pendulum of length \(0.800\) m has \(T_0=1.794\) s at \(g=9.81\) m/s². At a release of \(60.0^\circ\), \(k=\sin30^\circ=0.500\). Evaluating the integral gives \(K(0.500)=1.68575\) and \(T/T_0=2K/\pi=1.0732\), hence \(T=1.926\) s. The weak-angle approximation gives \(1+(\pi/3)^2/16=1.0685\). It misses the exact period by roughly \(0.008\) s per cycle; a many-cycle timing record resolves the accumulated discrepancy.

As \(\theta_0\) tends to \(\pi\), the integrand near \(u=\pi/2\) approaches the reciprocal of a vanishing cosine. The integral diverges logarithmically, expressing the long time spent close to the unstable upper equilibrium. No finite polynomial in amplitude can reproduce that divergence. A numerical timing experiment should therefore locate regular zero crossings rather than demand a turning-point velocity of exactly zero in sampled data. Comparing the crossing intervals with the regularized integral distinguishes numerical resolution error from the genuine increase in physical period.

A way to evaluate the elliptic integral without a special-function library is to form the arithmetic–geometric mean. Begin with \(a_0=1\) and \(b_0=\sqrt{1-k^2}\), then replace the pair by their arithmetic and geometric means. Both converge rapidly to a common \(M\), and \(K(k)=\pi/(2M)\). For the 60° release the first pair is \(1\) and \(0.86603\), the next pair is approximately \(0.93301\) and \(0.93060\), and the subsequent pair agrees near \(0.93181\). Taking the reciprocal of the common value directly gives the period ratio. This procedure supplies an accurate independent benchmark even when the small-amplitude expansion ceases to converge quickly.

Numerical quadrature and the arithmetic–geometric mean should agree at a fixed amplitude. If a period extracted from actual trajectories differs while these two evaluations agree, examine the dynamical time step, how release angle is measured, and whether the observed oscillations have suffered appreciable damping.

Chapter 12 · Bifurcation of a rotating hoop

Use an effective potential to locate competing equilibria and predict the frequency of small oscillations on either side of a stability change.

From first principles

Find the instability threshold of the rotating hoop

Assume a frictionless bead constrained to a vertical circular hoop of radius \(R\). The hoop rotates at prescribed constant angular speed \(\Omega\) about its vertical diameter. With angle \(\theta\) measured upward from the bottom, the bead moves along the hoop at speed \(R\dot\theta\) and around the axis at speed \(\Omega R\sin\theta\). These directions are perpendicular, and its gravitational height above the bottom is \(R(1-\cos\theta)\). Thus its Lagrangian is

\[L=\tfrac12mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)-mgR(1-\cos\theta).\]

Apply the one-coordinate Lagrange equation. The rotation term tends to move the bead outward while gravity acts toward the bottom:

\[\ddot\theta=\sin\theta(\Omega^2\cos\theta-g/R).\]

Linearizing about the bottom gives \(\ddot\theta\simeq(\Omega^2-g/R)\theta\). For \(\Omega^2<g/R\) the coefficient restores a small displacement; above threshold the bottom has growing small disturbances. Setting the exact right side to zero gives the additional equilibrium and its range:

\[\Omega_c=\sqrt{g/R},\]
\[\cos\theta_*=g/(R\Omega^2)\quad(\Omega\ge\Omega_c).\]

The two nonzero angles are related by reflection. Their existence follows from the prescribed rotation, so a freely slowing hoop would require an additional dynamical coordinate.

Derivation and applications

How high is the barrier between the two tilted equilibria?

In the rotating hoop, a new pair of stable angles appears where the bottom loses stability. Its curvature predicts a small-oscillation frequency; its depth determines whether a larger disturbance can carry the bead across the bottom. Consider a bead of mass \(m\) on a circular hoop of radius \(R\) spinning at prescribed constant angular speed \(\Omega\) about its vertical diameter. Ignore friction. Measure \(\theta\) upward along the hoop from the bottom. The vertical height change is \(R(1-\cos\theta)\) and the distance to the rotation axis is \(R\sin\theta\).

The constrained bead has kinetic energy \(mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)/2\). Collect the terms independent of \(\dot\theta\) as the potential governing motion in the rotating coordinate:

\[U_{\rm eff}(\theta)=mgR(1-\cos\theta)-\tfrac12mR^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta .\]

Write \(s=\Omega^2R/g\) and differentiate. A tilted equilibrium exists only for \(s>1\). At either stable angle \(\theta_*\) substitute \(\cos\theta_*=1/s\) into the potential and factor the result:

\[U'_{\rm eff}=mgR\sin\theta(1-s\cos\theta),\]
\[U_{\rm eff}(\theta_*)=-mgR\,\frac{(s-1)^2}{2s}.\]

The bottom has \(U_{\rm eff}(0)=0\). Subtracting the energies gives the barrier and the threshold angular speed for a bead launched from a tilted minimum toward the bottom:

\[\Delta U=mgR\,\frac{(s-1)^2}{2s},\]
\[|\dot\theta|_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2\Delta U/(mR^2)}.\]

The velocity here is angular speed relative to the hoop. At threshold the trajectory approaches the unstable bottom asymptotically; crossing takes slightly more initial energy. Near \(s=1\) the barrier collapses quadratically even though two distinct equilibria already exist. The externally maintained rotation can exchange ordinary laboratory energy with the bead, while the effective one-coordinate energy is conserved.

Worked example

Estimate the speed needed to change branches

Take \(m=0.0500\) kg, \(R=0.200\) m and choose \(\Omega\) so that \(s=2.00\). The two minima then occur at \(\pm60.0^\circ\) and the barrier is \(mgR/4=0.0245\) J. The tangential threshold speed is \(\sqrt{2\Delta U/m}=0.990\) m/s, or \(4.95\) rad/s along the hoop. A launch at \(0.800\) m/s has only \(0.0160\) J and returns to its original branch.

Second application

Use two measured angles to compare barrier depths

On the same hoop reduce the rotation until the two minima are at \(\pm36.9^\circ\). Their cosine is about \(0.800\), giving \(s=1.25\). The new barrier is \(mgR(0.25)^2/2.50=0.00245\) J, a tenth of the previous value. The new threshold tangential speed is \(0.313\) m/s. A smaller change in angle than this tenfold energy reduction might suggest is a measurable feature of a shallow double well.

Reduce the constraint to one coordinate

A bead slides on a vertical circular hoop of radius \(R\). An external motor rotates the hoop at prescribed constant angular velocity \(\Omega\) about its vertical diameter. Let \(\theta\) be the angle from the lowest point. The bead’s height relative to that point is \(R(1-\cos\theta)\) and its distance from the rotation axis is \(R\sin\theta\). Assume negligible friction and a rigid constraint. The bead moves both along the hoop and azimuthally with it, even if \(\theta\) is momentarily stationary.

In laboratory coordinates its speed squared is \(R^2\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta\). Subtract gravitational potential energy from this kinetic energy to construct a one-coordinate Lagrangian. Lagrange’s equation gives \(\ddot\theta=\sin\theta(\Omega^2\cos\theta-g/R)\). Although motion in the laboratory is constrained by a driven apparatus, the reduced equation is autonomous for constant \(\Omega\).

Track equilibria as the rotation rate changes

The torque vanishes at the bottom and the top because \(\sin\theta=0\). Off-axis equilibria also appear when \(\cos\theta=g/(\Omega^2R)\). They are possible only if the rotation exceeds \(\Omega_c=\sqrt{g/R}\). Each positive-angle equilibrium then has a negative-angle partner. The two have equal energies because the apparatus is symmetric.

For small displacement from the bottom, \(\ddot\theta\simeq-(g/R-\Omega^2)\theta\). Below threshold the bottom is stable with angular frequency \(\sqrt{g/R-\Omega^2}\). As the threshold is approached this frequency tends to zero; the potential flattens. Above threshold the sign reverses and a small perturbation grows. Linearising about either displaced equilibrium instead gives a positive frequency squared \(\Omega^2\sin^2\theta_{\rm eq}\). Measuring this small-amplitude response distinguishes two predictions: where the bead rests and how steeply it is restored when disturbed.

Interpret the effective energy

The reduced motion admits the effective potential \(U_{\rm eff}=mgR(1-\cos\theta)-m\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta/2\). Its derivative predicts the reduced equation. Its negative centrifugal term differs in sign from the positive laboratory azimuthal kinetic energy. The motor supplies or absorbs work as the bead’s distance from the rotation axis changes. Treating the bead’s laboratory mechanical energy alone as conserved would therefore lead to the wrong equilibrium.

At exactly the critical rate, the quadratic terms in a small-angle expansion cancel and the first restoring term in \(U_{\rm eff}\) is positive and quartic. A finite disturbance can still oscillate there, but its period depends on its amplitude. An arbitrarily small oscillation cannot be assigned a finite harmonic frequency at that point.

Estimate the transition in a real apparatus

With \(R=0.250\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s² the critical rate is 6.26 rad/s, or about 59.8 rpm. At 5.0 rad/s the bead returns to the bottom with small-oscillation frequency \(\sqrt{39.24-25}=3.77\) rad/s. At 8.0 rad/s the two new equilibria satisfy \(\cos\theta=39.24/64=0.613\), or \(\theta=\pm52.2°\). Small oscillations about either have angular frequency \(8\sqrt{1-0.613^2}=6.32\) rad/s.

When observing the transition, tilt of the axis or a small asymmetry of the hoop can favour one side. Hysteresis-like delays during a fast sweep can also arise from finite settling time in weak damping. Recording both equilibrium angles on slow sweeps and the independently measured oscillation frequency supplies a more discriminating test of the symmetric bifurcation than simply noting which side the bead occupies.

Break the symmetry with a weak bias

The two off-centre positions of the ideal rotating bead have equal energy. A small constant generalized torque \(\tau_b\) favouring positive \(\theta\) breaks that symmetry. Over a small working angular range it adds a term \(-\tau_b\theta\) to the effective potential. Write the original potential relative to its value at the bottom as \(mgR(1-\cos\theta)-m\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta/2\). Expanding near the critical rate \(\Omega_c^2=g/R\) gives quadratic coefficient \(mR^2(\Omega_c^2-\Omega^2)/2\). The quartic coefficient evaluated at threshold is \(mR^2\Omega_c^2/8\).

On the high-speed side define \(a=\Omega^2-\Omega_c^2>0\), \(b=\Omega_c^2/2\), and \(h=\tau_b/(mR^2)\). To leading order stationarity and local stability become

\[b\theta^3-a\theta-h=0,\qquad 3b\theta^2-a>0 .\]

With zero bias there are two symmetric minima and an unstable central position. Increasing positive bias deepens the positive minimum and eventually destroys the negative one. At the point where the negative minimum meets the adjacent maximum, both the stationarity equation and its derivative in \(\theta\) vanish. Thus \(\theta_*=-\sqrt{a/(3b)}\) and \(h_*=(2a/3)\sqrt{a/(3b)}\). The critical bias scales as \(a^{3/2}\). Near the threshold a surprisingly small imperfection can remove one of the two resting positions entirely.

Estimate the bias needed to select a branch

Use a bead of mass \(0.0500\) kg on a hoop of radius \(0.250\) m. Then \(\Omega_c^2=39.24\) s⁻². Set \(\Omega^2=\Omega_c^2+0.600\) s⁻², making \(b=19.62\) s⁻². The unbiased displaced equilibrium angles are approximately \(\pm\sqrt{0.600/19.62}=\pm0.175\) rad. As positive bias is increased, the negative branch disappears near \(\theta_*=-0.101\) rad. The corresponding generalized acceleration is \(h_*=0.0404\) s⁻², giving \(\tau_b=mR^2h_*=1.26\times10^{-4}\) N m. A tangential force with moment arm \(R\) would supply about \(0.505\) mN.

This estimate uses both small angles and operation close enough to the critical speed that quartic truncation is accurate. An exact calculation differentiates the full trigonometric potential twice and solves the two equations simultaneously. In a slow sweep with weak damping, a bead initially on the unfavoured branch can remain there until its minimum disappears. Vibration or thermal agitation may make it switch earlier by crossing the remaining barrier. Repeating the sweep in both directions and at several bias amplitudes helps distinguish an imperfect constraint from random switching in a nearly symmetric system.

The same model predicts increasing sensitivity before an actual switch. Differentiate the cubic stationarity condition with respect to bias while following a particular equilibrium branch: \(d\theta/dh=1/(3b\theta^2-a)\). Its denominator is the local dimensionless restoring curvature. Thus a branch nearing disappearance both moves a large angular distance under a small bias increment and shows a very low small-oscillation frequency. Measuring those two effects on the same branch tests the local shape of the potential without requiring a precise measurement of the noisy switching moment. Away from a minimum the static slope can instead be negative, describing an unstable mathematical solution.

The sharp loss of a branch is best resolved with bias steps small compared with the measured interval between its creation and disappearance.

Fit a weak cubic spring

A mass of 0.20 kg has a natural frequency of 10.00 Hz at negligible amplitude. At 10.0 mm amplitude it oscillates at 10.03 Hz. Assume a restoring force \(-kx-\beta x^3\). Estimate \(k\) and \(\beta\), predict the shift at 5.0 mm and test the perturbation assumption.

Show the stiffness estimates

The linear coefficient is \(k=m(2\pi f_0)^2=790\) N/m. Retaining the fundamental of \(\cos^3\omega t\) gives \(\Delta f/f_0\simeq3\beta A^2/(8k)\). Hence \(\beta=8k(0.0030)/(3\times10^{-4})=6.32\times10^4\) N/m³. The positive sign describes hardening. At half amplitude the shift falls by four, to \(0.0075\) Hz. The maximum cubic-to-linear force ratio at 10 mm is \(\beta A^2/k=0.0080\), compatible with a first-order estimate.

Which equilibria can hold a bead?

A bead slides without friction on a vertical hoop of radius 0.400 m rotating about its vertical diameter. Find the critical angular speed and stable positions at 6.00 rad/s. Determine the small oscillation frequency about either tilted position and compare with operation at 4.00 rad/s.

Show the stability calculation

With angle measured from the bottom the effective potential per unit mass is \(-gR\cos\theta-\Omega^2R^2\sin^2\theta/2\). Its derivative is \(R\sin\theta(g-\Omega^2R\cos\theta)\). The critical speed is \(\sqrt{g/R}=4.95\) rad/s. At 6.00 rad/s the tilted minima have \(\cos\theta=9.81/(36\times0.4)=0.6813\), hence \(\theta=\pm47.1^\circ\). Curvature divided by \(R^2\) gives \(\omega^2=\Omega^2\sin^2\theta\), or \(\omega=4.39\) rad/s. At 4.00 rad/s only the bottom is stable and \(\omega=\sqrt{g/R-\Omega^2}=2.92\) rad/s.

Reconstruct a binary orbit from a spectrum

Two spectral lines trace a circular binary with a period of 20.0 days. Their radial-velocity semi-amplitudes are 30.0 and 60.0 km/s. Take the orbit to be edge-on. Find the stellar separation, both masses and the orbital energy. How would the mass estimate change at an inclination of 60°? Use G = 6.6743 × 10⁻¹¹ in SI units and one solar mass = 1.9885 × 10³⁰ kg.

Worked solution

The two stars orbit their common centre of mass with the same angular frequency. Their speeds add to give a relative circular speed of 90,000 m/s. Over 1,728,000 s the relative separation vector describes a circle of circumference 2πa, hence a = 2.475 × 10¹⁰ m. The speed ratio gives the inverse mass ratio. The star moving at 30 km/s is twice as massive as the other star.

The relative acceleration is v²/a and the two-body attraction gives G(M₁+M₂)/a². Equating the expressions gives 3.00 × 10³⁰ kg in total, about 1.51 solar masses. The individual masses are approximately 1.01 and 0.50 solar masses. The reduced mass is two ninths of the total. Its kinetic energy is ½μv², and the total orbital energy is its negative, approximately −2.70 × 10³⁹ J.

Applied study

Use energy and angular momentum to find the turning points and period of an orbit.

What fixes an orbit?

An object at the same distance from Earth can follow a circle, a long ellipse or a path that escapes. Position sets the gravitational acceleration. The initial velocity distinguishes those trajectories. Begin in an inertial frame centred on a spherical Earth, and neglect atmospheric drag, other bodies and the motion of Earth in response to the much smaller satellite. With \(\mu=GM\), Newton’s law reads \(\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\mu\mathbf r/r^3\). Everything that follows concerns motion outside Earth.

Crossing this equation with \(\mathbf r\) shows that the specific angular momentum \(\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}\) is constant. The orbit lies in the plane perpendicular to \(\mathbf h\). In polar coordinates \(h=r^2\dot\theta\), so the area swept per second is \(h/2\). An object must move faster around the centre when it is closer to it.

Read the turning points from the energy

Take the scalar product of Newton’s equation with velocity. The derivative of \(v^2/2\) equals the negative derivative of \(-\mu/r\), so the specific energy \(\mathcal E=v^2/2-\mu/r\) is constant. Substituting the angular momentum for tangential speed gives a one-dimensional radial balance

\[\mathcal E=\frac{\dot r^2}{2}+U_{\rm eff}(r),\qquad U_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{h^2}{2r^2}-\frac{\mu}{r}.\]

Only radii with \(\mathcal E\ge U_{\rm eff}\) can be reached. At the minimum of \(U_{\rm eff}\) a circular orbit is possible. A bound eccentric orbit has two turning radii \(r_p,r_a\). At each, \(\dot r=0\), so both are roots of \(2\mathcal E r^2+2\mu r-h^2=0\). Their sum fixes \(\mathcal E=-\mu/(2a)\), with \(a=(r_p+r_a)/2\). Substitution into the energy gives the speed at any point, \(v^2=\mu(2/r-1/a)\).

Recover the shape and the clock

Set \(u=1/r\) and use \(d/dt=hu^2 d/d\theta\) in the radial force equation \(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=-\mu/r^2\). This gives \(u''+u=\mu/h^2\), with primes denoting angle derivatives. Choosing \(\theta=0\) at closest approach yields \(r=(h^2/\mu)/(1+e\cos\theta)\). For \(0<e<1\) the shape is an ellipse, with \(e=(r_a-r_p)/(r_a+r_p)\) and semiminor axis \(b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\).

An ellipse encloses area \(\pi ab\). Divide that area by \(h/2\) and use \(h^2=\mu a(1-e^2)\) to obtain \(T=2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}\). Eccentricity redistributes the time spent along the path. At a given semimajor axis it leaves the full period unchanged. Angular momentum also constrains a rotating body about its own centre. Torque-free rigid-body motion.

From force components to Lagrange’s equation

A constrained system often has fewer independent coordinates than Cartesian components. Consider a bead of mass \(m\) on a light rigid rod of length \(\ell\), moving in a vertical plane. The pivot fixes the radius. Its force is perpendicular to the allowed displacement, so the angle \(\theta\) measured from the downward vertical suffices. The kinetic and gravitational energies are \(K=m\ell^2\dot\theta^2/2\) and \(U=mg\ell(1-\cos\theta)\).

For conservative forces with ideal constraints, varying the action \(\int (K-U)\,dt\) with fixed endpoints gives \(d(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q)/dt=\partial\mathcal L/\partial q\) for each independent coordinate \(q\), with \(\mathcal L=K-U\). For the rod this yields \(m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta\). Close to the stable minimum \(U\simeq mg\ell\theta^2/2\), and the motion oscillates with period \(2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\). For \(\ell=1.00\) m and \(g=9.81\) m/s², the period is 2.01 s.

The same curvature test works for a near-circular orbit. Expand the specific effective potential at \(r_c=h^2/\mu\). Its positive curvature is \(U''_{\rm eff}(r_c)=\mu/r_c^3\), so a small radial displacement oscillates at \(\sqrt{\mu/r_c^3}\). The period of this small radial motion agrees with the circular orbital period obtained from the area law. Both a pendulum and a nearly circular orbit reveal a restoring force through the curvature of an energy minimum.

An ellipse from two distances

Take \(\mu=3.9860\times10^{14}\) m³/s², \(r_p=6628.137\) km and \(r_a=42164\) km, all radii measured from the centre. The semimajor axis is 24396.1 km and \(e=0.7283\). The speed equation at closest approach gives 10.195 km/s. The product \(r_pv_p=r_av_a\) then gives 1.603 km/s at the far end. Using \(a\) in metres in the period formula gives 37 922 s, about 10.534 hours.
