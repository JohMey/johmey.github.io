Translational symmetry and diffraction

Condensed matter and band theory

Work backwards from a scattering pattern to the repeating translations and the atoms within one cell. Learn which structural information an intensity measurement loses.

From first principles

Recover a lattice spacing from phase addition

Illuminate a row of \(N\) equally spaced atoms with a coherent plane wave and observe weak elastic scattering far away. Each atom sees an incident phase and contributes an outgoing phase. Their difference depends on \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}\). With the origin at the first atom, let \(\phi=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a\) be the phase difference between neighbours displaced by \(\mathbf a\). Up to an overall sign convention the total amplitude is a geometric sum:

\[A=f\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{ij\phi}=f\,\frac{1-e^{iN\phi}}{1-e^{i\phi}} .\]

Here \(f\) is the amplitude from one atom. Use \(|1-e^{ix}|=2|\sin(x/2)|\) and take the squared magnitude. When \(\phi=2\pi m\), evaluate the original sum directly:

\[I\propto |f|^2\frac{\sin^2(N\phi/2)}{\sin^2(\phi/2)},\]
\[I_{\rm peak}\propto N^2|f|^2 .\]

For a three-dimensional lattice every primitive translation must meet this phase condition. The resulting peak positions identify reciprocal-lattice vectors. For several atoms in each cell the single-cell factor becomes \(\sum_s f_s e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r_s}\). Its squared magnitude can vanish even at a permitted reciprocal vector. An absent peak can therefore reveal interference inside the cell.

Derivation and applications

Extract atomic displacement from the loss of Bragg intensity

A lattice can keep the same mean spacing while its Bragg peaks lose intensity on heating. Consider a crystal of identical atoms whose displacements about their mean positions fluctuate during the exposure. Treat scattering in the single-scattering approximation. At a reciprocal vector of magnitude \(Q\), every mean lattice position supplies the same phase. The instantaneous displacement projected along the scattering vector, \(u\), adds a phase \(Qu\). If this component is Gaussian with mean zero and variance \(s^2\), averaging the complex amplitude gives

\[\left\langle e^{iQu}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s^2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2/(2s^2)}e^{iQu}\,du=e^{-Q^2s^2/2}.\]

The last equality follows by Fourier transformation of the normalized Gaussian. The coherent peak measures the square of this average amplitude. Squaring the instantaneous phase factor first would give unity and erase the effect of phase differences between sites. With \(I_0(Q)\) the coherent intensity of the perfectly stationary array, the Debye–Waller attenuation is

\[I_{\rm Bragg}(Q)=I_0(Q)e^{-Q^2s^2}.\]

The formula describes the sharp coherent contribution. The difference between the total averaged intensity and the coherent intensity appears in diffuse scattering. Stronger attenuation at large \(Q\) follows because an identical displacement spans a larger fraction of a scattering phase cycle. For corrected integrated intensities of the same reflection at two temperatures, taken at essentially the same \(Q\), the unknown stationary intensity cancels,

\[s_2^2-s_1^2=-\frac{\ln[I_2(Q)/I_1(Q)]}{Q^2}.\]

This comparison assumes unchanged occupancies, instrument normalization and negligible changes in the mean structure. For isotropic displacement \(s^2\) is one third of the mean squared three-dimensional displacement.

Worked example

Recover a change in mean-square displacement

A reflection at \(Q=50.0\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) falls from 10,000 to 8,000 corrected counts. Its projected variance increases by \(-\ln(0.800)/2500=8.93\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{nm^2}\). If its original projected root-mean-square displacement was \(0.00600\,\mathrm{nm}\), the new value is \(\sqrt{0.00600^2+0.0000893}=0.0112\,\mathrm{nm}\). Adding the displacements directly would use the wrong measured quantity.

Second application

Test the angular dependence with another reflection

A second specimen retains 90.0 percent of its low-temperature peak intensity at \(Q=30.0\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\). For the same displacement direction at twice that wavevector, the attenuation exponent quadruples. The predicted retention is \(0.900^4=0.656\). A corrected retention near 0.90 for both reflections would instead suggest a common change in illuminated volume or flux that this displacement model cannot explain.

What repeats in a crystal?

A one-dimensional chain with identical atoms every \(a\) has translations \(R=na\). Replacing every other atom by a different species doubles the primitive translation, even if the nearest-neighbour separation stays fixed. The set of equivalent cell origins is the Bravais lattice; the atoms placed relative to each origin form the basis. Choosing a larger unit cell is allowed but folds distinct waves onto extra branches. Count independent degrees of freedom per unit length as a check on any cell choice.

In three dimensions write \(\mathbf R=\sum_i n_i\mathbf a_i\). Define reciprocal vectors by \(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}\). Then \(\mathbf G=\sum_i h_i\mathbf b_i\) has \(e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1\) at every equivalent origin. These vectors have units of inverse length. For a rectangular lattice doubling a real-space period halves the corresponding reciprocal spacing. The first Brillouin zone consists of wavevectors closer to the reciprocal origin than to any other reciprocal point. Later we will label collective vibrations and electronic states inside this region.

Construct reciprocal space from the primitive cell

A diffraction experiment measures momentum transfer. To predict its possible values, ask which plane waves acquire the same phase at every translated lattice point. For a Bravais lattice with translations \(\mathbf R=\sum_i n_i\mathbf a_i\), the condition is \(\exp(i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R)=1\) for every integer choice of \(n_i\). It suffices to require \(\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_i=2\pi h_i\). A reciprocal basis therefore satisfies \(\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}\) and generates all such vectors by integer combinations. In matrix form, if \(A\) holds the primitive vectors as columns, then \(B=2\pi(A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\). The transpose matters whenever the original axes are oblique.

For a triangular layer take \(\mathbf a_1=(a,0)\) and \(\mathbf a_2=(a/2,\sqrt3a/2)\). The constraints on \(\mathbf b_1\) first give \(b_{1x}=2\pi/a\) and then \(b_{1y}=-2\pi/(\sqrt3a)\). Similarly \(\mathbf b_2=(0,4\pi/(\sqrt3a))\). Verify that translating along \(\mathbf a_2\) leaves the phase of \(\mathbf b_1\) unchanged. Reciprocal vectors have units of inverse length.

How far can a wave vector move from the origin before an equivalent representative is nearer? The boundary shared with \(\mathbf G\) solves \(|\mathbf k|=|\mathbf k-\mathbf G|\), or \(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=|\mathbf G|^2/2\). The half-planes around the origin define the first Brillouin zone. Here the six shortest reciprocal vectors give a hexagon. With \(a=0.300\,\mathrm{nm}\) the shortest \(|\mathbf G|\) is \(4\pi/(\sqrt3 a)=24.18\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\), putting a face midpoint \(12.09\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) from the centre. The zone area is \((2\pi)^2/(\sqrt3a^2/2)=506.5\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}\), equal to one reciprocal primitive-cell area. Doubling all lattice lengths halves the distances in the diffraction pattern and quarters its zone area. These reciprocal positions follow from translation symmetry. The measured intensities also depend on what occupies each cell.

Add amplitudes before taking intensities

An elastic plane wave scattered from positions \(na+d_j\) accumulates a phase equal to the momentum-transfer wavevector \(Q\) times position. For \(N\) identical cells the amplitude factorises:

\[A(Q)=\underbrace{\sum_j f_j(Q)e^{iQd_j}}_{F(Q)}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{iQna},\qquad I(Q)\propto |A(Q)|^2 .\]

The last sum is geometric. Its magnitude is \(|\sin(NQa/2)/\sin(Qa/2)|\), with limiting value \(N\) at \(Qa=2\pi h\). Large finite crystals have narrow peaks of height proportional to \(N^2\). The separation to the adjacent first zero is \(2\pi/(Na)\); doubling coherent length halves this width. The basis factor \(F\) sets the relative integrated strengths for a given lattice. A finite instrument resolution also broadens measured peaks and should be characterised before inferring a sample size.

An extinction is evidence about the basis

Place two sites at \(d=0,a/2\), with real scattering amplitudes \(f_A=3\) and \(f_B=1\) held constant over the angular range. At \(G_h=2\pi h/a\), \(F_h=3+(-1)^h\). Even and odd orders have intensities proportional to 16 and 4 respectively, before angular or instrumental corrections. If both amplitudes become equal, the odd peaks vanish. If the two sites have identical chemistry as well as identical positions up to translation, a cell of length \(a/2\) would describe the structure more economically. Equal scattering amplitudes in a particular probe alone cannot establish chemical identity.

For incident wavelength \(\lambda\), with the total scattering angle \(2\theta\), \(|Q|=(4\pi/\lambda)\sin\theta\). At \(\lambda=0.154\) nm and spacing \(d=0.200\) nm the first Bragg peak therefore occurs at \(\theta=22.6^\circ\), or detector angle \(45.3^\circ\). A factor-of-two error in reading the detector would corrupt any inferred lattice constant.

What survives a change of origin?

Translating the entire basis by \(d_0\) multiplies \(A(Q)\) by \(e^{iQd_0}\). Intensity is unchanged, so diffraction cannot locate the absolute origin. More generally the phase of the amplitude is missing from a measurement of \(|A|^2\). Peak positions suggest candidate lattices, corrected intensities test a proposed basis and peak widths probe finite coherence or strain. These are different questions even when addressed with the same scan.

Derive the selection rules of a cubic powder pattern

A powdered crystal supplies many crystallite orientations, so a fixed detector angle can intercept every family of reciprocal planes with the appropriate spacing. For a cubic lattice with conventional cell parameter \(a\), reciprocal vectors have magnitude \(2\pi\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}/a\). Bragg's condition then gives \(\sin^2\theta=\lambda^2(h^2+k^2+l^2)/(4a^2)\). The sequence of squared sines can be used to index the pattern, but only reciprocal points with nonzero structure amplitude produce peaks.

For a monatomic face-centred cubic crystal, describe the lattice with conventional cubic coordinates and basis positions \((0,0,0)\), \((0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), \((\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)\) and \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)\). For identical atomic amplitude \(f\) their phase sum is

\[F_{hkl}=f[1+(-1)^{k+l}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{h+k}] .\]

If all indices are even or all are odd, every phase factor is positive and \(F=4f\). Mixed parity makes the sum zero. Thus the first allowed squared-index sums are 3 for (111), 4 for (200), 8 for (220), 11 for (311) and 12 for (222). These extinctions encode the centring translations; adding an internal basis to each lattice site can introduce additional missing peaks. Powder intensity also depends on multiplicity and on angle-dependent scattering factors, so the strongest peak need not have the smallest indices.

Infer a lattice parameter and a strain

Suppose powder peaks under wavelength 0.1540 nm occur at detector angles \(2\theta=38.48^\circ\), \(44.74^\circ\) and \(65.10^\circ\). Their squared half-angle sines are about 0.1086, 0.1448 and 0.2896. After division by the first they give \(1:1.33:2.67\), consistent with \(3:4:8\). Indexing the middle peak as (200) yields \(a=\lambda/\sin22.37^\circ=0.405\) nm. The other two peaks independently give the same value to the displayed precision, unlike an arbitrary assignment to successive integers.

Now let the (200) detector angle fall by \(0.100^\circ\) under load. Differentiating \(2d\sin\theta=\lambda\) gives \(\Delta d/d\simeq-\cot\theta\,\Delta\theta\). Here \(\Delta\theta=-0.0500^\circ=-8.73\times10^{-4}\) rad, hence \(\Delta d/d=+2.12\times10^{-3}\). That is tensile strain along the scattering vector. A single peak shift cannot establish an isotropic volume strain without further orientations. Multiple peaks can also separate a uniform scale error in wavelength from a genuine directional strain. In the unstrained limit the lattice parameter extracted independently from every correctly indexed family should agree within instrumental uncertainty.

Peak position and width respond differently to imperfections. If all sampled crystallites undergo the same fractional change of their plane spacing, their peaks shift together by the differential Bragg relation. A distribution of tensile and compressive strains can instead broaden the peak around an almost unchanged mean angle. A finite crystallite size can also broaden it, but its angular dependence across several reflection families differs from the leading strain broadening. Comparing several indexed peaks therefore gives more information than treating a wide single line as direct evidence for a particular mean lattice parameter.

Instrumental angle calibration should be checked with a reference powder so that a detector zero shift does not masquerade as a specimen strain.

Tell a finite-size minimum from a basis extinction

Eight identical cells contain sites at 0 and half a lattice period. Sum the eight cell amplitudes coherently, then multiply by the basis amplitude \(f_A+f_B e^{iQa/2}\). Here the form factors are real, positive and independent of angle.

Bloch waves, band edges and effective mass

Condensed matter and band theory

Derive an electronic band from both weak and strong binding, then use its slope and curvature to predict motion.

From first principles

Why translating a lattice state produces an energy band

In a perfect periodic chain the Hamiltonian is unchanged when the site labels are shifted by one. A wave may therefore have site amplitudes of the form \(c_j=C e^{ikja}\), with \(k\) defined up to \(2\pi/a\). To give that phase a physical energy, choose one localized orbital per cell of spacing \(a\). Assume an orthonormal basis, on-site energy \(\epsilon_0\), and a real hopping matrix element \(-t\) to either nearest neighbour. Projecting the stationary Schrödinger equation on orbital \(j\) gives

\[E c_j=\epsilon_0 c_j-t(c_{j-1}+c_{j+1}).\]

Successive amplitudes differ only by \(e^{\pm ika}\). Cancel \(c_j\), then combine these phases into a cosine:

\[E(k)=\epsilon_0-t(e^{ika}+e^{-ika})=\epsilon_0-2t\cos(ka).\]

For \(t>0\) the in-phase combination has the lowest energy. Comparing \(k=0\) and \(k=\pi/a\) gives bandwidth \(4t\). Form a narrow wave packet from neighbouring \(k\)'s. Its envelope speed is \(d(E/\hbar)/dk=(2ta/\hbar)\sin(ka)\). Translation symmetry determines the allowed phase labels, while the coupling strength determines the numerical dispersion. A real crystal can require several orbitals and longer-range couplings.

Derivation and applications

Use band curvature to predict acceleration off the force axis

Near a smooth, isolated band minimum the first derivative of energy vanishes. In a low-symmetry crystal its second derivatives can depend on direction. Choose principal axes \(x,y\) in the plane near the minimum \(\mathbf k_0\), and write \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k-\mathbf k_0\). Taylor expansion defines two positive effective masses through the curvatures,

\[E(\mathbf k_0+\mathbf q)\simeq E_0+\frac{\hbar^2 q_x^2}{2m_x}+\frac{\hbar^2 q_y^2}{2m_y}.\]

Treat a narrow wave packet semiclassically in a uniform force \(\mathbf F\), over a time short enough for scattering to be negligible. Neglect interband transitions and any anomalous velocity. Differentiating energy component by component gives group velocity. Combining this with the crystal-momentum equation gives its acceleration,

\[v_i=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial E}{\partial q_i}=\frac{\hbar q_i}{m_i},\]
\[\hbar\dot q_i=F_i,\]
\[a_i=\frac{F_i}{m_i}.\]

Thus a force rotated through the principal axes traces an ellipse of accelerations. To describe a longitudinal measurement, project the full vector onto the applied force. If the force makes angle \(\theta\) to the \(x\) axis and \(\phi\) is the direction of the resulting acceleration in the same quadrant, then

\[\frac{a_\parallel}{F}=\frac{\cos^2\theta}{m_x}+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{m_y},\]
\[\tan\phi=\frac{m_x}{m_y}\tan\theta .\]

The longitudinal inverse mass is a weighted average of inverse principal masses. The difference \(\phi-\theta\) vanishes along principal axes and for an isotropic band. The expansion requires the whole accelerated packet to stay inside the region of roughly constant curvature.

Worked example

Follow an oblique force pulse

Let \(m_x=0.20m_e\), \(m_y=0.80m_e\), where \(m_e\) is the free electron mass. A force pulse of magnitude \(1.00\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm N\) acts at \(45^\circ\) for \(50.0\,\mathrm{fs}\) on a packet initially at rest. Its velocity gains are \(F\Delta t/(\sqrt2m_i)\), or \(1.94\,\mathrm{m/s}\) along \(x\) and \(0.485\,\mathrm{m/s}\) along \(y\). The velocity points only \(14.0^\circ\) above the light-mass axis.

Second application

Recover an unseen principal curvature

A second band has longitudinal mass \(0.300m_e\) for a force at \(45^\circ\), and a measurement along its \(x\) axis gives \(m_x=0.200m_e\). In units of \(1/m_e\), inversion yields \(m_e/m_y=2/0.300-1/0.200=1.667\), so \(m_y=0.600m_e\). A pulse along \(y\) should produce one third of the acceleration measured along \(x\) for the same force, providing an independent check of the principal-axis assignment.

Why label a state by crystal momentum?

A lattice translation by \(a\) commutes with the Hamiltonian when \(V(x+a)=V(x)\). Translation eigenvalues have modulus one because translation preserves norm. Write them \(e^{ika}\); then \(\psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}u_k(x)\) with \(u_k(x+a)=u_k(x)\). Since \(e^{i(k+G)a}=e^{ika}\) for \(G=2\pi h/a\), a single Brillouin zone suffices. The index distinguishing states at the same \(k\) is the band index. Crystal momentum labels translation symmetry and can change by a reciprocal vector in scattering.

A weak potential couples a degenerate pair

Near \(k=G/2\), free states \(|k\rangle\) and \(|k-G\rangle\) have nearly equal kinetic energies. The periodic potential has Fourier coefficients \(V_G\) that couple these waves. Absorbing the mean potential in both energies, the two-state characteristic equation is \((E-E_k)(E-E_{k-G})=|V_G|^2\), with solutions

\[E_\pm=\tfrac12(E_k+E_{k-G})\pm\sqrt{\tfrac14(E_k-E_{k-G})^2+|V_G|^2}.\]

At equality the splitting is \(2|V_G|\). For a real coefficient the states are standing waves whose densities peak at different locations relative to the potential. Far from the degeneracy, the square root is dominated by the kinetic splitting and one obtains two nearly free waves again. A Fourier component that vanishes by symmetry cannot open a first-order gap at that crossing. Check other bands before interpreting a local splitting as a gap for the entire solid.

Build a band from a localised orbital

Start instead with one localised orbital \(|n\rangle\) per cell, all of energy \(\epsilon_0\), with overlap neglected in the basis and nearest-neighbour hopping matrix element \(-t\). A translation eigenstate has site amplitudes \(c_n=e^{ikna}/\sqrt N\). Acting with \(H\) on one site amplitude gives \(E c_n=\epsilon_0 c_n-t(c_{n-1}+c_{n+1})\), and hence \(E(k)=\epsilon_0-2t\cos ka\). The bandwidth \(4|t|\) measures the energy spread produced by hopping. In the limit of vanishing hopping the local energy repeats for every \(k\). More than one orbital per cell generates a matrix eigenproblem even in this simple construction.

The group velocity is \(v=\hbar^{-1}dE/dk\). Under a weak force with \(\hbar\dot k=F\), acceleration is \(F E''(k)/\hbar^2\). Define \(m^*=\hbar^2/E''(k)\), a local measure of curvature. Near the bottom of the cosine band with \(t>0\), \(E\simeq\epsilon_0-2t+t(ka)^2\) and \(m^*=\hbar^2/(2ta^2)\). Near the top the curvature changes sign. Describing the missing electrons in a nearly full band as positively charged holes with positive mass simplifies transport calculations.

Use filling to decide whether current can flow

For a complete time-reversal-symmetric band, the contributions of \(k\) and \(-k\) to current cancel. More generally the integral of the derivative of a periodic band over a whole zone is zero. A weak field can redistribute carriers near a partially filled band edge, whereas a fully filled isolated band has no vacant neighbouring states. With one spin-degenerate orbital per primitive cell there are two states per cell. One electron per cell half fills this noninteracting band; two fill it. Symmetry breaking, interactions and additional bands can change the conclusion. A single measured zone-edge splitting therefore gives incomplete information about conduction.

For further work on three-dimensional bands, Fermi surfaces and electron motion, see David Tong’s Solid State Physics lectures at Cambridge.

Open a gap by doubling a chain’s period

A uniform one-dimensional chain with one spin-degenerate orbital and one electron per site has a half-filled band. A distortion can change both the spectrum and the periodicity used to count states. Let the sites be separated by \(a\) before the distortion. Group successive sites into \(A,B\) pairs and allow alternating hopping amplitudes \(t_1,t_2>0\). The distorted unit cell has length \(2a\). For equal on-site energies, chosen as zero, the amplitude on \(A\) couples to \(B\) inside its own cell and to \(B\) one cell away. Bloch translation supplies a phase \(e^{-2ika}\) for the latter coupling. The sublattice equations give a two-by-two matrix with zero diagonal and off-diagonal elements \(-(t_1+t_2 e^{\mp 2ika})\). Its characteristic equation yields

\[E_\pm(k)=\pm\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2+2t_1t_2\cos(2ka)},\qquad |k|\leq\pi/(2a).\]

At the zone centre the hoppings add. At the new zone edge they subtract, opening a gap \(2|t_1-t_2|\). In the uniform limit the folded branches simply touch. Merely drawing a smaller zone did not change the crystal. It takes a physical alternation to split the states. With two electrons per doubled cell, the lower band is exactly filled when spin is counted. Its electronic energy falls on dimerization at fixed mean hopping: writing \(t_{1,2}=t\pm\delta\) gives \(E_-=-2\sqrt{t^2\cos^2(ka)+\delta^2\sin^2(ka)}\), which is at most the undistorted lower energy. The lattice also pays an elastic cost. Quantitative distortion amplitudes require that competing energy.

For \(t_1=1.20\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(t_2=0.80\,\mathrm{eV}\) the zone-centre energies remain \(\pm2.00\,\mathrm{eV}\), while the zone-edge energies become \(\pm0.40\,\mathrm{eV}\). The minimum direct excitation costs \(0.80\,\mathrm{eV}\), corresponding to a vacuum wavelength of about \(1.55\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) if the optical transition is allowed. The cause of this insulating filling is the pairing of bonds. Away from the commensurate filling the chemical potential can lie inside a band even if the same structural splitting remains visible.

Follow a wave packet through the whole Brillouin zone

In a weak static field a band electron's crystal momentum changes even though its group velocity need not keep rising. Take one isolated tight-binding band \(E(k)=E_0-2t\cos(ka)\), positive hopping energy \(t\), lattice spacing \(a\) and electron charge \(-e\). Let the wave packet be narrow in \(k\) compared with the zone and neglect scattering and transitions to other bands. The semiclassical force equation is \(\hbar\dot k=-e\mathcal E\), while velocity follows from the band gradient: \(v=(2ta/\hbar)\sin(ka)\).

Starting at \(k=0\) yields \(ka=-\omega_B t_{\rm lab}\) with Bloch angular frequency \(\omega_B=e\mathcal E a/\hbar\). Integrating velocity over laboratory time gives

\[x(t_{\rm lab})-x(0)=\frac{2t}{e\mathcal E}[\cos(\omega_Bt_{\rm lab})-1],\qquad T_B=\frac{h}{e\mathcal E a}.\]

Although the field never changes sign, velocity reverses when the packet samples regions of opposite band slope. Over a complete zone traversal there is no net displacement because the band energy is periodic. Energy conservation offers another derivation: the electrostatic potential-energy change of a negatively charged electron in a uniform field is \(+e\mathcal E\Delta x\), offsetting the change in band energy. The maximal excursion equals the band width \(4t\) divided by the force magnitude.

Estimate an observable oscillation in a superlattice

Use a one-dimensional superlattice with \(a=8.00\) nm, hopping \(t=5.00\) meV and field \(\mathcal E=2.00\times10^5\) V/m. The potential-energy change per lattice period is \(e\mathcal E a=1.60\) meV. Thus \(T_B=h/(1.60\,{\rm meV})=2.58\) ps and the peak-to-peak excursion is \(20.0\,{\rm meV}/(e\mathcal E)=100\) nm, about 12.5 lattice periods. The maximum speed from the band derivative is \(2ta/\hbar=1.22\times10^5\) m/s. At one quarter cycle velocity reaches its largest negative magnitude, while at half a cycle the electron reaches its furthest position and momentarily stops.

A momentum-relaxation time of 0.20 ps would interrupt most trajectories before a cycle completes, obscuring a Bloch oscillation despite the periodic dispersion. Conversely a scattering time several times longer than 2.58 ps would make coherent oscillatory transport plausible if the field does not tunnel carriers into other bands. In the weak-field limit the period and excursion both grow, so the mathematical limit of arbitrarily large excursions cannot be taken in a finite imperfect specimen. Recording the oscillation frequency as field is varied tests the predicted linear scaling separately from the much more sample-dependent amplitude.

The motion also clarifies the role of negative effective mass near a band top. The electron's crystal momentum continues to change in the direction of the applied force throughout the period. Its acceleration is proportional to the local band curvature, however, and can point in the opposite direction once that curvature changes sign. This reverses packet acceleration without a sudden elastic collision with a particular lattice plane. A spectrum measured along the direction of motion can predict the velocity through its local slope, giving a direct cross-check of the acceleration inferred from a time-domain trace.

The probe must resolve the oscillations before relaxation for that comparison to be meaningful.

Predicting a gap and testing it in a periodic medium

Condensed matter and band theory

Combine the translation argument, a measurable band edge and an independent numerical calculation to distinguish a gap from a folded crossing.

From first principles

Infer pass bands and stop bands from a repeated cell

For a normally incident monochromatic plane wave in a lossless layered dielectric, record its tangential electric and magnetic fields in a two-component state. Interface conditions transmit these same tangential fields between adjacent layers. Propagation through one complete cell of period \(a\) is a linear map represented by a matrix \(M(\omega)\). Forward and backward propagation in each lossless layer have reciprocal phase factors, so the cell transfer matrix has determinant one. A Bloch state reproduces its field after one cell up to a multiplier:

\[Mv=\lambda v,\]
\[\lambda=e^{ika},\]
\[\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}M)\lambda+1=0 .\]

The second root is \(1/\lambda\). Adding the two roots and using the phase relation gives the band condition

\[\cos(ka)=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr} M(\omega).\]

For a real lossless layered system the trace is real. Where its half lies between −1 and 1 there are real wave numbers. Outside this interval the two multipliers have different magnitudes and one solution decays with each successive cell. In a uniform material the trace simply reproduces homogeneous propagation: folding the dispersion into the first zone can make branches touch without opening a gap. Index contrast allows repeated reflections and can create finite stop bands.

Derivation and applications

Find the long-wavelength optical index of a layered cell

A finite wavelength can resolve two different dielectrics in a repeating stack. Far below the first band edge, what uniform medium would reproduce the phase accumulated per cell? Consider lossless, nonmagnetic layers of thickness \(d_1,d_2\), real refractive indices \(n_1,n_2\), and cell length \(a=d_1+d_2\). At normal incidence the field is tangential to the interfaces. Define \(k_0=\omega/c\) and the phase thickness \(\delta_j=k_0n_jd_j\). For Bloch wavevector \(K\), the exact trace relation is

\[\cos(Ka)=\cos\delta_1\cos\delta_2-\frac12\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}+\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)\sin\delta_1\sin\delta_2.\]

On the acoustic-like branch through zero frequency, all three phase angles are small. Keep the quadratic terms in each cosine and the product of the linear terms in the sines. Constant terms on both sides cancel. Substitution of the optical phases gives

\[(Ka)^2\simeq k_0^2\left[n_1^2d_1^2+n_2^2d_2^2+(n_1^2+n_2^2)d_1d_2\right].\]

The mixed term records the interfaces even at long wavelengths. Factor the bracket as \(a(n_1^2d_1+n_2^2d_2)\) and take the forward-propagating root. Writing the first layer’s thickness fraction as \(f=d_1/a\) yields

\[n_{\rm eff}=\lim_{\omega\to0}\frac{K}{k_0}=\sqrt{fn_1^2+(1-f)n_2^2}.\]

This is the thickness-weighted average of the relative permittivities under a square root. It agrees with a tangential electric field that is nearly uniform across a small cell. As a thickness fraction tends to unity the remaining material’s index is recovered. Near a band edge the omitted fourth-order terms in the trace matter, so a constant effective index cannot predict the gap.

Worked example

Measure the phase of a deeply subwavelength stack

A cell contains \(10.0\,\mathrm{nm}\) of index 2.00 and \(30.0\,\mathrm{nm}\) of index 1.50. Its effective index is \(\sqrt{0.25(4)+0.75(2.25)}=1.64\). At vacuum wavelength \(4.00\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), ten cells accumulate Bloch phase \(2\pi n_{\rm eff}(0.400/4.00)=1.03\) radians. The larger layer phase per cell is just \(0.071\) radians, consistent with the expansion.

Infer a layer fraction from a low-frequency slope

Another stack uses indices 3.00 and 1.50. Its long-wavelength measured phase velocity is \(c/2.10\). Solving the squared-index average gives \(f=(2.10^2-1.50^2)/(3.00^2-1.50^2)=0.320\). Transfer a field by one period

Consider a lossless stack of two nonmagnetic dielectrics at normal incidence. Layer \(j\) has real refractive index \(n_j\), thickness \(d_j\) and optical phase \(\delta_j=2\pi f n_j d_j/c\). Within a layer the electric field is a sum of forward and backward waves; the corresponding magnetic field has the opposite sign for the backward wave. Eliminating the wave amplitudes between the two faces gives the layer transfer matrix for electric field and magnetic field scaled by the vacuum impedance (one time-phase convention):

\[M_j=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta_j&i\sin\delta_j/n_j\\i n_j\sin\delta_j&\cos\delta_j\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det M_j=1 .\]

The tangential fields are continuous at a simple dielectric interface. Thus the matrix of one complete cell of size \(a=d_1+d_2\) is the product of the two layer matrices. Its eigenvalues multiply to one. For propagation in an infinite periodic stack the Bloch multipliers must be \(e^{\pm ika}\). Their sum fixes the half-trace:

\[\cos(ka)=\cos\delta_1\cos\delta_2-\tfrac12(n_1/n_2+n_2/n_1)\sin\delta_1\sin\delta_2 .\]

If the right side lies outside \([-1,1]\), no real \(k\) exists. One solution decays and the reciprocal one grows along the chosen direction. A semi-infinite passive medium selects the decaying solution inside a stop band. A finite sample can still transmit an evanescent field, especially when it contains few cells.

Derive a check that the numerical solver could fail

Set \(n_1=n_2=n\). The right side reduces to \(\cos(\delta_1+\delta_2)\) by the addition formula. The first two folded branches meet where the combined phase is \(\pi\), at \(fa/c=1/(2n)\). There is no interface reflection and no forbidden interval. With \(n=\sqrt{13}\) both branches should meet at about 0.138675. Two nearly equal eigenvalues on a finite mesh must be compared with the numerical resolution before reporting a narrow gap.

As another analytic limit, take each layer a quarter wavelength thick at the same frequency, so \(\delta_1=\delta_2=\pi/2\). The half-trace is \(-\tfrac12(r+r^{-1})\), \(r=n_1/n_2\). Its magnitude exceeds one whenever \(r\ne1\). For \(r=2\) the decaying multiplier has magnitude \(1/2\) per cell. The attenuation here arises from coherent reflections in a lossless model. Small transmission need not mean that electromagnetic energy was dissipated.

Read the finite-element eigenvalues against the calculation

In the OneSimulation band experiment, keep the layer fractions fixed and change only the second permittivity. For the pair (13,12) the two reported zone-edge frequencies are 0.1396 and 0.1433 in reduced units; the gap is about 2.6% of their mean. Compare this separation with the zero-contrast result and with the two roots of the half-trace equation at \(-1\). For the much larger contrast (13,1), using a weak-perturbation approximation would be hard to justify. The transfer matrix still includes repeated scattering exactly for these ideal layers.

When investigating an unfamiliar contrast, halve the spatial mesh size and repeat the edge calculation. Track modes by their fields as well as by sorted eigenvalue if two branches nearly meet. For the scalar one-dimensional normal-incidence problem the observed stop band has a precise meaning. It is an interval of optical frequencies for that propagation geometry. Inferring a complete three-dimensional gap would require other directions and polarisations. The link to electronic bands is the shared translation eigenvalue and coherent coupling at a crossing; electronic filling and the optical transfer-matrix test answer different physical questions.

Turn an infinite stop band into finite-stack reflectance

The forbidden Bloch interval of an infinite periodic medium does not imply perfect reflection from a finite sample. Its termination and number of periods matter. Consider normal incidence on alternating lossless dielectric layers with refractive indices \(n_H,n_L\), each of optical thickness \(\lambda_0/4\) at the selected vacuum wavelength. Treat all permeabilities as \(\mu_0\) and use normalized optical admittance \(Y=n\). Across a quarter-wave layer the characteristic matrix mapping the fields on its far face to its entrance has zero diagonal and off-diagonal entries \(i/Y\) and \(iY\), with an overall phase convention fixed consistently.

For a terminating medium of admittance \(Y_{\rm load}\) the resulting input admittance is \(Y_{\rm layer}^2/Y_{\rm load}\). Applying this inversion first to the rear low-index layer and then to the front high-index layer multiplies load admittance by \((n_H/n_L)^2\). After \(N\) identical pairs, with the high-index layer first as seen by incident light,

\[Y_{\rm in}=n_s(n_H/n_L)^{2N},\qquad R=\left|\frac{n_0-Y_{\rm in}}{n_0+Y_{\rm in}}\right|^2 .\]

Here \(n_s\) is the substrate index and \(n_0\) the incident medium index. This design-frequency result follows from phase accumulation in each layer. For a general wavelength the matrices are no longer simple inversions and both diagonal entries must be retained. Interchanging the order of high and low layers in a finite stack also changes the input admittance and reflection phase.

Select a period count for a required mirror

Let \(n_0=n_s=1.00\), \(n_H=2.00\) and \(n_L=1.50\) at \(\lambda_0=600\) nm. Quarter-wave physical thicknesses are \(600/(4\times2.00)=75.0\) nm and \(600/(4\times1.50)=100\) nm. Four pairs give \(Y_{\rm in}=(4/3)^8=9.99\) and \(R=[(9.99-1)/(9.99+1)]^2=0.669\). Eight pairs give \(Y_{\rm in}\simeq99.8\) and \(R=0.961\). Thus a fairly clear infinite-medium stop band can coexist with substantial transmission through a short specimen.

For at least 99.0 percent reflectance at the design wavelength the required admittance ratio is \((1+\sqrt{0.990})/(1-\sqrt{0.990})\simeq398\). Solving \((4/3)^{2N}\ge398\) gives \(N\ge10.4\), hence eleven complete pairs. If the indices become equal and match both external media, \(Y_{\rm in}=n_0\) for any period count and the reflection vanishes. This finite-stack result tests a different observable from an infinite cell eigenfrequency. It connects the gap calculation to a transmission or reflectance measurement while exposing the additional data, particularly termination and thickness, needed for that prediction.

At the chosen wavelength the reflection amplitude for high-index-first ordering is negative because the effective admittance exceeds the incident admittance. Measuring reflected intensity alone loses this phase sign. Interference with a calibrated reference reflector can recover the sign and identify termination differences even when two samples have similar reflectances. This matters when the periodic mirror forms one end of a resonant cavity: the reflection phase contributes to the total round-trip phase and can move the resonance frequency. A pair of finite mirrors can therefore share a stop-band range while giving different cavity phase conditions.

Derive the quarter-wave stop-band width

The finite mirror is easy to evaluate at the design wavelength. The same stack admits an analytic expression for the width of its infinite first stop band. In both layers the optical phase thickness is \(\delta=(\pi/2)(\omega/\omega_0)\) when refractive indices are treated as nondispersive. The one-period transfer matrix has unit determinant and half-trace \(\cos(Ka)\), where \(K\) is the Bloch wavenumber and \(a\) the physical period. Multiplication of the two layer matrices gives

\[\cos(Ka)=\cos^2\delta-\frac12\left(\frac{n_H}{n_L}+\frac{n_L}{n_H}\right)\sin^2\delta .\]

At band centre the half-trace is less than minus one for unequal indices, so \(K\) has a nonzero imaginary component. At either neighbouring edge set the half-trace equal to minus one and solve for \(\delta\). The two edge phases are \(\pi/2\pm\arcsin[(n_H-n_L)/(n_H+n_L)]\) for \(n_H>n_L\). Their separation gives fractional angular-frequency width

\[\frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0}=\frac4\pi\arcsin\left(\frac{n_H-n_L}{n_H+n_L}\right).\]

With indices 2.00 and 1.50 this is \(0.183\). A central frequency of 500 THz thus has edges near 454 and 546 THz. The frequency interval is symmetric about the centre in this ideal quarter-wave construction; the corresponding wavelength interval is asymmetric because wavelength is reciprocal to frequency. As the two indices merge, the edge separation vanishes even though an artificially repeated unit cell still has a Brillouin-zone boundary.

At centre the decaying Bloch amplitude falls by \(n_L/n_H=0.750\) per period. A physical period of 175 nm therefore gives a field-amplitude decay length \(175/\ln(2/1.5)=608\) nm. This infinite-medium length explains the strong dependence of the finite mirror on period count. The external-medium matching factors are still needed to turn that internal decay into the actual reflected intensity.

How alternating bonds split a one-dimensional band

Condensed matter and band theory

Two periodic chains contain precisely the same number of atoms at the same spacing. One has equal bonds and the other alternates strong and weak bonds. The difference changes which electron energies can propagate, even without changing the on-site energy of any atom.

Choose a primitive cell that repeats the bonds

Let a cell of length \(a\) contain orbitals A and B. An electron hops between A and B in the same cell with real amplitude \(t_1>0\), and between B and A in the next cell with amplitude \(t_2>0\). Use one orbital per site, neglect interactions and set both on-site energies to zero. We can absorb an overall hopping sign into the B basis state. Translation by one cell multiplies each component by \(e^{ika}\), although the relative amplitudes on A and B remain unknown. Writing those amplitudes as \(A,B\) turns the real-space nearest-neighbour equations into

\[EA=(t_1+t_2e^{-ika})B,\]
\[EB=(t_1+t_2e^{ika})A.\]

The cell boundary crossed by the second hopping accounts for its phase. The two coefficients are complex conjugates as required by Hermiticity. Substitute the second relation into the first without dividing by energy. A nonzero component then requires \(E^2=(t_1+t_2e^{ika})(t_1+t_2e^{-ika})\). The two energies at each wavevector are

\[E_\pm(k)=\pm\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2+2t_1t_2\cos ka}.\]

For positive hoppings their magnitude is largest at \(k=0\), namely \(t_1+t_2\), and smallest at \(ka=\pi\), namely \(|t_1-t_2|\). The gap is \(2|t_1-t_2|\). At one spinless electron per cell the lower band is full, so this is also the independent-particle excitation gap in a large periodic chain.

Read the band formation from two limits

Take \(t_1=1.0\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(t_2=0.6\,\mathrm{eV}\). The lower band runs from \(-1.6\) to \(-0.4\,\mathrm{eV}\), the upper from \(0.4\) to \(1.6\,\mathrm{eV}\). The gap is \(0.8\,\mathrm{eV}\), while each bandwidth is \(1.2\,\mathrm{eV}\). If \(t_2\) tends to zero, the cell becomes an isolated pair with two discrete bonding energies \(\pm t_1\). Bands flatten because cells can no longer exchange probability. At \(t_1=t_2=t\), the spectrum becomes \(\pm2t|\cos(ka/2)|\) in the reduced zone. The two pieces meet at its edge. The two-site construction then folds the simple uniform chain into a smaller wavevector interval.

One can also read off a velocity. Away from a crossing, differentiate \(E_\pm\) to obtain \(v_\pm=\mp a t_1t_2\sin(ka)/[\hbar|E_\pm|]\). At extrema of a gapped band it vanishes. At the equal-hopping crossing the derivative written as a ratio has a removable directional ambiguity, because the ordered upper and lower bands meet with opposite slopes.

Exchange the two bonds

Keep \(t_1\) fixed and move \(t_2\) through it. Compare both the gap and the bandwidth. Interchanging the numerical values of the two hoppings exactly preserves the bulk energies. The experiment in the next chapter distinguishes these patterns by opening the chain at a specified bond. A termination supplies physical information that the unordered hopping pair and the energy graph alone cannot encode.

Finding an end state one bond at a time

Condensed matter and band theory

The bulk spectrum of an alternating chain is unchanged when strong and weak bonds exchange places. A boundary with a named first site breaks this equivalence. The zero-energy equations provide its wavefunction without diagonalising a large matrix.

Start at an exposed A site

Use the alternating chain with intracell link \(t_1\) and intercell link \(t_2\), both positive. End the left edge at \(A_1\). The first B site connects \(A_1\) and \(A_2\). Seek an eigenstate at \(E=0\) supported only on A sites. Each A-site equation then holds because all neighbouring B amplitudes vanish. Each B-site equation requires cancellation between its two neighbouring A amplitudes. Hence

\[0=t_1 A_j+t_2 A_{j+1},\]
\[A_j=(-t_1/t_2)^{j-1}A_1.\]

Relative signs matter in the cancellation, even though a local probability measurement sees only squared magnitudes. On a semi-infinite chain the geometric series for the norm converges only for \(r=t_1/t_2<1\). Normalising gives \(|A_1|^2=1-r^2\). The amplitude decay length satisfies \(e^{-a/\ell}=r\), so \(\ell=a/\ln(t_2/t_1)\). The probability decay length is half that. As the bulk gap closes, the decay length diverges and the bound state spreads into the interior.

Impose the far boundary too

A finite chain can have \(M\) A sites and \(M-1\) B sites, ending on A at both edges. Every B site still imposes exactly the recurrence above, leaving one overall free amplitude. The resulting state has exactly zero energy for any positive hoppings. Its finite norm is the sum of \(M\) successive powers of \(r^2\). At \(r=1\) the apparent zero over zero in the closed formula is resolved by summing \(M\) unit terms. The state then has probability \(1/M\) at every A site.

When \(r>1\) its norm remains finite in this finite sample and the weight accumulates at the right edge. An equal-sublattice finite chain ending on B instead has an additional boundary equation. Two exponentially localised end solutions can then hybridise, shifting their energies slightly above and below zero. Equal sublattice counts alone provide no exact zero eigenvalue. A change of boundary conditions can therefore alter the interpretation of a measured near-zero peak.

Connect the recurrence to the bulk phase

The off-diagonal matrix element \(h=t_1+t_2e^{ika}\) traces a circle centred at \(t_1\) with radius \(t_2\) as \(k\) crosses the Brillouin zone. If \(t_2>t_1\) it winds once around zero. If \(t_2<t_1\) it misses zero. Passing between the two with fixed cell choice requires the gap \(2|h|\) to close. A diagonal potential on just one sublattice would break the off-diagonal symmetry used to pin energies to zero. The winding statement and the boundary argument require a consistent assignment of the unit cell and termination.

For \(r=0.5\) the first three semi-infinite A probabilities are 0.75, 0.1875 and 0.046875. For \(r=0.90\), the first site has only 0.19 and the amplitude decay length increases from \(1.44a\) to \(9.49a\). Use the finite odd chain below to move the density from one end to the other. At reciprocal hopping ratios the two distributions must be mirror images. Check this using the endpoint readouts.

Why a honeycomb lattice has Dirac cones

Condensed matter and band theory

In an alternating chain the two paths from A to B can cancel at a special wavevector. The honeycomb lattice adds a third path. Their cancellation now occurs at isolated points in a two-dimensional Brillouin zone and produces a linear low-energy dispersion.

Add the three Bloch phases

Each honeycomb A site has three nearest B neighbours at displacements \(d(0,1)\), \(d(\sqrt3/2,-1/2)\), and \(d(-\sqrt3/2,-1/2)\), where \(d\) is the bond length. For equal real hopping magnitude \(t\), sum the translation phase for each displacement. The common off-diagonal factor is

\[f(\boldsymbol k)=e^{ik_yd}+2e^{-ik_yd/2}\cos(\sqrt3 k_xd/2),\]
\[E_\pm=\pm t|f|.\]

This uses a basis phase convention that includes the orbitals' intra-cell positions. A rephasing can change the phase of \(f\) but leaves its magnitude invariant. Along \(k_y=0\), cancellation occurs where \(1+2\cos(\sqrt3 k_xd/2)=0\). Choose \(K=(4\pi/(3\sqrt3d),0)\). The counterpart at \(-K\) is inequivalent up to primitive reciprocal vectors. Near either valley many wavevectors represent distinct states even when their energies almost coincide.

Linearise at a zero, then test the approximation

Write \(\boldsymbol k=K+\boldsymbol q\) with \(qd\ll1\). At \(K\) the cosine equals \(-1/2\) and the sine \(\sqrt3/2\). Differentiating the cosine term with respect to \(k_x\) consequently gives \(-3d/2\). Differentiating the two exponential factors with respect to \(k_y\) gives \(id+id/2=3id/2\). The constant terms cancel. Therefore

\[f(K+\boldsymbol q)\simeq \tfrac{3d}{2}(-q_x+iq_y),\]
\[E_\pm\simeq\pm\hbar v_F|\boldsymbol q|,\]
\[v_F=\frac{3td}{2\hbar}.\]

With \(t=2.7\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(d=0.142\,\mathrm{nm}\) and \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{eV\,fs}\), the speed is \(0.874\,\mathrm{nm/fs}=8.74\times10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\). At \(qd=0.05\) the leading energy magnitude is \(0.2025\,\mathrm{eV}\). The energy varies linearly with momentum displacement even though the microscopic Hamiltonian contains only hopping between neighbours. The two components describe sublattice amplitude, distinct from the real electron spin.

Break the equivalence of the sublattices

Assign on-site energies \(+\Delta\) to A and \(-\Delta\) to B. The characteristic equation of the two-by-two Hamiltonian becomes \(E^2=\Delta^2+t^2|f|^2\). A gap \(2|\Delta|\) opens at K. For \(\Delta=0.10\,\mathrm{eV}\), the edge sits at \(0.10\,\mathrm{eV}\) and the predicted conduction energy at \(qd=0.05\) becomes about \(0.226\,\mathrm{eV}\). Far enough from the origin the slopes recover their nearly linear values. Close to it, expansion of the square root gives \(E_+\simeq|\Delta|+\hbar^2v_F^2q^2/(2|\Delta|)\). A band-edge mass \(|\Delta|/v_F^2\) has emerged. The expansion requires \(\hbar v_Fq\ll|\Delta|\).

Compare the exact positive-energy cut along \(q_x\) with the local cone in the graph. Unequal deviations to the right and left of K are allowed by the lattice and cannot be captured by the lowest-order isotropic cone. The reflected cut at the other valley restores the time-reversal relation.

When a periodic dielectric has no propagating Bloch wave

Fields and the response of matter

Derive a band gap using two lossless transparent materials and distinguish evanescent Bloch propagation from absorption of optical power.

Construct a unit cell whose waves interfere

Stack two transparent, lossless, nonmagnetic dielectrics periodically in one dimension. Their refractive indices are \(n_1,n_2\), their physical thicknesses \(d_1,d_2\), and the period \(a=d_1+d_2\). Work at normal incidence, so polarisation directions are equivalent for isotropic layers. Each separate material admits a propagating wave at the chosen optical frequency, yet the periodic stack may not. The effect comes from coherent interface reflections, not from a negative refractive index or an absorptive constituent.

Describe the local field by its tangential electric and magnetic components. Propagation through one homogeneous layer connects them by a \(2\times2\) transfer matrix with diagonal \(\cos\phi_j\), off-diagonal terms proportional to \(iZ_j\sin\phi_j\) and \(i\sin\phi_j/Z_j\), where \(\phi_j=\omega n_jd_j/c\) and \(Z_j=Z_0/n_j\). Changing the chosen propagation direction or time convention may reverse the two imaginary signs together. The real trace and unit determinant of the cell matrix below are unaffected.

Read the dispersion relation from the trace

At an interface both tangential components are continuous in the absence of imposed sheet currents. Multiply the two propagation matrices to form one cell. Its determinant is one because each layer's determinant is \(\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi=1\). Thus the two eigenvalues are reciprocal. For a Bloch mode they are \(e^{\pm i k_Ba}\) on a pass band. Taking half the trace gives

\[\cos(k_Ba)=\cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2-\frac12\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}+\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)\sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2 .\]

When the right side lies between −1 and 1 a real Bloch phase exists. If its magnitude exceeds one, a decaying and a growing Bloch factor replace a pair of unit-modulus factors. For a bounded excitation of a semi-infinite stack choose the decaying direction. The attenuation per cell is \(\operatorname{arcosh}|\operatorname{trace}(M)/2|\). A lossless semi-infinite crystal in a stop band reflects incident power. Spatial attenuation of a Bloch amplitude in this region is compatible with zero volumetric heat production.

Obtain a quantitative stop-band width

Choose \(n_1d_1=n_2d_2=\lambda_0/4\). Writing \(s=\omega/\omega_0\), both layer phases are \(\phi=\pi s/2\). At \(s=1\) the half trace is \(-\tfrac12(n_2/n_1+n_1/n_2)\). Recognising \(\cosh(\log r)=(r+r^{-1})/2\) gives centre attenuation \(|\log(n_2/n_1)|\) per cell. For \(n_1=1.5,n_2=2.0\) this is 0.2877, so a decaying eigencomponent has amplitude factor 0.75 per cell. This statement about an eigencomponent should not be confused with the exact transmittance of a specific finite stack, which also depends on entrance and exit impedances.

The first gap edges obey half trace −1. With \(\cos^2\phi=1-\sin^2\phi\), solve for the edge sine or cosine. The two edge phases are symmetric about \(\pi/2\) and satisfy \(|\cos\phi|=|n_2-n_1|/(n_1+n_2)\). Hence the fractional angular-frequency width is \((4/\pi)\arcsin(|n_2-n_1|/(n_1+n_2))\), about 0.183 for the example. The lowest gap width tends continuously to zero as index contrast vanishes. A gap in a plotted numerical spectrum at \(n_1=n_2\) would indicate an implementation or plotting problem.

Partition functions and thermal fluctuations

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Calculate the occupation, heat capacity and energy fluctuations of a two-level system from a single partition function.

From first principles

Obtain Boltzmann weights by counting the bath’s available states

Put a small system in weak thermal contact with a much larger bath. Treat the combined pair as isolated with energy \(E_{\rm tot}\). If the small system occupies a particular microstate \(i\) of energy \(E_i\), the bath must have energy \(E_{\rm tot}-E_i\). The probability of that microstate is proportional to the number of bath states compatible with the remaining energy,

\[P_i\propto\Omega_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_i)=\exp[S_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_i)/k_B].\]

A large bath changes temperature negligibly over the small system’s range of exchanged energies. Expand its entropy to first order, using \(\partial S_{\rm bath}/\partial E=1/T\); an arbitrary energy reference contributes only a common factor. Normalizing the resulting probabilities gives

\[P_i=\frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z},\]
\[Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j},\]
\[\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.\]

The sum called the partition function counts every microstate, so equal-energy states contribute separately. Differentiating it yields the mean energy \(U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z\) when the energy levels are temperature independent. A constant shift of all levels changes \(Z\) and \(U\) but leaves the normalized probabilities unchanged.

Derivation and applications

Measure a static response from spontaneous fluctuations

Consider a coordinate \(x\) that can exchange energy with a heat bath at fixed temperature \(T\). A weak constant force \(f\) changes its energy from \(H_0\) to \(H_f=H_0-fx\). This sign gives lower energy to positive displacement under a positive force. Assume that the accessible states themselves do not depend on the force and that the instrument allows enough time for the system to sample equilibrium. Write \(d\Gamma\) for a sum or integral over states and \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). Differentiation of the Boltzmann weight gives

\[Z(f)=\int e^{-\beta(H_0-fx)}d\Gamma,\]
\[\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial f}=\beta\langle x\rangle_f .\]

A second derivative acts on both the weighted numerator for the mean and its normalization. The quotient rule subtracts the square of the mean. Hence the static compliance, measured with temperature held fixed, obeys

\[\left(\frac{\partial\langle x\rangle_f}{\partial f}\right)_T=\beta\left(\langle x^2\rangle_f-\langle x\rangle_f^2\right).\]

For a harmonic confining potential \(H_0=kx^2/2\), with spring constant \(k>0\), completing the square makes the interpretation especially direct. The position distribution remains Gaussian and its centre shifts with the load,

\[\tfrac12kx^2-fx=\tfrac12 k(x-f/k)^2-\frac{f^2}{2k},\]
\[\langle x\rangle_f=\frac{f}{k},\]
\[\operatorname{var}x=\frac{k_BT}{k}.\]

The same stiffness controls response and equilibrium noise. For an anharmonic potential the general variance identity still gives the local response at the current force, although the compliance can change at larger loads. A finite exposure time can average away rapid motion, so measured positions must resolve the fluctuating coordinate.

Worked example

Calibrate a bead trap at thermal equilibrium

A bead suspended in a harmonic optical trap at \(295\,\mathrm K\) has a measured one-coordinate standard deviation of \(40.0\,\mathrm{nm}\). Its stiffness is \(k=k_BT/(40.0\times10^{-9})^2=2.55\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N/m}\). A steady force of \(0.100\,\mathrm{pN}\) should shift the mean by \(39.3\,\mathrm{nm}\). Resolving such a shift requires averaging over many statistically independent positions.

Second application

Predict noise from a measured mechanical compliance

A second device near a fixed bias force responds to an extra \(0.500\,\mathrm{pN}\) with a mean displacement of \(10.0\,\mathrm{nm}\). Its local compliance is \(2.00\times10^4\,\mathrm{m/N}\). At \(310\,\mathrm K\) the expected displacement standard deviation is \(\sqrt{k_BT(2.00\times10^4)}=9.25\,\mathrm{nm}\). This inference uses only local linear response and thermal equilibrium, so it remains applicable even if the device is nonlinear over a larger displacement range.

Put a small system in a heat bath

A spectroscopic probe may resolve two energy levels even when a bulk thermometer gives only one temperature. Consider many independent units with ground energy zero and excitation energy \(\Delta>0\). Each unit has two nondegenerate states and is weakly coupled to a much larger bath at temperature \(T\). Weak coupling lets us assign energies to the unit while still permitting energy exchange over the measurement time.

Under the canonical distribution the probability of a microstate \(j\) is \(p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). The factor \(Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\) normalises probabilities. This form comes from expanding the bath entropy about its energy before the exchange: the number of bath states compatible with system energy \(E_j\) is proportional to \(\exp[-E_j/(k_BT)]\). A bath with appreciable temperature change after one exchange would call for a finite-reservoir correction.

Differentiate the normalisation

The mean energy is \(\sum_j p_j E_j\). Differentiating \(\ln Z\) with respect to \(\beta\) inserts a factor \(-E_j\) in the weighted sum, giving \(\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\ln Z\). Differentiating a second time produces \(\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2\), which is the energy variance. Changing variables from \(\beta\) to \(T\) produces the fluctuation relation

\[C=\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T}=\frac{\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2}{k_BT^2}.\]

Hold fixed the external parameters that set the energy levels while taking this derivative. A change of magnetic field or volume can shift the levels and introduce an additional energy response. For independent identically prepared units, variances add. The standard deviation of total energy grows as the square root of the number of units, while its mean grows in proportion to that number. Relative fluctuations consequently shrink as a macroscopic sample is assembled.

Locate the heat-capacity peak

For one two-level unit, \(Z=1+e^{-x}\) with \(x=\Delta/(k_BT)\). Its excited-state probability is \(p=1/(1+e^x)\). Hence \(\langle E\rangle=\Delta p\), the variance is \(\Delta^2p(1-p)\) and the heat capacity is \(C/k_B=x^2e^x/(1+e^x)^2\). At low temperature the excited population is exponentially small and little energy can be absorbed by a small temperature increase. At high temperature the two populations approach one half, so changing temperature redistributes them only weakly. A maximum must occur between these limits.

Setting the derivative of \(\ln C\) with respect to \(x\) to zero gives \(2/x=\tanh(x/2)\). Its positive solution is about 2.40, placing the peak at \(T\simeq0.417\Delta/k_B\). This broadened heat-capacity feature is the Schottky anomaly of a two-level population. No collective phase transition is required to produce it. Degeneracy of either level would change the population ratio and modify the peak position.

Infer an energy scale and observe a fluctuation

Take \(\Delta=12.0\) meV, so \(\Delta/k_B\simeq139\) K. A peak near 58 K is expected. At \(T=139\) K one has \(x\simeq1\), \(p=0.269\), mean energy 3.23 meV and \(C\simeq0.197k_B\) per unit. For a single unit the standard deviation of the energy is \(\Delta\sqrt{p(1-p)}=5.32\) meV. The standard deviation exceeding the mean is possible because most single observations return the ground energy and a minority return the full excitation energy.

For a million independent units at the same temperature, the mean total energy scales up by a million and the standard deviation by a thousand. A calorimeter measuring the bulk value will therefore see a much smoother response than a single-unit spectroscopic probe. If measured fluctuations scale faster than this independent-unit prediction, correlations, common temperature fluctuations or instrumental noise deserve investigation before assigning a microscopic heat capacity.

For a noninteracting collection the total partition function factorizes into one factor per unit. Taking its logarithm converts that product into a sum, so mean energy and heat capacity become extensive. The variance of total energy also grows in proportion to the number of units when correlations vanish. The standard deviation grows only as the square root of that number, explaining how a large specimen can display a reproducible macroscopic energy while its microscopic constituents exchange energy continuously with the bath.

Predict particle-number fluctuations in an open subsystem

The canonical partition function sums over energies for a fixed particle number. A small region that exchanges both particles and energy with a large bath requires another weight. If the region gains energy \(E\) and \(N\) particles at fixed volume, the bath entropy changes to first order by \((-E+\mu N)/T\), from \(dS=(dU-\mu\,dN)/T\). With \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) and fixed reservoir chemical potential \(\mu\), sum over the states of each particle-number sector,

\[\Xi(T,V,\mu)=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}e^{\beta\mu N} Z_N(T,V),\qquad \langle N\rangle=\partial_{\beta\mu}\ln\Xi,\qquad \operatorname{var}N=\partial_{\beta\mu}^2\ln\Xi .\]

These derivatives are taken at fixed temperature and volume. Each derivative brings down a factor of \(N\) from the exponential, and differentiating the normalization supplies the subtraction of \(\langle N\rangle^2\). The grand potential is \(-k_BT\ln\Xi\). Number fluctuations therefore measure a thermodynamic response, \(\operatorname{var}N=k_BT(\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu)_{T,V}\).

Suppose 200 independent adsorption sites are exposed to a large particle reservoir. Each site can be empty with zero energy or contain one particle of energy \(\epsilon\). The single-site grand sum is \(1+e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}\) and the sample sum is its 200th power. Choose \(\mu-\epsilon=-k_BT\ln3\): occupancy probability is \(1/4\), mean population is 50, variance is \(200(1/4)(3/4)=37.5\) and standard deviation is 6.12 particles. Doubling the number of sites doubles mean and variance, while relative fluctuations fall by \(\sqrt2\). If the entire sample were sealed with exactly 50 particles, its total-number variance would instead be zero. A small observation window inside it could still fluctuate.

Count rotational states of a diatomic molecule

A partition sum can also show how a continuous-looking thermal degree of freedom switches on. Model a heteronuclear diatomic molecule as a rigid rotor of fixed bond length and moment of inertia \(I\) about an axis perpendicular to the bond. Ignore rotation about the bond, vibrational excitation, electronic excitation and intermolecular interactions. Its quantum levels are \(E_J=\hbar^2J(J+1)/(2I)=k_B\Theta_rJ(J+1)\), with degeneracy \(2J+1\) from the independent projections of angular momentum. Thus

\[Z_{\rm rot}=\sum_{J=0}^\infty(2J+1)e^{-\Theta_rJ(J+1)/T},\qquad \Theta_r=\frac{\hbar^2}{2Ik_B}.\]

At temperatures large compared with \(\Theta_r\), many \(J\) values contribute and the sum is well approximated by an integral. Substitute \(u=J(J+1)\), which supplies \(du=(2J+1)dJ\). The integral is then \(T/\Theta_r\). Differentiating \(\ln Z\) gives \(U_{\rm rot}\simeq k_BT\) and \(C_{\rm rot}\simeq k_B\) per molecule. The two classical quadratic rotational degrees of freedom each supply one half of this energy.

At very low temperature the first excited level lies \(2k_B\Theta_r\) above the ground state and has degeneracy three. Keeping just \(J=0,1\) gives \(Z\simeq1+3e^{-2\Theta_r/T}\). Its excitation probability is the second term divided by \(Z\). The mean rotational energy and heat capacity become exponentially small. If nuclei are identical, exchange symmetry can change allowed rotational populations through nuclear-spin weights; the deliberately heteronuclear model avoids that extra counting condition.

Infer when the first rotational line becomes populated

Use an illustrative rotor with \(\Theta_r=25.0\) K. At \(T=10.0\) K the weight of \(J=1\) is \(3e^{-5}=0.0202\) and that of \(J=2\) is \(5e^{-15}=1.53\times10^{-6}\). Neglecting the latter gives excited fraction \(0.0198\). The gap is \(50.0k_B\) K, giving mean energy per molecule approximately \(0.990k_B\) K. The two-level variance formula gives \(C_{\rm rot}/k_B=(50/10)^2p(1-p)=0.486\). The heat capacity is a derivative and can already be appreciable even when the excited-state fraction is only two percent.

At \(T=250\) K many rotational states contribute and the leading high-temperature sum gives \(Z\simeq10\) and molar rotational heat capacity near \(R\). The line positions also constrain structure. Inverting \(\Theta_r=\hbar^2/(2Ik_B)\) gives \(I=1.61\times10^{-47}\) kg m². With independently known reduced nuclear mass \(\mu=1.50\times10^{-27}\) kg, the effective bond length \(\sqrt{I/\mu}=0.104\) nm. Calorimetry and rotational spectroscopy can thus test the same moment of inertia through distinct observables.

Absolute line intensities provide an additional check on the rotor model. A line originating from the first excited rotational manifold samples its occupation, but a measured signal also contains transition matrix elements and instrument response. To compare with the 1.98-percent prediction, either calibrate those factors or compare the same line across temperatures. The gap can then be estimated from the slope of a logarithmic population ratio against inverse temperature, while the ratio's intercept contains the degeneracy. This separates a genuine error in moment of inertia from an omitted factor counting the allowed projection states.

At higher temperatures vibrational excitation can add to rotational calorimetry. An energy-resolved line measurement helps identify which internal degree of freedom has actually become populated.

Indistinguishable particles and quantum gases

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Use single-state occupancy to derive Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions and estimate when classical gas statistics cease to apply.

From first principles

Derive the mean population of one quantum state

Consider a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\) exchanging particles and energy with a large reservoir at temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\). In a noninteracting gas, occupation \(n\) of this state changes \(E-\mu N\) by \(n(\epsilon-\mu)\). Its statistical weight is \(q^n\), where \(q=e^{-(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}\). For one specified state including its spin label, fermions allow occupations 0 and 1. Bosons allow every nonnegative integer,

\[Z_F=1+q,\]
\[Z_B=1+q+q^2+\cdots=\frac{1}{1-q}.\]

The Bose sum requires \(q<1\). Differentiating a weight with respect to \(q\) brings down its occupancy, so the normalized average is \(q\,\partial_q\ln Z\). Applying this to the two sums gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1},\]
\[\bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

For \(q\ll1\), both means approach \(q\); visits by additional particles to the same state are then rare. The difference between the distributions follows entirely from which occupation numbers the quantum statistics allow.

Derivation and applications

Derive the first exchange correction to ideal-gas pressure

Before quantum degeneracy becomes strong, exchange statistics already affect the relation between pressure and density. Consider noninteracting particles of mass \(m\) in a large three-dimensional box. All \(g\) internal states have the same energy and chemical potential. Introduce fugacity \(z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\), and a sign \(s=+1\) for bosons or \(-1\) for fermions. At small fugacity the occupation of a one-particle energy \(\epsilon\) is a geometric expansion,

\[\bar n(\epsilon)=z e^{-\beta\epsilon}+s z^2e^{-2\beta\epsilon}+O(z^3),\]
\[\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}.\]

Integrating the first term over momentum gives \(g/\lambda_T^3\) times \(z\). In the second term, doubling the inverse temperature narrows each Cartesian Gaussian by \(\sqrt2\). The density integral therefore gains a factor \(2^{-3/2}\). At fixed temperature the Gibbs–Duhem relation \(dp=n\,d\mu\) gives \(d[p/(k_BT)]=n\,dz/z\). Integration from the empty-gas limit gives the pressure series,

\[\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{3/2}}+O(z^3),\]
\[\frac{p\lambda_T^3}{g k_BT}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(z^3).\]

Experiments often fix density. Put \(y=n\lambda_T^3/g\) and invert the density series once to get \(z=y-s y^2/2^{3/2}+O(y^3)\). Substituting into the pressure eliminates the unmeasured chemical potential,

\[\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1-\frac{s}{2^{5/2}}\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}+O(y^2).\]

At fixed density exchange raises the pressure of fermions and lowers that of bosons. The magnitude grows on cooling because the thermal wavelength increases. Real interactions can also alter the pressure, and the truncated expansion requires small \(y\).

Worked example

Estimate a pressure excess for a dilute Fermi gas

A spin-balanced ideal Fermi gas has \(g=2\), \(n=2.00\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(\lambda_T=400\,\mathrm{nm}\). It has \(y=0.0640\) and \(p/(nk_BT)\simeq1.0113\). A measured classical pressure scale \(nk_BT=20.0\,\mathrm{pPa}\) would therefore acquire an exchange correction of about \(0.226\,\mathrm{pPa}\).

Second application

Recover density from a bosonic pressure deficit

A single-state Bose gas at known \(\lambda_T=500\,\mathrm{nm}\) gives \(p/(nk_BT)=0.9950\). To leading order the deficit fixes \(y=0.0050\times2^{5/2}=0.0283\). The inferred density is \(y/\lambda_T^3=2.26\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). At unchanged temperature halving this density halves the fractional pressure deficit, whereas the absolute pressure correction falls by a factor of four.

Decide which exchanges leave the state unchanged

An ideal classical gas can often be treated with Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. Cooling the gas or increasing its density eventually makes wave packets overlap appreciably. For indistinguishable quantum particles, merely assigning names to exchanged particles must not create extra states. In an ideal gas one can instead specify the occupation of each single-particle state. Identical fermions occupy a given quantum state at most once, including its spin label. Identical bosons can accumulate in the same state.

Use a reservoir that exchanges both energy and particles with the system. Its temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\) are fixed. A microstate with energy \(E\) and particle number \(N\) has grand-canonical weight proportional to \(\exp[-\beta(E-\mu N)]\). This follows by applying the thermodynamic entropy differential to a reservoir that loses both \(E\) and \(N\). The chemical potential sets the mean particle number through these statistical weights.

Sum the allowed occupancies of one state

For a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\), define \(z_\epsilon=\exp[-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)]\). An occupancy of \(n\) contributes weight \(z_\epsilon^n\). For fermions the single-state grand partition sum is \(1+z_\epsilon\), so the mean occupancy is \(z_\epsilon/(1+z_\epsilon)\). For bosons the sum runs over all nonnegative integers and equals \(1/(1-z_\epsilon)\), provided \(z_\epsilon<1\). Differentiating the logarithm of each sum with respect to \(\ln z_\epsilon\) gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad \bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.\]

These expressions describe one state, not the total population within an energy interval. To get the latter, multiply by the density of states and integrate. If the exponential is large then both occupancies reduce to \(e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\). The upper sign suppresses double occupancy while the lower sign enhances occupation near the lowest available energy.

Count the occupied sphere of a cold electron gas

In a large cubic volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries, allowed wave vectors fill reciprocal space with one state per volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) for each spin. At zero temperature the noninteracting electron gas fills all states inside a sphere of radius \(k_F\) and leaves higher ones empty. Including two spin states per wave vector gives \(N=2V(4\pi k_F^3/3)/(2\pi)^3\), so \(k_F=(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}\). For electrons of mass \(m_e\), the Fermi energy is \(\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m_e)\).

With density \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(k_F=1.33\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\simeq6.77\) eV. The associated temperature \(T_F=E_F/k_B\) is approximately \(7.9\times10^4\) K. At room temperature \(k_BT\) is small compared with this energy, so only a narrow region around the Fermi surface changes occupation noticeably. A classical equipartition estimate of the electron contribution to heat capacity would ignore this restriction.

Test the dilute limit with a cold atomic gas

A useful scale for translational wave packets is the thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\). For a single internal state the dimensionless product \(n\lambda_T^3\) compares the volume per particle with the thermal wave volume. Small values support the dilute classical approximation. Near unity the occupancy of low-energy states can become appreciable and the statistics of indistinguishability matter.

For particles of mass \(6.6\times10^{-27}\) kg at 1.0 K the thermal wavelength is approximately 0.88 nm. At density \(10^{25}\) m⁻³, \(n\lambda_T^3\simeq0.0068\). Reducing temperature to 0.040 K at unchanged density multiplies that product by \(25^{3/2}=125\), giving about 0.85. This is a degeneracy estimate, not a prediction of the phase of a real interacting gas. Once quantum statistics matter, particle interactions, dimensionality and confinement can also affect which phase the experiment actually finds.

Internal degeneracy enters these comparisons through the density per available internal state. Suppose the same total particle density is spread equally across two spin states with identical spatial dispersion. Each can fill the same momentum levels independently. In a cold ideal Fermi gas the Fermi wavevector at fixed total density is then smaller than in a fully spin-polarized gas. A measured pressure change after altering spin populations can therefore contain a statistical contribution even if the mass, volume and total particle count remain fixed.

Determine the capacity of excited Bose states

Ideal bosons can accumulate macroscopically in one spatial state. Consider a uniform three-dimensional noninteracting gas of identical spinless particles with fixed particle number in a large box. Set the lowest energy to zero. The excited-state occupation at energy \(\epsilon\) is \(1/[\exp((\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT))-1]\) and requires \(\mu\le0\). In the thermodynamic limit, counting momentum states in a sphere gives an excited-state density per volume proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\). Consequently, as the density rises, taking \(\mu\) toward zero from below can increase the excited population only up to a finite value.

Explicitly integrate \(d^3p/(2\pi\hbar)^3\) over positive energies \(\epsilon=p^2/(2m)\). At \(\mu=0\), setting \(x=\epsilon/(k_BT)\) reduces the integral to \(\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}/(e^x-1)dx=\Gamma(3/2)\zeta(3/2)\). The result is

\[n_{\rm ex,max}=\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\lambda_T^3},\qquad \lambda_T=\frac h{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}},\qquad \zeta(3/2)=2.6124\ldots .\]

Below the temperature where this capacity equals the total density, additional particles occupy the lowest state. Solving gives \(T_c=(2\pi\hbar^2/mk_B)[n/\zeta(3/2)]^{2/3}\) and \(N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^{3/2}\). The state count explains why a high phase-space density matters. Reaching this regime does not require individual particles to lose their identity at a sharply defined minimum separation.

Estimate the onset temperature in a box

Take \(n=1.00\times10^{20}\) m⁻³ and particle mass \(1.44\times10^{-25}\) kg. At the ideal transition the thermal wavelength is \((2.6124/n)^{1/3}=297\) nm. Inverting its definition gives \(T_c=h^2/(2\pi m k_B\lambda_T^2)=0.398\) μK. At \(T=0.250\) μK, the excited fraction is \((0.250/0.398)^{3/2}=0.498\) and the condensate fraction is approximately 0.502. Doubling the density raises \(T_c\) by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\) at fixed particle mass.

The predicted fraction tends to zero continuously as \(T\) approaches \(T_c\) from below, and tends to one as temperature approaches zero for the ideal gas. A harmonic trapping potential has a different density of states and hence a different temperature exponent from a uniform box. Finite size smooths the mathematical onset; interactions and inaccurate thermometry can shift its inferred location. Comparing the number in the narrow lowest-momentum component with the thermal-cloud population requires that the analysis resolve these two contributions rather than simply count all particles near the centre of a spatial image.

The dimensionality of the gas is as essential as Bose exchange itself. In a uniform two-dimensional ideal box the single-particle density of states tends to a constant at low energy. At chemical potential approaching zero, Bose occupation varies as \(k_BT/\epsilon\) there. Its energy integral consequently diverges logarithmically rather than approaching a finite excited-state capacity. The three-dimensional derivation of a nonzero transition temperature cannot then be carried across unchanged. A finite box, a confining trap or interparticle interactions can all alter the observed low-energy behaviour and must be identified when comparing different experimental geometries.

Repeat the counting in a harmonic trap

A harmonic trap changes the number of available excited states even for particles with the same mass and statistics. For three oscillator frequencies \(\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z\), measure energy above the lowest level and suppose \(k_BT\) greatly exceeds each \(\hbar\omega_i\). The quantum labels \(n_i\) are nonnegative integers with energy \(\sum_i\hbar\omega_i n_i\). The count below energy \(E\) is approximately the volume of the positive-coordinate tetrahedron bounded by this plane, namely \(E^3/(6\hbar^3\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)\). Differentiating gives an energy density of states proportional to \(E^2\).

At the onset of condensation integrate this density against \(1/(e^{E/k_BT}-1)\). The identity \(\int_0^\infty x^2/(e^x-1)\,dx=2\zeta(3)\) then gives

\[N_{\rm ex,max}=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar\omega}\right)^3,\qquad \bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}.\]

For \(N=1.00\times10^5\) particles in an isotropic 100 Hz trap, \(\hbar\bar\omega/k_B=4.80\) nK and \([N/\zeta(3)]^{1/3}=43.7\), so the predicted transition temperature is \(0.210\) μK. At \(0.150\) μK the condensate fraction is \(1-(0.150/0.210)^3=0.636\). An analysis using the uniform-box exponent at the same temperature ratio would instead give 0.396. The difference comes entirely from the confinement-dependent state count.

Reducing all three trap frequencies at fixed particle number lowers the onset temperature in direct proportion. This differs from enlarging a uniform box at fixed mean density, which leaves its bulk critical temperature unchanged. Specifying how particle number, volume and external confinement are controlled is therefore essential when interpreting a measured condensate fraction.

Thermal radiation in a cavity

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Count electromagnetic states, derive the temperature scaling of radiation energy and connect a cavity measurement to emitted power.

From first principles

Derive the cavity spectrum by counting electromagnetic modes

Consider a large empty cavity at uniform temperature \(T\). With periodic boundaries each independent wavevector occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\), and each nonzero wavevector admits two transverse polarizations. Counting a spherical shell of radius \(k\) and thickness \(dk\), then using \(\omega=ck\), gives the number of modes per unit cavity volume and angular-frequency interval,

\[\frac{g(\omega)}{V}\,d\omega=\frac{2(4\pi k^2dk)}{(2\pi)^3}=\frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}\,d\omega .\]

The cavity walls can create and absorb photons, so equilibrium photon number is unrestricted and its chemical potential is zero. A mode has mean occupation \(1/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]\). Multiplying by its photon energy \(\hbar\omega\) and the density of modes gives the thermal spectrum,

\[u_\omega(T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]} .\]

This quantity is energy density per unit angular frequency. Integrating over frequency and changing variable to \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) gives a total proportional to \(T^4\). The continuum mode count assumes a cavity large compared with the wavelengths contributing most of that energy.

Derivation and applications

Locate the peak that a spectrometer actually measures

A cavity at temperature \(T\) emits thermal radiation through a small aperture. An instrument can report energy in equal intervals of frequency or equal intervals of wavelength. The energy in any given physical band agrees, but the positions of the spectral-density maxima depend on the bin coordinate. Assume an ideal blackbody and a calibrated detector reporting energy, with response corrections applied. The spectral energy density per ordinary frequency \(\nu\) has the Planck form

\[u_\nu=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

The energy in corresponding bins must be equal. Substitute \(\nu=c/\lambda\) and include the magnitude of its derivative to obtain the density per unit wavelength,

\[u_\lambda=u_\nu(c/\lambda)\left|\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\right|=\frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.\]

For frequency bins set \(x=h\nu/(k_BT)\); for wavelength bins set \(x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)\). Temperature-dependent prefactors can be ignored in locating the maximum at fixed \(T\). Both maximizations reduce to differentiating \(x^m/(e^x-1)\), with different powers \(m\). Its logarithmic derivative vanishes when

\[\frac{m}{x}-\frac{1}{1-e^{-x}}=0,\]
\[x=m(1-e^{-x}).\]

The positive interior roots are \(x_\nu=2.82144\) for \(m=3\) and \(x_\lambda=4.96511\) for \(m=5\). The stationary points are maxima because both tails fall below the interior peak. Bin widths and what the detector counts are part of the observable. A photon counter without energy weighting or a spectrometer recording per logarithmic bandwidth would require its own density transformation.

Worked example

Compare two instruments viewing the same furnace

For \(T=1200\,\mathrm K\), the wavelength instrument peaks at \(hc/(4.96511k_BT)=2.41\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The frequency instrument peaks at \(2.82144k_BT/h=70.5\,\mathrm{THz}\), corresponding to \(4.25\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). Both spectra describe the same beam. A wavelength interval of a given width spans more frequency bandwidth at shorter wavelengths, which shifts its maximum toward higher photon energies.

Second application

Infer temperature and forecast another peak

A calibrated equal-frequency instrument finds a maximum at \(35.0\,\mathrm{THz}\) from a second cavity. This gives \(T=h\nu/(2.82144k_B)=595\,\mathrm K\). An equal-wavelength instrument should then peak at \(2.898\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}/595\,\mathrm K=4.87\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The wavelength \(c/\nu=8.57\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) still identifies the same photons as the frequency maximum and can be used to match a narrow band between instruments.

Photons in thermal equilibrium

An empty cavity with walls at temperature \(T\) contains electromagnetic radiation. The walls absorb and emit photons, so equilibrium does not constrain their number. The chemical potential of equilibrium photons is zero. Each mode of angular frequency \(\omega\) carries a thermal mean energy \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) above its temperature-independent vacuum energy. The spectrum follows by multiplying this occupation by the number of modes in a frequency interval.

Take a large box of volume \(V\) and periodic boundary conditions for counting. One state per polarization occupies a reciprocal-space cell \((2\pi)^3/V\). A thin spherical shell contributes \(4\pi k^2dk\) times two independent transverse polarizations. Using \(\omega=ck\) gives the state density per volume \(\omega^2/(\pi^2c^3)\). Hence

\[u_\omega(T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}.\]

At low frequency the mean mode energy approaches \(k_BT\) as in classical equipartition. Extending that classical formula to arbitrarily high frequencies would produce an infinite integral. Quantum occupation suppresses these modes exponentially when a single photon costs much more than the thermal energy.

Energy, pressure and flux

Integrate over positive \(\omega\), putting \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\). The integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) gives energy density \(u=aT^4\) with \(a=\pi^2k_B^4/(15\hbar^3c^3)\). Pressure follows from radiation momentum: a photon carries momentum \(E/c\), while crossing a plane weights directions by their projected speed. Evaluating the normal momentum flux over isotropic directions introduces \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\), hence \(p=u/3\).

A small hole in a black cavity admits only outgoing directions. The half-sphere average of the normal speed gives net emitted flux \(cu/4\), which is the Stefan–Boltzmann law \(\sigma T^4\) with \(\sigma=ac/4\). The different factors of three and four describe different angular averages and observables. A wall facing another large black surface also receives thermal radiation, so its net radiative heat loss depends on the fourth powers of both temperatures.

Observe cooling power

A 1.00 cm² aperture faces a large black surrounding enclosure at 300 K. The cavity temperature is 600 K. The outgoing power is \(\sigma A T^4=0.735\) W while incoming power is \(0.0459\) W. The difference, \(0.689\) W, must be replaced to maintain temperature if all other thermal exchanges are negligible. The interior radiation energy density is only \(9.81\times10^{-5}\) J/m³; repeated replenishment at the walls sustains the outgoing flux.

A wavelength spectrometer and a frequency spectrometer plot different spectral densities. To convert use \(u_\lambda=u_\nu|d\nu/d\lambda|\), since both must assign the same energy to corresponding intervals. Merely substituting \(\nu=c/\lambda\) into a density per hertz shifts the plotted maximum incorrectly. The integrated energy and the predicted net aperture power are independent of which spectral coordinate is chosen.

The photon equation of state also predicts a reversible adiabatic temperature change. Write \(U=aVT^4\) and \(p=aT^4/3\), then set \(dU=-p\,dV\). Collecting terms gives \(dT/T=-dV/(3V)\), or \(TV^{1/3}=\mathrm{constant}\). Halving the linear dimension of a cubical equilibrium radiation cavity would double its temperature. This scaling agrees with the shift of every electromagnetic mode frequency under uniform contraction.

Determine the entropy carried by equilibrium radiation

A beam emitted through a small black-cavity opening carries entropy as well as energy. For large equilibrium photon systems, extensivity gives the Euler relation \(U=TS-pV+\mu N\). The photon chemical potential is zero and \(p=u/3\), where \(u=U/V=aT^4\). Solving for entropy density gives \(s=(u+p)/T=4aT^3/3\). The same result follows independently by integrating \(dS=C_VdT/T\) at fixed volume, since \(C_V=4aVT^3\).

Inside the cavity the radiation is isotropic. Photons leaving through a small aperture sample each outgoing direction in proportion to its projected velocity, giving \(c/4\) times the interior density for either energy or entropy. Thus the emitted energy flux is \(\sigma T^4\) and entropy flux is \((4/3)\sigma T^3\). The latter exceeds energy flux divided by \(T\) by a factor of \(4/3\). Opening the cavity to a colder environment is an irreversible emission process, not the reversible transfer of an equal amount of heat through a material boundary at the source temperature.

Consider a device intercepting ideal black radiation from a hot reservoir at \(T_h\) while exchanging a counterpropagating equilibrium field with an environment at \(T_0\). The net incoming energy flux per area is \(\sigma(T_h^4-T_0^4)\) and the corresponding net entropy flux is \((4/3)\sigma(T_h^3-T_0^3)\). A reversible converter must return this entropy to the ambient reservoir, requiring at least \(T_0\) times the net entropy flux as rejected heat. The remaining energy is the maximum possible work for these specified radiation streams,

\[\frac{\dot W_{\max}}A=\sigma T_h^4\left[1-\frac43\frac{T_0}{T_h}+\frac13\left(\frac{T_0}{T_h}\right)^4\right].\]

Evaluate an optical energy and entropy budget

Take a 1.00 cm² aperture, \(T_h=600\) K and \(T_0=300\) K. Outgoing energy is \(0.735\) W and incoming ambient radiation \(0.0459\) W. Net energy input is therefore \(0.689\) W. The net entropy supply is \((4/3)\sigma A(600^3-300^3)=1.43\times10^{-3}\) W/K. Returning this to 300 K requires at least \(0.429\) W, leaving approximately \(0.260\) W available as work. The result is about 35.4 percent of the hot aperture's outgoing radiation power.

As \(T_h\) tends to \(T_0\), net energy and net entropy transport vanish and the maximum work vanishes as well. A grey, directional or spectrally filtered source may have different relationships between intensity and entropy from these two ideal black streams. Using only a bolometer reading and a colour temperature to infer available work is therefore justified only if the assumed angular and spectral distributions are also credible.

A check on the limiting expression is to compare it with an ideal material heat engine fed at \(T_h\). Such an engine can in principle extract a fraction \(1-T_0/T_h\) of a reversible heat input. In the optical problem the incoming stream already has a specified entropy-to-energy ratio. Delivering it to a converter cannot undo the entropy generated by producing that stream in the first place. This accounts for the smaller work fraction at the same two nominal temperatures and makes clear which boundary is being used when assigning a maximum work to emitted radiation.

Vibrations and the Debye heat capacity

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Count vibrational modes in a solid and explain why a crystal’s low-temperature heat capacity follows a cube law.

From first principles

Derive the low-temperature heat capacity from acoustic modes

In a large three-dimensional crystal, long-wavelength acoustic vibrations have approximately linear dispersion \(\omega=vk\). Count allowed wavevectors inside a sphere of radius \(k\). Its volume grows as \(k^3\), so the number of modes below \(\omega\) grows as \(\omega^3\). The frequency density is its derivative. In a single-speed model normalized to the \(3N\) displacements of \(N\) atoms,

\[g(\omega)=\frac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3},\]
\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N .\]

Each mode stores mean thermal energy \(\hbar\omega/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]\). Multiply by the mode count and substitute \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) to expose the temperature dependence,

\[U_{\rm th}=\frac{9N(k_BT)^4}{\hbar^3\omega_D^3}\int_0^{\hbar\omega_D/(k_BT)}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx .\]

When \(T\ll\hbar\omega_D/k_B\), the omitted tail above the upper limit is exponentially small. The integral approaches a constant, giving \(U_{\rm th}\propto T^4\) and \(C_V=(\partial U/\partial T)_V\propto T^3\). The cube law relies on both three-dimensional mode counting and linear low-frequency dispersion.

Derivation and applications

Recover the Debye velocity from distinct sound branches

A three-dimensional solid usually carries one longitudinal and two transverse acoustic polarizations. Their speeds can differ substantially. Consider a large elastically isotropic crystal of volume \(V\), with linear dispersion \(\omega=v_s k\) for each polarization \(s\) in the thermally populated range. Impose periodic boundaries; one wavevector state occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For each polarization count all points in a sphere of radius \(\omega/v_s\), which includes opposite propagation directions. Differentiating gives the total density of modes,

\[N_s(<\omega)=\frac{V\omega^3}{6\pi^2v_s^3},\]
\[g(\omega)=\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi^2}\left(v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}\right).\]

A slower sound branch has more low-frequency states because the same frequency reaches a larger wavevector sphere. Define one effective velocity \(v_D\) to preserve this acoustic density of states. Requiring an isotropic monatomic Debye model to contain \(3N\) vibrations, with number density \(n=N/V\), fixes its frequency cutoff and temperature scale,

\[\frac{3}{v_D^3}=\frac{1}{v_L^3}+\frac{2}{v_T^3},\]
\[\omega_D=v_D(6\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\]
\[\Theta_D=\frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B}.\]

At low temperature the cutoff can be moved to infinity in the thermal energy integral. Changing variables to \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) uses \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\). A temperature derivative yields the directly measurable coefficient

\[\frac{C_V}{VT^3}=\frac{2\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3}\left(v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}\right).\]

The inverse cubes explain why replacing the three velocities by their arithmetic mean can noticeably underestimate the heat capacity. Optical or electronic contributions must be identified separately in specimens where they are thermally active.

Worked example

Predict the acoustic temperature scale

For \(v_L=6000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(v_T=3000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), the longitudinal branch contributes only \(1/17\) of the low-temperature coefficient. The effective velocity is \(3000(24/17)^{1/3}=3365\,\mathrm{m/s}\). With \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), the cutoff wavevector is \(1.44\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), giving \(\Theta_D\simeq369\,\mathrm K\). The long-wave estimates apply well below that scale.

Second application

Extract the transverse speed from calorimetry

Suppose a separate specimen gives \(v_D=2500\,\mathrm{m/s}\) from its low-temperature heat capacity and \(v_L=5000\,\mathrm{m/s}\) from a longitudinal pulse. Solving for the missing speed gives \(v_T=[2/(3/v_D^3-1/v_L^3)]^{1/3}=2.22\times10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}\). A shear-wave transit across \(10.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) should consequently take \(4.51\,\mathrm{\mu s}\). This timing test uses a distinct mechanical measurement of the same states counted by calorimetry.

What a solid can store

Consider an electrically insulating three-dimensional crystal of \(N\) atoms, volume \(V\), sufficiently cold that its structure remains fixed and normal-mode frequencies vary weakly with temperature. In a harmonic approximation displacements about equilibrium separate into \(3N\) independent oscillators. Their excitations are called phonons. Phonon number is not conserved when the solid exchanges heat, so each mode has a Bose mean occupation with zero chemical potential. The modes exist because the crystal can deform, including at very long wavelengths.

A model assigning the same frequency to every oscillator predicts an exponentially small low-temperature heat capacity, since exciting any mode would require a fixed nonzero energy. Acoustic vibrations instead approach zero frequency as their wavenumber approaches zero. Even in a cold macroscopic crystal, long-wavelength modes can remain thermally accessible. The key quantity is how many have energies of order \(k_BT\) or less.

Test the single-frequency model before introducing a spectrum

The premise of the Einstein solid is deliberately restrictive. All \(3N\) vibrational coordinates of a crystal are independent harmonic oscillators with one frequency \(\omega_E\). For one coordinate the levels are \(E_n=\hbar\omega_E(n+1/2)\). Write \(x=\hbar\omega_E/(k_BT)=\Theta_E/T\). Summing the geometric series gives \(Z_1=e^{-x/2}/(1-e^{-x})\). The partition function of the crystal is \(Z_1^{3N}\), because each normal coordinate can be occupied independently and phonon number is free to change.

Apply \(-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\), to obtain \(U=3N\hbar\omega_E[1/2+1/(e^x-1)]\). The zero-point energy contributes no heat capacity at fixed volume. Differentiating the occupation term and using \(dx/dT=-x/T\) gives

\[C_{V,E}=3Nk_B\,\frac{x^2e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}.\]

At high temperature \(1-e^{-x}\simeq x\), so the heat capacity per mole of atoms approaches \(3R\). At low temperature the denominator tends to one, and the surviving factor \(x^2e^{-x}\) measures the cost of the lowest available excitation. For \(\Theta_E=240\) K the same mole would have \(C_V=18.06\) J/(mol K) at 120 K, \(7.585\) at 60 K and \(0.536\) at 30 K. Halving temperature does not give a fixed ratio. The curve crosses from a classical plateau to thermally rare excitations.

A real three-dimensional macroscopic crystal supports collective vibrations whose frequencies approach zero with wavelength increasing. Replacing the single frequency by a distribution puts modes below any fixed nonzero thermal scale. The next calculation finds the density of those modes, and therefore a low-temperature power law. High-temperature agreement alone cannot distinguish the two models since both count the same total number of coordinates.

Count to a cutoff

Use a continuum approximation with three acoustic polarizations and a common sound speed \(v\). The modes in a shell of wavevectors grow as \(k^2dk\), as for photons. Real crystals generally have distinct longitudinal and transverse speeds. An effective Debye speed averages their inverse cubes. Converting via \(\omega=vk\) gives density \(g(\omega)=3V\omega^2/(2\pi^2v^3)\). The continuum would supply infinitely many modes, whereas a crystal with \(N\) atoms has exactly \(3N\). Choose an upper cutoff \(\omega_D\) to match that count,

\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N,\qquad g(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3 .\]

This cutoff is an approximate representation of the lattice at high wavenumber. The low-temperature prediction depends on the long-wave acoustic spectrum and is insensitive to precise details near the cutoff. In a multi-atom cell additional optical branches change the intermediate-temperature behaviour, which cannot generally be reconstructed with a single effective speed.

Two limits and a measurement

The thermal energy is the integral of \(g(\omega)\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) over the band. Define \(\Theta_D=\hbar\omega_D/k_B\). When \(T\ll\Theta_D\), extend the upper limit of the dimensionless integral to infinity. The resulting energy scales as \(T^4\) and differentiation yields

\[C_V=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3.\]

At high temperature each mode instead approaches \(k_BT\) of thermal energy and the heat capacity tends to \(3Nk_B\). The acoustic model has the correct count by construction. The constant zero-point energy never enters either heat capacity because it disappears upon differentiation.

For one mole of atoms with \(\Theta_D=300\) K, the low-temperature prediction at 10.0 K is \(0.0720\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below the classical \(3R=24.9\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 20.0 K the cubic asymptote predicts \(0.576\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Measuring several temperatures and plotting \(C/T^3\) tests the constant coefficient directly. In a conducting solid an electronic term proportional to \(T\) can dominate at low temperatures. Separating that term through a plot of \(C/T\) against \(T^2\) allows the intercept and slope to reveal the two physically distinct energy stores.

Finite size eventually supplies a lower frequency cutoff related to the crystal dimensions, so the continuum counting argument presupposes a specimen large enough to have many wavelengths populated throughout the temperature interval.

Connect vibrations to thermal expansion

A purely harmonic crystal with force constants independent of volume can store vibrational energy yet has no vibrational mechanism for thermal expansion. Real vibrational frequencies usually vary as the lattice spacing changes. The quasiharmonic approximation treats each mode as harmonic at each volume while allowing its angular frequency \(\omega_j(V)\) to change smoothly. Define the mode Grüneisen parameter \(\gamma_j=-\partial\ln\omega_j/\partial\ln V\). A positive value means expansion softens that mode.

For one oscillator the Helmholtz free energy is \(F_j=\hbar\omega_j/2+k_BT\ln[1-e^{-\hbar\omega_j/(k_BT)}]\). Differentiation at fixed \(T\) gives \(\partial F_j/\partial\omega_j=U_j/\omega_j\), where \(U_j\) includes the zero-point energy and the mean thermal excitation. Since vibrational pressure is \(-\partial F/\partial V\), the chain rule gives \(p_{\rm vib}=V^{-1}\sum_j\gamma_j U_j\). A temperature derivative at fixed volume suppresses the constant zero-point contribution. The mechanical relation \((\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha_V B_T\) finally yields

\[\alpha_V=\frac{\sum_j\gamma_j C_{V,j}}{B_TV} .\]

Here \(B_T\) is the isothermal bulk modulus and \(\alpha_V\) the volume expansion coefficient. The mode sum weights each Grüneisen parameter by its thermally active heat capacity. A low-frequency mode that softens under compression can contribute negatively and can dominate in a cold crystal while high-frequency modes remain frozen. A single positive average parameter is therefore a material model rather than a thermodynamic necessity.

Infer a mode average from dilatometry

For a cubic crystal take measured molar heat capacity \(C_{V,m}=12.0\) J/(mol K), molar volume \(V_m=1.20\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol and bulk modulus \(B_T=100\) GPa. A measured linear expansion coefficient \(6.00\times10^{-6}\) K⁻¹ corresponds to \(\alpha_V\simeq3\alpha_L=1.80\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹. The effective heat-capacity-weighted parameter is then \(\bar\gamma=\alpha_VB_TV_m/C_{V,m}=1.80\). The factor of three comes from differentiating the cube of a length, not from counting acoustic polarizations again.

If the same positive average and bulk modulus remain approximately constant down to a regime where phonons give \(C_V\propto T^3\), the volume expansion coefficient should also fall as \(T^3\). Doubling temperature there increases the coefficient by about eight, although the accumulated length change requires integrating it over temperature. A comparison of \(\alpha_V/C_V\) at several temperatures can test whether the active modes really share a common volume sensitivity. Molar heat capacity and molar volume must be taken on the same chemical counting basis before inferring any dimensionless Grüneisen parameter.

Hydrostatic pressure spectroscopy gives an independent way to measure a mode parameter. By the chain rule, the definition yields \(\gamma_j=B_T(\partial\ln\omega_j/\partial p)_T\) within the quasiharmonic description. For the numerical bulk modulus, a mode with parameter 1.80 would increase its frequency by approximately 1.8 percent under a 1.00 GPa hydrostatic pressure increment. Agreement between this slope and the value inferred from expansion requires that the probed mode be representative of the thermally populated spectrum. A strong optical mode visible to a spectrometer need not dominate low-temperature heat capacity or expansion.

Quoted pressure derivatives must therefore specify the mode, the temperature and whether hydrostatic or directional stress was applied in the measurement.

Why only a narrow shell of a Fermi gas carries heat

Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Heating a filled Fermi sea does not give every particle a classical thermal energy. Deep below the chemical potential, nearby states are already occupied. Quantifying the accessible states predicts both the low-temperature heat capacity and the shift of the chemical potential at fixed particle number.

Convert a sharp Fermi surface into a narrow weight

For independent fermions with occupancy \(f(E)=1/[1+\exp((E-\mu)/(k_BT))]\), consider integrals \(I=\int_0^\infty H(E)f(E)\,dE\). Choose \(F(E)=\int_0^E H(s)ds\). Integration by parts gives \(I=\int_0^\infty F(E)[-\partial_E f(E)]dE\), since the boundary terms vanish. The derivative is a peak of width \(k_BT\) around \(\mu\). With \(x=(E-\mu)/(k_BT)\) the weight becomes \(w(x)=1/[4\cosh^2(x/2)]\).

For \(\mu\) many thermal units above the bottom and \(H\) smooth over the thermal shell, extend the lower limit to \(-\infty\) when expanding locally around \(\mu\). The weight has unit integral and is even, so the linear term in \(F(\mu+k_BTx)\) integrates to zero. Its second moment is \(\pi^2/3\), obtained by expanding \(w(x)=\sum_{\ell\ge1}(-1)^{\ell+1}\ell e^{-\ell x}\) on \(x>0\) and integrating \(x^2\) termwise. Since \(F''=H'\),

\[I=\int_0^\mu H(E)dE+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2H'(\mu)+O(T^4).\]

The first correction samples the slope of the smooth weighting function. If a band edge or singularity lies within the shell, replacing the lower limit and Taylor expanding are poor approximations.

Conserve the number of particles

For a three-dimensional parabolic dispersion the density of states is \(D(E)=A\sqrt E\). At \(T=0\), \(N=(2A/3)E_F^{3/2}\). At small \(T\), use the expansion with \(H=D\) and write the chemical potential as \(E_F+\delta\mu\), where \(\delta\mu=O(T^2)\). Subtract the zero-temperature count. To quadratic order \(0=D(E_F)\delta\mu+(\pi^2/6)(k_BT)^2D'(E_F)\). Because \(D'/D=1/(2E_F)\),

\[\mu/E_F=1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}(T/T_F)^2+O[(T/T_F)^4],\]
\[T_F=E_F/k_B.\]

The increasing density of states offers more available levels just above the Fermi energy than just below it. The chemical potential shifts down to preserve the count. At \(T/T_F=0.10\) the predicted shift is \(-0.00822E_F\). For an ideal gas with \(E_F=5.0\,\mathrm{eV}\), this particular reduced temperature corresponds to about 5800 K, so reduced temperature and ordinary laboratory temperature should be distinguished.

Differentiate the energy at fixed number

Use \(H=ED\) in the same integral, including the just-derived shift of \(\mu\). The boundary shift contributes \(E_FD(E_F)\delta\mu\). In the Sommerfeld term the derivative is \(D(E_F)+E_FD'(E_F)\). The terms involving the slope of \(D\) cancel after fixing number. Thus \(U-U_0=(\pi^2/6)D(E_F)(k_BT)^2\), and \(C_V=(\pi^2/3)D(E_F)k_B^2T\). In three dimensions \(D(E_F)=3N/(2E_F)\), giving \(C_V/(Nk_B)=(\pi^2/2)T/T_F\). This is an electronic contribution for independent particles. Other degrees of freedom such as phonons can dominate a measured material heat capacity.

The experiment reconstructs \(\mu\) by numerical integration and bisection at fixed number. It overlays the asymptotic prediction, allowing the fractional error to be checked before using it at larger \(T/T_F\). The quadrature uses the momentum-like substitution \(E/E_F=y^2\) to remove the square-root endpoint singularity.

Filling electronic states and measuring the Fermi surface

Condensed matter and band theory

Use exclusion and state counting to predict carrier energies, thermal response and the effect of dimensionality.

From first principles

Count the states in a filled Fermi sphere

Take independent spin-one-half electrons in a large cube of volume \(V=L^3\) with periodic boundary conditions. The condition \(e^{ik_xL}=1\) permits \(k_x=2\pi n_x/L\), with the same spacing in the other directions. Thus each allowed wave vector occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For the free-particle energy \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\), all states below energy \(E\) lie in a sphere. Counting its volume and two spin states gives

\[N(E)=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.\]

At zero temperature the exclusion principle fills these single-particle states up to \(E_F\). Writing \(n=N/V\), solve the counting formula at that upper limit:

\[k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\]
\[E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}.\]

The number of available states in a thin energy shell is the derivative \(D(E)dE\). In three dimensions \(D(E)\propto\sqrt E\) because the shell area grows with \(k^2\) while \(dk/dE\) falls as \(1/k\). This counting assumes an isotropic parabolic band. Additional valleys or a different effective dimension change the result.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the pressure of a filled Fermi sea by compressing its states

Even at zero temperature a box of mobile electrons exerts a pressure on its boundaries. Consider \(N\) noninteracting spin-one-half fermions in a three-dimensional volume \(V\), with fixed isotropic mass \(m\) and quadratic energy. Take the bottom of the band as a volume-independent energy reference. Every state below \(E_F\) is occupied once per allowed spin state. Since the density of states including spin has the form \(D(E)=C\sqrt E\), dividing two elementary integrals eliminates the normalization constant,

\[\frac{U}{N}=\frac{\int_0^{E_F} E\,C\sqrt E\,dE}{\int_0^{E_F} C\sqrt E\,dE}=\frac35 E_F .\]

State counting gives \(E_F=(\hbar^2/2m)(3\pi^2 N/V)^{2/3}\). During a uniform compression at fixed \(N\) the upper occupied energy rises. At zero entropy the energy derivative with respect to box volume is the negative pressure, so

\[P=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac{2U}{3V}=\frac25 nE_F,\]
\[n=\frac NV .\]

Density appears both explicitly and through \(E_F\). A second volume derivative gives the positive resistance to a small fractional compression,

\[B=-V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac53P=\frac23 nE_F .\]

Both \(P\) and the bulk modulus \(B\) have units of energy per volume. The factor \(5/3\) distinguishes the change of pressure from pressure itself. An actual solid also has electrostatic and lattice contributions. Extracting the electronic contribution from a measured solid modulus requires information about these other stresses and the volume dependence of the band parameters.

Worked example

Estimate the electronic stiffness of a simple metal

Take \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(E_F=5.00\,\mathrm{eV}\). With \(1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J\), the product \(nE_F\) is \(4.01\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The pressure contribution is \(16.0\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and the modulus is \(26.7\,\mathrm{GPa}\). A one percent volume decrease would raise this pressure by approximately \(0.267\,\mathrm{GPa}\) in the linear response regime.

Second application

Predict a finite compression without linearizing

Suppose a separate trapped gas starts at pressure \(12.0\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and is compressed slowly to \(0.800\) of its original volume at fixed particle number and negligible entropy. Its pressure becomes \(12.0(1/0.800)^{5/3}=17.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Its energy per particle rises by only \((1/0.800)^{2/3}=1.16\) times. The larger pressure ratio also contains the increase in particles per unit volume.

Count occupied states at zero temperature

Consider a cubic box of volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries and free electrons of effective model mass \(m\). Allowed wavevectors occupy a volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) per state for each spin. At zero temperature each state below the chemical potential is filled. For \(\epsilon=\hbar^2 k^2/2m\) the filled region is a sphere and each \(k\) has two spin states. Hence

\[N=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3},\qquad k_F=(3\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2 k_F^2}{2m}.\]

With \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ and the free electron mass, \(k_F\) is about \(1.36\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\) about 7.0 eV. The corresponding temperature \(E_F/k_B\) is roughly 81,000 K. Ordinary room temperature therefore changes occupation only near the outer surface of this sea in a simple metal. This is a model estimate; anisotropic bands and multiple pockets change its interpretation.

The density of states is a derivative

Replacing \(k_F\) by \(k(E)=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) gives the integrated state count below energy \(E\). Its derivative is

\[D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E,\qquad D(E_F)=\frac{3N}{2E_F}.\]

Check dimensions by integrating \(D(E)\,dE\) to recover a count. In two dimensions the occupied area grows as \(k^2\) and the density per energy is constant for a parabolic band. In one dimension the count grows as \(k\) and the density has an integrable \(E^{-1/2}\) singularity at its bottom. A large density of states means many states fit in a narrow energy window. It does not imply a large group velocity: a flat dispersion may create the large density.

Why only a shell contributes to heat capacity

At finite temperature the occupation is \(f(E)=[1+e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT}]^{-1}\). Heating can promote an electron only into a vacant state. Deep in the filled sea all nearby destinations are occupied. The active shell has width of order \(k_BT\), involving about \(D(E_F)k_BT\) states; the typical energy change is another factor \(k_BT\). Thus the thermal energy increases quadratically and the heat capacity rises linearly with \(T\).

A coefficient follows by expanding the smooth density of states across that shell. At fixed particle number, the relevant excess energy to leading order is \(\int (E-E_F)D(E_F)[f(E)-\Theta(E_F-E)]\,dE\). The filled and empty sides contribute equally to this order. Substituting \(x=|E-E_F|/k_BT\) and using \(\int_0^\infty x/(e^x+1)\,dx=\pi^2/12\) gives \(C_{\rm el}=(\pi^2/3)D(E_F)k_B^2T\). This expansion requires the density to be smooth on the thermal scale; a band edge at the chemical potential needs separate treatment.

Separate two low-temperature contributions

In a three-dimensional simple metal combine \(C_\mathrm{el}=\gamma T\) with the phonon result \(C_\mathrm{ph}=\beta T^3\). Plot \(C/T\) against \(T^2\); the intercept estimates \(\gamma\), and the slope estimates \(\beta\). If two points are \((4,3)\) and \((16,6)\) in K² and mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻², respectively, \(\beta=0.25\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴ and \(\gamma=2.0\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻². Curvature across the fitted interval would be evidence against this two-term description. To go beyond a free sphere we next retain the periodic potential of the ions.

Count charges and electronic compressibility in two dimensions

A two-dimensional electron layer illustrates how changing available spatial dimensions alters the density of states and an electrical measurement. Assume one parabolic subband \(E=E_c+\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)/(2m^*)\), spin degeneracy two and no valley degeneracy. A square sample of area \(A\) with periodic boundary conditions supplies one state per spin per reciprocal area \((2\pi)^2/A\). Counting the disk up to \(k_F\) gives areal density \(n_s=k_F^2/(2\pi)\), hence

\[E_F-E_c=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m^*},\qquad D_A(E)=\frac{dn_s}{dE_F}=\frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}\]

above the subband edge at zero temperature. Unlike the square-root density in three dimensions, each occupied parabolic two-dimensional subband contributes a constant step. When an additional subband begins to fill it supplies another step, possibly with different degeneracy or mass. Temperature and disorder broaden the step in a real measurement.

An electrostatic gate must both create a field across the insulating spacer and raise the layer’s chemical potential. For areal capacitance \(C_g'\) of the spacer and increment of sheet charge magnitude \(dQ'=e\,dn_s\), the required voltage is \(dV=dQ'/C_g'+dE_F/e\). The second term defines the quantum capacitance \(C_q'=e^2D_A\). Their inverse capacitances add in series. For a small density of states the electronic contribution measurably lowers the total capacitance even when the geometric dimensions are known precisely.

Measure which capacitance limits a device

Take \(m^*=0.067m_e\) and \(n_s=2.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻². Then \(k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_s}=1.12\times10^8\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F-E_c=7.15\) meV. At 4 K the thermal scale \(k_BT=0.345\) meV is smaller than this separation, supporting the degenerate estimate. The electronic capacitance per area is \(0.0448\) F/m². For a spacer with \(C_g'=0.0100\) F/m², the measured series value is \((1/0.0100+1/0.0448)^{-1}=0.00818\) F/m².

Over a gate area of \(100\) μm², the predicted capacitance is \(0.818\) pF rather than the purely geometric \(1.00\) pF. Treating that measured value as a change of dielectric thickness would misidentify an electronic effect. In the limit of a very high density of states, the quantum capacitance becomes large and the geometric value is recovered. Near depletion the assumptions of a sharp occupied subband and uniform layer require scrutiny. Comparing capacitance with an independent sheet-density measurement can test whether a low reading reflects depleted electronic states or simply a different insulating spacer.

Using the series result, a small gate swing can be split into two measurable energy changes. A 10.0 mV swing at the stated operating point changes sheet charge magnitude by \(8.18\times10^{-5}\) C/m², or sheet density by \(5.10\times10^{14}\) m⁻². The geometric voltage increment is \(8.18\) mV and the chemical-potential contribution is \(1.82\) mV, corresponding to a Fermi-level shift of 1.82 meV. Their sum recovers the imposed swing. This decomposition assumes the layer remains in the same subband and equilibrates through its electrical contact on the capacitance measurement timescale.

A slow measurement with significant leakage instead mixes capacitance with conductance and can corrupt both estimated voltage contributions.

Magnetic moments and susceptibility

Condensed matter and band theory

Derive thermal magnetisation for a two-state moment and distinguish its signature from the response of a filled electronic sea.

From first principles

Obtain the Curie law by counting two magnetic orientations

Take many identical, independent localized moments per volume \(n\), each with component \(+\mu\) or \(-\mu\) along an applied induction \(B\). The magnetic energies are \(-\mu B\) and \(+\mu B\). At temperature \(T\) their probabilities are proportional to their Boltzmann factors. With \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\), normalize the two weights and subtract their contributions to the mean:

\[Z=e^x+e^{-x},\]
\[\langle m_z\rangle=\mu\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}=\mu\tanh x .\]

Multiply by number density to get magnetization. In weak fields or at high temperatures \(x\ll1\), so the leading term in \(\tanh x\) gives

\[M\simeq\frac{n\mu^2 B}{k_BT},\]
\[\chi=\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\simeq\frac{\mu_0 n\mu^2}{k_BT}.\]

The susceptibility expression uses a weakly magnetic sample with \(B\simeq\mu_0 H\) and negligible demagnetizing correction. At low temperature and fixed nonzero field both the expansion and the \(1/T\) extrapolation fail. The exact two-state average saturates at \(n\mu\) because no moment can contribute more than \(\mu\). Exchange between neighbours can also invalidate the independent weights.

Derivation and applications

Distinguish the saturation moment from the Curie moment

An ion with angular momentum quantum number \(J\) can have more than two projections on a magnetic field. Consider independent identical ions of number density \(n\) in thermal equilibrium, with isotropic Landé factor \(g\) and negligible splitting at zero field. Label the levels \(m=-J,\ldots,J\) so that their moments along the applied induction \(B\) are \(g\mu_Bm\). Adjacent Zeeman levels are then separated by \(g\mu_B B\). Introduce \(x=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)\). Weighing each level with its Boltzmann factor gives

\[Z=\sum_{m=-J}^{J}e^{mx},\]
\[M=ng\mu_B\,\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial x}.\]

In weak fields each zero-field state is almost equally likely. The zeroth-order sum of \(m\) vanishes by symmetry. The next term in the numerator is \(x\sum m^2\), while the denominator is \(2J+1\) to this order. Using the sum of squares of the allowed projections gives

\[\langle m\rangle\simeq x\frac{\sum_{m=-J}^J m^2}{2J+1}=\frac{J(J+1)}{3}x .\]

For a dilute specimen take \(B\simeq\mu_0H\). The dimensionless volume susceptibility is the derivative of \(M\) with respect to magnetic field strength \(H\). In strong fields the highest projection alone survives. These limits identify two different combinations of the microscopic parameters,

\[\chi=\frac{\mu_0 n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_BT},\]
\[M_{\rm sat}=ng\mu_B J .\]

The Curie-law effective moment is \(g\mu_B\sqrt{J(J+1)}\), whereas the aligned projection is \(g\mu_BJ\). Their ratio approaches one for large \(J\). A susceptibility fit requires \(Jx\ll1\); a saturation measurement requires the opposite tendency and sufficiently weak interactions between moments.

Worked example

Predict the response of dilute spin-one ions

Take \(J=1\), \(g=2.00\), \(n=1.00\times10^{26}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), and \(T=10.0\,\mathrm K\). The aligned magnetization is \(1.85\times10^3\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The Curie formula gives \(\chi=2.09\times10^{-4}\). At \(B=0.100\,\mathrm T\), \(Jx=0.0134\), and the predicted magnetization is only \(\chi B/\mu_0=16.6\,\mathrm{A/m}\), consistent with weak polarization.

Second application

Identify a moment from two calibrated measurements

Suppose independent concentration measurements in another material yield a saturation moment of \(3.00\mu_B\) per ion and an effective Curie moment of \(3.87\mu_B\). The squared ratio is \(1+1/J=(3.87/3.00)^2=1.66\), giving \(J\simeq1.50\). The saturation value then gives \(g\simeq2.00\). Susceptibility alone fixes only a product of the factor \(g\) and the size of the angular momentum.

A moment has an energy as well as a direction

Consider \(N\) independent identical magnetic moments in volume \(V\), each with allowed projection \(\pm\mu\) along a uniform applied induction \(B\). The energies are \(\mp\mu B\); the state aligned with the field has lower energy. For \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) the one-moment partition function is \(Z_1=2\cosh(\beta\mu B)\). From \(\langle m_z\rangle=k_BT\,\partial_B\ln Z_1\) obtain

\[M=n\mu\tanh(\mu B/k_BT),\qquad n=N/V .\]

The full curve approaches \(n\mu\) at high field or low temperature. In the weak-field regime \(\mu B\ll k_BT\), expansion of the hyperbolic tangent gives \(M\simeq n\mu^2 B/(k_BT)\). Under weak magnetisation and negligible demagnetising corrections use \(B\simeq\mu_0H\); the dimensionless SI susceptibility \(\partial M/\partial H\) is then \(\chi=\mu_0n\mu^2/(k_BT)\). Be explicit about differentiating with respect to \(B\) or \(H\), which carry different units.

Work a moment count from two measurements

Suppose a dilute two-state sample exhibits saturation magnetisation \(9.27\times10^3\) A/m and the moments are known to have \(\mu=\mu_B=9.27\times10^{-24}\) J/T. The implied density is \(1.00\times10^{27}\) m⁻³. At 10 K and 1 T the argument \(\mu B/k_BT\) is 0.0672, safely in the linear range for a rough estimate: \(M\simeq622\) A/m. At the same temperature a field of about 14.9 T gives argument one and \(M/M_\mathrm{sat}=0.762\). Fitting a straight line up to that field would underestimate the zero-field slope.

If the moment magnitude is unknown, saturation determines \(n\mu\) and the small-field slope supplies \(n\mu^2\). Taken together they can separate density from moment within this model. A mixture of moment species, orbital contributions or interactions can defeat this two-equation inference. Plotting curves at several temperatures against \(B/T\) tests the independent-moment scaling without needing to know the overall density beforehand.

Compare with itinerant spin response

For a weakly perturbed degenerate metal, the two spin species shift by \(\mp\mu B\). Almost all states below \(E_F\) remain doubly occupied, so only a thin shell at the Fermi surface changes its net spin. At low temperature and nearly constant total density of states \(D_V(E_F)\), the spin imbalance is approximately \(D_V(E_F)\mu B\), giving \(M\simeq\mu^2D_V(E_F)B\). The leading Pauli response is nearly temperature independent, in contrast to the \(1/T\) scaling of independent localised moments. This argument addresses spin susceptibility; orbital diamagnetism also contributes to a real measured susceptibility.

Diagnose a small impurity contribution

If bulk data show a roughly constant offset plus a low-temperature upturn, test a simple additive model \(\chi=\chi_0+C/T\) over a weak-field interval. Plot \(\chi\) against \(1/T\) rather than taking an inverse before subtracting the offset. An upturn can arise from a dilute impurity population even when the host electronic sea dominates heat capacity. If scaling fails systematically, examine interactions between moments; the next chapter shows how cooperative ordering changes the small-field susceptibility.

Cool local moments by adiabatic demagnetization

Independent moments have an entropy that depends on the ratio of magnetic field to temperature. For a two-state moment of magnitude \(\mu\), energies are \(\mp\mu B\). Define \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\). The one-moment partition function is \(2\cosh x\), average energy is \(-\mu B\tanh x\), and the magnetic entropy follows from \(S=k_B\ln Z+U/T\),

\[\frac{S_{\rm spin}}{Nk_B}=\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x .\]

At zero field for fixed positive temperature the moment has two equiprobable orientations and contributes \(k_B\ln2\). At large \(x\) nearly all moments favour the lower state and the spin entropy tends to zero. If field is increased at fixed temperature, this reduction of spin entropy expels heat to a bath. Now isolate the material and reduce the field quasistatically. In the ideal spin-dominated limit entropy remains fixed, so \(x\) remains fixed and \(T/B\) is constant.

Thermal isolation and sufficiently slow variation are both required. The first prevents external heat from entering, and the second allows internal degrees of freedom to follow equilibrium. Other stores of entropy, particularly lattice vibrations or mobile electrons, participate in an actual specimen. The pure spin scaling can then be modified even when the process as a whole is perfectly adiabatic.

Estimate temperature and cooling capacity

Take \(N=1.00\times10^{21}\) independent moments each of magnitude \(\mu_B\), initially at 2.00 K. Magnetize isothermally from negligible field to 3.00 T, using \(\mu_B/k_B=0.6717\) K/T. The final magnetic parameter is \(x=1.01\), giving spin entropy per particle about \(0.362k_B\) compared with the initial \(0.693k_B\). The heat passed to the bath in that reversible isothermal step is \(T N k_B(0.693-0.362)=9.14\) mJ. Work is supplied by the field source during preparation.

Isolate and reduce field from 3.00 T to 0.300 T. Holding \(x\) fixed predicts \(T_f=2.00(0.300/3.00)=0.200\) K. Further field reduction in the ideal noninteracting equations would approach zero temperature. Real moments interact, however, and internal effective fields or ordering lift their near-zero-field degeneracy. Thermal leaks and finite relaxation rates also become increasingly important. A practical test records temperature against field on several ramp rates; dependence on the rate can distinguish irreversible heating from a reproducible correction to the equilibrium entropy model. The final cold sample can absorb only finite heat before its moments become disordered, even if the predicted endpoint temperature is very low.

A second check comes from magnetization along the ideal isolated sweep. Since \(M=N\mu\tanh x\) at fixed volume and \(x\) is conserved, the spin magnetization should stay almost constant while both the external field and the temperature fall. In contrast, an isothermal field decrease reduces \(x\) and magnetization. Measuring temperature alone cannot establish that the sample really followed an isentropic spin path. A simultaneous magnetic reading tests the fixed-population interpretation. Deviations concentrated at the weakest fields can signal interactions between moments rather than a general failure of the high-field entropy estimate.

Pairing, excitation gaps and what a spectrum measures

Condensed matter and band theory

Use a two-component excitation model to predict spectroscopic signatures, then distinguish three physically different gaps encountered across the course.

From first principles

See how pairing opens a spectroscopic gap

Begin with a single normal-state band and let \(\xi\) be its energy measured from the chemical potential. A minimal mean-field model for a uniform paired state mixes an electron amplitude with a hole amplitude through a complex pairing energy \(\Delta\). The two diagonal energies have opposite signs. To find its stationary excitations write the eigenvalue condition:

\[H=\begin{pmatrix}\xi&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\det(H-EI)=(\xi-E)(-\xi-E)-|\Delta|^2=0 .\]

It follows that the two branches are \(E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi^2+|\Delta|^2}\). At the former Fermi surface \(\xi=0\), even the cheapest positive excitation costs \(|\Delta|\). To predict a tunnelling spectrum for constant isotropic gap and nearly constant normal density of states, count the normal states mapping into an energy interval \(dE\). The Jacobian from \(\xi=\pm\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) gives the normalized density, with the electron and hole weights combined:

\[\frac{D_s(E)}{D_n(0)}=\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}}\quad (|E|>|\Delta|).\]

There are no states within the ideal gap at this level of description. Finite temperature, lifetime broadening and instrumental resolution change the measured conductance near its edges. The model assumes a nonzero \(\Delta\); it predicts consequences of pairing without deriving the attractive interaction that supports it.

Derivation and applications

Predict the weights of a particle peak and its partner

A pairing gap determines excitation energies, but a momentum-resolved spectrum also measures how strongly an electron couples to each peak. Consider a clean, uniform superconductor with a real positive gap \(\Delta\), at temperature low enough to neglect thermal broadening. Measure the normal-state energy \(\xi\) at one wavevector from the chemical potential. In the electron and hole basis the positive-energy eigenvector has components \(u,v\). The first row of its eigenvalue equation requires

\[(\xi-E)u+\Delta v=0,\]
\[\frac{v}{u}=\frac{E-\xi}{\Delta},\]
\[E=\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}.\]

Normalize the vector to unit probability. Since \((E-\xi)(E+\xi)=\Delta^2\), the squared amplitude ratio can be written without subtracting two nearly equal energies. The resulting coherence factors are

\[|u|^2=\frac{1}{1+(E-\xi)/(E+\xi)}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{\xi}{E}\right),\]
\[|v|^2=\frac12\left(1-\frac{\xi}{E}\right).\]

The negative-energy eigenvector is orthogonal to the positive one and consequently has electron weight \(|v|^2\). In the ideal single-particle spectral function at this momentum the two sharp peaks have unit total weight,

\[A(\epsilon)=|u|^2\delta(\epsilon-E)+|v|^2\delta(\epsilon+E),\]
\[\int A(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon=1 .\]

Thus the weights exchange as the wavevector passes through the normal Fermi surface. At \(\xi=0\) the two weights are equal. Well above the surface the positive-energy peak carries almost all the weight. To compare both sides quantitatively an experiment must account for occupation and matrix elements; ordinary photoemission alone samples the occupied side most directly.

Worked example

Resolve a weak partner peak

At a selected momentum let \(\xi=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=4.00\,\mathrm{meV}\). The spectral energies are \(+5.00\) and \(-5.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), with respective electron weights \(0.800\) and \(0.200\). At a momentum with the opposite \(\xi\) and the same gap the two peak energies stay fixed while their weights reverse. Such a weight exchange tests particle–hole mixing more specifically than the peak separation alone.

Second application

Reconstruct a gap using corrected peak areas

In a different sample the peaks are separated by \(6.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), and corrected electron spectral areas have positive-to-negative ratio \(3:2\). Their normalized weights are \(0.600\) and \(0.400\), so \(\xi/E=0.200\). With \(E=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) this gives \(\xi=0.600\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=\sqrt{3.00^2-0.600^2}=2.94\,\mathrm{meV}\). Half the separation overestimates the gap here because the probed momentum lies away from the normal Fermi surface.

Build excitations on a paired state

For a conventional fully gapped superconductor, a mean-field description mixes particle and hole amplitudes near the Fermi surface. With \(\xi_k=\epsilon_k-\mu\) and gap parameter \(\Delta\), a simplified matrix for a time-reversed pair of states is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\xi_k&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi_k\end{pmatrix}\binom{u_k}{v_k}=E\binom{u_k}{v_k},\qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta|^2}.\]

A positive-energy quasiparticle has minimum energy \(|\Delta|\) where the normal band crossed the chemical potential. Breaking a pair into two excitations can cost at least \(2|\Delta|\). These are different thresholds, so the probe and transition being discussed must be specified. The matrix displays level repulsion, reminiscent of the nearly free electron calculation, but its basis states and the physical origin of the coupling are different.

Transform a density of states

Suppose the normal-state density is approximately constant over the gap scale and the gap is isotropic. For \(E>|\Delta|\), \(|\xi|=\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) and \(d|\xi|/dE=E/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\). Counting the paired branches relative to the normal-state convention yields \(D_s(E)/D_n=|E|/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) outside the gap and zero inside in the ideal zero-broadening model. The edge singularity is integrable. States removed from the gap reappear near its edges. Thermal broadening, disorder, nodes or an anisotropic gap can change the measured shape.

A low-temperature tunnel junction with a normal counterelectrode of nearly constant density can use differential conductance as a probe of this spectrum, subject to matrix-element and broadening effects. Peaks at biases \(\pm1.5\) mV suggest \(|\Delta|\simeq1.5\) meV in this geometry. Peak separation on the voltage axis is 3.0 mV. Using the weak-coupling BCS ratio \(2|\Delta(0)|/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.53\) gives \(T_c\simeq9.86\) K. Measure \(T_c\) independently; agreement with this estimate supports the simple model, while deviation calls for closer investigation.

Select a second observation that distinguishes the mechanism

A nearly free electron band gap separates eigenvalues near a zone boundary. For conduction it also matters where the chemical potential sits. A superconducting gap opens at the Fermi surface on entering an ordered state, and has magnetic screening and a characteristic temperature dependence. A photonic stop band instead excludes real propagating wavevectors in a frequency range for the specified periodic optical medium. All three can produce missing spectral weight or reduced transmission. Their energies, frequency units, occupation rules and control parameters differ.

Return to the numerical optical stack with a prediction: making both layers identical closes its first stop band even though the artificial zone boundary remains. For a proposed superconducting gap, repeat the tunnelling and magnetisation measurements as temperature crosses the transition. For an electronic band edge, relate spectroscopy to filling, dispersion and transport. This combination of an analytic limit, a controlled change and an independent observable is what makes an interpretation of a spectrum persuasive.

For continued study, see the MIT lectures on Theory of Solids, including quasiparticles and superconducting electrodynamics.

Read the phase difference across a superconducting weak link

A single-particle tunnelling spectrum can reveal an excitation gap. A weak junction between two superconductors can also carry a coherent pair current that responds directly to their relative condensate phase. Use the gauge-invariant phase difference \(\varphi\), including the vector-potential line integral across the junction. For a simple tunnel junction in the low-transparency regime, its leading coupling energy is \(U_J=-E_J\cos\varphi\). Its \(2\pi\) periodicity reflects invariance under an integer phase winding and the energy minimum at aligned phase for a conventional junction.

A potential difference \(V\) between the condensates causes their relative phase to advance at \(\dot\varphi=2eV/\hbar\), choosing a consistent orientation of current and voltage. Combining electrical power \(IV\) with the rate of change of junction energy gives \(IV=E_J\sin\varphi\,\dot\varphi\), hence

\[I=I_c\sin\varphi,\qquad I_c=\frac{2eE_J}{\hbar}.\]

Under a steady subcritical current the phase can remain constant while \(V=0\). At constant nonzero voltage the phase winds and the ideal coherent current oscillates with frequency \(f_J=2e|V|/h\). These two statements distinguish a DC pair current with no terminal voltage from an AC pair response under bias. A real junction includes quasiparticle conduction and capacitance, so its observed total current can contain additional components.

Convert a microwave frequency into a voltage

A junction biased at 5.00 μV has Josephson oscillation frequency \(f_J=(2e/h)V=2.42\) GHz. Illuminate the junction with a stable microwave reference of frequency 10.0 GHz. If its mean phase advance locks to one full winding per reference cycle, then \(\langle\dot\varphi\rangle=2\pi f_{\rm ref}\), requiring mean voltage \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)=20.7\) μV. Locking to two windings per cycle gives 41.4 μV. Such voltage steps probe phase dynamics and the charge associated with coherent transfer.

Suppose the same device has measured critical current \(I_c=30.0\) nA at the operating temperature. At a bath temperature of 50 mK thermal energy is appreciably smaller, although phase fluctuations can still depend on capacitance and the surrounding circuit. If either electrode is driven normal, the equilibrium phase-link energy disappears and the ideal supercurrent relation ceases to apply. Observing quantized phase locking and an excitation gap therefore accesses two different signatures of the superconducting state; their characteristic frequencies need not be numerically identical.

Why a polar crystal has two optical phonon frequencies

Condensed matter and band theory

A uniform relative displacement of positive and negative ions creates an electric polarisation. A longitudinal vibration induces a macroscopic restoring field while a transverse displacement has a different electrical boundary condition. An infrared spectrum can reveal both frequencies.

Separate the ionic and electronic contributions

For one infrared-active optical mode let \(u\) be relative displacement per primitive cell, \(M\) the effective reduced mass, \(Z^*\) the effective charge in coulombs, and \(N\) the number of cells per volume. The oscillator equation driven by the macroscopic field is \(M(\ddot u+\gamma\dot u+\omega_{\rm TO}^2u)=Z^*E\). The zero-macroscopic-field stiffness defines the transverse optical frequency in this oscillator model. Bound electronic response at these frequencies supplies a real relative background permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\). The ionic polarisation is \(P_{\rm ion}=NZ^*u\), so \(D=\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty E+P_{\rm ion}\).

With time convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\), solving for \(u\) gives

\[\epsilon_r(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{S}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\]
\[S=\frac{N(Z^*)^2}{\epsilon_0 M}.\]

The signed effective charge appears twice, once as drive and once in the dipole density. Its square therefore sets oscillator strength. At zero frequency the ions can follow the field and \(\epsilon_s=\epsilon_\infty+S/\omega_{\rm TO}^2\). Far above the phonon resonance but below electronic absorption the ionic term is small, making \(\epsilon_\infty\) the observed plateau.

Apply Gauss's law to a longitudinal vibration

Consider a long-wavelength, nonzero wavevector \(\boldsymbol k\) with no external free charge. Gauss's law gives \(\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol D=0\). For a longitudinal field \(D_L=0\), so the ions create \(E_L=-NZ^*u/(\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty)\). Substituting this opposing field into the undamped oscillator equation adds a restoring term. Thus

\[\omega_{\rm LO}^2=\omega_{\rm TO}^2+S/\epsilon_\infty,\]
\[\frac{\omega_{\rm LO}^2}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2}=\frac{\epsilon_s}{\epsilon_\infty}.\]

Equivalently the longitudinal collective mode is the zero of the lossless dielectric function. The transverse optical frequency is its pole. For a single isotropic mode this is the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation. With several infrared modes, anisotropy, damping or mobile free carriers, assigning one measured peak and one dielectric ratio to a single oscillator needs further justification.

Connect a reflectance band with the two modes

In the lossless limit rewrite \(\epsilon_r=\epsilon_\infty(\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega^2)/(\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2)\). Between the two frequencies this is negative. A transverse electromagnetic wave satisfies \(k^2=\epsilon_r\omega^2/c^2\), so its wavenumber becomes imaginary there in a nonmagnetic lossless crystal. At a planar vacuum boundary a semi-infinite crystal therefore has unit ideal reflectance over this interval. Finite damping makes the refractive index complex and allows absorption, smoothing the edges. The two mode frequencies have been deduced from mechanical response and electric boundary conditions; an electromagnetic wave interacting with the lattice is a coupled phonon–polariton excitation.

For \(f_{\rm TO}=6.0\,\mathrm{THz}\), \(\epsilon_\infty=4\) and \(\epsilon_s=9\), \(f_{\rm LO}=9.0\,\mathrm{THz}\). At 7.5 THz in the lossless limit \(\epsilon_r=4(81-56.25)/(36-56.25)=-4.89\). Below the mode, normal-incidence static-index reflectance is \([(\sqrt9-1)/(\sqrt9+1)]^2=0.25\). Above the phonon plateau the corresponding background reflectance approaches \(1/9\). Thermal radiance, étendue and detector calibration

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A square millimetre of a heated object emits thermal radiation into an entire hemisphere. A detector sees only the rays accepted by its optics and only wavelengths to which it responds. Derive the equilibrium spectrum, follow it through an optical collection system and infer what temperature or emissivity a calibrated reading can actually determine.

Count optical states in a large equilibrium cavity

Take a cubic airless cavity of volume \(V\) in thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\). For its bulk mode density we can use periodic boundary conditions. Allowed wavevectors are separated by \(2\pi/L\) in each coordinate. Count a spherical shell in wavevector space, divide by the volume per state and include two independent transverse polarisations. Substituting \(k=2\pi\nu/c\) gives \(g(\nu)d\nu/V=8\pi\nu^2d\nu/c^3\). Using standing waves in an ideally reflecting large box gives the same leading volume density when directions and polarisations are counted consistently.

One oscillator mode of frequency \(\nu\) can hold \(m=0,1,2,\ldots\) photons. Its thermal occupation has probabilities proportional to \(e^{-mh\nu/k_BT}\). Summing the geometric series and taking its logarithmic derivative gives \(\langle m\rangle=1/(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)\). Multiplying by the density of modes and energy per photon gives spectral energy density \(u_\nu\). The zero-point contribution of a harmonic oscillator is not part of the thermal power exchanged between these black surfaces. At low frequency the result approaches an energy \(k_BT\) per mode; extending that classical limit to arbitrarily high frequency would make the integrated power diverge.

An isotropic distribution of energy travelling at speed \(c\) produces a normal flux through a small aperture weighted by the direction cosine. Per unit solid angle its radiance is \(B_\nu=cu_\nu/(4\pi)\). Transform to equal wavelength intervals using \(B_\lambda d\lambda=B_\nu|d\nu|\). The two useful spectral forms are

\[B_\nu=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac1{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1},\]
\[B_\lambda=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.\]

The wavelength expression has units of watts per square metre per steradian per metre of wavelength. To express it per micrometre multiply by one millionth. At 400 K and 10 micrometres the result is 33.56 W per square metre per steradian per micrometre. At 295 K it is 9.14 in the same units. Equal wavelength and equal frequency bins carry different widths, so their distributions reach their maxima at different converted wavelengths. A spectrum's peak should always name its horizontal variable and spectral-density convention.

Distinguish emitter power from brightness in a chosen direction

For a black surface with angle-independent radiance into its outward hemisphere, integrate \(\cos\theta\,d\Omega\). The result is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\). The emitted spectral power per unit area is \(\pi B_\lambda\). Integrate over frequency using \(x=h\nu/(k_BT)\). The remaining integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) follows by expanding \(1/(e^x-1)\) as a sum of exponentials and integrating term by term. This yields Stefan–Boltzmann exitance \(M=\sigma T^4\) and identifies the constant \(\sigma=2\pi^5k_B^4/(15h^3c^2)\).

A black patch of 1.0 square millimetre at 400 K emits about 1.45 mW into its entire outward hemisphere over all wavelengths. The same patch at 295 K emits about 0.429 mW. Replacing one by the other changes all-wavelength hemispherical power by about 1.02 mW. This is not the power a small infrared receiver sees through a narrow filter. The integrated emitter number is valuable for energy conservation, but by itself contains no receiver area, direction, passband or optical transmission.

The surface spectrum of a body in thermal equilibrium has directional spectral emissivity equal to its absorptivity in the corresponding reciprocal optical modes under the usual reciprocal-material conditions. An opaque diffuse grey surface with emissivity \(\epsilon\) in an isotropically illuminated background can be modelled by \(\epsilon B_\lambda(T)+(1-\epsilon)B_\lambda(T_{\rm bg})\). It emits less than a black surface but also reflects background. A shiny object viewed in a warm room may consequently display a radiance temperature nearer the reflected scene than its own physical temperature. Specular surfaces require explicit directional knowledge of that scene.

Derive the geometric acceptance without counting the angle twice

An area element \(dA\) normal to the collection axis emits into \(d\Omega\) at angle \(\theta\) a spectral power \(B_\lambda\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\). For a uniformly filled cone of half-angle \(\alpha\) and a uniform patch of area \(A\), integrating gives \(A\pi\sin^2\alpha\). This is its geometrical throughput in air when there is no vignetting. Optical étendue more generally uses \(n^2dA\cos\theta\,d\Omega\). A lossless passive optical relay in geometrical optics preserves this phase-space measure; correspondingly \(L/n^2\) is conserved along a ray in transparent media under the usual stationary conditions.

A direct lens example makes the restriction tangible. An image relay enlarging a uniformly bright patch by a factor of two in each transverse direction forms an image four times the area. In a lossless paraxial design filling the original pupil, each image point then occupies one quarter the solid angle. A detector covering the whole enlarged image and accepting that cone can receive the original power. Placing a fixed small sensor at the image plane intercepts less area instead. Higher magnification has not created additional radiance, even if it makes a small detail easier to resolve.

For the same 1.0 square millimetre patch seen within ten degrees, the projected acceptance is \(9.47\times10^{-8}\) square metre steradian. A wavelength-independent combined transmission and detector absorption of 0.65 leaves effective acceptance \(6.16\times10^{-8}\) square metre steradian. Integrating the Planck curve from 8 to 12 micrometres gives source and background band radiances 133.74 and 35.43 W per square metre per steradian. Their difference yields only 6.05 microwatts in the receiver. Keeping a single midpoint radiance times four micrometres happens to be fairly close here, but there is no fixed universal midpoint correction across temperatures or filters.

Convert received power to calibrated voltage or counts

A thermal detector may respond to absorbed power through a temperature rise and an electrical responsivity. A photon-counting detector measures absorbed photons, so its count rate contains a factor \(\lambda/(hc)\) multiplying spectral power before integration. Two receivers with identical geometric acceptance and nominal quantum efficiency can respond differently to a changing source temperature if one output is proportional to energy and the other to photon number. Calibrate the actual electrical transfer and make the time interval explicit for accumulated counts.

For an ideal linear power detector let \(V=V_0+gP\), with offset \(V_0\) and gain \(g\). Two calibrated sources spanning the use range determine both. A black reference port at the same ambient temperature can also reject a common background in a chopped measurement. A filter, window or warm lens that absorbs thermal radiation also emits it. If these optics remain unchanged and stable, part of their own emission cancels in a reference difference. A reference traversing a different path generally leaves a residual input that is not captured by an electronic dark measurement.

Suppose effective source-minus-ambient powers of 2.00 and 8.00 microwatts yield 0.120 and 0.420 V. The fitted slope is 0.0500 V per microwatt with an offset of 0.020 V. A reading of 0.320 V implies 6.00 microwatts after offset correction. Substituting the same reading directly into a single-point gain with zero assumed offset would bias the inferred temperature. Rechecking a third reference near six microwatts tests linearity, while repeated zero-input readings at realistic instrument temperature test offset drift.

Invert the radiance and propagate uncertainties

A narrowband blackbody radiance measurement can be inverted analytically by solving Planck's expression for \(T\). In a broad passband use numerical quadrature across the calibrated spectral response and solve a scalar monotone equation when emissivity and background are known. The derivative at a single wavelength is particularly informative for estimating precision. With \(x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)\),

\[\frac{\partial\log B_\lambda}{\partial\log T}=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}},\]
\[T=\frac{hc/(\lambda k_B)}{\log[1+2hc^2/(\lambda^5B_\lambda)]}.\]

At 10 micrometres and 400 K the logarithmic sensitivity is 3.70. A one-percent relative radiance uncertainty therefore gives about 1.08 K temperature uncertainty if all other quantities are exact. An unknown five-percent emissivity error can dominate that estimate. A two-colour temperature ratio may reduce uncertainty from an emissivity that is constant across the two narrow bands, but cannot cancel an arbitrary spectral emissivity or a large reflected ambient component.

As an example consider a grey object at 400 K and ambient 295 K with emissivity 0.70. At ten micrometres its outgoing radiance is \(0.70(33.56)+0.30(9.14)=26.24\) in the earlier spectral units. Inverting as a perfect blackbody gives a brightness temperature of about 375 K. The difference is a physical radiance–temperature degeneracy. Repeat at known ambient temperatures while keeping the object thermally stabilised to constrain the reflected fraction. If heating the background changes the actual object temperature, a second independent temperature sensor or a transient thermal model is necessary.

When detector heat capacity sets the measured signal

Consider a thermal receiver of heat capacity \(C_d\) coupled to its surroundings with thermal conductance \(G_d\). For small excursions its temperature obeys \(C_d\dot\theta+G_d\theta=P_{\rm abs}(t)\), where \(\theta\) is the excess above its operating point. A voltage thermometer with responsivity \(r_T\) converts this into output. Its frequency-dependent power responsivity is \(r_T/(G_d-i\omega C_d)\). The geometric and spectral calculations determine absorbed optical power, while this thermal equation determines how much of its modulation appears at the electrical output.

Take \(C_d=2.0\) microjoules per kelvin and \(G_d=0.10\) milliwatt per kelvin. The thermal time constant is 20 ms and ordinary corner frequency is 7.96 Hz. A sinusoidal absorbed-power amplitude of 6.0 microwatts would give a static temperature rise of 0.060 K. At 20 Hz the amplitude is reduced by \(1/\sqrt{1+(2\pi f\tau)^2}=0.370\), leaving about 22.2 mK with a phase lag magnitude near 68 degrees. A receiver calibrated with a steady optical power is not automatically calibrated for this chopped experiment.

A square-wave chopper also changes the relation between time-domain peak differences and a lock-in's fundamental reading. For equal hot and reference dwell times, a square wave switching between powers \(P_1,P_0\) has fundamental sinusoidal peak amplitude \(2(P_1-P_0)/\pi\), with different factors for rms and peak reports. The thermal transfer attenuates this component at the chopper frequency and the odd harmonics at their respective frequencies. Substituting the full hot-minus-reference power directly into the first-harmonic response would overestimate the expected signal.

Measure a frequency sweep on a stable reference and test whether amplitude and phase share a single time constant. A geometrical error rescales the amplitude but ordinarily leaves this thermal phase unchanged. A second thermal mass or an electronic filter can introduce another pole. Lowering chop frequency increases thermal signal until the static limit but can expose low-frequency environmental drift. Choose the frequency by comparing the calibrated response with the measured noise spectrum.

Finally, a reference and an unknown source must illuminate the same active area if responsivity varies across the receiver. Imaging one source as a small spot and the other as a filled patch can create a calibration bias even when total spectral powers agree. Translating a reference spot across the detector or imaging the same aperture successively at both temperatures tests that spatial uniformity assumption.

If the sensor is used on a source much smaller than the calibrated reference aperture, record an image or a field-stop scan to determine its filling fraction. Otherwise even exact knowledge of filter transmission and thermodynamic temperature leaves an unknown geometrical scale factor. Aperture filling is part of the measurement equation, not an electronic gain setting.

Cyclotron motion and quantised magnetic orbits

Condensed matter and band theory

Relate a magnetic-field measurement to band curvature, level spacing and a cross-section of the Fermi surface.

From first principles

Find the orbit and energy spacing imposed by a magnetic field

Consider an electron in an isotropic parabolic band with effective mass \(m^*\), no electric field and a uniform magnetic induction \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\). The Lorentz force is perpendicular to velocity, so it changes the direction of the transverse velocity without changing its magnitude. Taking absolute values in the radial force balance gives

\[\frac{m^* v_\perp^2}{r_c}=e v_\perp B,\]
\[r_c=\frac{m^*v_\perp}{eB},\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m^*}.\]

To obtain energy levels one also needs quantum mechanics. Choose \(\mathbf A=(0,Bx,0)\) and electron charge \(-e\) with \(e>0\). The Hamiltonian \((\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2/(2m^*)\) is independent of \(y,z\), so their canonical momenta can be treated as fixed in a plane-wave state. Complete the square:

\[H=\frac{p_x^2}{2m^*}+\frac{m^*\omega_c^2}{2}\left(x+\frac{p_y}{eB}\right)^2+\frac{p_z^2}{2m^*}.\]

This is a harmonic oscillator about a centre set by \(p_y\). Its transverse levels have energies \(\hbar\omega_c(n+1/2)\); longitudinal translation remains free. Spin splitting, anisotropic band curvature and collision broadening require further information. The same frequency occurs in the classical orbit and the quantum level spacing for distinct reasons made explicit by these two constructions.

Derivation and applications

Determine which mass a cyclotron resonance measures

An orbit on an anisotropic band need not be circular. Consider a weak uniform magnetic induction \(B\) along \(z\), and motion in a single band with elliptical constant-energy contours in the \(xy\) plane. Neglect scattering over an orbit, electric fields, and spin splitting. Write the local dispersion as \(E=\hbar^2 k_x^2/(2m_x)+\hbar^2 k_y^2/(2m_y)\), measured from its minimum. For charge \(-e\), the semiclassical force law and the velocity components give

\[\dot k_x=-\frac{eB}{m_y}k_y,\]
\[\dot k_y=\frac{eB}{m_x}k_x .\]

Taking a second derivative cancels the other component. Both coordinates oscillate at the same fundamental angular frequency even though their amplitudes differ,

\[\ddot k_x=-\frac{e^2B^2}{m_xm_y}k_x,\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m_c},\]
\[m_c=\sqrt{m_xm_y}.\]

A geometric measurement leads to the same mass. The two semiaxes of the contour at energy \(E\) are \(\sqrt{2m_iE}/\hbar\). Its reciprocal-space area \(A(E)\) and energy derivative are

\[A(E)=\frac{2\pi E}{\hbar^2}\sqrt{m_xm_y},\]
\[\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\frac{dA}{dE}=m_c .\]

The derivative explains how a measurement of orbit area over a small energy range tests the resonance result. Two bands with the same area at one energy can have different derivatives there. For a circle, both principal masses and the cyclotron mass coincide. A single resonance frequency for this orientation measures their geometric mean and leaves their ratio undetermined.

Worked example

Predict the resonance of an elliptical pocket

A layer has \(m_x=0.100m_e\), \(m_y=0.900m_e\) and is placed in \(B=1.50\,\mathrm T\). The cyclotron mass is \(0.300m_e\), so the ordinary frequency is \(f_c=eB/(2\pi m_c)=140\,\mathrm{GHz}\). The period is \(7.14\,\mathrm{ps}\). The long reciprocal-space semiaxis lies along \(y\) and is three times the short one, even though both components complete their cycle in exactly the same time.

Second application

Use two nearby contour areas as a mass measurement

In a different sample the orbit area rises by \(0.200\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}\) when energy rises by \(20.0\,\mathrm{meV}\). Treat the measured slope as locally constant. In SI units \(dA/dE=2.00\times10^{17}/(3.204\times10^{-21})\,\mathrm{m^{-2}J^{-1}}\). The area formula gives \(m_c=1.10\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}=0.121m_e\). An independent determination of \(m_x=0.0600m_e\) would then imply \(m_y=m_c^2/m_x=0.245m_e\) at the displayed precision.

Follow a wave packet in a band

A narrow wave packet in a well-isolated band moves with \(\mathbf v=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}E\). In a static uniform magnetic field the semiclassical force law is \(\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=-e\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\). Thus \(\nabla E\cdot\dot{\mathbf k}=0\): the energy is constant because a magnetic field does no work. The wavevector component along \(\mathbf B\) is also constant. The orbit in reciprocal space lies along the intersection of a constant-energy surface and a plane perpendicular to the field. Tilting the field samples different sections of a nonspherical Fermi surface.

For an isotropic parabolic band, motion transverse to the field is circular at \(\omega_c=eB/m^*\). The real-space orbit radius is \(v_\perp/\omega_c\). One can measure an effective mass from the resonant angular frequency without separately knowing the elastic scattering time. Whether the motion survives long enough to be resolved depends on \(\omega_c\tau\). A very short scattering time smears the resonance and suppresses oscillatory magnetotransport.

Derive the quantised spacing

For a two-dimensional electron choose \(A_y=Bx\) and \(A_x=0\). Since the Hamiltonian for charge \(-e\) is \(H=[p_x^2+(p_y+eBx)^2]/(2m^*)\), a state of definite \(p_y=\hbar k_y\) leaves a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator in \(x\), centred at \(-\hbar k_y/(eB)\). Its levels are \(E_j=(j+1/2)\hbar\omega_c\), neglecting spin. At fixed level, changing \(k_y\) moves the orbit centre without changing its energy. The centres allowed inside a rectangle of area \(A\) count to \(eBA/h\) per spin, up to edge corrections. In three dimensions motion along the field adds a dispersing kinetic-energy term.

Take \(B=5\) T and \(m^*=0.10m_e\). The orbital spacing is about 5.79 meV, equivalent to \(k_B T\) at 67 K. At 4 K the thermal scale is about 0.345 meV, smaller than the spacing. An observation still requires sufficiently weak disorder broadening and appropriate carrier filling. As the field changes, both the level spacing and the number of states carried by each level change.

Extract a reciprocal-space area from a measurement

For a closed semiclassical orbit of area \(A_k(E)\), quantisation gives \(A_k=2\pi eB(j+\gamma)/\hbar\), where the phase offset \(\gamma\) depends on the orbit. Successive levels crossing the Fermi energy give oscillations periodic in reciprocal field with frequency \(F=\hbar A_k(E_F)/(2\pi e)\), expressed in tesla. For \(F=100\) T, \(A_k\simeq9.55\times10^{17}\) m⁻². A circular section of that area has radius \(5.51\times10^8\) m⁻¹. In a three-dimensional metal dominant oscillations come from extremal sections. Inferring a full Fermi volume from one frequency requires additional assumptions about shape and symmetry; measurements at several orientations can test them.

Count orbit centres and Hall filling

Landau level spacing determines the energy cost of changing orbital index. A second question is how many independent states fit into each level. Take a rectangular two-dimensional layer of area \(L_xL_y\), magnetic field \(B\) normal to it and Landau gauge \(A_y=Bx\). Translation symmetry along \(y\) gives wavevectors \(k_y=2\pi j/L_y\). In the transverse Hamiltonian the term \((\hbar k_y+eBx)^2/(2m^*)\) describes a harmonic oscillator with centre \(x_0=-\hbar k_y/(eB)\).

To place an orbit centre inside the sample, \(k_y\) must range over an interval of length \(eBL_x/\hbar\). Dividing by the allowed spacing \(2\pi/L_y\) gives \(N_\phi=eBL_xL_y/h\) bulk states per spin-resolved orbital level, neglecting edge corrections. Each magnetic flux quantum \(h/e\) adds one state per level. For sheet density \(n_s\) define filling factor \(\nu=n_sh/(eB)\). This counts occupied spin-resolved levels when it is an integer; if a residual spin degeneracy is unresolved, levels arrive in pairs.

In an ideal low-temperature integer quantum Hall state, with the chemical potential in a mobility gap, longitudinal dissipation can vanish and Hall conductance magnitude is \(\nu e^2/h\). The classical expression \(n_se/B\) has the same value at exact integer filling, but alone cannot explain persistence of plateaus over a finite interval of field. Localization and edge transport are essential for that observation.

Locate an integer filling in a measurement

A two-dimensional electron system has \(n_s=3.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻² and effective mass \(0.067m_e\). The field for total filling \(\nu=2\) is \(B=n_sh/(2e)=6.20\) T. At 4.2 K the thermal energy is 0.362 meV, so thermal smearing is smaller than the orbital gap; disorder and spin structure still matter.

Increasing the field at fixed density decreases filling and can move the Fermi level into another broadened level. A simultaneous longitudinal voltage measurement is indispensable: a Hall voltage of the right numerical size at a single field can also occur in a classical conductor. A broad flat Hall trace together with a longitudinal-resistance minimum gives stronger evidence for quantized transport.

Changing the sample area at constant sheet density and magnetic field increases the number of occupied particles and the number of orbit centres in the same proportion. Filling factor and the ideal Hall resistance therefore remain unchanged, even though total particle count can be very different. In contrast, a gate that varies sheet density at fixed field moves the layer through successive fillings. The expected density spacing per spin-resolved level is \(eB/h\). A gate-calibrated scan can thus test Landau degeneracy independently of the cyclotron energy spacing inferred from optical or thermal observations.

Superconductivity, magnetic screening and flux quantisation

Condensed matter and band theory

Separate persistent current from magnetic flux expulsion and derive two measurable length and flux scales of a superconducting state.

From first principles

Connect a material current law to magnetic screening

A vanishing electrical resistance by itself allows an already existing current to persist. To predict the equilibrium field profile in a thick superconductor one needs additional physical input. In a uniform isotropic superconductor with no vortices the second London relation connects the curl of the supercurrent to the induction. Here \(n_s\) counts carriers of mass \(m\) and signed charge \(q\). Combine this constitutive law with static Ampère’s law:

\[\nabla\times\mathbf j_s=-\frac{n_s q^2}{m}\mathbf B,\]
\[\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j_s .\]

Take a second curl of the field law and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). The sign from the vector identity cancels that in the London relation:

\[\nabla^2\mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf B}{\lambda_L^2},\]
\[\lambda_L=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0n_s q^2}}.\]

Fill \(x>0\) with the material and impose a parallel surface field \(B(0)=B_0\). With no field growth deep in the bulk, only one exponential survives: \(B(x)=B_0e^{-x/\lambda_L}\). Thus the single measured length constrains a ratio \(m/(n_s q^2)\). This construction assumes linear local response and a field low enough for the vortex-free state and its prescribed surface condition to apply.

Derivation and applications

Determine how deeply a parallel field penetrates a finite slab

The exponential London solution assumes a sample extending far beyond a single exposed surface. A film has two surfaces whose screening currents can overlap. Consider an infinite slab of total thickness \(2d\), occupying \(-d<x<d\). Apply a static induction \(B_a\) parallel to its faces, along \(z\), and assume a uniform London penetration length \(\lambda\). Work in the vortex-free Meissner state with fields small enough for linear response. Translational invariance parallel to the slab gives the boundary problem

\[\frac{d^2 B_z}{dx^2}=\frac{B_z}{\lambda^2},\]
\[B_z(-d)=B_z(d)=B_a .\]

A general solution has a hyperbolic cosine and a hyperbolic sine. Reflection about the midplane makes the field even, which removes the sine term. The surface field fixes the remaining coefficient. Ampère’s law then determines the screening current, whose sign reverses at the midplane,

\[B_z(x)=B_a\frac{\cosh(x/\lambda)}{\cosh(d/\lambda)},\]
\[J_y(x)=-\frac{B_a}{\mu_0\lambda}\frac{\sinh(x/\lambda)}{\cosh(d/\lambda)}.\]

A probe at the centre and a probe integrating flux over the cross-section read different quantities. The latter reports the thickness average obtained by integrating the field profile,

\[\frac{\overline B}{B_a}=\frac{1}{2dB_a}\int_{-d}^d B_z(x)\,dx=\frac{\lambda}{d}\tanh(d/\lambda).\]

For a thick slab the mean fraction tends to \(\lambda/d\), consistent with two active surface layers. For a thin slab it is \(1-(d/\lambda)^2/3\) to leading order. Thin samples can therefore exhibit weak parallel-field exclusion even when the material is fully superconducting throughout their volume.

Worked example

Compare central and average field in a thick slab

A sample of thickness \(600\,\mathrm{nm}\) with \(\lambda=100\,\mathrm{nm}\) has \(d/\lambda=3\). In \(B_a=2.00\,\mathrm{mT}\) its central field is \(2.00/\cosh3=0.199\,\mathrm{mT}\). The mean induction is \(2.00\,\tanh(3)/3=0.663\,\mathrm{mT}\). The higher mean follows directly from the substantial contribution of material close to both exposed faces.

Second application

Extract a penetration length using a local probe

A probe halfway through a \(120\,\mathrm{nm}\) film measures \(0.800 B_a\). The central-field solution gives \(d/\lambda=\operatorname{arcosh}(1.25)=0.693\), so \(\lambda=86.6\,\mathrm{nm}\). The same film should transmit a mean fraction \(\tanh(0.693)/0.693=0.866\). Comparing this value with an independent flux integral tests the assumptions of uniform thickness and a single penetration length.

Compare two magnetic histories

A normal conductor dissipates energy from a sustained current. A superconductor can support persistent current and, in an appropriate low-field equilibrium state, expels magnetic flux from its bulk. Imagine cooling a specimen through its transition in a field and subsequently measuring its interior. Perfect conductivity alone would imply that a previously existing flux could remain frozen. Equilibrium flux expulsion supplies additional evidence about the state; sample shape, vortices and pinning affect what a real experiment detects. A resistance measurement and a magnetisation measurement therefore probe different properties.

Derive the London screening length

Let mobile condensate carriers have density \(n_s\), mass \(m_s\) and charge \(q_s\), using a consistent convention for particles or pairs. In a bulk region without vortices the London relation is \(\nabla\times\mathbf j_s=-(n_s q_s^2/m_s)\mathbf B\). Combine this with magnetostatic Ampère's law \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j_s\) and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). Taking a curl gives

\[\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2,\qquad \lambda_L=\sqrt{m_s/(\mu_0n_s q_s^2)} .\]

For a half-space \(x>0\) with surface field parallel to the surface and deep interior field zero, \(B(x)=B(0)e^{-x/\lambda_L}\). At depth \(3\lambda_L\), 4.98% of the surface amplitude remains. Taking \(n_s=2.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ electrons and electron mass in the simple carrier convention gives \(\lambda_L\approx37.6\) nm. Real effective masses, temperature-dependent superfluid density and nonlocality can change this value. Mixing pair density with single-electron charge would introduce a systematic factor error.

Quantise a circulating phase

A coherent condensate has a phase \(\phi\) whose change around a closed path must be \(2\pi n\) for integer \(n\) if the complex order parameter is single-valued and nonzero along the path. The gauge-invariant momentum is \(m_s\mathbf v_s=\hbar\nabla\phi-q_s\mathbf A\). Integrating around a loop relates circulation and flux. If a path lies sufficiently deep in a thick ring that its current density is negligible, the velocity term vanishes and enclosed flux comes in units \(\Phi_0=h/|q_s|\). For paired carriers \(|q_s|=2e\) the unit is \(2.068\times10^{-15}\) Wb. In thin rings where the kinetic term matters, it is the full fluxoid combination that is quantised.

Use length scales to interpret the experiment

The penetration depth characterises field screening. A separate coherence length \(\xi\) measures the distance over which the condensate amplitude recovers after a perturbation. In bulk Ginzburg–Landau theory near the transition, the ratio \(\lambda/\xi\) distinguishes type I and type II at \(1/\sqrt2\). In a type-II material between the lower and upper critical fields, flux can penetrate in quantised vortices with normal-like cores surrounded by screening currents. A specimen in this mixed state can retain superconducting regions while admitting magnetic flux. Stating field, temperature and cooling history is essential when interpreting the observed signal.

Locate the upper critical field from the linearized order parameter

The London equation assumes an established superconducting density. Near the loss of superconductivity at a high applied field, the order parameter itself becomes small and its spatial variation matters. For a bulk type-II material near its transition use Ginzburg–Landau free-energy density terms \(\alpha|\psi|^2+\beta|\psi|^4/2+|(-i\hbar\nabla-q_s\mathbf A)\psi|^2/(2m_s)\) with \(\alpha<0\), \(\beta>0\) and pair charge magnitude \(|q_s|=2e\). Very close to the upper critical field the density is small, field screening is weak and a uniform induction \(B\) is a suitable leading approximation.

Minimizing the free energy with respect to \(\psi^*\) and discarding the cubic term gives a charged-particle magnetic eigenproblem. The lowest transverse kinetic eigenvalue is the lowest Landau energy \(\hbar|q_s|B/(2m_s)\), achieved with no longitudinal variation. A nontrivial small solution is possible at the onset when this cancels \(\alpha\). Defining \(\xi^2=\hbar^2/(2m_s|\alpha|)\) and \(\Phi_0=h/|q_s|\) gives

\[B_{c2}=\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi\xi^2}.\]

Thus a coherence length inferred from upper critical induction measures the scale of condensate recovery. Penetration depth instead controls field screening in an already developed condensate. The GL derivation is controlled near the critical temperature and for orbital suppression. Strong paramagnetic pair breaking or pronounced anisotropy can complicate the interpretation of a measured high-field transition.

Connect critical induction to vortex spacing

At a specified temperature close to the transition a bulk specimen loses its superconducting response at \(B_{c2}=1.20\) T. The inferred coherence length is \(\sqrt{2.068\times10^{-15}/(2\pi\times1.20)}=16.6\) nm. An independent penetration-depth measurement at the same temperature yields \(\lambda=120\) nm, giving \(\lambda/\xi=7.24\), above the type-II boundary. Using lengths measured at unrelated temperatures would weaken that check.

At mean induction 0.200 T within the mixed state each singly quantized vortex carries one flux quantum. Its share of sample area is \(\Phi_0/B=1.03\times10^{-14}\) m². For a triangular lattice that area also equals \(\sqrt3\,a_v^2/2\), giving vortex spacing \(a_v=109\) nm. Increasing induction fourfold halves the spacing if the array remains in the mixed state and retains its geometry. Near \(B_{c2}\) cores overlap and an isolated-vortex picture becomes unreliable even though the linearized onset scale remains useful. Comparing a measured spacing with \(\Phi_0/B\) tests flux counting independently of the inferred coherence length and of the magnetic field profile around any single vortex.

A useful unit check compares the superconducting flux quantum with the electron flux scale from ordinary Landau-level counting. A condensate pair has twice the charge magnitude of one electron, giving half the flux quantum for a phase winding. At 0.200 T a square micrometre should contain about \(BA/\Phi_0=96.7\) singly quantized vortices away from edges. Counting vortices in an image therefore gives a bulk average induction estimate independent of a local peak-field calibration. A surface barrier, pinning or an insufficiently large field of view can account for a local deviation from the average.

Imaging successively larger regions tests how well such local counts represent the bulk.

Counting carriers and weighing a cyclotron orbit

Condensed matter and band theory

A conical band has no constant quadratic band mass at its touching point. A carrier on a finite-energy orbit still has a measurable cyclotron mass. Counting states fixes the Fermi circle and determines how this mass changes when a gate adds charge.

Count states in a two-dimensional disc

Put a two-dimensional sheet of area \(A=L_xL_y\) under periodic boundary conditions. Neighbouring allowed wavevectors differ by \(2\pi/L_x\) and \(2\pi/L_y\), so each state occupies reciprocal-space area \((2\pi)^2/A\). At zero temperature fill all conduction states up to radius \(k_F\), measured from a Dirac point. There are two spin states and two inequivalent valleys in ideal monolayer graphene, giving total degeneracy \(g=4\). Density relative to charge neutrality is therefore

\[n=g\frac{\pi k_F^2}{(2\pi)^2}=\frac{k_F^2}{\pi},\]
\[E_F=\hbar v_F\sqrt{\pi n}.\]

For electron and hole densities use \(|n|\) under the square root and give \(E_F\) the sign of \(n\) relative to the touching point. Differentiating the state count with respect to energy gives the density of states per area \(D(E)=g|E|/[2\pi(\hbar v_F)^2]\). It tends to zero at an ideal neutral cone. Thermal broadening, disorder and a gap all change what a finite-resolution probe observes near neutrality.

Let a perpendicular field bend the orbit

For a wavepacket in a band with weak magnetic field, \(\hbar\dot{\boldsymbol k}=-e\,\boldsymbol v\times\boldsymbol B\), where \(e\) denotes the positive elementary charge. An isotropic band has velocity normal to a circular constant-energy contour. The orbit is traversed with speed \(|\dot{\boldsymbol k}|=e v_F B/\hbar\). Dividing its circumference \(2\pi k_F\) by that speed gives the period. Express its angular frequency as \(eB/m_c\) to identify

\[m_c=\frac{\hbar k_F}{v_F}=\frac{|E_F|}{v_F^2}.\]

The mass measures the time needed to travel around a contour. It depends on the radius even when the magnitude of the group velocity stays fixed. For a general band area \(\mathcal A(E)\), neighbouring contours separated perpendicular to themselves by \(dk_\perp\) differ in energy by \(\hbar v\,dk_\perp\). Thus \(|d\mathcal A/dE|=\oint dl/(\hbar v)\), which gives \(m_c=\hbar^2|d\mathcal A/dE|/(2\pi)\) by integrating the same equation of motion.

Convert a gate density into an energy and a mass

At \(n=10^{12}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}=10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}\) and \(v_F=10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\), one obtains \(k_F=1.772\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(E_F=0.117\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(m_c=1.87\times10^{-32}\,\mathrm{kg}=0.0205m_e\). Quadrupling the density doubles the Fermi energy and cyclotron mass. By contrast a single isotropic parabolic band \(E=\hbar^2 k^2/(2m)\) has the same counting geometry but constant \(m_c=m\). Transport mobility or disorder scattering time does not enter the state count, although scattering can obscure the cyclotron motion.

Change velocity and signed density. Raising the velocity at fixed density increases \(|E_F|\) while reducing the mass because the orbit radius stays fixed and the wavepacket moves faster. Exactly at charge neutrality, a single classical Fermi orbit no longer describes the carriers. Finite-field quantum levels replace the continuum picture in the next chapter.

Quantising the orbit and finding the zero Landau level

Condensed matter and band theory

In a magnetic field the two kinetic momentum components cannot be specified simultaneously. In a conical band their commutator turns the two sublattice equations into coupled oscillator equations. Their spectra differ by one state, leaving an unpaired level at zero energy.

Construct the oscillator from kinetic momentum

In one valley of a gapless monolayer use the long-wavelength Hamiltonian \(H=v_F(\sigma_x\Pi_x+\sigma_y\Pi_y)\), with Pauli matrices acting on sublattices. For an electron of charge \(-e\), \(\boldsymbol\Pi=\boldsymbol p+e\boldsymbol A\). In a uniform perpendicular field \(B>0\), applying \([p_j,A_k]=-i\hbar\partial_j A_k\) gives \([\Pi_x,\Pi_y]=-ie\hbar B\). Introduce \(a=(\Pi_x-i\Pi_y)/\sqrt{2e\hbar B}\). Its adjoint uses the opposite sign, giving \([a,a^\dagger]=1\). The magnetic length \(\ell_B=\sqrt{\hbar/(eB)}\) sets the spatial size of the oscillator.

\[H=v_F\sqrt{2e\hbar B}\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\a^\dagger&0\end{pmatrix},\]
\[H^2=2e\hbar Bv_F^2\begin{pmatrix}aa^\dagger&0\\0&a^\dagger a\end{pmatrix}.\]

In the B component the squared Hamiltonian has oscillator numbers \(0,1,2,\ldots\). In the A component \(aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+1\), so its sequence starts at one. For integer \(n\ge1\) combine \(|n-1\rangle\) on A with \(\pm|n\rangle\) on B in equal magnitudes. Acting with \(H\) confirms energies \(E_{n,\pm}=\pm v_F\sqrt{2e\hbar Bn}\). For \(n=0\), the B-only spinor with \(|0\rangle\) is annihilated by \(a\). Its energy vanishes. Valley and field conventions can exchange which sublattice carries that state.

Count independent centres at the same energy

Choose gauge \(A_y=Bx\) and retain translation along \(y\). Quantisation gives \(k_y=2\pi j/L_y\). The kinetic momentum \(\Pi_y=\hbar k_y+eBx\) places the orbit centre at \(x_0=-\hbar k_y/(eB)\). Successive centres differ by \(2\pi\ell_B^2/L_y\). Counting centres in a width \(L_x\) gives \(L_xL_y/(2\pi\ell_B^2)\), or \(eB/h\) states per area for each spin and valley, apart from edge corrections. This degeneracy is independent of level index. Spin splitting, lattice effects and interactions are set aside here.

At \(B=1\,\mathrm T\), \(v_F=10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_B=25.7\,\mathrm{nm}\) and the first positive level is \(36.3\,\mathrm{meV}\) above zero. The next sits at \(51.3\,\mathrm{meV}\). The spacing between the first two positive levels is consequently only 15.0 meV. At 4 T all these energies double while the number of orbit centres per unit area quadruples. A parabolic band would instead give equally spaced levels at fixed field.

Identify the measurable dependence

Compare the first few positive levels as field changes. Dividing \(E_n\) by \(\sqrt{Bn}\) should return one material-dependent coefficient. An optical measurement sees transitions between occupied and available states, subject to matrix-element selection rules. A measured transition energy therefore need not coincide with the energy of an individual level. At zero field this discrete-level construction loses its normalisable magnetic oscillator; the limiting energies on the graph describe the approach from finite field.

Identify mobile charge with a transverse field

Fields and the response of matter

Derive a Hall-voltage measurement from transverse force balance, reverse current and field to reject offsets and explore what fails when two carrier types conduct.

Choose signs from a drawing

A rectangular conducting sample has its long axis along \(x\), width \(w\) along \(y\), and thickness \(t\) along \(z\). A uniform magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\) is applied. An external circuit maintains conventional current \(I>0\) in \(+x\). High-impedance transverse probes draw negligible current. Define the measured signal as \(V_H=V(y=0)-V(y=w)\). For uniform transverse field this convention gives \(V_H=E_yw\). Reversing the voltmeter leads reverses the reported sign; writing a carrier sign without a lead convention is incomplete.

With one charge carrier species of number density \(n\) and signed charge \(q\), the drift velocity satisfies \(j_x=nqv_x=I/(wt)\). If the transverse velocity vanishes, its magnetic force component is \(-qv_xB\), opposed by \(qE_y\). Thus \(E_y=v_x B=j_xB/(nq)\). The resulting port measurement is

\[V_H=\frac{IB}{nq t}.\]

The width cancels for a uniform bar; changing the contact geometry or using a current source with a nonuniform injection profile can spoil that simplification near the terminals. The cancellation also exposes the sign. For electrons \(q<0\), positive conventional current means that the mobile charge drifts in \(-x\). The inferred transverse field is negative for positive \(B\) in the chosen convention.

Reconstruct density and mobility from separate observations

Take \(t=0.50\) mm, \(I=5.0\) mA, \(B=0.20\) T and a transverse signal of \(-1.25\) mV. The inferred signed Hall coefficient is \(R_H=tV_H/(IB)=-6.25\times10^{-4}\ {\rm m^3/C}\). Using singly charged electrons gives \(n=1/(e|R_H|)\simeq1.00\times10^{22}\ {\rm m^{-3}}\). A longitudinal voltage measurement, after correcting for probe separation, can yield the material conductivity. If \(\sigma=80\) S/m, the single-carrier mobility is \(\mu=\sigma |R_H|=0.050\ {\rm m^2/(V\,s)}\).

These operations use different pieces of information. The longitudinal measurement alone gives only the product \(n|q|\mu\). The transverse measurement supplies a sign and, in the one-carrier model, the density independently of mobility. This is an inverse experiment whose two observables have different sensitivities, not a more precise measurement of the same scalar.

One can derive the same result from a stationary Drude equation \(0=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)-m\mathbf v/\tau\). The longitudinal drag gives \(E_x=mv_x/(q\tau)\); force balance in \(y\) gives the transverse field just obtained. In a homogeneous single-carrier sample, \(E_y/E_x=q\tau B/m\), the signed Hall angle tangent. At fixed sample and field, doubling \(I\) doubles both voltages in this linear regime and leaves their ratio unchanged. Significant current-dependent apparent density can indicate heating or nonlinear contacts.

Isolate the transverse voltage from a misaligned contact

Practical side contacts seldom sit at exactly the same \(x\). A fraction of the longitudinal voltage can leak into the transverse pair. Write the readout at a fixed current as \(V_\mathrm{m}(B)=V_0+\alpha V_{xx}+V_H(B)\). A background that is even in field cancels in \([V_\mathrm m(B)-V_\mathrm m(-B)]/2\). For example, readings of 3.05 mV and 5.55 mV at \(+B,-B\) return \(-1.25\) mV Hall signal and 4.30 mV of even background. Taking a single measured sign would have diagnosed the wrong carriers.

When a Hall density stops being a particle count

With electrons and holes, longitudinal conductivities add. In a weak magnetic field their transverse contributions also depend on squared mobilities. For positive mobilities \(\mu_e,\mu_h\) and electron and hole densities \(n,p\), the two-carrier weak-field coefficient in the sign convention above is \(R_H=(p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2)/[e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2]\). To see where the square appears, note that one factor of mobility converts the longitudinal field into drift; a second converts the resulting transverse magnetic force into a drift contribution. Summing species currents and imposing zero total transverse current gives the denominator management through the summed longitudinal conductivity.

Equal electron and hole densities with equal mobilities give zero Hall signal while current still flows readily. A single-species inversion would suggest unbounded density. At equal densities but unequal mobilities, the faster species can set the sign even though neither species is more numerous. Additional field dependence, temperature variation and independent structural information are needed before treating a Hall number as a carrier census.

Distinguish magnetic screening from electrical diffusion

Fields and the response of matter

Perfect conductivity and magnetic expulsion are different statements

A normal conductor has current density \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E\). Combining Faraday's and low-frequency Ampère laws in a homogeneous medium gives \(\partial_t\mathbf B=(\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf B\). A step in field diffuses inwards over a length of order \(\sqrt{t/(\mu\sigma)}\). Under sustained sinusoidal drive the skin depth is \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\). As \(\omega\) tends to zero, this skin depth tends to infinity. An ordinary nonmagnetic conductor in its final static equilibrium generally admits a uniform applied magnetic field.

The London description of an isotropic superconductor in a Meissner state makes a distinct, equilibrium constitutive assumption: \(\nabla\times\mathbf J_s=-\mathbf B/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2)\), with penetration length \(\lambda_L\). In a homogeneous specimen carrying supercurrent and at frequencies where displacement current and normal losses may be neglected, \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J_s\). Curl once more and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). The resulting field satisfies \(\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2\). Unlike the diffusive skin depth, the equilibrium length remains finite for a static applied field.

Taking the formal limit \(\sigma\to\infty\) in a normal conductor suppresses the electric field for a finite current and can freeze a pre-existing flux during changes. That argument does not determine which static field a specimen adopts when cooled through its transition. Magnetic expulsion is extra thermodynamic physics. The comparison below assumes a weak enough field and a suitable state, omitting flux vortices, trapped flux and nonlinear or nonlocal effects.

Solve a slab with equal applied fields on both faces

Let a broad slab fill \(-d/2<x<d/2\), and impose identical tangential magnetic fields \(B_a\hat{\mathbf z}\) on its two faces. Symmetry requires \(B'(0)=0\). Applying both boundaries to the London equation gives

\[B(x)=B_a\frac{\cosh(x/\lambda_L)}{\cosh[d/(2\lambda_L)]},\qquad\]

\frac{\overline B}{B_a}=\frac{2\lambda_L}{d}\tanh\frac{d}{2\lambda_L}.

Differentiating gives the screening current \(J_y=-B'_z/\mu_0\). Currents flow in opposite directions at opposite faces; their net transport current across the thickness is zero. For a thick slab each face approaches a half-space with exponential decay. For \(d\ll\lambda_L\), the two surface layers overlap. Expanding the average gives \(1-d^2/(12\lambda_L^2)+\cdots\), so even a superconductor can have a substantial mean internal field in a sufficiently thin specimen under this parallel-field geometry.

Take \(d=100\) nm and \(\lambda_L=50\) nm. The centre field is \(B_a/\cosh 1\simeq0.6481 B_a\); the mean is \(\tanh1\simeq0.7616 B_a\). The distinction between centre value and volume average is already larger than many instrumental error bars. A measurement of magnetic moment in a finite non-slab geometry additionally requires its demagnetising field. Do not directly substitute the infinite-slab mean into an arbitrary sample-shape calibration.

Obtain the equilibrium length as an energy minimum

The same static profile follows from minimising \(\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}[B^2+\lambda_L^2(B')^2]\,dx/(2\mu_0)\) with boundary values imposed. The first term is magnetic field energy; the second is the energy associated with the screening current in this effective description. Vary \(B\) by a function \(\eta\) vanishing at the faces. Integrating \(B'\eta'\) by parts leaves an interior factor \(B-\lambda_L^2B''\). Its vanishing returns the London equation. This route makes the physical tradeoff clear: complete expulsion would require extremely sharp gradients and costly currents, while uniform penetration pays magnetic energy throughout the bulk.

The reduction assumes a fixed superconducting condensate amplitude. Near a critical temperature or critical field, allowing that amplitude to change introduces an additional coherence length and may alter both the field state and the validity of London theory. A numerical fit giving a stable value of \(\lambda_L\) at one temperature does not prove this approximation over an entire phase diagram.

How a magnetic field rotates the polarisation of transmitted light

Fields and the response of matter

Resolve a linearly polarised wave into circular eigenmodes, infer a refractive-index difference from an analyser and distinguish nonreciprocal rotation from a simple change of reference axes.

Derive rotation from propagation rather than drawing an arrow

Let monochromatic light propagate along \(+z\) through a transparent magneto-optic sample, with an externally prepared axial magnetic field. Define fixed laboratory vectors \(\mathbf e_\pm=(\hat x\pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2\) and use phasors \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\). These definitions specify the handedness algebraically. Calling a vector simply “right circular” without stating the viewing and time conventions can reverse a reported rotation sign.

An input linearly polarised in \(x\) is \((\mathbf e_++\mathbf e_-)/\sqrt2\). Suppose the medium supports wave numbers \(k_\pm=2\pi n_\pm/\lambda_0\) for the two bases, with negligible absorption. After a distance \(L\), factor out the common phase \(e^{i(k_++k_-)L/2}\). The remaining Cartesian phasor is \(\hat x\cos\Delta-\hat y\sin\Delta\), where \(\Delta=(k_+-k_-)L/2\). Thus the polarisation angle in the fixed \(x,y\) plane is

\[\theta=\frac{\pi L}{\lambda_0}(n_- - n_+).\]

Equal transmissions of the two components preserve a linearly polarised output. Unequal absorption makes the circular amplitudes unequal and generally produces an ellipse. Rotating a linear analyser then finds a finite minimum, even for a perfectly coherent field. Residual intensity at the nominal extinction angle can also arise from stray light or imperfect polariser extinction, which must be measured independently.

Derive a magneto-optic eigenbasis

For axial magnetisation, a simple lossless transverse relative-permittivity block is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\epsilon&i g\\-i g&\epsilon\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) with real \(g\) at the frequency of observation. Acting on \((1,i)\) gives \((\epsilon-g)(1,i)\); acting on \((1,-i)\) gives \((\epsilon+g)(1,-i)\). For relative permeability near one, \(n_+^2=\epsilon-g\), \(n_-^2=\epsilon+g\). At small \(g\) their difference \(n_- - n_+\simeq g/\sqrt\epsilon\). Reversing magnetisation reverses the sign of the off-diagonal coefficient and interchanges the two eigenvalues. Both the existence of circular modes and the sign-reversal test now follow from a constitutive model.

If \(n_--n_+\) is proportional to magnetic field over a specified range, write \(\theta=VBL\), with Verdet constant \(V\) in rad/(T m). This is a measurable material and wavelength dependent coefficient. It is not determined by the mean refractive index alone. A strong nearby absorption line can give substantial frequency dependence and dichroism, invalidating a lossless narrow-band extrapolation.

Work through an analyser calibration

For \(\lambda_0=600\) nm, \(L=0.20\) mm and measured index difference \(5.0\times10^{-4}\), the rotation is \(\pi(2.0\times10^{-4})(5.0\times10^{-4})/(6.0\times10^{-7})=\pi/6\), or 30°. An ideal analyser at angle \(\alpha\) transmits \(I/I_\mathrm{in}=\cos^2(\alpha-\theta)\). Its maxima occur at \(30^\circ\) and \(210^\circ\); an intensity measurement determines a linear polarisation angle modulo 180°. For a sample thick enough to rotate several half turns, an intensity maximum alone cannot give an unwrapped \(\theta\). Vary field or path length continuously from a known reference.

If the applied field is independently 0.40 T in that example, \(V=\theta/(BL)\simeq6.54\times10^3\) rad/(T m). The arithmetic is dimensionally correct, but the large value warrants a physical check of thickness, field calibration, units and material spectrum before reporting it as an ordinary glass coefficient. Degrees substituted for radians would make the inferred material coefficient too large by \(180/\pi\).

Ask what happens on a return pass

For the ideal Faraday medium at fixed magnetisation, the circular eigenmodes relative to a fixed laboratory basis are determined by its transverse material tensor. On propagation back through the sample, the accumulated positive propagation path again distinguishes the same laboratory circular basis after accounting for the reflection boundary. With a suitable simple mirror at normal incidence, the resulting material rotation doubles on a round trip in fixed transverse coordinates. In a reciprocal optically active material, the eigenvalue assignment with respect to the laboratory basis reverses on reversing propagation, and the single-pass rotation cancels on the ideal return. Write both transformations in one fixed basis before comparing signs.

A real double-pass setup includes mirror phase shifts, stress birefringence of windows and possible ellipticity. Reversing magnetic field, removing the sample and recording both analyser quadratures provide separate checks. A nonzero rotation in one transmitted beam proves only that its polarisation changed; establishing nonreciprocity requires comparing a reversed propagation experiment with a stated polarisation convention.

Relativistic particle dynamics and the field tensor

Waves, radiation and relativity

A circular track measures momentum per charge. A clock following the particle registers proper time, and a detector records how much kinetic energy it has gained. We will put these observations in one dynamical equation, solve that equation in uniform fields and exhibit a numerical pusher that can get the energy right while its orbit phase slowly slips.

What the track, clock and calorimeter measure

Consider a particle of invariant mass \(m\), signed charge \(q\) and ordinary velocity \(\mathbf v\). Inertial observers agree on the proper time between neighbouring events on its timelike worldline, \(d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}\). Adopt coordinates \(x^\mu=(ct,x,y,z)\) and metric \(g=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)\). Dividing the displacement by proper time produces a vector of fixed Minkowski norm. Multiplying it by invariant mass gives four-momentum.

\[u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v),\]
\[p^\mu=mu^\mu=(\mathcal E/c,\mathbf p),\]
\[\mathcal E^2-c^2|\mathbf p|^2=m^2c^4 .\]

Energy includes the rest term. Momentum is \(\gamma m\mathbf v\). Differentiating the last equation on a trajectory gives \(d\mathcal E/dt=\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf p/dt\). This identity replaces the nonrelativistic step of dotting \(m\mathbf a\) with velocity. A magnetic field can rotate momentum with fixed magnitude and energy, whereas an electric field can change energy by doing work.

Suppose an electron begins at rest and gains 10.0 keV of kinetic energy across an electrostatic potential difference in vacuum. Its rest energy is 511 keV, giving \(\gamma=1.01957\) and \(v/c=\sqrt{1-1/\gamma^2}=0.1950\). The nonrelativistic estimate is 0.1978. In a modest energy measurement the discrepancy could lie below the experimental spread. For a precise orbit calculation the 1.96 percent change in relativistic cyclotron period is measurable independently of that discrepancy in speed.

A linear map that produces the Lorentz force

Seek a local equation linear in field and four-velocity and covariant under boosts. For the energy component to give \(q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\), and for the spatial components to give the electric force and the oriented cross product, construct the antisymmetric field tensor. With the stated metric and \(\epsilon^{123}=1\), set

\[F^{0i}=-E_i/c,\]
\[F^{i0}=E_i/c,\]
\[F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B_k,\]
\[\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu .\]

Lowering the index on \(u\) reverses the signs of its spatial components. The spatial product becomes \(q\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\), and division by \(\gamma\) converts the proper-time derivative to \(d\mathbf p/dt\). The time component similarly yields \(d\mathcal E/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\). Contract the force with \(u_\mu\). The symmetric product \(u_\mu u_\nu\) annihilates the antisymmetric tensor, which proves that the evolution preserves the mass shell.

To recover Maxwell's kinematic constraints from potentials, put \(A^\mu=(\phi/c,\mathbf A)\) and \(F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu\). Then \(F^{0i}=(\partial_t A_i+\partial_i\phi)/c=-E_i/c\), while the spatial-spatial components are the magnetic curl. Adding a four-gradient to \(A^\mu\) leaves the tensor unchanged because ordinary derivatives commute. The particle action \(S=-mc^2\int d\tau-q\int A_\mu dx^\mu\) gives the same force after integrating variations by parts with fixed endpoints. Gauge changes add only an endpoint term to this action. A trajectory measured with local forces cannot distinguish such potential representatives.

Constant force, variable coordinate acceleration

Put a particle initially at rest in a uniform static electric field along \(x\). Let \(a_0=qE/m\) be signed. Integration of \(dp_x/dt=qE\) gives \(p_x=ma_0t\), so the speed tends to \(c\) even while momentum increases linearly. Proper-time evolution is simplest in terms of rapidity \(\eta\), where \(u^0=c\cosh\eta\), \(u^x=c\sinh\eta\). The two tensor equations imply \(d\eta/d\tau=a_0/c\).

\[t=\frac{c}{a_0}\sinh(a_0\tau/c),\]
\[x=\frac{c^2}{a_0}\{\cosh(a_0\tau/c)-1\},\]
\[\frac{dv_x}{dt}=\frac{a_0}{\gamma^3}.\]

The initial time and position are zero. The work \(qEx=mc^2(\cosh\eta-1)\) equals kinetic energy, which checks both the integration constant and the dimensional factor in the displacement. For an electron use the direction of the electric force as positive to simplify signs. At 20.0 kV/m it gains 1.0 keV in 5.0 cm. The energy gain follows from the static potential even though the speed does not grow exactly as the square root of distance. The field region must last long enough for the particle to cross it and fringe fields can alter the entry trajectory.

Helical motion, radius and period

A uniform magnetic field along \(z\) keeps \(p_z\), energy and \(|\mathbf p_\perp|\) fixed. Write \(p_x+ip_y\) as a complex number. The Lorentz force reduces to a first-order rotation equation and a single time integration gives the corresponding position. With positive \(B\), positive charges starting along \(+x\) initially turn towards \(-y\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}(p_x+ip_y)=-i\frac{qB}{\gamma m}(p_x+ip_y),\]
\[R=\frac{p_\perp}{|q|B},\]
\[T=\frac{2\pi\gamma m}{|q|B}.\]

The helical pitch is \(v_zT\). Radius gives transverse momentum and a calibrated period gives total energy through \(\gamma\). A track seen only in projection along the field gives no direct evidence that longitudinal momentum vanishes. A second view or a timing measurement can resolve this ambiguity.

At 10.0 keV the electron has \(pc=\sqrt{K(K+2mc^2)}=101.6\) keV. With entry perpendicular to a 2.0 mT field its radius is 0.169 m and its period 18.2 ns. Doubling the field halves both radius and period at fixed injection energy. Doubling momentum at fixed field doubles radius but changes period by the ratio of Lorentz factors. These are different tests of a track calibration. A beam with a spread of energies will dephase relative to a narrow timing gate even when every single particle remains in a perfectly uniform field.

A drift frame gives an exact crossed-field solution

For constant crossed fields with \(E<cB\), transform to the frame moving at \(\mathbf v_d=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). There \(E'=0\) and \(B'=\sqrt{B^2-E^2/c^2}\); calculate the transformed initial momentum and integrate a helix. Transform two successive equal-phase events back to the lab. Their perpendicular displacement in the electric-free frame is zero. If the motion has no component parallel to \(\mathbf B\), the entire displacement there is zero, and the average lab cycle velocity is exactly \(\mathbf v_d\). The detailed lab trace need not be circular and the instantaneous lab energy may oscillate.

Take \(E=30.0\) kV/m along \(y\) and \(B=0.50\) mT along \(z\). The drift is along \(+x\) at \(6.00\times10^7\) m/s. A particle injected at that velocity crosses the uniform fields on a straight line because the forces cancel. An initially stationary positive particle instead begins by accelerating along \(+y\). These observations can use the same field with different injected particles. If \(E\) exceeds \(cB\), this electric-free frame is unavailable. Transform to a magnetic-free frame and integrate electric acceleration instead.

Continuous rotation and a discrete experiment

A naive forward step \(\mathbf p_{n+1}=\mathbf p_n+q\Delta t\,\mathbf v_n\times\mathbf B\) adds a vector perpendicular to the previous momentum. Its squared norm increases by the squared magnitude of that kick. The apparent heating will persist in perfectly static uniform magnetic fields. For a purely magnetic update, evaluate the direction using the mean of old and new momenta. At fixed \(\gamma\), the resulting implicit two-dimensional linear map can be solved algebraically and is an exact rotation by a modified angle.

\[\delta=2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega_c\Delta t}{2}\right),\]
\[\omega_c=\frac{qB}{\gamma m}.\]

The sign of rotation follows the earlier complex equation. Dotting the update with the mean momentum gives zero change in squared norm, which verifies the energy property even for relatively large time steps. A common electromagnetic pusher surrounds the magnetic rotation by two electric half-kicks. In spatially varying fields, specify at what time and position each field is sampled, and check spatial interpolation and time convergence. For ultrarelativistic nearly cancelling electric and magnetic fields, preserving rotation in a pure field alone does not ensure cancellation is represented accurately.

Solve the constant tensor without transforming every time step

When uniform electric and magnetic fields are constant in spacetime, the Lorentz equation is a linear constant-coefficient equation for four-velocity as a function of proper time. Nonlinearity in ordinary time hides that simple structure, because the rate at which proper time accumulates changes along the orbit. Define the mixed tensor \(M^\mu{}_\nu=(q/m)F^{\mu\alpha}g_{\alpha\nu}\). In a uniform field it does not vary along the trajectory. A formal solution is then

\[u(\tau)=e^{M\tau}u(0),\]
\[x(\tau)-x(0)=\int_0^\tau e^{Ms}u(0)\,ds .\]

The coordinate formula is written as an integral since \(M\) can be singular, as it is in a purely magnetic field. Blindly replacing this integral by \(M^{-1}(e^{M\tau}-I)\) fails in exactly such simple cases. A power series for the integral or a block matrix exponential treats zero eigenvalues without an artificial inverse. Antisymmetry of the tensor implies \(M^{\mathsf T}g+gM=0\). Differentiating \(e^{M^{\mathsf T}\tau}ge^{M\tau}\) shows that it stays equal to \(g\), making preservation of \(u^\mu u_\mu=c^2\) a structural property of the exact mapping.

For parallel constant fields along \(z\), the matrix decomposes into a boost in the \(ct,z\) subspace and a rotation in \(x,y\). Write \(\alpha=qE/(mc)\), \(\Omega=qB/m\), and denote the initial four-velocity components by \(u_0^\mu\). The proper-time solution is

\[u^0=u_0^0\cosh\alpha\tau+u_0^z\sinh\alpha\tau,\]
\[u^z=u_0^z\cosh\alpha\tau+u_0^0\sinh\alpha\tau,\]
\[u^x+i u^y=(u_0^x+i u_0^y)e^{-i\Omega\tau}.\]

The longitudinal and transverse operations commute because they act in different subspaces. In lab time the transverse rotation slows as the electric field increases energy, although the proper-time rotation frequency stays constant. A particle therefore traces a helix whose transverse radius \(p_\perp/(|q|B)\) is fixed while the pitch evolves. A single measured turn time without a tagged position in the accelerating region is insufficient for an energy reconstruction.

As a numerical test use an electron, quoting magnitudes, with \(B=1.0\) mT, \(E=10.0\) kV/m and initial purely transverse speed \(0.10c\). One magnetic turn takes 35.72 ns in proper time and accumulates electric rapidity magnitude \(|\alpha|\tau\simeq0.2096\). The final Lorentz factor is \(\gamma_i\cosh(0.2096)=1.0272\), giving final kinetic energy about 13.9 keV, compared with 2.57 keV initially. The lab-time turn duration is \(\gamma_i\sinh(|\alpha|\tau)/|\alpha|\simeq36.2\) ns. Reversing the electric field changes the direction of the axial displacement but leaves final energy and turn duration unchanged for this initially transverse particle. Reversing the initial longitudinal velocity would generally break that energy symmetry.

Infer uncertainty and test units in a momentum reconstruction

A track fitter commonly reports field, radius, pitch, time and a fitted covariance matrix. The dynamical relation turns these observed quantities into momentum. Let \(L_z\) denote a signed pitch per turn. With known signed direction of circulation and calibrated absolute field magnitude, \(|\mathbf p|=|q|B\sqrt{R^2+(L_z/2\pi)^2}\). For small errors, logarithmic differentiation gives weights for fractional radius and pitch errors:

\[d\log p=d\log B+w_R\,d\log R+w_L\,d\log |L_z|,\]
\[w_R=\frac{R^2}{R^2+(L_z/2\pi)^2},\]
\[w_L=1-w_R .\]

For \(R=0.20\) m and \(L_z=0.30\) m the radius accounts for 94.6 percent of the squared transverse-plus-longitudinal geometric factor. Independent relative uncertainties of 0.20 percent in field, 0.50 percent in radius and 2.0 percent in pitch yield about 0.525 percent uncertainty in total momentum. A common detector scale controlling both radius and pitch would enter with correlated signs and should not be counted as two independent errors. When the pitch tends to zero, use the original derivatives with respect to \(L_z\); a reported fractional error on a quantity consistent with zero is ill defined.

Differentiating \(K=\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}-mc^2\) gives \(d\log K=(\gamma+1)d\log p/\gamma\). The coefficient approaches two at low energy and one at very high energy. At the approximately 3.70 keV energy of the tutorial the above momentum error would imply about a 1.05 percent energy error. A kinetic-energy estimate from a small difference of nearly equal numbers can also lose numerical precision at much lower energies. Rationalise it as \(K=p^2c^2/(\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}+mc^2)\) when working near rest.

Several independent checks use this reconstruction without assuming the track fitter is correct. The turn time inferred from the fitted \(\gamma\) can be compared with a separate timing channel. Reverse the magnetic field to reverse the direction of curvature while keeping the speed and energy approximately fixed. Translate the beam within a mapped uniform field and look for changing fitted radius that correlates with position, an indication of geometric or field-map bias. Finally plot the residual of the magnetic force law separately for transverse magnitude and angle. A time integrator may conserve momentum magnitude for symmetry reasons even when its field interpolation biases the phase or shifts the guiding centre systematically.

Why momentum and kinetic energy do not gain fixed fractions together

In an electrostatic accelerating gap, the kinetic energy increment for a particle passing between two equipotential surfaces is its charge times the potential drop, regardless of injection energy. This statement follows by integrating the Lorentz power law over the actual path. A magnetic guiding field can alter the path and transit time while doing no direct work. A time-dependent electric field, a missed equipotential endpoint or appreciable radiation changes the simple inference and must be addressed separately.

For an electron entering with 10 keV and acquiring an additional 10 keV, kinetic energy doubles. Its initial momentum times \(c\) is about 101.6 keV and its final value about 144.4 keV. The radius in the same subsequent perpendicular magnetic field increases by a factor approximately 1.42. Doubling the radius would instead require doubling momentum, which demands a substantially greater energy increment at this low initial energy. Comparing radii before and after a known voltage supplies an independent check on the sign and magnitude of a particle's charge-to-mass model.

A measured energy gain below the nominal voltage does not by itself diagnose radiation. The potential actually traversed, finite angular acceptance, entrance or exit fringe fields, collision losses and biases in reconstructing longitudinal momentum can all matter. Repeat with reversed field polarity or several injection energies and compare the kinetic-energy difference rather than the two radii directly.

Eigenvalues, modes and spectral gaps

Numerical linear algebra

Estimate a dominant vibration or diffusion mode, explain the role of eigenvalue separation and use a residual to check a computed eigenpair.

From first principles

Watch a power iteration select a mode

Start with a real symmetric matrix \(A\) and express a trial vector \(v_0\) in its orthonormal eigenvectors \(u_j\). Order the eigenvalues so \(|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\ge\cdots\) and suppose the starting component \(c_1\) is nonzero. Applying \(A\) repeatedly multiplies each component by its own eigenvalue,

\[v_0=\sum_jc_j u_j,\]
\[A^k v_0=\lambda_1^k\left[c_1u_1+\sum_{j>1} c_j(\lambda_j/\lambda_1)^k u_j\right].\]

Normalizing after each application keeps vector lengths manageable without changing the relative size of the components. The largest competing component therefore shrinks relative to the first at a geometric rate. A scalar estimate of the eigenvalue comes from projecting the action back onto the trial vector,

\[\rho(v)=\frac{v^TAv}{v^Tv}.\]

For an exact eigenvector the quotient equals its eigenvalue. Equal leading magnitudes can prevent selection of a single direction. A negative leading eigenvalue can make the displayed vector alternate sign even while its one-dimensional direction converges.

Derivation and applications

Derive the eigenvalue shift produced by weak coupling

Two individually measured modes can move apart in frequency or stiffness when they are coupled. Model a real symmetric operator in an orthonormal basis by diagonal values \(\alpha,\beta\) and off-diagonal coupling \(g\). All three numbers must have the same units. A nonzero eigenvector exists only when the determinant of the shifted matrix vanishes,

\[(\alpha-\lambda)(\beta-\lambda)-g^2=0 .\]

Set \(m=(\alpha+\beta)/2\) and \(d=(\alpha-\beta)/2\). The quadratic becomes \((\lambda-m)^2=d^2+g^2\), so the two eigenvalues and their separation are

\[\lambda_\pm=m\pm\sqrt{d^2+g^2},\]
\[\lambda_+-\lambda_-=2\sqrt{d^2+g^2}.\]

Even when the uncoupled values coincide, a nonzero coupling leaves a gap \(2|g|\). For well-separated diagonal entries, assume \(\alpha>\beta\) and expand the square root for \(|g|\ll\alpha-\beta\). The largest eigenvalue has the shift

\[\lambda_+-\alpha=\frac{g^2}{\sqrt{d^2+g^2}+d}\simeq\frac{g^2}{\alpha-\beta}.\]

The rationalized form gives an accurate small shift without subtracting almost equal eigenvalues. The eigenvalue sum remains exactly \(\alpha+\beta\) because the trace is unchanged. A mass-normalized vibration model typically returns squared angular frequencies, so any measured ordinary frequencies must first be converted to the variables of the operator.

Worked example

Predict a shift away from a crossing

With \(\alpha=10\), \(\beta=6\) and \(g=0.5\), the upper shift is \(0.25/(\sqrt{4.25}+2)=0.0616\); the leading approximation is \(0.0625\). The lower eigenvalue is \(5.9384\) and the upper is \(10.0616\). The opposite signs of the shifts preserve the trace of 16.

Second application

Recover coupling and detuning from a measured gap

A second two-mode system has eigenvalues 19 and 25, giving a mean of 22 and half-gap of 3. An independent uncoupled calibration gives \(|\alpha-\beta|=4\), so \(d^2=4\). The inferred coupling magnitude is \(\sqrt{3^2-2^2}=\sqrt5=2.24\). The eigenvalues do not determine the sign of \(g\), which also changes when one basis vector is reversed.

What an eigenproblem measures

In an undamped mechanical model \(M\ddot u+Ku=0\), insert \(u=v\cos(\omega t)\). Nonzero amplitudes require \(Kv=\omega^2Mv\). The normal frequencies are square roots of generalised eigenvalues and the mode shapes are determined only up to scale and sign. For symmetric positive definite \(M\), substituting \(w=M^{1/2}v\) gives the ordinary symmetric operator \(M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}\). This step explains why mass weighting matters when comparing shapes from two different meshes.

For an ordinary symmetric matrix \(A\) with orthonormal eigenvectors, a normalised trial vector \(v=\sum c_i u_i\) has Rayleigh quotient \(\rho=v^TAv=\sum_i\lambda_i c_i^2\). Hence it lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues. Near an isolated mode its value can be much more accurate than the vector direction: shifting a small amount of amplitude into another orthogonal mode changes the quotient by a term proportional to the square of that amplitude.

Select a mode by repeated application

The power iteration repeatedly forms \(Av_k\) and normalises it. Before normalisation, \(A^kv_0=\sum c_i\lambda_i^k u_i\). If one eigenvalue has strictly largest magnitude and the initial vector overlaps its eigenspace, the ratio of any competing component to the leading one gains a factor \((\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k\). Normalisation avoids overflow while preserving those ratios. A negative leading eigenvalue can cause sign changes between successive normalised vectors. Comparing their raw difference then misrepresents convergence.

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(4,2)\) and a starting vector proportional to \((1,1)^T\), the ratio of the second component to the first is \(1/2\) after one multiplication and \(1/4\) after two. A vector proportional to \((4,1)^T\) has Rayleigh quotient \((4\cdot16+2)/17=66/17=3.88235\). After four steps the ratio is \(1/16\), and the quotient is \(1026/257=3.99222\). Check that the quotient error decreases roughly fourfold per additional step, although the directional error decreases only twofold.

Use shifts to find another part of the spectrum

Power iteration selects magnitude. To find a mode near a target \(\sigma\), apply the inverse of \(A-\sigma I\) implicitly by solving a linear system each iteration. The eigenvectors are the same and the eigenvalues are \(1/(\lambda_i-\sigma)\). For eigenvalues 2 and 4 with \(\sigma=2.1\), the transformed magnitudes are 10 and about 0.526. The eigenvalue near 2 dominates for a suitable starter. A fixed shift permits factor reuse, but choosing it exactly equal to an eigenvalue makes the shifted solve singular.

Test a candidate pair by forming \(r=Av-\rho v\) with \(\|v\|_2=1\). In the orthonormal basis, \(\|r\|^2=\sum_i(\lambda_i-\rho)^2 c_i^2\). At least one eigenvalue must lie within \(\|r\|\) of \(\rho\). This is a numerical certificate for a symmetric problem. To certify a particular eigenvector, the gap to neighbouring eigenvalues also matters. At a repeated eigenvalue every normalised linear combination of its modes is valid. Comparing subspaces or projecting one span onto the other is then more meaningful than matching individual component signs.

Follow the whole spectrum by QR iteration

To find several eigenvalues of a small dense operator, factor \(A_k-\mu I=QR\) and form \(A_{k+1}=RQ+\mu I\). Orthogonality gives \(A_{k+1}=Q^TA_kQ\), an exact similarity. Hence every step preserves eigenvalues, trace and determinant while changing the coordinates used to represent the operator. This use of QR is different from solving a triangular least-squares system. Repeated unshifted steps amount to an orthogonalised power iteration on multiple directions. A shift near a separated eigenvalue can make the last coordinate nearly invariant and accelerate its separation.

For a symmetric matrix a preliminary sequence of orthogonal similarities reduces it to tridiagonal form. When the coupling between the last two coordinates is negligible relative to the desired accuracy, setting that coupling and its transpose to zero separates a scalar eigenproblem. This removal is called deflation. If the off-diagonal magnitude is \(e\), setting the pair to zero is a symmetric perturbation of spectral norm \(|e|\), giving a direct measure for that decision. For a general real nonsymmetric matrix, the converged result can also contain real two-by-two blocks representing complex eigenvalue pairs.

A shifted step with a check on the trailing mode

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(\mu=2\). Normalising the first shifted column gives \((2,1)^T/\sqrt5\). Complete the orthogonal basis with \((-1,2)^T/\sqrt5\). Multiplying \(Q^TAQ\) gives diagonal \((22/5,8/5)\) and off-diagonal \(-1/5\). The trace remains six and the determinant remains seven. The last-coordinate Rayleigh estimate, initially two, is now 1.6 compared with the exact lower eigenvalue \(3-\sqrt2\approx1.586\). The residual of this coordinate has fallen from one to 0.2. A subsequent shift can use the improved estimate. Preserving eigenvalues alone would never certify that a coordinate is ready for deflation.

Which boundary modes are physical?

An elastic assembly suspended in free space admits rigid translations, and in three dimensions also rigid rotations when its geometry allows them. In a correctly assembled model these motions cost zero strain energy, so the stiffness matrix has a nullspace and some generalised eigenvalues are zero. Attempting unshifted inverse iteration on that stiffness matrix cannot locate a lowest positive vibration by simply inverting the operator. One can instead constrain rigid motions consistently or project into the mass-orthogonal complement of their span.

For a two-mass system with unit masses and a unit spring connecting them, \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Translation \((1,1)^T\) has zero squared frequency; opposite displacement \((1,-1)^T\) has squared frequency two. Grounding the first mass eliminates translation but changes the second mode to an oscillator with frequency one. Projection leaves the original free-system vibration at \(\sqrt2\). These are physically different comparisons; the treatment of a nullspace determines which frequency an eigenvalue calculation is actually reporting.

Convert an eigenpair residual into an angle bound

Given a real symmetric matrix \(A\), a unit-vector approximation \(v\) and a scalar \(\theta\), write its residual \(r=Av-\theta v\). Expand \(v=\sum_j c_jq_j\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis. The squared residual is \(\sum_j(\lambda_j-\theta)^2|c_j|^2\). This identity always implies that at least one eigenvalue lies within \(\|r\|\) of \(\theta\). It does not by itself show whether the reported vector represents one particular physical mode. Two almost coincident eigenvalues can produce a tiny residual for many different mixtures of their eigenvectors.

To bound leakage out of a target eigenspace, let \(\gamma\) be a lower bound on the distance from \(\theta\) to every eigenvalue outside that space. The squared residual is at least \(\gamma^2\sum_{\rm outside}|c_j|^2\). Consequently the sine of the angle between \(v\) and the target space is at most \(\|r\|/\gamma\). This result assumes a symmetric or Hermitian operator under the chosen inner product. For a mass-weighted mechanical problem, transform to mass-normal coordinates or perform the argument in the proper mass inner product. An ordinary Euclidean angle between unscaled coordinates may have little relevance to vibrational energy.

Resolve a cluster more reliably than either member

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(10,10.02,30)\), and \(v=(1,1,0)^T/\sqrt2\). Its Rayleigh quotient is 10.01. Multiplication gives \(r=(-0.01,0.01,0)^T/\sqrt2\), of length 0.01. Relative to the cluster near ten this residual is small, yet the angle from the first coordinate eigenvector is 45 degrees. Using only the other low eigenvalue as a separation gives \(\gamma=0.01\), so the bound on that individual angle has sine at most one and provides no useful orientation guarantee.

Now take the target as the entire first two-dimensional subspace. Its gap from the reported quotient to the outside eigenvalue is \(30-10.01=19.99\). The same residual implies leakage no greater than \(0.01/19.99\approx0.000500\). In fact the true leakage of this particular vector is exactly zero. If the measured eigenvalues are squared angular frequencies, the two low modes have nearly the same frequency but can mix strongly under a small asymmetric manufacturing perturbation. Their combined subspace may remain very stable and may therefore be the more sensible object to compare with measurements.

A laboratory comparison can project independent excitation patterns onto the computed cluster and compare the total energy in that cluster. Such a result is invariant under rotations of its basis, which a simple table of pairwise eigenvector differences is not. If the gap to the rest of the spectrum closes as well, the subspace estimate becomes weak. Measuring the residual alone cannot repair that missing separation information.

For example the sum of squared projections of the excitation \((1,0,0)^T\) onto the first two computed orthonormal modes is one for any orthonormal basis of the exact low cluster. Individual projections can range between zero and one as the basis rotates. A test based on cluster projection assesses the reproducible quantity without requiring a uniquely labelled first mode.

Watch the QR iteration resolve two close eigenvalues

Rotate each diagonal matrix with eigenvalues \(1,\rho\) by 0.6 radians, then factor \(A_k=Q_kR_k\) and form \(A_{k+1}=R_kQ_k\). Each step is an orthogonal similarity. Dividing the off-diagonal by the gap lets all three matrices start with the same normalized mixing.

Householder reflections and practical QR

Numerical linear algebra

Build one reflection explicitly, use it to triangularise a rectangular system and locate the residual in transformed coordinates.

From first principles

Construct a reflection that zeros a column tail

A reflection reverses the component of a vector perpendicular to a chosen plane and leaves the component in the plane fixed. If a nonzero vector \(v\) is normal to the plane, the perpendicular projection of \(x\) is \(v(v^Tx)/(v^Tv)\). Subtracting twice that projection defines

\[Hx=x-2v\frac{v^Tx}{v^Tv},\]
\[H=I-2\frac{vv^T}{v^Tv}.\]

The reflection preserves lengths because the parallel and perpendicular components remain orthogonal. To reduce a column \(x\), target an axis vector \(y=\alpha e_1\) of equal length, so \(|\alpha|=\|x\|_2\). Set \(v=x-y\). Expanding the dot products gives \(2v^Tx=v^Tv\) because \(x\) and \(y\) have equal norm. Consequently

\[Hx=x-v=y .\]

Choosing \(\alpha\) opposite in sign to \(x_1\) avoids cancellation in \(v_1\). Reflecting only the unprocessed rows of each successive column yields an upper triangular factor while preserving previously generated zeros. An already zero column needs no transformation.

Derivation and applications

Recover the least-squares residual from Householder coordinates

A QR solver can report how much an orthogonal transformation sends into rows below a triangular factor. That part has a precise measurement interpretation. Assume a real matrix \(A\) with \(m>n\) and independent columns. Apply the same sequence of Householder transformations to the augmented matrix \([A\ b]\). Their product \(Q^T\) is orthogonal. Partition its result into an upper block with \(n\) rows and a lower block,

\[Q^T[A\ b]=\begin{pmatrix}R&c\\0&d\end{pmatrix}.\]

Because orthogonal transformations preserve the Euclidean norm, the squared residual of any trial vector decomposes into the sum of two nonnegative terms. Only the first can be changed by the fit,

\[\|b-Ax\|_2^2=\|c-Rx\|_2^2+\|d\|_2^2 .\]

The optimal \(x\) solves \(Rx=c\), leaving residual norm \(\|d\|_2\). In exact arithmetic the original-coordinate residual is obtained by reversing the transforms on a vector whose upper block is zero,

\[r=b-Ax=Q\binom{0}{d},\]
\[A^Tr=R^T[\,I\ \ 0\,]\binom{0}{d}=0.\]

The lower coordinates are combinations of observations. Their individual values depend on the particular reflections, whereas their squared norm is invariant. Retaining that norm directly avoids subtracting two large nearly equal squared norms when the fit is excellent. If some triangular pivots are numerically negligible, ranking the columns and checking the model’s effective rank become necessary before interpreting all \(n\) fitted coefficients.

Worked example

Inspect residuals after fitting a constant

Fit \(A=(1,1)^T\) to readings \(b=(8,10)^T\). Rotate to the orthonormal directions \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((-1,1)/\sqrt2\). The transformed data are \(18/\sqrt2\) and \(2/\sqrt2\), while the transformed model is \((\sqrt2,0)^T x\). The fit gives \(x=9\), residual norm \(\sqrt2\), and original residual \((-1,1)^T\). Its sum is zero as the constant column requires.

Second application

Compare the numerical consequences of subtracting squared norms

Suppose transformed coordinates of another fit have \(\|c\|=10^8\) and \(\|d\|=0.1\). Computing \(\|b\|^2-\|c\|^2\) would subtract two values near \(10^{16}\) to recover \(0.01\). Binary64 spacing near \(10^{16}\) is 2, so the small addition can disappear entirely. Keeping the transformed tail returns residual norm \(0.1\) directly, subject to normal transformation errors.

Eliminate a column without stretching it

An orthogonal transformation applied simultaneously to the data matrix and right-hand side leaves every Euclidean residual length unchanged. A Householder reflector is \(H=I-2vv^T/(v^Tv)\) for a nonzero vector \(v\). Decompose any vector into components parallel and perpendicular to \(v\). The parallel component changes sign and the perpendicular component stays fixed, proving both length preservation and \(H^2=I\). Implementation requires just a dot product and a scaled vector subtraction rather than a dense matrix.

To make the entries below the first element of a column \(x\) vanish, choose \(v=x-\alpha e_1\) where \(|\alpha|=\|x\|_2\). A short calculation gives \(v^Tv=2(\|x\|^2-\alpha x_1)\) and \(v^Tx=\|x\|^2-\alpha x_1\), hence \(Hx=x-v=\alpha e_1\). Choose \(\alpha\) with sign opposite to \(x_1\) to avoid subtracting nearly equal positive numbers when computing \(v_1\). A zero column requires a separate decision about rank.

Follow the data into the triangular system

For \(x=(3,4)^T\), use \(\alpha=-5\) and \(v=(8,4)^T\). The reflection matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-.6&-.8\\-.8&.6\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Take a one-column least-squares problem with measurements \(b=(4,3)^T\). Multiplication sends \(x\) to \((-5,0)^T\) and \(b\) to \((-4.8,-1.4)^T\). The optimum coefficient matches the first transformed measurement, giving \(c=0.96\). The second transformed measurement remains a residual of magnitude 1.4. Back in physical coordinates the predicted vector is \((2.88,3.84)\), with residual \((1.12,-0.84)\) and the same length.

For several columns, apply the first reflector to the entire remaining block. Construct the next reflector only on rows below the first pivot, protecting the already triangular part. Saving the reflector vectors permits application of \(Q\) or \(Q^T\) later without explicitly forming either full matrix. Additional right-hand sides can then use the same orthogonal transformations and triangular solve.

Detect when a coefficient is poorly determined

If a new column is nearly in the span of earlier columns, little of it survives below the preceding pivots. A small trailing diagonal of \(R\) signals potential coefficient sensitivity, but its scale depends on column units and order. An instrument model with one parameter measured in micro-units can naturally have a small column. Scale the columns according to the intended range of the corresponding parameters before interpreting a near-zero diagonal.

Column-pivoted QR selects a remaining column with a large unrepresented component at each stage. It can help reveal effective rank and identify an independent set of measurements or basis functions. A numerical rank decision still needs a tolerance tied to data uncertainty or floating-point arithmetic. Reporting a fitted coefficient to ten digits after discarding an almost invisible direction would conceal the limitation that the factorisation has revealed.

Check a nearly aligned column

Consider \(x=(1,10^{-8})^T\). Its norm differs from one by only about \(5\times10^{-17}\), below the spacing between one and the next larger double precision number. Computing the exact difference \(x_1-\|x\|\) as that subtraction may therefore give zero. Choosing the target with opposite sign makes the first entry of the reflector vector about two and the remaining small entry is retained. This calculation explains the sign convention geometrically: two opposite points on a sphere provide valid reflection targets, and only one directs the subtraction away from severe cancellation.

Update a triangular fit when observations arrive sequentially

A Householder QR factorisation is efficient when the entire design matrix is available. Suppose instead that a new row arrives after the original observations have been reduced to an upper triangular factor \(R\), a transformed data vector \(z\), and an accumulated residual sum of squares \(S\). The original least-squares objective equals \(\|Rx-z\|^2+S\). Appending measurement row \(a^T\) and value \(b\) adds \((a^Tx-b)^2\). Stacking \(a^T\) below \(R\) produces a matrix that is triangular except for one row. Orthogonal plane rotations can restore triangular form by eliminating each incoming entry in turn.

To remove a pair \((r,s)^T\), let \(\rho=\sqrt{r^2+s^2}\), \(c=r/\rho,\ d=s/\rho\). Multiplication by the rotation with rows \((c,d)\) and \((-d,c)\) gives \((\rho,0)^T\). Apply precisely the same two-row operation to all remaining columns and to the transformed data. Its final bottom data entry has no corresponding design column, so its square adds to \(S\). If \(r=s=0\) there is nothing to eliminate. A numerical implementation obtains the length with a scaled hypotenuse evaluation to avoid overflow from squaring a large observation.

Insert a point into an existing two-parameter fit

Suppose the existing transformed problem has \(R=I,\ z=(1,2)^T,\ S=0\). A new sensor reports \(x_1+x_2=5\). The best prior estimate was \((1,2)^T\), which predicts three and therefore disagrees with the incoming reading by two. To reconcile all observations, stack the new row \((1,1\mid5)\) below \((1,0\mid1)\) and \((0,1\mid2)\). Rotate the first and last rows with \(c=d=1/\sqrt2\). The first becomes \((\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2\mid6/\sqrt2)\); the last becomes \((0,1/\sqrt2\mid4/\sqrt2)\).

The second rotation combines leading entries \(1\) and \(1/\sqrt2\), yielding \(\rho=\sqrt{3/2}\), \(c=\sqrt{2/3}\), \(d=1/\sqrt3\). Its surviving right-hand side is \(2\sqrt{2/3}+4/\sqrt6=8/\sqrt6\). Thus the second-coordinate estimate is \((8/\sqrt6)/\sqrt{3/2}=8/3\). Back substitution in the first row gives \(x_1=(6-8/3)/2=5/3\). The final bottom data entry is \(-2/\sqrt3+4/\sqrt3=2/\sqrt3\), contributing \(4/3\) to the residual sum of squares.

Verify directly: the three residual magnitudes are all \(2/3\), so their squared sum is \(4/3\). The new reading moved both coordinates equally because their original transformed information was identical and its row is symmetric. No historical measurement needed to be reread. Conversely deleting an earlier reading can expose rank deficiency and is a different numerical operation; simply running the same update with a negated data value would add another squared term rather than remove information.

When a stream contains different measurement variances, divide each arriving row and its right-hand side by its independently known standard deviation before applying the rotations. The accumulated \(S\) then measures squared standardised discrepancies. If the new reading shares an error with earlier readings, dividing its row alone is insufficient. One must first account for the conditional error and correlated design contribution or refactor a suitable joint whitening transformation. This is a measurement-model limitation, even when the rotations themselves remain perfectly orthogonal.

Repeated readings of a common reference are one practical source of precisely this kind of dependence.

Arnoldi iteration and GMRES

Numerical linear algebra

Construct an orthonormal basis for a nonsymmetric operator and use a small least-squares problem to choose an approximation.

From first principles

Project a large operator onto successive Krylov directions

For \(Ax=b\), start with residual \(r_0=b-Ax_0\ne0\). Choose the first basis vector as \(v_1=r_0/\beta\), where \(\beta=\|r_0\|_2\). Repeated matrix products generate a search space, but their columns can become nearly parallel. At step \(j\), remove previously resolved components of \(Av_j\) before normalizing the remainder,

\[h_{ij}=v_i^TAv_j,\]
\[w=Av_j-\sum_{i=1}^j h_{ij}v_i,\]
\[h_{j+1,j}=\|w\|_2 .\]

If \(h_{j+1,j}>0\), take \(v_{j+1}=w/h_{j+1,j}\). The resulting Arnoldi identity \(AV_k=V_{k+1}\bar H_k\) expresses each large product using a small upper-Hessenberg matrix. A correction \(V_ky\) gives the residual norm

\[\|r_0-AV_ky\|_2=\|\beta e_1-\bar H_k y\|_2 .\]

Orthonormality justifies the equality. GMRES solves the smaller least-squares problem to minimize the actual residual over this search space. When the new norm is zero the subspace is already invariant, and no further independent direction is available through another ordinary Arnoldi step.

Derivation and applications

Calculate exactly when the first GMRES step stalls

For one Krylov step the only available correction is a multiple of the initial residual \(r=b-Ax\). The resulting change in the residual is parallel to \(Ar\). Assume a real nonsingular \(A\) and \(r\ne0\), which guarantees \(Ar\ne0\). To choose the coefficient \(\alpha\), expand the squared residual norm,

\[\|r-\alpha Ar\|_2^2=r^Tr-2\alpha(Ar)^Tr+\alpha^2\|Ar\|_2^2 .\]

The derivative vanishes at the coefficient of the orthogonal projection of \(r\) onto \(Ar\). Hence the new residual is perpendicular to the direction in which it was allowed to change,

\[\alpha_*=\frac{(Ar)^Tr}{\|Ar\|_2^2},\]
\[(Ar)^T(r-\alpha_*Ar)=0 .\]

Let \(\theta\) denote the angle between \(r\) and \(Ar\). At the minimum the squared length removed is exactly the squared length of that projection. The remaining fraction of residual is

\[\frac{\|r_{\rm new}\|}{\|r\|}=\sqrt{1-\frac{[(Ar)^Tr]^2}{\|Ar\|^2\|r\|^2}}=|\sin\theta|.\]

Thus equal or opposite directions allow an exact one-step solve, while perpendicular directions deliver no change. This is purely geometric and applies without symmetry. It also clarifies why restarting after one step can be costly: later basis vectors could expose a direction inaccessible on the first line.

Worked example

Compute the residual reduction for a shear

Use \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(r=(0,1)^T\). Then \(Ar=(2,1)^T\), \(\alpha_*=1/5\), and \(r_{\rm new}=(-0.4,0.8)^T\). Its norm is \(\sqrt{0.8}=0.894\). The dot product with \(Ar\) is \(-0.8+0.8=0\), an independent check that the selected coefficient minimizes the residual along this line.

Second application

Detect stagnation despite a nonsingular operator

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,-1)\) and \(r=(1,\sqrt2)^T\). The dot product \(r^TAr=2-2=0\) forces the first coefficient to zero. A second direction completes a two-dimensional space because \(Ar=(2,-\sqrt2)^T\) is independent of \(r\). In fact \(A^{-1}r=(0.5,-\sqrt2)^T=-0.5r+0.5Ar\). The two-direction correction solves the problem exactly although a one-step restart would repeat the same state.

A residual as a polynomial

Consider a square nonsingular matrix and an initial residual \(r_0=b-Ax_0\). A correction in the span of \(r_0,Ar_0,\ldots,A^{k-1}r_0\) is a polynomial in \(A\) applied to \(r_0\). After subtracting the action of \(A\), the new residual is \(p_k(A)r_0\) with \(p_k(0)=1\). This constraint matters because the zero polynomial would certify convergence before the calculation began. Generalised minimal residual iteration, or GMRES, minimises the Euclidean norm over all such residuals. The spaces are nested, so the exact-arithmetic residual norms cannot increase during a cycle.

Replacing large products by small coordinates

Start with \(v_1=r_0/\beta\), where \(\beta=\|r_0\|_2\). For each new direction form \(w=Av_j\), subtract its projections onto the existing orthonormal columns, and normalise what remains. Each projection coefficient occupies one row of column \(j\) of a small matrix. The final normalising length occupies its first subdiagonal. This Arnoldi construction gives \(AV_k=V_{k+1}\bar H_k\). If the length vanishes, the current subspace is invariant and no further normalisation is possible.

\[\|b-A(x_0+V_ky)\|_2=\|V_{k+1}(\beta e_1-\bar H_ky)\|_2=\|\beta e_1-\bar H_ky\|_2.\]

The second equality uses orthonormality. Solve this small rectangular least-squares problem, for example by updating triangular factors using plane rotations. The resulting residual is orthogonal to the range of \(AV_k\). Repeated projection may be necessary when rounding spoils orthogonality. A freshly calculated physical residual can test the small model's estimate before reporting convergence.

One eigenvalue and two directions

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&4\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(x_0=0\), \(b=e_2\). The first correction is \(\alpha e_2\), so the squared residual is \(16\alpha^2+(1-\alpha)^2\). Differentiation gives \(\alpha=1/17\) and length \(4/\sqrt{17}\), about 0.9701. Almost the entire initial residual survives despite every eigenvalue being one. The second direction comes from the component of \(Ae_2\) perpendicular to \(e_2\), namely \(4e_1\). With both directions available the exact solution \((-4,1)^T\) is accessible, and its residual vanishes.

For a diagonal matrix equal to the identity, a single step would suffice for every right-hand side. Here a nonzero off-diagonal entry couples two coordinates and the minimal polynomial is \((z-1)^2\). Counting distinct eigenvalues alone misses that coupling.

Paying for a long memory

Each new direction needs storage and projections against earlier directions. Restarting after a fixed number releases memory, but discards those directions. A new cycle still starts from the best current iterate. It lacks the previous cycle's simultaneous freedom to alter its old coefficients. Record matrix-vector products and the residual after each restart when comparing costs. On the example, two directions retained together already achieve the exact answer, providing a direct check on a proposed implementation.

Read a preconditioned residual

Left preconditioning with \(M^{-1}\) makes GMRES minimise \(\|M^{-1}(b-Ax)\|\) instead. If \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,1000)\), a physical residual \((0,1)^T\) has preconditioned norm 0.001. A small number in the stopping display could therefore leave the second physical balance comparatively inaccurate. Right preconditioning solves \(AM^{-1}y=b\), then forms \(x=M^{-1}y\); for exact preconditioner actions its minimisation still uses the original residual. Compare work and physically meaningful residuals when assessing either choice, especially if the equations use different units.

How restarting can erase a necessary direction

GMRES minimises the full residual norm over its current correction space. A one-step restart retains only the first correction direction generated from the new residual. If that direction makes no angle with the gradient of the residual objective in the appropriate sense, progress can cease even for an invertible, perfectly conditioned matrix. To see the geometry, suppose the current residual is \(r\). The one-dimensional trial residual is \(r-\alpha Ar\). Minimising its squared norm for real arithmetic yields \(\alpha=(Ar)^Tr/\|Ar\|^2\). Thus no reduction occurs when \(Ar\) is perpendicular to \(r\). Restarting immediately recreates precisely the same subproblem.

Unrestarted GMRES can still add \(Ar\) and further powers to its Krylov space. In exact arithmetic it can therefore escape one-step stagnation. This distinction matters for storage allocation: reducing the basis length can change the convergence mechanism itself. Merely multiplying the allowed number of restart cycles cannot recover directions if every cycle begins and ends with the same residual. Conversely increasing the restart length helps only if the additional space contains directions that produce a lower residual.

Compare restart lengths for a quarter-turn operator

Set \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(b=(1,0)^T\), beginning at zero. Its transpose times itself is the identity, so its two-norm condition number is one. Yet \(Ab=(0,1)^T\) is perpendicular to the initial residual \(b\). The residual after trial step \(\alpha b\) is \((1,-\alpha)^T\), whose squared length is \(1+\alpha^2\). The best step is exactly zero. GMRES restarted every step thus leaves the residual norm at one forever.

With a two-vector basis use corrections \(x=\alpha b+\beta Ab=(\alpha,\beta)^T\). The residual is \((1+\beta,-\alpha)^T\), which vanishes for \(\alpha=0,\beta=-1\). The exact solution is reached. In polynomial language no degree-one real polynomial satisfying \(p(0)=1\) can vanish at both eigenvalues \(+i,-i\). The degree-two choice \(p(z)=1+z^2\) does so because \(A^2=-I\). Both formulations explain the same measurement without attributing the failure to singularity or loss of precision.

For a nonsingular real skew-symmetric matrix the one-step numerator vanishes for every real \(r\), since a scalar \(r^TAr\) equals its own negative after transposition. This family therefore offers a repeatable stress test for short-restart implementations. A preconditioner or a shift that alters the search geometry can change the result, but any claimed gain should be checked using the residual of the original equations and a comparable count of matrix-vector products.

There is also a simple residual recomputation check at convergence: for the two-step state \((0,-1)^T\), multiplying by the original matrix gives \((1,0)^T\) exactly. If a code reports the same state after one iteration, it has either used a different search direction or mislabelled its iteration count. Exposing the current basis length alongside the total matrix-vector count makes a restart experiment easier to reproduce.

For complex arithmetic, use the real or complex least-squares minimisation appropriate to the actual correction space; the perpendicularity argument above assumes real data.

How a moderately accurate mode can give a precise frequency

Numerical linear algebra

Use the Rayleigh quotient to estimate an eigenvalue, understand its stationary error and check a proposed mode with a residual.

A frequency estimate from a trial shape

For \(Kq=\lambda Mq\) with symmetric \(K\) and positive definite mass \(M\), a trial vector \(v\) fixes a direction but leaves an amplitude free. Substituting \(q(t)=a(t)v\) in kinetic and elastic energy gives effective mass \(v^TMv\) and stiffness \(v^TKv\). Their ratio \(R(v)\) estimates the squared frequency. Rescaling \(v\) has no effect. For \(M=I\), differentiating \(v^TKv/(v^Tv)\) gives a gradient proportional to \(Kv-Rv\). Its zeros are precisely the eigenvectors.

Angular error and the residual

Take exact orthonormal modes \(u_1,u_2\) and a trial \(v=u_1\cos\theta+u_2\sin\theta\). Orthogonality removes all cross terms. Then

\[R(v)-\lambda_1=(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\sin^2\theta,\]
\[\|Kv-R(v)v\|_2=|\lambda_2-\lambda_1|\,|\sin\theta\cos\theta|.\]

The eigenvalue error is second order in a small angular error. The residual is first order. For general dimension write \(v=\sum c_i u_i\) with unit norm. The squared residual is \(\sum |c_i|^2(\lambda_i-R)^2\), so at least one eigenvalue lies within one residual norm of \(R\). Identifying which mode requires more information about the spectrum.

Two measurements

For squared frequencies 4 and 9 in s⁻² and a 10-degree error in the first trial mode, \(R=4+5(0.03015)=4.1508\) s⁻². The estimated angular frequency is 2.0373 rad/s. The relative error in frequency is about half the relative error in its square at small errors.

For eigenvalues 4 and 4.01, a 45-degree mixture gives a residual of just 0.005. This very small number is consistent with a large uncertainty in shape. If the two eigenvalues were identical, every combination in the eigenspace would be an exact mode.

Estimating a cantilever frequency from energy

Machine dynamics

A clamped beam carries a small sensor at its tip. Its precise mode shape is unknown. Use the static deflection shape to approximate the fundamental frequency and see why concentrating a small mass at the tip has a large effect.

Choose a displacement field that respects the support

Write \(w(x,t)=q(t)\phi(s)\) with \(s=x/L\), taking \(q\) as tip displacement. Choose the shape from a uniform cantilever under a static transverse tip force. The bending moment varies linearly from root to tip. Integrating curvature twice with \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\), then normalizing at \(s=1\), gives \(\phi=s^2(3-s)/2\). The shape satisfies both essential clamp conditions. Its free-tip shear need not match the exact unloaded vibration mode. The energy formulation can use such admissible trial shapes.

Compute effective stiffness and mass from the same field

The strain energy density of a slender beam in bending is \(\tfrac12 EI w_{xx}^2\). Its translational kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\rho A w_t^2\) per unit length. Changing variables from x to s introduces different powers of L into the two integrals. Include a point mass M at the tip, neglecting its rotary inertia.

\[U={EI q^2\over 2L^3}\int_0^1(\phi'')^2\,ds,\]
\[K={\dot q^2\over2}\left[\rho AL\int_0^1\phi^2\,ds+M\right].\]

Since \(\phi''=3(1-s)\), the stiffness integral is 3. Squaring \(s^2(3-s)/2\) and integrating \( (9s^4-6s^5+s^6)/4\) gives \(33/140\). The Euler–Lagrange equation for q is consequently a single oscillator equation with

\[\omega_R^2={3EI/L^3\over(33/140)\rho AL+M}.\]

For M=0 this gives the dimensionless coefficient \(\sqrt{140/11}=3.5675\). Solving the exact unloaded cantilever condition \(\cosh\beta\cos\beta=-1\) gives \(\beta_1^2=3.5160\). The estimate is high by about 1.5%. The Rayleigh minimum principle explains that direction of error for conservative systems with admissible trial shapes. Restricting motion to a single shape restricts the set over which the energy quotient can attain its minimum.

Choose a sensor after estimating its dynamic effect

Take \(EI=10\) N m², \(L=0.50\) m and total beam mass 0.40 kg. The tip stiffness is 240 N/m and the distributed effective mass is only 0.0943 kg. The estimate gives 8.03 Hz without a sensor and 6.49 Hz with a 0.05 kg tip sensor. The sensor weighs one eighth of the beam, yet it has over half the effective mass associated with the chosen tip coordinate.

Place the same sensor at half-span instead. There \(\phi(0.5)=0.3125\). Its kinetic energy contributes \(M\phi(0.5)^2=0.00488\) kg to the effective mass, so the estimate rises to 7.83 Hz. The sensor measures a smaller local motion as it perturbs the frequency less. Compare that trade-off at different attachment positions with the plot.

Recover a trajectory from a family of actions

Classical mechanics

In Hamilton–Jacobi theory a partial differential equation for one function replaces several trajectory equations. A falling bead makes the different roles of position, energy and arrival time explicit.

Use the end of the path as a variable

Let a bead move between a fixed initial event and an adjustable final event. Denote by \(S(q,t)\) the action evaluated on the actual trajectory ending at \((q,t)\). For a small change in the endpoint, the interior variation cancels by the Euler–Lagrange equation. If time stays fixed the boundary term is \(p\,\delta q\). If time changes, part of the endpoint displacement comes from continuing along the existing path by \(\dot q\,\delta t\). The remaining independent displacement is \(\delta q-\dot q\,\delta t\). Thus

\[\delta S=p(\delta q-\dot q\,\delta t)+L\,\delta t=p\,\delta q-H\,\delta t .\]

It follows that \(p=\partial_q S\) and \(-H=\partial_t S\). Eliminate \(p\) to obtain

\[\partial_t S+H(q,\partial_q S,t)=0 .\]

The equation involves partial derivatives because it describes an entire family of possible endpoints. By contrast, \(dS/dt=L\) along a single trajectory. A useful dimensional check is that a derivative of action with respect to length is momentum, whereas a derivative with respect to time is energy.

Separate time and build a clock from energy

For a time-independent Hamiltonian try \(S=W(q;E)-Et\). With \(H=p^2/(2m)+U(q)\), this gives \((\partial_qW)^2=2m(E-U)\). A trajectory on the increasing-position branch has positive momentum, so an integral from a fixed reference position \(q_0\) supplies \(W\). Differentiate at fixed final position with respect to the family parameter \(E\):

\[W=\int_{q_0}^{q}\!\sqrt{2m(E-U(s))}\,ds,\]
\[\partial_EW=\int_{q_0}^{q}\!\frac{m\,ds}{\sqrt{2m(E-U(s))}} .\]

The integrand of the second integral is \(1/v\). Hence \(\partial_E W\) is the travel time from the reference point. Setting \(\partial_E S=0\) chooses a departure time of zero with this lower integration limit. A different constant amounts to shifting the clock. At a turning point \(p=0\), the one-way branch must be changed to continue the motion.

Work two arrival-time problems

Let \(q=x\) point downwards, \(U=-mgx\), and release the bead through \(x=0\) with speed \(u\). Its energy is \(E=mu^2/2\). The integral yields \(t=(\sqrt{u^2+2gx}-u)/g\). Squaring the relation after isolating the square root gives \(x=ut+gt^2/2\), the same trajectory as Newton's equation. For \(u=2.0\) m/s, \(g=9.81\) m/s² and \(x=3.0\) m the arrival time is about \(0.604\) s. The endpoint momentum uses the final speed, about \(7.93\) m/s, rather than the average over the fall.

Reverse the experiment by pointing \(x\) upward in the same field. If \(u=4.0\) m/s, the maximum height is \(0.815\) m. A gate \(0.50\) m above launch is crossed twice. From \(x=ut-gt^2/2\) the two times are \(0.154\) s and \(0.661\) s. The need for two branches of \(W\) is experimentally visible in those two timestamps. A calculation that takes the positive square root everywhere loses the return journey.

Read a period from the area enclosed by an orbit

Classical mechanics

A track with a V-shaped potential has straight force segments but a strongly amplitude-dependent period. Its area in phase space gives a useful way to predict both the period and a slow change of the track.

Construct the closed area

For one bound degree of freedom at energy \(E\), position runs between two turning points. In a mechanical Hamiltonian \(p^2/(2m)+U(q)\), the upper branch of the phase portrait has positive momentum and the lower branch has its negative. The directed integral around one full orbit counts the enclosed area. Define the action \(J\) using the area divided by \(2\pi\). This choice makes its conjugate phase an angle measured in radians:

\[J(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dq=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\sqrt{2m(E-U(q))}\,dq .\]

Now change the energy slightly. The integrand vanishes at each regular endpoint, so the moving-endpoint contributions to the derivative vanish. In the interior \(\partial p/\partial E=m/p=1/v\). The derivative of the upper-branch area therefore measures the time to reach the opposite turning point.

\[\frac{dJ}{dE}=\frac{T(E)}{2\pi},\]
\[\omega(E)=\frac{dE}{dJ}.\]

This argument also explains why the construction becomes delicate near a separatrix. A trajectory can spend an arbitrarily long time near an unstable equilibrium, and \(T\) can diverge even though the enclosed area stays finite.

Carry out the integral for a constant restoring force

Use \(U(q)=F|q|\) with \(F>0\). The turning amplitude is \(A=E/F\). Integrate the positive branch from \(0\) to \(A\) and use reflection symmetry. Substitution \(s=E-Fq\) gives

\[J=\frac{4\sqrt{2m}}{3\pi F}E^{3/2},\]
\[T=\frac{4\sqrt{2mE}}{F}=4\sqrt{\frac{2mA}{F}} .\]

The answer has an independent mechanical check. From a turning point the bead accelerates uniformly with magnitude \(F/m\) until the centre. Covering a distance \(A\) from rest takes \(\sqrt{2mA/F}\), a quarter period. Doubling the amplitude increases the full period by \(\sqrt{2}\). The abrupt change of force direction at the centre leaves the velocity continuous.

Slowly steepen the track

A slowly adjusted parameter can preserve the orbit action to leading order when the motion remains periodic and many cycles fit inside the adjustment time. For this trap \(m\) stays constant, so constant \(J\) requires \(E^{3/2}/F\) to stay fixed. Therefore \(E\propto F^{2/3}\), \(A=E/F\propto F^{-1/3}\) and \(T\propto F^{-2/3}\). Work done by adjusting the slope changes the energy despite the approximate conservation of action.

Start with \(m=0.25\) kg, \(F=2.0\) N and \(A=0.080\) m. The initial energy is \(0.160\) J and the period \(0.566\) s. A slow eightfold increase of \(F\) makes the energy four times larger, \(0.640\) J, and the amplitude half as large, \(0.040\) m. The period becomes \(0.141\) s. In contrast, switching slopes instantaneously at the centre leaves the kinetic energy and position unchanged at that instant. The same final slope would then give amplitude \(0.010\) m. The route by which a parameter changes matters.

Why two finite-element wave solvers propagate the same mode at different speeds

Numerical partial differential equations

Build the element mass matrix by integrating shape functions and track the effect of diagonal lumping on the wave spectrum.

From a continuum wave to nodal masses

For \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\), multiply by a test function and integrate by parts. On a periodic line the boundary contributions cancel. A piecewise-linear element of width \(h\) uses shape functions \(1-\xi,\xi\), with \(\xi\in[0,1]\). Integrating products of shape functions gives \(M_e=(h/6)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Integrating derivative products gives \(K_e=(c^2/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Row-sum lumping replaces each mass row by its sum on the corresponding diagonal, \(h/2\). This preserves the mass associated with a uniform velocity but changes the response to a velocity that varies within an element.

Diagonalise the assembled periodic problem

A periodic nodal mode has \(u_j=e^{ij\theta}\). Each neighbour contributes \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) relative to node \(j\). Thus \(K\) multiplies by \(2c^2(1-\cos\theta)/h\), consistent \(M\) by \(h(2+\cos\theta)/3\), and lumped \(M\) by \(h\). Dividing stiffness by mass gives the discrete squared frequency. Relative to the exact frequency \(c\theta/h\),

\[\frac{\omega_L}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta},\]
\[\frac{\omega_C}{c\theta/h}=\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2+\cos\theta}}.\]

Both tend to one for long waves; the leading quadratic errors have opposite signs. This is a spatial effect, present even with exact time integration.

Two wavelengths in one mesh

At four cells per wavelength \(\theta=\pi/2\). The lumped ratio is \(2\sqrt2/\pi\approx0.9003\); the consistent ratio is about 1.1027. One wave solver lags by roughly ten per cent and the other leads by roughly ten per cent. At eight cells per wavelength the ratios move much closer to one, but repeated propagation over many wavelengths can still accumulate visible phase error.

At the shortest grid mode \(\theta=\pi\), the consistent frequency is \(\sqrt{12}c/h\) and the lumped frequency \(2c/h\). A central-difference time integrator stable for \(h_t\omega\le2\) therefore requires a smaller step for the consistent mass. Accuracy at one wavelength and stability at the grid scale are different design criteria.
