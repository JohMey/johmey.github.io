From first principles

Derive the radial equation from three-dimensional motion

For a reduced mass \(\mu\) in a central potential \(V(r)\), rotational symmetry makes the angular-momentum operators compatible with the Hamiltonian. Write a stationary state as \(\psi=R(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\) with \(L^2Y_{\ell m}=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m}\). In spherical coordinates the kinetic operator separates radial and angular changes:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu r^2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_r)+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.\]

Divide the eigenvalue equation by the angular factor. To work with a one-dimensional probability measure, substitute \(u=rR\). Then \(\int|R|^2r^2dr=\int|u|^2dr\) and direct differentiation gives

\[(r^2R')'=r u''.\]

The resulting equation is

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''+\left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u=Eu .\]

The extra positive term at nonzero \(\ell\) is the angular kinetic energy, which inhibits penetration near the origin. For ordinary Coulomb bound states regularity imposes \(u(0)=0\) and normalizability requires decay at infinity. The radial half-line and its boundary conditions are part of the eigenvalue problem.

Derivation and applications

What fraction of a ground-state atom lies outside a chosen radius?

The hydrogenic ground-state amplitude is largest at the nucleus, yet a measurement of radius is most likely to land away from it. To derive the distribution use a single electron bound to a point nucleus of charge \(+Ze\), neglecting relativistic and finite-size corrections. Let \(\mu\) be the reduced electron–nucleus mass, and write \(\kappa=Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\). For a spherically symmetric stationary state try \(\psi=A e^{-r/a}\). Inserting its radial derivatives into the three-dimensional Schrödinger equation gives

\[\frac{H\psi}{\psi}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2}+\frac1r\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{\mu a}-\kappa\right).\]

The right side must be independent of radius. Cancel the inverse-radius term to fix \(a=\hbar^2/(\mu\kappa)\). Normalization integrates probability over spherical shells, each of volume \(4\pi r^2dr\):

\[1=4\pi |A|^2\int_0^\infty r^2 e^{-2r/a}\,dr=\pi a^3|A|^2,\]
\[P_r(r)=\frac{4r^2}{a^3}e^{-2r/a}.\]

Thus \(P_r(r)\,dr\) is the probability for a radius in an interval. Differentiating it places its maximum at \(r=a\). To count the atoms found outside radius \(R\), substitute \(z=2r/a\) and integrate the quadratic times an exponential twice by parts:

\[\Pr(r>R)=\tfrac12\int_{2R/a}^\infty z^2 e^{-z}\,dz=e^{-2R/a}\!\left(1+\frac{2R}{a}+\frac{2R^2}{a^2}\right).\]

At \(R=0\) the result is one, as required. The tail remains nonzero for every finite radius; a quote of the “atomic radius” must therefore specify the observable or convention used.

Worked example

Quantify the extent of ordinary hydrogen

Take \(a=52.9\) pm. A radius \(R=105.8\) pm equals \(2a\). Evaluating the tail gives

\[13e^{-4}=0.238.\]

Nearly one quarter of radial outcomes fall beyond twice the most probable radius. Using only the relative density \(e^{-4}=0.0183\) at that point would omit both shell volume and integration over larger radii.

Second application

Compare two ions through the same physical sphere

For a one-electron helium ion and a heavy nucleus approximation, doubling \(Z\) halves \(a\) to \(26.45\) pm. At a physical radius of \(52.9\) pm, the hydrogen tail is \(5e^{-2}=0.677\), while the ion tail is

\[13e^{-4}=0.238.\]

The same fractional containment would be recovered by comparing radii in proportion to \(1/Z\). This comparison assumes an isolated ion with no second electron to screen the nuclear field.

Identify the symmetry before choosing coordinates

An isolated electron and nucleus interacting through a central potential can be separated into centre-of-mass motion and relative motion. Let \(\mu\) be the reduced mass of the two particles. The relative Hamiltonian is \(\hat p^2/(2\mu)+V(r)\) with \(r\) their separation. Simultaneously rotating position and momentum does not change the energy. Angular momentum provides the observables that express this symmetry and organise the many possible three-dimensional wavefunctions.

Orbital angular momentum is \(\hat{\mathbf L}=\hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf p}\). Its components obey \([L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z\) and cyclic permutations. The total magnitude squared \(\hat L^2\) commutes with each component. We can therefore specify eigenvalues of energy, \(L^2\) and one projection, conventionally \(L_z\), together. The respective angular eigenvalues are \(\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2\) and \(m\hbar\), with integer \(\ell\ge0\) and \(m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\). Spherical harmonics \(Y_{\ell m}\) represent those angular states, with probability density proportional to their squared magnitude.

Separate angular and radial motion

Write \(\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\). Volume in spherical coordinates contains a factor \(r^2\), so normalising the angular function leaves radial probability \(r^2|R(r)|^2dr\). Defining \(u(r)=rR(r)\) both simplifies the radial normalisation and removes the first derivative from the radial equation:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''+\left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u=Eu.\]

For ordinary bound atomic states regularity at the origin gives \(u(0)=0\) and normalisability gives decay at large radius. The angular momentum term penalises small radius when \(\ell>0\). Although it resembles the effective centrifugal energy of classical central motion, here its coefficient comes from the angular eigenvalue rather than an adjustable classical angular momentum.

Derive the lowest Coulomb state

For hydrogen set \(V=-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)=-C/r\) with \(C>0\). For \(\ell=0\) try \(R=Ae^{-r/a}\), hence \(u=Ar e^{-r/a}\). The second derivative satisfies

\[u''/u=1/a^2-2/(ar).\]

Substitute this ratio in the radial equation. The terms proportional to \(1/r\) cancel only when \(a=\hbar^2/(\mu C)\), leaving \(E_1=-\hbar^2/(2\mu a^2)\). This nodeless solution is the lowest bound state. Choosing another exponential length would leave a residual \(1/r\) term, giving a useful check on the derivation.

For hydrogen with a heavy proton approximation, \(a\simeq0.0529\) nm and \(E_1\simeq-13.6\) eV. The three-dimensional density of the ground state is largest at the origin. The probability per unit radial distance instead contains \(r^2\) and peaks at \(r=a\). A claim about the “most likely radius” must specify which measure is in use. Measuring a shell of equal thickness at a larger radius includes more volume.

Distinguish spectra from angular distributions

Solving the regular bound-state radial equation for general \(\ell\) gives \(E_n=-\mu C^2/(2\hbar^2n^2)\) with \(n=1,2,\ldots\) and \(0\le\ell\le n-1\). States with different \(m\) are degenerate by rotational symmetry. The additional degeneracy among different \(\ell\) at fixed \(n\) is a special property of the Coulomb potential. Corrections such as fine structure and external fields can separate lines that the present model puts at one energy.

The transition from \(n=3\) to \(n=2\) releases \(13.6(1/2^2-1/3^2)=1.89\) eV, giving wavelength \(hc/\Delta E\simeq656\) nm. The energy gap predicts the wavelength but not the strength of every possible transition. Electric dipole emission involves an angular matrix element of position, so it connects orbital angular momenta differing by one. An excited state can have the right energy gap while a particular dipole transition remains forbidden by symmetry. This is why both radial energies and angular labels belong in a spectroscopy record.

The reduced mass supplies a further spectroscopic test. Replacing a proton by a deuteron approximately doubles the nuclear mass while preserving its charge. Since \(\mu=m_e/[1+m_e/M]\), the electronic reduced mass increases by a relative amount close to \(m_e/(2m_p)=0.000272\). All gross transition frequencies rise by the same fraction, while wavelengths fall. A protium line at 656 nm should have a deuterium counterpart roughly 0.179 nm to its short-wavelength side. Resolving the two lines requires sufficient spectral resolution and a source with both isotopes. Small additional shifts reveal corrections beyond the point-nucleus, nonrelativistic model.

Couple orbital and spin angular momenta

The angular labels of a central potential organize an electron's spatial and spin states separately. A weak spin–orbit interaction can split levels without breaking overall rotational symmetry. Model it within a fixed radial and orbital manifold by \(H_{\rm so}=\lambda\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar^2\), with \(\lambda\) an energy representing the radial coupling average. The model assumes coupling small relative to separations from other radial or orbital terms. Let \(\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S\). The identity \(J^2=L^2+S^2+2\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\) expresses the interaction entirely through simultaneous angular eigenvalues,

\[\Delta E_j=\frac{\lambda}{2}[j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-s(s+1)] .\]

The allowed total values run from \(|\ell-s|\) to \(\ell+s\) in unit steps. Each \(j\) has \(2j+1\) possible magnetic projections. The interaction is a rotational scalar, so all projections of the same \(j\) remain degenerate here. The original labels \(m_\ell\) and \(m_s\) taken separately no longer label eigenstates in general, since terms \(L_+S_-\) and \(L_-S_+\) exchange projection while keeping their sum fixed.

For \(\ell=1\) and electron spin \(s=1/2\), the product space has three orbital choices and two spin choices, six states in all. The allowed \(j=3/2\) quartet shifts upward by \(\lambda/2\) and the \(j=1/2\) doublet downward by \(\lambda\) for positive coupling. Weighted over all six states the shifts sum to zero, giving a useful check on energy bookkeeping.

Reconstruct a split spectral term

Suppose the two energies in such a \(p\) term are separated by 4.50 meV and the fourfold component is the upper one. The model gives splitting \(3\lambda/2\), hence \(\lambda=3.00\) meV. The quartet lies 1.50 meV above the unsplit term and the doublet 3.00 meV below it. If the observed quartet energy is 2.00150 eV on a common reference, the inferred unsplit centre is 2.00000 eV and the doublet lies at 1.99700 eV. The corresponding difference in optical frequencies for transitions from a common lower level is \(4.50\,{\rm meV}/h=1.09\) THz.

Relative line intensities are generally not fixed solely by the quartet-to-doublet multiplicity, because lower-state populations and dipole matrix elements enter. An external magnetic field can additionally split different \(m_j\) values and eventually mix the angular couplings. In the special limit \(\ell=0\), there is only \(j=s\) and the shift vanishes identically. Observing a doublet from an apparently orbital-singlet term would therefore point to another interaction or a mistaken assignment.

A measurement of angular momentum projection can distinguish the coupling assignments independently of transition wavelengths. The completely aligned state with \(m_\ell=1\) and \(m_s=1/2\) has total projection \(3/2\) and can belong only to the \(j=3/2\) manifold. For smaller total projection a given \(j\) state generally contains more than one orbital–spin product. The small-field magnetic response then reflects the combined angular content rather than the separate labels of one arbitrarily chosen component. Preparing or identifying a stretched state provides a particularly direct check on which member of a fine-structure doublet is the quartet.
