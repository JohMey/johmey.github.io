Semiclassical quantization

Use a local wavelength to construct the quantization rule for a smooth well and recognize where that construction breaks down.

From first principles

Build the semiclassical phase and the turning-point condition

In a classically allowed interval define \(p(x)=\sqrt{2m[E-V(x)]}>0\) and seek a one-dimensional stationary wave \(\psi=A(x)e^{iS(x)/\hbar}\). Assume \(p\) varies little over a local wavelength. Substitute into the Schrödinger equation and separate terms by powers of \(\hbar\). The leading real and next imaginary balances give

\[(S')^2=p^2,\]
\[2A'S'+AS''=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^2 S'=\text{constant}.\]

Thus the phase increment is \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\) and the travelling-wave amplitude varies as \(p^{-1/2}\). This approximation fails where \(p=0\). Near each smooth simple turning point the potential is locally linear; the decaying exterior Airy solution matches an interior sine with phase shift \(\pi/4\). The waves entering from the left and right must describe the same standing wave. Combining the two end phases gives

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\]
\[n=0,1,\ldots .\]

The offset comes from the two soft turning points. A hard wall imposes its own node condition and changes the quantization offset. The construction becomes more reliable when the state samples many local wavelengths inside the well.

Derivation and applications

Read a quantum level spacing from a classical clock

The semiclassical quantization rule uses the area swept in phase space. Its change with energy also carries information about time. Consider a smooth one-dimensional binding potential with two simple turning points \(x_\pm(E)\), mass \(m\), and many accessible levels. Write the half-cycle action using the positive momentum branch \(p(x,E)=\sqrt{2m[E-V(x)]}\):

\[I(E)=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x,E)\,dx,\]
\[I(E_n)\simeq\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12).\]

The endpoints move with energy, but their boundary contributions when differentiating the integral contain \(p(x_\pm)=0\). Inside the well \(\partial_Ep=m/p\). Its divergence near a simple turning point behaves as an inverse square root and is integrable. Using \(p/m=\dot x\) on the forward leg now gives

\[\frac{dI}{dE}=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{m}{p(x,E)}\,dx=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{dx}{\dot x}=\frac{T_{\rm cl}(E)}{2}.\]

Two neighbouring quantized actions differ by \(\pi\hbar\). If the classical period varies little over a level spacing, linearizing the action about one level leads to

\[E_{n+1}-E_n\simeq\frac{2\pi\hbar}{T_{\rm cl}(E_n)}=\frac{h}{T_{\rm cl}(E_n)} .\]

Thus a densely spaced part of the spectrum is associated with a long classical excursion. For a harmonic well the period is constant and the result is exactly the known spacing; near a separatrix the rapidly varying period makes a one-step linearization less reliable. Only the period of a complete return to the starting phase-space point belongs in this relation.

Worked example

Estimate a frequency from adjacent lines

Consider an approximately harmonic vibrational well with nearby levels separated by \(2.50\) meV. With \(h=4.136\) meV ps, the inferred classical period is \(1.65\) ps and the ordinary oscillation frequency is \(0.604\) THz. A transit from the left turning point to the right should take about \(0.827\) ps. Equating this one-way transit with the period would overestimate the energy spacing by two.

Second application

Predict the scaling change for a quartic well

In \(V(x)=\lambda x^4\) the turning point is \(x_t=(E/\lambda)^{1/4}\). Substituting \(x=x_tu\) into \(T_{\rm cl}=2\int_{-x_t}^{x_t}m\,dx/p\) leaves an energy-independent integral and gives

\[T_{\rm cl}\propto E^{-1/4}.\]

Therefore local level spacing grows as \(E^{1/4}\). At sixteen times the energy a spacing of \(0.180\) meV should become about \(0.360\) meV, assuming both regions contain enough levels for semiclassical counting. The quadratic trap would show no comparable growth.

A locally oscillating solution

Consider one-dimensional bound motion of mass \(m\) in a smoothly varying potential. In allowed regions the stationary equation is \(\psi''+p(x)^2\psi/\hbar^2=0\) with \(p=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}\). If the potential changes little over a local wavelength, a small interval resembles a free-particle region. Accumulating its local phase produces the action integral \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\). Yet a wave with changing local momentum must also change amplitude in order to conserve current.

Substitute \(\psi=A(x)\exp[iS(x)/\hbar]\). The leading real terms give

\[S'^2=p^2.\]

The next imaginary terms give \(2A'S'+AS''=0\), or \((A^2S')'=0\). Therefore each travelling contribution has amplitude proportional to \(1/\sqrt p\). Equivalently a classical particle dwells longer where it is slow, and the smoothed semiclassical probability mirrors this dwell time. The neglected derivative terms are small away from turning points when \(\hbar|p'|\ll p^2\).

Match through a turning point

At an ordinary turning point \(V(x_t)=E\) with nonzero slope, the local wavelength diverges and the previous criterion fails. Linearizing \(V\) there gives the Airy equation. Its decaying forbidden-side solution continues to an oscillatory allowed-side standing wave with a phase offset of \(\pi/4\). Matching decaying exterior tails on both sides of a well bounded by two such turning points imposes

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\]
\[n=0,1,2,\ldots .\]

The offset encodes the two smooth reflections. An infinite hard wall instead imposes a node directly and has a different reflection phase. Applying this rule unchanged to a hard box would count its levels incorrectly. For slowly varying barriers the forbidden action also controls exponential attenuation, but the two-turning-point integral above determines the energies of a classically allowed well.

A trap with measurable spacings

For \(V=m\omega^2x^2/2\) let \(x_t=\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}\). Put \(x=x_t\sin u\) in the action integral. The integrand becomes \((2E/\omega)\cos^2u\,du\) between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), yielding \(\pi E/\omega\). The rule gives \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)\), exceptionally exact even for the oscillator’s lowest levels.

For a mechanical trap at frequency 100 kHz, \(E_4=4.5hf=2.98\times10^{-28}\) J, equivalent to \(21.6\) μK on division by \(k_B\). The spacing is \(hf\) or \(4.80\) μK. These temperatures express energy units; no equilibrium ensemble was assumed. As a transfer test consider \(V=gx^4\). Rescaling \(x=(E/g)^{1/4}y\) makes the action proportional to \(E^{3/4}\). Quantization gives \(E_n\propto(n+1/2)^{4/3}\), so successive spacings grow with excitation. At sufficiently high level number the action changes slowly over a wavelength, making the predicted trend more trustworthy than a low-level numerical prefactor.

An independent check on the energy spacing follows by differentiating the action with respect to energy. The integrand vanishes at the turning points, so their moving endpoints add no first-order term. Inside the integral \(\partial p/\partial E=m/p=1/v\). Consequently the derivative of the one-way action is the time for the classical particle to travel from one turning point to the other. Adjacent quantum levels at high excitation then have separation approximately \(h\) divided by the full classical period. Measuring a variation of level spacing with excitation is therefore also probing the dependence of the classical orbit period on energy.

Estimate tunnelling between two bound wells

A nearly symmetric double well can have pairs of quantum states concentrated mainly in classically separate regions. For one such pair take approximately orthogonal localized states \(|L\rangle,|R\rangle\) with equal isolated energy \(E_0\) and a small real tunnelling matrix element \(-J\). The sign of either localized basis state can be chosen so \(J>0\). The two-level Hamiltonian has symmetric and antisymmetric eigenstates with energies \(E_0-J\) and \(E_0+J\), respectively. A local preparation \(|L\rangle\) is a superposition of both and acquires a relative phase at rate \(2J/\hbar\).

On evolution the probability to be in the other well is \(P_R(t)=\sin^2(Jt/\hbar)\). The first complete transfer occurs at \(t_{\rm tr}=\pi\hbar/(2J)=h/(2\Delta E)\) with \(\Delta E=2J\). In a high smooth barrier the localized wave tails overlap weakly. A semiclassical estimate gives

\[J\simeq J_0 e^{-\mathcal S/\hbar},\]

with \(\mathcal S=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m[V(x)-E_0]}\,dx\) between inner turning points. This is an amplitude action. The through-barrier transmission probability of a scattering experiment instead contains twice that exponent. Confusing the two quantities gives an incorrect predicted change in the oscillation time.

Predict how a gate changes a tunnelling period

Suppose a symmetric electron double well shows a spectroscopic splitting \(\Delta E=40.0\) μeV. Its predicted first full transfer time is

\[h/(2\Delta E)=51.7\,\mathrm{ps}.\]

Use the measured splitting to eliminate the unknown semiclassical prefactor. A gate modification adds a nearly flat forbidden segment of width \(0.300\) nm where \(V-E_0=0.150\) eV and otherwise changes the wells negligibly. Using \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV nm² gives decay wavenumber \(\kappa=\sqrt{0.150/0.0381}=1.98\) nm⁻¹. The added dimensionless action is 0.595.

If the prefactor and individual well frequencies remain similar, the new splitting is \(40.0e^{-0.595}=22.1\) μeV, and transfer time rises to \(93.6\) ps. Doubling the width change multiplies the splitting by the square of the original ratio, not by twice the original ratio. These estimates concern coherent dynamics before energy relaxation or phase noise destroys the relative phase.

A gate can also shift the two well bottoms differently. With detuning \(\delta\) the splitting is \(\sqrt{\delta^2+4J^2}\), while maximum transfer falls to \(4J^2/(\delta^2+4J^2)\). A larger measured splitting can therefore accompany poorer population transfer if symmetry is lost. Comparing spectroscopy and time-resolved populations distinguishes a changed tunnel coupling from an unintended potential offset.

The small tunnel coupling also sets a time criterion for disorder: a fluctuating left–right offset comparable with the splitting can produce substantial changes in both transfer amplitude and oscillation frequency. A static distribution of offsets across repeated preparations can wash out ensemble oscillations even when each individual preparation evolves coherently. Repeating the experiment after compensating the measured detuning separates this ensemble averaging from intrinsic energy relaxation. The semiclassical action comparison is most reliable when the two localized levels remain nearly resonant throughout the modification and no higher intrawell level enters the same small energy window.

Independent measurements of the individual well minima supply a practical detuning calibration.
