From first principles

Build a joint spin probability from a two-particle amplitude

Two distinguishable spin-one-half measurements have a product basis \(|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle\). In a singlet the normalized state is \((|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Measure the first spin along \(z\) and the second along an axis tilted by \(\theta\) in the \(xz\) plane. A positive eigenstate along the new axis is

\[|+\theta\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|\uparrow\rangle+\sin(\theta/2)|\downarrow\rangle .\]

The first positive outcome selects the singlet's first product term. Its remaining second-particle amplitude along the tilted axis is the overlap with \(|\downarrow\rangle\). The Born rule for the joint projector now gives

\[P(+z,+\theta)=|\langle\uparrow,+\theta|\psi^-\rangle|^2=\tfrac12\sin^2(\theta/2)=\tfrac14(1-\cos\theta).\]

For the other tilted outcome the squared overlap is \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\). Adding the two joint outcomes gives \(P(+z)=1/2\). Dividing the joint result by this marginal yields the conditional probability for the partner. The same single-particle count occurs at all analyser angles even though the coincidence pattern changes.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the two-spin coincidence law from projectors

A pair prepared in a spin singlet has no preferred axis, yet coincidence counts change when one analyser rotates. Start with two spin-one-half particles in \(|\psi^-\rangle=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Ideal spin measurements along unit vectors \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\) have recorded outcomes \(s,t=\pm1\), in units of \(\hbar/2\). Neglect detector losses. Since the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) are \(\pm1\), its projector onto result \(s\) is

\[\Pi_s(\mathbf a)=\frac{I+s\,\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2},\]
\[P(s,t)=\langle\psi^-|\Pi_s(\mathbf a)\otimes\Pi_t(\mathbf b)|\psi^-\rangle .\]

Tracing over either particle gives \(I/2\), so terms linear in one spin have zero expectation. To evaluate the remaining product, choose both axes initially along \(z\). The singlet basis components always have opposite outcomes, giving \(\langle\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z\rangle=-1\). Rotational invariance of the singlet requires its two-index correlation matrix to be proportional to the identity. The \(zz\) result fixes the coefficient:

\[\langle\sigma_i\otimes\sigma_j\rangle=-\delta_{ij},\]
\[\langle(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\otimes(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\rangle=-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b .\]

Expand the product of projectors and substitute this correlation. For angle \(\theta\) between the analysers the full coincidence probabilities and their sum over the second analyser become

\[P(s,t)=\tfrac14(1-st\cos\theta),\]
\[\sum_tP(s,t)=\tfrac12 .\]

Parallel axes forbid equal outcomes. Perpendicular axes give four equally likely outcomes despite the pure preparation. Local counts alone reveal neither the relative angle nor the phase coherence of the joint state.

Worked example

Predict a coincidence histogram

With \(1200\) independently prepared pairs and a \(60.0^\circ\) angle, the equal-outcome cells each have probability \((1-0.5)/4=0.125\) and the opposite-outcome cells each have probability \(0.375\). The expected counts, ordered as \(++,+-,-+,--\), are \(150,450,450,150\). Given a positive result for the first analyser, the probability of a negative result at the second is

\[450/(150+450)=0.750.\]

Second application

Separate a singlet from a classical preparation

A source instead produces an equal incoherent mixture of the two product states anti-aligned along \(z\). Both sources are perfectly anti-correlated along that axis. Align both analysers along \(x\). Each product preparation now gives independent, equally probable outcomes, so \(P(\mathrm{equal})=0.500\). The singlet predicts zero equal outcomes. For 200 ideal pairs the predictions are 100 versus zero equal coincidences, while either local detector still reports each sign about half of the time.

Build the state space of a pair

A spin-½ measurement along a chosen axis yields two possible outcomes. Label its dimensionless results \(+1\) and \(-1\), corresponding to spin angular momenta \(\pm\hbar/2\). For two separately addressable particles A and B, a joint state belongs to the tensor product of their two spin spaces. In the \(z\) basis we need four product vectors: both up, A up and B down, A down and B up, or both down. A general normalised state contains four complex amplitudes whose squared magnitudes sum to one.

An uncorrelated pure preparation can be written \(|\psi_A\rangle\otimes|\psi_B\rangle\). Its product-structure forces a relation among the four amplitudes. For example the state \((|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\) has two nonzero amplitudes for opposite spins and zero amplitude for equal spins. No product of two individual pure states has that pattern. This entangled state is the spin singlet. Spin labels here refer to the internal states at the two measurement locations.

Project along the same and different axes

Measure both particles along \(z\). The two opposite outcomes have probability one half each, and the product of their dimensionless outcomes always equals \(-1\). Alice's result alone is random. Conditional on her obtaining \(+1\), the corresponding outcome for Bob along the same axis is definitely \(-1\). A classical mixture of the two opposite \(z\) product states with equal weights would give exactly the same statistics in this one measurement arrangement.

To distinguish the preparations, rotate Bob’s analyser through angle \(\theta\) in the \(xz\) plane while Alice continues to measure \(z\). A spin initially up along \(z\) has probability \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\) of being up along Bob’s axis and \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\) of being down along it. For the singlet, conditional on Alice's \(+1\) outcome, Bob starts in the opposite state. His probabilities are therefore interchanged. Summing over Alice's equally likely outcomes gives the joint correlation \(E(z,b)=-\cos\theta\). Its single-party marginals remain one half.

Find the coherence that an ensemble can hide

Rotational invariance of the singlet gives \(E(a,b)=-\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\) for any chosen axes. In particular if both measure \(x\), their results are again perfectly anticorrelated. Verify this directly by expressing \(|\uparrow_z\rangle=(|\uparrow_x\rangle+|\downarrow_x\rangle)/\sqrt2\) and \(|\downarrow_z\rangle=(|\uparrow_x\rangle-|\downarrow_x\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The two equal-\(x\) amplitudes cancel in the superposition.

In the classical mixture of the two opposite \(z\) states those amplitudes cannot cancel across different ensemble members. Each particle measured along \(x\) is individually random and the two \(x\) outcomes are independent conditional on either preparation. The mixture consequently has \(E(x,x)=0\). Measuring repeated pairs in two bases reveals an off-diagonal coherence invisible to either single-party measurement. Local outcome statistics alone stay random regardless of the distant analyser setting.

Predict counts and a Bell combination

At \(\theta=60°\) the singlet gives \(E=-1/2\). The probability of opposite outcomes is

\[(1-E)/2=0.75.\]

For 400 ideal detected pairs one therefore expects about 300 opposite and 100 equal outcomes, with ordinary statistical fluctuations. Rotating both analysers together preserves these fractions because only their relative angle matters for a singlet.

The distinction can be sharpened with four settings. In one plane choose Alice’s angles 0° and 90° and Bob’s 45° and −45°. Form \(S=E(a,b)+E(a,b')+E(a',b)-E(a',b')\). The three first correlations are \(-1/\sqrt2\) and the last is \(+1/\sqrt2\), giving \(|S|=2\sqrt2\). A model assigning local predetermined binary outcomes to all four settings gives for each emitted pair a sum \(A(B+B')+A'(B-B')\) of magnitude two; averaging bounds \(|S|\) by two if the sampled ensemble is independent of the choices of settings. A laboratory interpretation must also account for detection statistics and the sampling protocol. The calculation concerns joint correlations and grants no control over the individual random outcomes.

If one observer discards the other observer’s records, the local spin outcomes remain equally likely for each measurement axis. Sorting the same data into subsets according to the partner’s outcomes reveals the correlations. This sorting requires comparing the two records through an ordinary communication channel.

Classify a pure bipartite state by its Schmidt weights

The spin singlet has maximally mixed individual spins even though its joint state is pure. A partially entangled pair allows a continuous test of that distinction. Any pure state of two finite systems can be written as \(\sum_{ij}A_{ij}|i\rangle|j\rangle\). Tracing out the second system gives

\[\rho_A=AA^\dagger.\]

Singular-value decomposition rotates the two local bases independently and writes the same state as \(\sum_k\sqrt{\lambda_k}|u_k\rangle|v_k\rangle\), where \(\lambda_k\ge0\) and their sum is one. The reduced states on both sides have the same nonzero eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\).

A pure product state has just one nonzero Schmidt weight. Two or more weights imply entanglement, because separate rotations of the local bases cannot remove singular values. The reduced purity is \(\sum_k\lambda_k^2\), and the entanglement entropy for a pure pair is \(-\sum_k\lambda_k\ln\lambda_k\). A nonzero reduced entropy describes uncertainty in local observations. It can occur jointly with zero entropy for the complete pure state. If the joint preparation is already mixed, reduced entropy alone is insufficient to quantify its entanglement.

Choose a measurement basis for the partner

Prepare \(|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.90}|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.10}|11\rangle\). Its local populations are 0.90 and 0.10, giving reduced purity \(0.90^2+0.10^2=0.820\) and entropy \(-0.90\ln0.90-0.10\ln0.10=0.325\) in natural-log units. Measuring the second spin in the computational basis prepares the first in \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) with those respective probabilities. Each branch has a definite computational outcome.

Now measure the partner in \(|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Both outcomes have probability one half. Conditional on the result, the first spin is in \(\sqrt{0.90}|0\rangle\pm\sqrt{0.10}|1\rangle\), already normalized. A subsequent plus-basis measurement on the first spin gives plus with probability \([1\pm2\sqrt{0.90\times0.10}]/2\), namely 0.800 or 0.200. Without receiving the partner's result the first observer averages these two values and sees 0.500, exactly as predicted by the unchanged reduced matrix.

As the smaller Schmidt weight tends to zero the state approaches a product and the two conditional plus probabilities coalesce. Equal weights give maximal local entropy \(\ln2\). A classical mixture of \(|00\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\) with the same 0.90 and 0.10 weights produces identical computational-basis statistics but gives 0.500 in both conditional plus measurements. The pair of rotated analysers therefore tests coherence between the alternatives rather than merely confirming their occupation probabilities.

Experiments verify the conditional prediction by collecting both detector outcomes and matching them trial by trial. If one sorts first-spin data without correctly identifying the partner from the same pair, the two 0.800 and 0.200 conditional probabilities both tend back toward the unconditional value of one half. This loss of contrast can thus result from timing or pairing errors even when each local detector has excellent single-spin fidelity. Repeating the computational-basis correlation measurement supplies an independent pairing check before interpreting the more delicate rotated-basis signal as a property of the prepared wavefunction.
