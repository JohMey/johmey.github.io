When a free quantum packet initially gets narrower

A position distribution and a momentum distribution alone do not determine the early motion of a wave packet. Reconstruct free expansion from their covariance and compare two preparations with the same widths.

Ask for a width, rather than a single trajectory

Prepare many identical particles, release them in free space and measure one position from each preparation at a chosen delay. The spread in that collection of positions is the observable \(\sigma_x(t)\). A particle detected early cannot also contribute an undisturbed late-time position to the same release. Quantum expectations below describe an ensemble of repeated preparations.

For a free particle in one dimension \(H=p^2/(2m)\). In the Heisenberg picture the preparation stays fixed while the operators carry the time dependence. The identity \([AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B\) and \([x,p]=i\hbar\) give

\[\dot p=(i/\hbar)[H,p]=0,\]
\[\dot x=(i/\hbar)[H,x]=p/m,\]
\[x(t)=x(0)+tp(0)/m .\]

Subtract the expectation value on both sides. Squaring this centred operator requires both multiplication orders for its cross term because \(x\) and \(p\) do not commute. Set \(C=(\langle\Delta x\Delta p+\Delta p\Delta x\rangle)/2\), a real number with units of action. The exact result for any free preparation with these finite moments is

\[\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_x^2(0)+\frac{2t}{m}C+\frac{t^2}{m^2}\sigma_p^2(0).\]

Give a Gaussian a spatially varying phase

Let the initial wavefunction be proportional to \(\exp[-x^2/(4s^2)+ibx^2/(4s^2)]\), with \(s>0\). Its density is a Gaussian of variance \(s^2\) for every real \(b\). Act with \(-i\hbar\,\partial_x\) before taking moments. The derivative gives

\[p\psi=\hbar(b+i)x\psi/(2s^2).\]

Consequently \(\langle p\rangle=0\), \(\sigma_p^2=\|p\psi\|^2=\hbar^2(1+b^2)/(4s^2)\), and \(C=\hbar b/2\), the real part of \(\langle xp\rangle\). For negative \(b\), probability on the right initially flows left and probability on the left flows right.

\[\frac{\sigma_x^2(t)}{s^2}=1+2b\,u+(1+b^2)u^2,\]
\[u=\frac{\hbar t}{2ms^2}.\]

Stationarity of the quadratic gives

\[u_*=-b/(1+b^2).\]

A future focus occurs for negative \(b\), and its width is \(s/\sqrt{1+b^2}\). The preparation saturates \(\sigma_x^2\sigma_p^2-C^2=\hbar^2/4\), so a position focus does not violate uncertainty. It removes the initial position–momentum correlation and leaves the same large momentum spread.

Predict two releases

Take \(b=-2\) and \(s=4.0\) µm. At \(u=0.40\) the width falls to

\[4/\sqrt5=1.79\,\mu\mathrm m.\]

With \(b=+2\), both initial marginal widths are unchanged, but at the same time the packet has width \(4\sqrt{3.4}=7.38\) µm. An initial image cannot distinguish the two releases. Measuring a second image at known positive delay resolves the sign.

For a third preparation use \(b=0\). This one starts with the smallest compatible momentum spread for the specified \(s\) and reaches \(s\sqrt2\) at \(u=1\). In all three cases the mean stays at the origin. Translating the packet or giving it a common mean momentum would translate that mean trajectory without changing the variance formula.

Tutorial on a missing phase

A Gaussian initially 3.0 µm wide focuses to 1.5 µm after release. Find its initial chirp and dimensionless focusing time.

Work through the solution

The ratio \(s/s_{\min}=2\) gives \(1+b^2=4\). A future focus selects \(b=-\sqrt3\). Hence

\[u_*=\sqrt3/4=0.4330.\]

A packet with the opposite sign would have reached that same minimum before the stated release time under free evolution.

Two detectors measure packet widths at scaled delays \(u=+0.20\) and \(u=-0.20\) relative to a chosen reference preparation. Their squared widths differ by \(-25.6\,\mu\mathrm m^2\), with \(s=4\,\mu\mathrm m\). Infer \(b\) from just this difference.

Work through the solution

Subtracting the width-squared formula at opposite delays cancels the constant and quadratic terms, giving \(4s^2bu\). Hence

\[b=-25.6/(4\times16\times0.20)=-2.\]

The sign comes entirely from the position–momentum covariance. Choosing the reference preparation partway through free expansion makes measurements on both sides physically meaningful.

Can a non-Gaussian free state with finite second moments initially contract? What minimum variance follows from the general expression?

Work through the solution

Differentiate the moment formula to get

\[t_*=-mC/\sigma_p^2.\]

A future minimum requires \(C<0\). Its variance is \(\sigma_x^2(0)-C^2/\sigma_p^2\), bounded below by \(\hbar^2/(4\sigma_p^2)\) using the Schrödinger–Robertson inequality. The Gaussian in this section attains the bound, but initial contraction requires only a negative covariance.
