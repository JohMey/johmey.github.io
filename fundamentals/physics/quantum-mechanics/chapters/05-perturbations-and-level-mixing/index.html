From first principles

Resolve an energy correction into virtual couplings

Take \(H=H_0+\lambda V\) with a known orthonormal spectrum \(H_0|n\rangle=E_n^0|n\rangle\). Assume the target level is isolated and the coupling weak relative to the relevant energy gaps. Write its perturbed state in intermediate normalization as \(|n\rangle+\lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle+\cdots\) with \(\langle n|n^{(1)}\rangle=0\). Insert this and the energy expansion in the eigenvalue equation. Terms at first order give

\[(H_0-E_n^0)|n^{(1)}\rangle=(E_n^{(1)}-V)|n\rangle .\]

Projection onto \(n\) gives

\[E_n^{(1)}=V_{nn}.\]

Projection onto each \(m\ne n\) gives the amplitude admixed from that other state. At second order the projection back onto \(n\) therefore gives

\[\langle m|n^{(1)}\rangle=\frac{V_{mn}}{E_n^0-E_m^0},\]
\[E_n^{(2)}=\langle n|V|n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^0-E_m^0}.\]

Higher unperturbed levels contribute negatively to the ground-state energy, while lower levels shift an excited state upward. A vanishing denominator signals that nearby states must be diagonalized together before expanding the effect of more distant states.

Derivation and applications

Read a mixed eigenstate from the slope of its energy

A level diagram around an avoided crossing contains more information than the minimum gap. Its slopes can reveal how much of each underlying basis state is present. Consider two orthonormal states with Hamiltonian \(H=(\Delta/2)\sigma_z+g\sigma_x\), real constant coupling \(g\), and a tunable signed energy difference \(\Delta\). We call the state with \(\sigma_z=+1\) the positive basis state. For a normalized, nondegenerate eigenstate, differentiate \(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\) with respect to \(\Delta\). Left multiplication by \(\langle n|\) gives

\[\langle n|\partial_\Delta H|n\rangle+\langle n|H|\partial_\Delta n\rangle=\partial_\Delta E_n+E_n\langle n|\partial_\Delta n\rangle .\]

The last terms cancel by Hermiticity. Since \(\partial_\Delta H=\sigma_z/2\), the measured slope equals half the population difference. If \(P_+\) is the probability of the positive basis state in this eigenstate, normalization gives

\[\partial_\Delta E_n=\tfrac12\langle\sigma_z\rangle=P_+-\tfrac12 .\]

The characteristic equation \(E^2=\Delta^2/4+g^2\) supplies the eigenenergies. For the lower branch, evaluate its derivative explicitly:

\[E_-=-\tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2},\]
\[P_+^{(-)}=\tfrac12\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}}\right).\]

At zero detuning both basis states have equal weight. At large positive detuning the positive basis state belongs mainly to the upper branch. These are stationary-state probabilities at the chosen setting. Preparing that state by moving the setting additionally requires a sweep slow enough compared with the gap.

Worked example

Estimate the minority component of a spectroscopic state

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

For \(g=0.150\) meV and \(\Delta=0.400\) meV, the measured branch separation is \(0.500\) meV. The lower slope is \(-0.400\) meV per meV, so

\[P_+^{(-)}=0.100.\]

A projective measurement resolving the original basis should yield about 100 positive outcomes in 1000 independent preparations of the lower state.

Second application

Infer a coupling without reaching the crossing

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

At a second setting a fitted lower-branch slope is \(-0.300\) while the energy gap is \(0.800\) meV. The measured slope requires \(\Delta/\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}=0.600\), giving \(\Delta=0.480\) meV. Squaring the gap then yields

\[g=\tfrac12\sqrt{0.800^2-0.480^2}=0.320\,\mathrm{meV}.\]

The predicted minimum gap is \(0.640\) meV; a separate zero-detuning scan checks the inference.

Predict a weak response from a known spectrum

A tunable control can move two energy levels towards one another. Far apart, weak coupling shifts them only slightly. Near coincidence the observed spectrum can change substantially even if the same coupling is small on every other energy scale. Begin with a Hamiltonian \(\hat H_0\) with known orthonormal eigenstates \(|n\rangle\) and energies \(E_n^0\). A weak time-independent perturbation \(\lambda\hat V\) changes both energies and states, with \(\lambda\) a bookkeeping parameter eventually set to one.

Insert expansions \(E_n=E_n^0+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\ldots\) and \(|\psi_n\rangle=|n\rangle+\lambda|\delta n\rangle+\ldots\) into the eigenvalue equation. At first order \(\hat H_0|\delta n\rangle+\hat V|n\rangle=E_n^0|\delta n\rangle+E_n^{(1)}|n\rangle\). Projection onto \(\langle n|\) cancels the two terms containing the unperturbed Hamiltonian and gives

\[E_n^{(1)}=\langle n|\hat V|n\rangle.\]

This shift is a weighted average of a spatial perturbation in the original eigenstate.

Follow the change in the state

Projection onto a different eigenstate \(\langle m|\) instead gives

\[\langle m|\delta n\rangle=V_{mn}/(E_n^0-E_m^0).\]

The dimensionless ratio of the coupling matrix element to the level separation controls the amount of mixing. Substitution of this corrected state at the next order gives

\[E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^0-E_m^0}.\]

A nondegenerate ground state has only higher-energy neighbours and therefore a nonpositive second-order shift. A vanishing diagonal element can make this second-order shift the leading effect. For example a symmetric bound state under a perturbation odd in position has zero first-order average. That fact alone does not establish that the state or its energy is unchanged.

As the energy separation approaches zero, the small-ratio condition fails. Apparent divergences signal that choosing one unperturbed vector as a unique starting state has become inappropriate. Within a degenerate subspace, diagonalise the perturbation first. Couplings to well-separated states outside the subspace can still be treated by ordinary perturbation theory if their matrix elements remain small compared with the external gaps.

Solve a pair exactly

Use two uncoupled levels \(-\Delta/2\) and \(+\Delta/2\), referenced to their midpoint, and take the off-diagonal coupling \(v\) real by a phase choice. The characteristic equation of the two-by-two Hamiltonian is

\[E^2-(\Delta/2)^2-v^2=0.\]

Thus \(E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+v^2}\). At zero detuning the gap is \(2|v|\). The equal-weight combinations of the two basis states diagonalise the Hamiltonian, even when the coupling would have been negligible far from coincidence.

Expanding the positive root for \(|v|\ll|\Delta|\) gives \(|\Delta|/2+v^2/|\Delta|\) to leading correction; the lower level shifts down by the same amount. Their sum equals the original trace for every detuning. A measured change in the midpoint as well as the gap could therefore require a common diagonal shift in addition to this off-diagonal coupling.

Read coupling from an avoided crossing

Let \(\Delta=10.0\) meV and \(v=1.0\) meV. The unperturbed energies are \(\pm5.0\) meV and the exact energies are \(\pm5.099\) meV. The second-order prediction of a 0.100 meV shift is accurate to about 0.001 meV. At \(\Delta=1.0\) meV the exact energies are \(\pm1.118\) meV and the perturbative correction \(v^2/\Delta=1.0\) meV is already larger than the original positive energy. Diagonalising the pair remains well conditioned there.

A spectrometer measuring the smallest separation in a detuning sweep would infer \(|v|=1.0\) meV from a minimum gap of 2.0 meV. Level-dependent transition strengths can supply a separate test: as the eigenstates exchange their dominant basis character across the avoided crossing, a probe coupling mainly to one basis state will redistribute intensity between the two observed lines.

An alternative experimental strategy fits states well away from the mixing region first. There the slopes with respect to the control parameter reveal the approximate unperturbed levels. Extrapolating those lines locates the crossing that would occur if the coupling vanished. Measuring the two actual levels near that point then separately determines the energy origin and the magnitude of the off-diagonal interaction. A phase change of either basis state reverses the sign of a real coupling, leaving both energies unchanged. Its sign relative to another amplitude requires an interference measurement.

Sweep through an avoided crossing

A spectrum measured at a fixed control setting reveals a gap. To follow a state while turning the control, we must also know how quickly its instantaneous eigenvector rotates. Consider smooth \(H(t)\) and two nondegenerate eigenstates. Differentiate \(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\) and take the inner product with another eigenstate \(m\). The term containing \(\dot E_n\) vanishes by orthogonality, giving

\[\langle m|\dot n\rangle=\frac{\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle}{E_n-E_m}.\]

When the state is expanded in the instantaneous basis with its dynamical phases, this coupling drives the amplitude of \(m\). The relative phase oscillates at angular frequency \(|E_n-E_m|/\hbar\). A slowly changing coupling then averages out over many oscillations. The scale \(\eta=\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|/|E_m-E_n|^2\) compares basis rotation with that phase rate. For a smooth isolated sweep, keeping it small throughout is a useful adiabaticity test. The final probability also depends on the full drive and preparation.

Take \(H=\Delta(t)\sigma_z/2+g\sigma_x\) with fixed \(g>0\) and detuning increased linearly through zero. The gap is \(\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\). At the centre the eigenstates point along the \(x\) direction in spin space, so the off-diagonal matrix element of \(\dot H=(\dot\Delta/2)\sigma_z\) has magnitude \(|\dot\Delta|/2\). This is also where the sweep is most demanding:

\[\eta(0)=\frac{\hbar|\dot\Delta|}{8g^2}.\]

With \(g=0.100\,\mathrm{meV}\), \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{meV\,ps}\) and \(|\dot\Delta|=0.00100\,\mathrm{meV/ps}\), the minimum splitting is \(0.200\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\eta(0)=0.00823\). Starting sufficiently far away in the lower eigenstate, a slow sweep transfers the occupation to the opposite uncoupled basis state as the lower eigenvector exchanges character. Multiplying the rate by twenty gives \(\eta(0)=0.165\) and a weaker separation of time scales. At \(g=0\) the crossing is exact and this finite-gap argument breaks down.

Apply degenerate perturbation theory to hydrogen in an electric field

A nondegenerate atomic parity eigenstate has zero permanent electric dipole along \(z\). It is tempting to infer that a weak uniform field always shifts an energy only at second order. For hydrogen in the nonrelativistic Coulomb model, however, the \(2s\) and \(2p\) levels are degenerate. A field \(\mathcal E\) along \(z\) adds \(V=e\mathcal E z\) for an electron, with positive \(e\) denoting the elementary charge. The perturbation is odd under inversion. Diagonal parity-basis elements vanish, but an off-diagonal matrix element can survive between opposite parities.

Choose \(|2s\rangle\) and \(|2p,m=0\rangle\), since \(z=r\cos\theta\) preserves magnetic projection. With conventional real hydrogen phases, the angular integral \(\langle Y_{00}|\cos\theta|Y_{10}\rangle=1/\sqrt3\). Writing \(\rho=r/a_0\), the normalized radial functions are proportional to \((2-\rho)e^{-\rho/2}\) and \(\rho e^{-\rho/2}\). Including radial volume and the extra \(r\) from \(z\) gives

\[\int_0^\infty R_{20}R_{21}r^3dr=\frac{a_0}{8\sqrt3}\int_0^\infty(2-\rho)\rho^4 e^{-\rho}d\rho=-3\sqrt3\,a_0 .\]

Thus the off-diagonal position element is \(-3a_0\). Its sign depends on orbital phases, while the magnitude and energy shifts do not. The perturbation matrix in this degenerate subspace has zero diagonal and equal off-diagonal entries \(-3e\mathcal E a_0\). Its eigenvalues are \(\pm3e\mathcal E a_0\) with orthogonal equal superpositions of the two original orbitals. The remaining \(m=\pm1\) states have no first-order partners here and remain unshifted in this model.

Assess a resolvable Stark splitting

Let \(\mathcal E=2.00\times10^6\) V/m and \(a_0=5.29\times10^{-11}\) m. The field-induced voltage across one Bohr radius is \(1.058\times10^{-4}\) V, giving each mixed level an energy shift magnitude \(0.317\) meV. Their separation is \(0.635\) meV or \(0.154\) THz. This is far smaller than the electronvolt-scale separation from other principal shells, supporting restriction to the \(n=2\) manifold. The absolute laboratory field, line widths and finer internal splittings still set the domain where the ideal doublet picture is appropriate.

If the two unperturbed orbitals are split by a small residual gap \(\delta\), the new separation is \(\sqrt{\delta^2+4(3e\mathcal E a_0)^2}\). At extremely weak field the change from \(|\delta|\) is then quadratic, crossing to approximately linear splitting once the off-diagonal coupling dominates. Varying the field over this crossover can distinguish truly degenerate first-order splitting from a barely resolved avoided crossing without assigning a permanent dipole to either original parity orbital.

The slopes of the split energies also have an observable interpretation. By differentiating a normalized eigenvalue with respect to the applied field, the Hellmann–Feynman relation gives \(dE/d\mathcal E=e\langle z\rangle\) in the same sign convention. The two mixed states therefore have opposite mean electron displacements of magnitude \(3a_0\) in the degenerate first-order model. Their charge distributions are oriented along or against the applied field although neither original parity state had an orientation. Following a field reversal requires care about which eigenbranch or prepared state is being tracked when a much smaller unresolved zero-field splitting is present.

Measuring slopes on both sides of field reversal tests the branch assignment independently of any common instrumental drift in the energy reference.
