Chapter 1 · Wave packets and probability current

Normalise a quantum wave packet and derive a local balance law that predicts the passage of probability through a detector plane.

From first principles

Derive local probability balance from Schrödinger evolution

Take a spinless particle on a line with constant mass and a real local potential \(V(x,t)\). The inputs are the Schrödinger equation as a dynamical postulate and the Born rule \(\rho=|\psi|^2\) as the probability density. To see how probability moves, differentiate \(\psi^*\psi\) and substitute the time derivative of the state and its complex conjugate:

\[i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi+V\psi,\]
\[\partial_t\rho=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\psi^*\partial_x^2\psi-\psi\partial_x^2\psi^*).\]

The potential terms cancel because \(V\) is real. The remaining difference is a spatial derivative: applying the product rule makes the two first-derivative cross terms cancel. This identifies the current and its sign:

\[\partial_t\rho+\partial_x j=0,\]
\[j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\psi^*\partial_x\psi-\psi\partial_x\psi^*).\]

Integrate over a detector interval \([a,b]\). The probability inside changes at rate \(j(a)-j(b)\), giving direct physical meaning to the local derivative. Vanishing exterior current conserves the total normalization. A complex absorbing potential would add a local sink and requires a different balance.

Derivation and applications

Predict the minimum width of a freely propagating packet

Two spatial snapshots of a beam can differ even when no forces act. To calculate this spreading without evaluating a Fourier integral, work with the position and momentum operators for a free, nonrelativistic particle of mass \(m\). Assume motion along a line, a normalized state with finite second moments, and negligible interaction during flight. In the Heisenberg picture \(H=p^2/(2m)\) is constant, momentum is conserved and velocity is \(p/m\). Integrating the operator equations gives

\[p(t)=p(0),\]
\[x(t)=x(0)+\frac{t}{m}p(0).\]

Subtract the mean trajectory. The square of \(\delta x(t)=\delta x(0)+(t/m)\delta p(0)\) contains both product orders because position and momentum do not commute. Defining \(C_{xp}=\langle\delta x\,\delta p+\delta p\,\delta x\rangle/2\), the exact variance is

\[\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_x^2(0)+\frac{2t}{m}C_{xp}+\frac{t^2}{m^2}\sigma_p^2(0).\]

Now specialize to an unchirped Gaussian at release, with standard deviation \(s\) in position, constant mean phase gradient and no quadratic phase. Then \(C_{xp}=0\) and the momentum standard deviation is \(\hbar/(2s)\). Substitution gives

\[\sigma_x(t)=s\sqrt{1+(t/\tau)^2},\]
\[\tau=2ms^2/\hbar .\]

The characteristic time marks the point at which propagation and initial preparation contribute equally to the variance. A narrower launch has a larger momentum spread and reaches this crossover sooner. The general variance equation also shows why free evolution can initially narrow a correlated preparation with \(C_{xp}<0\).

Worked example

Predict an electron-beam width at the detector

Take \(m=9.109\times10^{-31}\) kg, \(s=20.0\) nm and \(\hbar=1.055\times10^{-34}\) J s. The spreading time is \(6.91\) ps. After \(10.0\) ps the width is \(20.0\sqrt{1+(10.0/6.91)^2}=35.2\) nm. An overall boost translates the centroid while leaving this predicted width unchanged.

Second application

Choose the launch width for the smallest final spot

At fixed flight time \(t\), minimize \(s^2+(\hbar t/2m)^2/s^2\) over unchirped Gaussian preparations. Differentiating with respect to \(s^2\) gives \(s^2=\hbar t/(2m)\) at the minimum. For the same electron and \(10.0\) ps, the best launch width is \(24.1\) nm and the final width is \(\sqrt2\) times larger, \(34.0\) nm. Tightening the initial spot below that value makes this detector spot wider.

OneSimulation laboratory · Turn spatial eigenvalues into confinement energies

Specify a preparation

Prepare many independent particles of the same mass in the same one-dimensional state and record one position from each preparation. The frequencies in small position bins reveal a probability density. Quantum theory assigns a complex wavefunction \(\psi(x,t)\) to a pure state. The probability for \(a<x<b\) is \(\int_a^b|\psi|^2dx\). The integral over the whole line equals one for a normalised packet. Thus \(|\psi|^2\) has units of inverse length and can numerically exceed one without any probability exceeding one.

A single position histogram fixes the modulus of the wavefunction but contains no direct information about its phase. A global phase is unobservable, whereas a phase that varies across space changes the momentum distribution and subsequent evolution. Two preparations with the same initial histogram can reach a detector at different times. To predict how probability moves we need the phase information together with an equation of motion.

Obtain a continuity equation

For a particle in a real scalar potential \(V(x,t)\), the Schrödinger equation is \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-(\hbar^2/2m)\partial_{xx}\psi+V\psi\). Multiply by \(\psi^*\), subtract the conjugate equation multiplied by \(\psi\) and use the product rule. The two potential terms cancel because \(V\) is real. The result takes the conservative form

\[\partial_t|\psi|^2+\partial_x j=0,\qquad j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*\partial_x\psi-\psi\partial_x\psi^*\right).\]

Integrated between two fixed points, the rate of change of probability inside equals the inward current at the left minus the outward current at the right. In this one-dimensional normalisation \(j\) has units of inverse seconds. Vanishing current at both infinities ensures that a normalised state stays normalised even if the shape of its density changes appreciably.

Writing \(\psi=\sqrt\rho\,e^{i\varphi}\) gives \(j=(\hbar/m)\rho\,\partial_x\varphi\) where the phase is regular. For an initially real packet the current vanishes at that instant. A spatial phase ramp \(e^{ikx}\) leaves \(\rho\) unchanged yet gives current \(\hbar k\rho/m\). Current is a signed net flux. Interpreting every crossing of a point as a first arrival requires additional information about the detector and possible returns.

Count momenta as well as positions

Use the Fourier convention \(\psi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int\tilde\psi(k)e^{ikx}dk\). Parseval’s identity preserves the normalisation. The probability in a wave-number interval is \(|\tilde\psi(k)|^2dk\), with momentum \(p=\hbar k\). A Gaussian packet having density variance \(\sigma_x^2\) at preparation can be written as \(\psi=(2\pi\sigma_x^2)^{-1/4}\exp[-(x-x_0)^2/(4\sigma_x^2)+ik_0x]\). Fourier transforming this Gaussian gives mean momentum \(\hbar k_0\) and standard deviation \(\hbar/(2\sigma_x)\). Tighter initial localisation broadens the momentum distribution.

For a free particle the plane-wave components advance with \(\omega(k)=\hbar k^2/(2m)\). Different wave numbers give different group velocities. An initially minimal, unchirped Gaussian spreads to \(\sigma_x(t)=\sigma_x\sqrt{1+[\hbar t/(2m\sigma_x^2)]^2}\). Transferring this expression to an initially focused or phase-curved packet requires its position–momentum correlation as well as its initial width.

When does a wave packet follow Newton’s equation?

A position measurement on a single particle produces one draw from its distribution. Suppose we repeat the preparation at successive flight times. What determines the motion of the measured centroid? Write the ensemble mean of a Hermitian observable as \(\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle\). Differentiate both states and the operator. The Schrödinger equation gives \(|\dot\psi\rangle=-iH|\psi\rangle/\hbar\), while its adjoint gives the opposite sign on the bra. Thus

\[\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,A]\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}\right\rangle.\]

For a particle on a line let \(H=p^2/(2m)+V(x)\), with a real differentiable potential and states decaying sufficiently rapidly to drop boundary terms. Since \([p^2,x]=-2i\hbar p\) and \([V,p]=i\hbar V'(x)\), the first moment satisfies

\[m\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2}=-\langle V'(x)\rangle.\]

A uniform force accelerates every part of the density equally. In a harmonic trap the force is linear, so averaging it gives the force at the centroid for a packet of any width. An anharmonic force samples higher moments. The centroid and width can then influence each other even when no external noise is present.

Consider a quartic trap \(V=\lambda x^4/4\) with \(\lambda=1.00\,\mathrm{meV\,nm^{-4}}\). At one instant the position density is Gaussian, centred at \(a=2.00\,\mathrm{nm}\) with standard deviation \(s=1.00\,\mathrm{nm}\). Writing \(x=a+\xi\) eliminates odd central moments, giving \(\langle x^3\rangle=a^3+3as^2=14.0\,\mathrm{nm^3}\). The mean force is therefore \(-14.0\,\mathrm{meV/nm}=-2.24\,\mathrm{pN}\). Evaluating the force only at the centroid gives \(-8.00\,\mathrm{meV/nm}\). The high-force side of the distribution contributes disproportionately. As \(s/|a|\to0\) the fractional correction \(3s^2/a^2\) vanishes. This is an instantaneous prediction. Evolution in a quartic trap need not preserve a Gaussian shape.

Predict a free-flight measurement

An electron has initial width 100 nm and mean wave number \(1.0\times10^8\) m⁻¹. Its mean speed is \(\hbar k_0/m_e=1.16\times10^4\) m/s and its velocity spread is about 579 m/s. After 1.0 ns the centre has moved 11.6 µm. For the unchirped preparation the width grows to \(\sqrt{(0.100\,\mu\mathrm m)^2+(0.579\,\mu\mathrm m)^2}=0.588\) µm. The packet is well inside the nonrelativistic regime.

The solution predicts two independent observables: the motion of the centre and the broadening about that centre. Reversing the phase ramp reverses the former while leaving the latter unchanged. The initial position histograms for the two preparations are identical. A downstream record is needed to discriminate their motion.

One can measure spreading by repeating the preparation and recording the position distribution after several flight times. For an initially uncorrelated position and momentum, the free-particle variance is \(\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_x^2(0)+t^2\sigma_p^2/m^2\). This follows from the operator equation \(x(t)=x(0)+p(0)t/m\). A known initial position–momentum covariance adds a term linear in time and can even make the packet contract initially. Measuring a minimum downstream width alone therefore does not establish the width or the momentum uncertainty at release.

Infer current from two interfering momentum components

An interference pattern can make density strongly position dependent even when probability current is uniform. Put a free particle on a ring of circumference \(L\) and use periodic boundary conditions. Two states with wavenumbers \(+k\) and \(-k\), where \(k=2\pi n/L\) and \(n\) is a nonzero integer, have the same energy \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\). A normalized superposition has wavefunction \(\psi=[a e^{ikx}+be^{-ikx}]e^{-iEt/\hbar}/\sqrt L\) with \(|a|^2+|b|^2=1\). Integration around the ring makes the two opposite travelling waves orthogonal.

Squaring the amplitude gives a density containing a constant term and a spatial fringe, \(\rho=[1+2\mathrm{Re}(ab^*e^{2ikx})]/L\). To find current, differentiate \(\psi\) before taking the imaginary part of \(\psi^*\partial_x\psi\). The two diagonal products contribute with opposite signs. The cross terms in that product are real, so their contribution to current vanishes. Thus

\[j=\frac{\hbar k}{mL}(|a|^2-|b|^2),\qquad \partial_t\rho=-\partial_xj=0 .\]

The relative weights determine the net rate of passage past a fixed point; the relative phase shifts the fringe positions without changing that rate. Equal weights give a standing wave with stationary nodes and zero current. An unequal pair can have a stationary density pattern with nonzero circulating current, because every segment loses probability at one end at exactly the rate it gains it at the other.

Distinguish two preparations with one density pattern

Take an electron on an ideal ring of circumference \(1.00\) μm with \(n=2\), so \(k=1.257\times10^7\) m⁻¹. Choose \(a=\sqrt{0.80}\) and \(b=\sqrt{0.20}\) real. Fringe spacing is \(\pi/k=0.250\) μm and normalized density ranges from \(0.20/L\) to \(1.80/L\). The velocity scale is \(\hbar k/m_e=1.46\times10^3\) m/s. The probability current is this speed times \(0.60/L\), or \(8.73\times10^8\) s⁻¹. These units describe crossings per unit time for a normalized one-dimensional state rather than probability density per metre.

Interchange the weights assigned to clockwise and anticlockwise components while keeping their relative phase fixed and real. Density everywhere on the ring is identical but current reverses sign. A position histogram alone therefore cannot resolve which preparation was made. A momentum measurement over repeated preparations would instead return the two momenta with swapped probabilities. For a physical charged particle, multiplication by its charge gives the circulating electric current, whose sign is also reversed relative to probability current for an electron. A weak magnetic probe couples to that circulation rather than to the fringe visibility.

A relative phase change offers another comparison. Multiplying the anticlockwise amplitude by \(e^{i\alpha}\) moves the density pattern around the ring by \(\alpha/(2k)\) with the sign fixed by the chosen wave convention. At \(\alpha=\pi\) a previous maximum becomes a minimum, yet neither the probabilities of the two momentum outcomes nor the current changes. Conversely replacing the coherent preparation by a random mixture with the same two momentum probabilities leaves the circulating current intact while removing the interference fringes. Current, momentum populations and position fringes are therefore independent pieces of information about even this simple state.

Follow a freely spreading wave packet

A freely evolving minimum-uncertainty Gaussian starts at rest with position width \(\sigma_0\). Its momentum spread is \(\hbar/(2\sigma_0)\). Free motion gives \(\sigma(t)^2=\sigma_0^2+[\hbar t/(2m\sigma_0)]^2\) in the absence of an initial position–momentum correlation.

Chapter 2 · Scattering and tunnelling in one dimension

Match stationary waves across a potential discontinuity and use probability current to distinguish reflection, propagation and an evanescent field.

From first principles

Obtain reflected and transmitted probabilities at a potential step

Suppose a constant-mass particle reaches a flat potential step from the left with energy above both sides. In a region of constant potential the stationary Schrödinger equation has waves \(e^{\pm ikx}\) with \(\hbar^2k^2/2m=E-V\). Set the incoming amplitude to one and use asymptotic waves \(e^{ik_1x}+r e^{-ik_1x}\) at left and \(t e^{ik_2x}\) at right. Integration of the differential equation across the finite step makes the derivative continuous; the wave function itself is continuous as well:

\[1+r=t,\]
\[k_1(1-r)=k_2 t .\]

Solving the two linear conditions gives \(r=(k_1-k_2)/(k_1+k_2)\) and \(t=2k_1/(k_1+k_2)\). The current from the previous chapter for one travelling component is \(\hbar k|A|^2/m\) in its propagation direction. Ratios to incoming current therefore yield

\[R=\frac{(k_1-k_2)^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2},\]
\[T=\frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2},\]
\[R+T=1 .\]

Equal potentials give unity transmission. Near a right-hand threshold \(k_2\to0\), a finite boundary amplitude can coexist with vanishing transmitted flux because the outgoing velocity also tends to zero.

Derivation and applications

Match both faces of a finite barrier

A decaying wave inside a classically inaccessible interval does not alone determine its transmission. Boundary matching at the exit also contributes. Let \(V(x)=V_0\) for \(0<x<L\) and zero outside, with a particle of constant mass and \(0<E<V_0\) incident from the left. Define \(k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) and \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\). Set the phase reference at the exit so the outgoing wave is \(t e^{ik(x-L)}\). At \(x=L\), continuity gives both the exit value \(t\) and its derivative \(ikt\).

Inside, \(\psi''=\kappa^2\psi\). Propagate these exit data back to the entrance using the even and odd solutions:

\[\psi(0)=t[\cosh(\kappa L)-i(k/\kappa)\sinh(\kappa L)],\]
\[\psi'(0)=t[-\kappa\sinh(\kappa L)+ik\cosh(\kappa L)].\]

On the left write \(e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}\). Adding its value to its derivative divided by \(ik\) removes the reflected amplitude. Inserting the propagated data yields

\[2=t\left[2\cosh(\kappa L)+i(\kappa/k-k/\kappa)\sinh(\kappa L)\right].\]

Both asymptotic waves propagate in the same material, so their velocity factors cancel in the current ratio. Take the squared magnitude and use \(\cosh^2u=1+\sinh^2u\):

\[T=|t|^2=\left[1+\frac{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2(\kappa L)\right]^{-1}.\]

A barrier of vanishing width transmits fully. An exponential estimate at large \(\kappa L\) neglects the interface-dependent prefactor. Close to the top, the \(\sinh\) factor and its apparently singular coefficient must instead be taken to the limit together.

Worked example

Infer a width from a tunnelling measurement

At \(E=V_0/2\), \(k=\kappa\) and the result simplifies to \(T=\operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa L)\). A device with \(\kappa=2.00\) nm⁻¹ has \(T=0.250\). Thus \(\cosh(\kappa L)=2\) and \(L=\operatorname{arcosh}(2)/2.00=0.658\) nm. Doubling that width gives \(\cosh(2\kappa L)=2(2)^2-1=7\), hence \(T=1/49=0.0204\).

Second application

Approach the top from below

Let \(E\to V_0\) at fixed \(L\). With \(\sinh(\kappa L)\simeq\kappa L\) the transmission becomes \(T=[1+(kL)^2/4]^{-1}\). For \(k=3.00\) nm⁻¹ and \(L=0.500\) nm this is \(0.640\). A vanishing inverse decay length therefore does not guarantee complete transmission. Exactly at the barrier top the internal solution becomes linear and still has to match the slopes on both sides.

Build a scattering state

Send a narrow band of particles from the left towards a region where the potential changes. Far from the obstacle a stationary energy component has incoming and outgoing travelling waves. Such plane waves are not normalisable on the whole line, but their flux ratios describe what happens to a sufficiently long incident packet whose momentum distribution is narrowly centred. Assume a particle of constant mass and a real, finite, time-independent potential.

The stationary Schrödinger equation is \(-\hbar^2\phi''/(2m)+V\phi=E\phi\). Integrating it over a shrinking interval around a finite step gives continuity of \(\phi'\); a finite jump in \(\phi\) itself would introduce a more singular derivative, so \(\phi\) is continuous as well. These matching laws change for an ideal delta potential, an infinite wall or an effective mass discontinuity. Stating the model at an interface matters as much as solving the constant-potential regions.

Partly reflect above a potential step

Take \(V=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(V=V_0\) on the right. For \(E>V_0\), write \(\phi_L=e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}\) and \(\phi_R=t e^{iqx}\), with \(k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) and \(q=\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}/\hbar\). Matching gives \(1+r=t\) and \(k(1-r)=qt\). Thus \(r=(k-q)/(k+q)\) and \(t=2k/(k+q)\). A wave can reflect from a change of wavelength even if a classical particle has enough energy to proceed.

A right-moving component \(Ae^{ikx}\) carries current \(\hbar k|A|^2/m\). Consequently \(R=|r|^2\) while \(T=(q/k)|t|^2\), and direct substitution gives \(R+T=1\). For \(E=4V_0/3\), \(q=k/2\), so \(r=1/3\), \(t=4/3\), \(R=1/9\) and \(T=8/9\). Although the transmitted wavefunction amplitude is larger than the incident one, its reduced group velocity keeps the transmitted probability flux below the incident flux.

Follow an evanescent field

If \(0<E<V_0\), the right-hand wave number becomes \(i\kappa\) with \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\). The physical solution for a semi-infinite step decays as \(e^{-\kappa x}\). This single exponential times a constant has zero current, since its spatial derivative has the same complex phase. It nevertheless has finite density a short distance into the forbidden region. Matching at the interface now gives a reflected wave of unit magnitude with an energy-dependent phase.

A finite barrier differs because an allowed region begins again at \(x=L\). Both decaying and growing exponentials must be retained within the barrier, and their relative complex coefficient allows nonzero current. Match the wavefunction and derivative at both ends. For equal zero potentials on the outside the transmission probability reduces to

\[T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa L)}{4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1}.\]

The large-width dependence comes from \(\sinh(\kappa L)\sim e^{\kappa L}/2\). At fixed barrier height it is the exponential sensitivity to width that makes tunnelling useful as a distance probe. A single semi-infinite exponential fitted to density cannot by itself supply the finite-barrier prefactor.

Estimate the effect of a subnanometre change

For an electron with \(E=1.0\) eV and \(V_0=2.0\) eV, \(\kappa\simeq5.12\) nm⁻¹ and the prefactor of \(\sinh^2\) is exactly one. At width 0.50 nm, \(T=1/\cosh^2(2.56)\simeq0.0236\). At 1.00 nm, \(T\simeq1.43\times10^{-4}\). Doubling this subnanometre gap suppresses transmitted current by roughly a factor of 165 for equal incident current.

The calculation assumes a sharp rectangular barrier, one occupied incident energy and elastic coherent scattering. A measured electric tunnel current generally combines many available occupied and empty states across an energy window. A current ratio is therefore most directly compared with the single-energy exponential when barrier shape and occupation stay sufficiently similar across the distance change.

A more detailed test uses scattering from opposite sides of a real, static barrier at the same energy. Time-reversal symmetry implies equal transmission probabilities for a single channel, even when the spatial barrier profile is asymmetric. The two reflection phases can differ. If the exterior potentials differ, the transmitted amplitude ratios from the two sides need not coincide because their travelling states carry different current per unit amplitude. Normalizing the prepared and detected states to unit flux removes that ambiguity. Checking incident current against the sum of outgoing currents separately tests whether the model conserves probability.

Solve for scattering and binding at one narrow defect

A point interaction illustrates how the same Hamiltonian predicts both scattering and bound states. On an infinite line take \(V(x)=g\delta(x)\), with \(g\) having units of energy times length. This limiting model replaces a narrow feature when the wavelength of interest greatly exceeds its microscopic width. The wavefunction remains continuous, but integrating the stationary Schrödinger equation from \(-\eta\) to \(+\eta\) before taking \(\eta\to0\) gives the derivative jump

\[\psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-)=\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}\psi(0).\]

For a particle incident from the left, write \(\psi=e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx}\) on the left and \(te^{ikx}\) on the right. Continuity gives \(t=1+r\). Inserting both derivatives into the jump condition and defining \(\xi=mg/(\hbar^2 k)\) gives \(t=1/(1+i\xi)\) and \(r=-i\xi/(1+i\xi)\). The external speeds are equal on both sides, so \(T=1/(1+\xi^2)\) and \(R=\xi^2/(1+\xi^2)\). The sum is one for real \(g\). Transmission probability depends only on the magnitude of the interaction, but the complex phase of the transmitted wave distinguishes its sign.

For negative energies the exterior solution must decay as \(e^{-\kappa|x|}\). Its derivatives jump by \(-2\kappa\psi(0)\), giving \(\kappa=-mg/\hbar^2\). A normalizable state requires \(\kappa>0\), hence only an attractive defect binds. The bound energy is \(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)\). A one-dimensional narrow attractive defect can therefore support binding even when a scattering-probability measurement alone cannot distinguish its sign.

Compare two defects with the same transmission

Use electron mass and take \(|g|=0.0762\) eV nm. Since \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV nm², \(m_e|g|/\hbar^2=1.00\) nm⁻¹. An incoming wave with \(k=2.00\) nm⁻¹ has energy \(0.152\) eV and \(|\xi|=0.500\). Both signs of \(g\) transmit 0.800 and reflect 0.200 of the incident probability current. The transmission phases, however, are \(\mp\arctan(0.500)=\mp26.6^\circ\), a difference observable against a reference path.

For the attractive sign the decay length of the bound state is \(1/\kappa=1.00\) nm and its energy is \(-0.0381\) eV. The repulsive sign has no such state. At high incident \(k\) both become nearly transparent; at small \(k\) both produce nearly complete reflection for fixed nonzero strength. The order of these limits matters: taking \(g\) to zero first gives perfect transmission at any finite incident wavenumber.

An attractive defect with the inferred decay constant has normalized bound state \(\psi=\sqrt{\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa|x|}\). The probability outside distance \(b\) from the defect is \(e^{-2\kappa b}\) after integrating both tails. With \(b=1.00\) nm in the example this gives 0.135, despite each wave amplitude at that distance being \(1/e\) of its central value. A numerical calculation in a box can use this estimate to choose outer boundaries. Placing artificial zero-value boundaries only one decay length away would remove substantial tail probability and shift the apparent bound energy upward.

Increasing the box symmetrically and tracking the bound energy is more informative than merely refining the spatial mesh in the original box. Mesh convergence at fixed outer boundaries can certify a well-resolved solution of the wrong finite-domain physical problem.

Problem sheet 1 · Flux, packets and barriers

Use the probability balance of Lecture 1 and the interface matching of Lecture 2 to interpret measured particle counts.

Give two packets the same density

Two packets have wavefunctions \(f(x)e^{ikx}\) and \(f(x)e^{-ikx}\), where \(f\) is real and normalised. Compare their densities and currents. For electrons with \(k=2.0\times10^8\) m⁻¹ and local density \(4.0\times10^5\) m⁻¹, find both signed currents. Does either current by itself give the chance of finding a particle inside a chosen interval?

Differentiate the phase of each packet

Both densities are \(f^2\), while their phase gradients are \(+k\) and \(-k\). The speeds entering the flux are \(\pm\hbar k/m_e=\pm2.32\times10^4\) m/s, giving currents \(\pm9.27\times10^9\) s⁻¹ at the stated point. The probability within an interval instead comes from integrating the density. Its rate of change requires the difference of the currents at both ends. A large numerical rate per second can describe a normalised packet passing a point in a very short time.

Determine an unknown step

A beam with kinetic energy 9.0 eV approaches an upward step. Its reflected probability fraction is 0.040. Infer the ratio of wave numbers, the step energy and the transmitted amplitude for unit incident amplitude. Then recover the transmitted probability from current.

Invert the matching conditions

For an upward step below the incident energy \(r\) is positive, so \(r=0.20\). The ratio \(q/k=(1-r)/(1+r)=2/3\) gives \((E-V_0)/E=4/9\) and \(V_0=5.0\) eV. Displacement continuity gives \(t=1.20\). The current fraction is \((2/3)(1.20)^2=0.96\), closing the balance with \(R=0.04\). The step energy in this inverse calculation depends on having specified the sign of the step.

Infer a tunnelling decay length

At fixed incident energy a wide rectangular barrier transmits \(2.0\times10^{-4}\) of incident particles. Increasing its width by 0.20 nm reduces that fraction to \(2.0\times10^{-5}\). Estimate \(\kappa\), the field-amplitude decay length and \(V_0-E\) for an electron. State which part of the inference would fail for a thin barrier.

Use the ratio to eliminate the prefactor

For \(\kappa L\gg1\) the ratio is \(e^{-2\kappa\Delta L}=0.10\), so \(\kappa=\ln10/(0.40\,\mathrm{nm})=5.76\) nm⁻¹ and \(1/\kappa=0.174\) nm. With \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV·nm², \(V_0-E=0.0381(5.76)^2=1.26\) eV. In a thin barrier the complete hyperbolic-sine matching expression is needed; taking a log ratio then need not yield a width-independent slope.

Chapter 3 · Operators and the quantum harmonic oscillator

Use commutators and ladder operators to derive an energy spectrum and connect its ground state with the position–momentum uncertainty bound.

From first principles

Build the oscillator ladder from the canonical commutator

Near a stable minimum use the one-dimensional harmonic Hamiltonian \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2x^2/2\) and the canonical commutator \([x,p]=i\hbar\). Combine displacement and momentum into dimensionless operators so the two quadratic energy terms acquire the same coefficient:

\[a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\,x+\frac{ip}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}},\]
\[[a,a^\dagger]=1.\]

Multiplying \(a^\dagger a\) produces the two energy terms and a cross term proportional to the commutator. Retaining that cross term fixes the offset. Commuting the Hamiltonian with \(a\) then shows how it acts on an energy eigenstate:

\[H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+\tfrac12),\]
\[H(a|E\rangle)=(E-\hbar\omega)a|E\rangle .\]

The squared norm \(\|a|E\rangle\|^2=E/(\hbar\omega)-1/2\) cannot be negative. Repeated lowering must end at a state annihilated by \(a\), with energy \(\hbar\omega/2\). Raising creates successive normalized states and gives \(E_n=(n+1/2)\hbar\omega\). The spacing follows from the algebra; the added half quantum reflects the impossibility of independently setting both noncommuting fluctuations to zero.

Derivation and applications

Build position and momentum fluctuations from adjacent levels

A number-state oscillator has a definite energy and an uncertain position. How large are its fluctuations, and what survives when the mean position vanishes? Assume a one-dimensional harmonic trap with mass \(m\) and angular frequency \(\omega\). The ladder algebra gives \(a|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle\) and \(a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle\). Define the ground-state length \(x_0=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}\) and momentum scale \(p_0=\sqrt{m\hbar\omega/2}\).

\[x=x_0(a+a^\dagger),\]
\[p=ip_0(a^\dagger-a).\]

Both operators take \(|n\rangle\) into orthogonal neighbouring states. Their diagonal means vanish. To get a variance, compute the squared norm of the state after application. This avoids dropping the commutator when expanding the square:

\[\|x|n\rangle\|^2=x_0^2\big(\|\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle\|^2+\|\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle\|^2\big)=(2n+1)x_0^2 .\]

The relative minus sign in \(p|n\rangle\) disappears on taking the norm because the neighbours are orthogonal. It follows that

\[\Delta x=\sqrt{2n+1}\,x_0,\]
\[\Delta p=\sqrt{2n+1}\,p_0,\]
\[\Delta x\,\Delta p=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar .\]

The ground state attains the minimum uncertainty. Excited number states exceed that lower bound despite having no energy uncertainty. The same calculation gives equal kinetic and potential energy means, each \((n+1/2)\hbar\omega/2\). Each adjacent-level matrix element also specifies which spectral lines a weak displacement-dependent perturbation can drive.

Worked example

Estimate a trapped ion’s spatial spread

For \(m=6.64\times10^{-26}\) kg and \(\omega=2\pi(1.00\,\mathrm{MHz})\), the natural width is \(x_0=11.2\) nm. The \(n=2\) preparation has \(\Delta x=\sqrt5\,x_0=25.1\) nm and \(\Delta x\Delta p=2.50\hbar\). Dividing the latter by the position spread gives \(\Delta p=1.05\times10^{-26}\) kg m/s. The width would remain the same at later times in this stationary energy state.

Second application

Distinguish two preparations with the same energy mean

Compare the pure state \(|1\rangle\) with an equal incoherent mixture of \(|0\rangle\) and \(|2\rangle\). Both have mean energy \(1.50\hbar\omega\) and \(\langle x^2\rangle=3x_0^2\), because these quantities are linear in \(n\). In the mixture, the two energy outcomes differ from the mean by \(\pm\hbar\omega\), so \(\Delta E=\hbar\omega\); the pure number state has \(\Delta E=0\). An energy-resolved measurement distinguishes the preparations even when a width measurement cannot.

Quantise near a stable minimum

A molecular bond stretched slightly from equilibrium has a potential \(V(x)\simeq V(0)+kx^2/2\) if the equilibrium derivative vanishes and the curvature \(k\) is positive. For relative motion the relevant mass is the reduced mass of the two nuclei. Discard the additive constant and write \(k=m\omega^2\). Quantum mechanics assigns the Hamiltonian \(\hat H=\hat p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2\hat x^2/2\). Its two terms do not commute because position and momentum obey \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\).

For a normalised state, the expectation of an operator is \(\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle\). Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to \((\hat x-\langle x\rangle)|\psi\rangle\) and \((\hat p-\langle p\rangle)|\psi\rangle\) shows that the product of their norms is at least the magnitude of their inner product. The imaginary part of that inner product is controlled by the commutator. One obtains \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\), where the spreads are standard deviations of repeated measurements on equally prepared systems.

Factor a positive Hamiltonian

Define dimensionless operators \(a=\sqrt{m\omega/(2\hbar)}\,\hat x+i\hat p/\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}\) and \(a^\dagger\) as its adjoint. Multiplying them in the indicated order and using the commutator yields \([a,a^\dagger]=1\) and

\[\hat H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+\tfrac12).\]

For any state the expectation of \(a^\dagger a\) is \(\|a\psi\|^2\), which cannot be negative. The Hamiltonian therefore has a lower bound \(\hbar\omega/2\). Interchanging operator order while treating \(x\) and \(p\) as ordinary numbers would miss this term. It accounts for the fact that a state cannot simultaneously place the particle at the bottom of the well and give it precisely zero momentum.

Now compute the commutator of \(\hat H\) with \(a\). The identity \([AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B\) gives \([\hat H,a]=-\hbar\omega a\). Operating with \(a\) on an energy eigenstate either gives zero or produces a new eigenstate lower by \(\hbar\omega\). Successive lowering must terminate since the energy cannot become negative. The final state obeys \(a|0\rangle=0\). Raising from it gives \(E_n=(n+1/2)\hbar\omega\) and the normalisation factors \(a|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle\) and \(a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle\).

Find the shape behind the lowest level

Use \(\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\) in the ground-state condition. It reduces to \(d\psi_0/dx=-(m\omega/\hbar)x\psi_0\), whose square-integrable solution is proportional to \(\exp[-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)]\). Normalising the squared Gaussian gives position variance \(\hbar/(2m\omega)\). Its momentum variance is \(m\hbar\omega/2\). The product of standard deviations is exactly \(\hbar/2\), so the ground state saturates the general uncertainty bound.

The mean potential and kinetic energies in this state are both \(\hbar\omega/4\). Increasing the stiffness narrows the position distribution and raises the momentum spread. This provides a physical interpretation of the increasing ground energy which is independent of the ladder notation. In an excited energy eigenstate the mean position still vanishes by parity, yet its variance is larger.

Read a vibrational line and a selection rule

Take effective mass \(1.0\times10^{-26}\) kg and harmonic frequency \(f=5.0\) THz. Then \(\omega=2\pi f\), adjacent levels differ by \(hf=20.7\) meV, and the zero-point energy is 10.3 meV. The ground-state position standard deviation is \(1.30\times10^{-11}\) m. A spectral measurement of adjacent level spacings determines the curvature-to-mass ratio but cannot separate mass and stiffness without another measurement.

A weak oscillatory perturbation proportional to displacement has matrix elements of \(\hat x\propto a+a^\dagger\). It therefore connects adjacent number states, giving the dipole selection rule \(\Delta n=\pm1\) within the harmonic model and a linear coupling. If a real molecular spectrum contains overtones or nonuniform spacings, anharmonicity or a nonlinear dependence of dipole moment on displacement can supply the missing physics.

It is possible to recover the zero-point energy without solving a differential equation. A centred packet of spatial variance \(\sigma_x^2\) has potential energy \(m\omega^2\sigma_x^2/2\). The uncertainty relation bounds its kinetic energy below by \(\hbar^2/(8m\sigma_x^2)\) when mean momentum vanishes. Minimizing their sum over width gives \(\sigma_x^2=\hbar/(2m\omega)\) and an energy bound \(\hbar\omega/2\). A Gaussian saturates the uncertainty inequality and reaches the bound. Making a classical oscillator sit identically at rest would require both energy contributions to vanish simultaneously, which the commutator forbids.

Displace an oscillator ground state

A harmonic oscillator can move almost like a classical oscillator while retaining its smallest quantum position uncertainty. Prepare the ground state and suddenly shift the centre of its trapping potential by distance \(d\), without changing frequency. In the coordinates of the new well the old Gaussian starts displaced by \(-d\) with mean momentum zero. Define a coherent state as an eigenstate of the lowering operator, \(\hat a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle\). Its displacement and momentum are encoded by \(\alpha=\sqrt{m\omega/(2\hbar)}\langle x\rangle+i\langle p\rangle/\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}\).

To find its energy distribution expand \(|\alpha\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n\rangle\). Applying the lowering operator and equating the coefficient of \(|n\rangle\) gives \(\sqrt{n+1}c_{n+1}=\alpha c_n\). Recursion and normalization give \(c_n=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\alpha^n/\sqrt{n!}\) up to a common phase. The energy index follows a Poisson distribution,

\[P_n=e^{-\bar n}\frac{\bar n^n}{n!},\qquad \bar n=|\alpha|^2,\qquad \langle E\rangle=\hbar\omega(\bar n+\tfrac12).\]

Free evolution multiplies each coefficient by \(e^{-in\omega t}\) apart from a common factor, turning \(\alpha\) into \(\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\). Position and momentum expectations execute the corresponding classical orbit, and the packet stays a fixed-width Gaussian because adjacent energy levels have exactly uniform spacing. A potential with appreciably unequal spacings instead allows these components to lose their phase alignment over time.

Estimate the number of excited quanta

Let an effective mass \(m=1.00\times10^{-26}\) kg oscillate at \(\omega=1.00\times10^{13}\) rad/s. Its ground-state position standard deviation is \(x_{\rm zpf}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}=2.30\times10^{-11}\) m. Shift the well by \(d=2x_{\rm zpf}=46.0\) pm. In the new coordinate \(\alpha=-d/(2x_{\rm zpf})=-1\), giving \(\bar n=1\). Hence ground and first excited energies each occur with probability \(e^{-1}=0.368\), and the second excited energy occurs with probability \(e^{-1}/2=0.184\).

The energy added above the ground state is \(\hbar\omega=6.58\) meV, identical to the classical change \(m\omega^2d^2/2\) immediately following the shift. Equality of mean energies does not make a single measurement deterministic: the energy standard deviation is also \(\hbar\omega\) in this preparation. Moving the minimum quasistatically instead of suddenly can leave the system close to its instantaneous ground state and add essentially no excitation, provided the motion of the trap is slow on the oscillation timescale.

Number statistics provide a stringent check on the sudden-shift model. A coherent state with mean occupation one has probability \(1-2.5/e=0.0803\) of occupying level three or higher. Failure to observe those outcomes in a small sample would be unsurprising, but persistent absence in a well-resolved large sample would contradict the ideal state. Two displaced packets with opposite signs of \(\alpha\) have the same number distribution and different later position records. Thus energy spectroscopy checks the magnitude of a displacement while a timed position measurement also checks its phase around the oscillator orbit.

A time sequence with the same mean added energy can still have different number statistics if the trap frequency changes along with its centre. A frequency jump changes the natural Gaussian width and can prepare a squeezed state. Observing a breathing position variance in addition to centre motion would reveal this extra change in the restoring curvature.

Chapter 4 · Angular momentum and hydrogenic atoms

Use rotational symmetry to organise atomic states and derive the hydrogen ground-state length and energy scales from the radial equation.

From first principles

Derive the radial equation from three-dimensional motion

For a reduced mass \(\mu\) in a central potential \(V(r)\), rotational symmetry makes the angular-momentum operators compatible with the Hamiltonian. Write a stationary state as \(\psi=R(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\) with \(L^2Y_{\ell m}=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m}\). In spherical coordinates the kinetic operator separates radial and angular changes:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu r^2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_r)+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.\]

Divide the eigenvalue equation by the angular factor. To work with a one-dimensional probability measure, substitute \(u=rR\). Then \(\int|R|^2r^2dr=\int|u|^2dr\) and direct differentiation gives \((r^2R')'=r u''\). The resulting equation is

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''+\left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u=Eu .\]

The extra positive term at nonzero \(\ell\) is the angular kinetic energy, which inhibits penetration near the origin. For ordinary Coulomb bound states regularity imposes \(u(0)=0\) and normalizability requires decay at infinity. The radial half-line and its boundary conditions are part of the eigenvalue problem.

Derivation and applications

What fraction of a ground-state atom lies outside a chosen radius?

The hydrogenic ground-state amplitude is largest at the nucleus, yet a measurement of radius is most likely to land away from it. To derive the distribution use a single electron bound to a point nucleus of charge \(+Ze\), neglecting relativistic and finite-size corrections. Let \(\mu\) be the reduced electron–nucleus mass, and write \(\kappa=Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\). For a spherically symmetric stationary state try \(\psi=A e^{-r/a}\). Inserting its radial derivatives into the three-dimensional Schrödinger equation gives

\[\frac{H\psi}{\psi}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2}+\frac1r\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{\mu a}-\kappa\right).\]

The right side must be independent of radius. Cancel the inverse-radius term to fix \(a=\hbar^2/(\mu\kappa)\). Normalization integrates probability over spherical shells, each of volume \(4\pi r^2dr\):

\[1=4\pi |A|^2\int_0^\infty r^2 e^{-2r/a}\,dr=\pi a^3|A|^2,\]
\[P_r(r)=\frac{4r^2}{a^3}e^{-2r/a}.\]

Thus \(P_r(r)\,dr\) is the probability for a radius in an interval. Differentiating it places its maximum at \(r=a\). To count the atoms found outside radius \(R\), substitute \(z=2r/a\) and integrate the quadratic times an exponential twice by parts:

\[\Pr(r>R)=\tfrac12\int_{2R/a}^\infty z^2 e^{-z}\,dz=e^{-2R/a}\!\left(1+\frac{2R}{a}+\frac{2R^2}{a^2}\right).\]

At \(R=0\) the result is one, as required. The tail remains nonzero for every finite radius; a quote of the “atomic radius” must therefore specify the observable or convention used.

Worked example

Quantify the extent of ordinary hydrogen

Take \(a=52.9\) pm. A radius \(R=105.8\) pm equals \(2a\). Evaluating the tail gives \(13e^{-4}=0.238\). Nearly one quarter of radial outcomes fall beyond twice the most probable radius. Using only the relative density \(e^{-4}=0.0183\) at that point would omit both shell volume and integration over larger radii.

Second application

Compare two ions through the same physical sphere

For a one-electron helium ion and a heavy nucleus approximation, doubling \(Z\) halves \(a\) to \(26.45\) pm. At a physical radius of \(52.9\) pm, the hydrogen tail is \(5e^{-2}=0.677\), while the ion tail is \(13e^{-4}=0.238\). The same fractional containment would be recovered by comparing radii in proportion to \(1/Z\). This comparison assumes an isolated ion with no second electron to screen the nuclear field.

Identify the symmetry before choosing coordinates

An isolated electron and nucleus interacting through a central potential can be separated into centre-of-mass motion and relative motion. Let \(\mu\) be the reduced mass of the two particles. The relative Hamiltonian is \(\hat p^2/(2\mu)+V(r)\) with \(r\) their separation. Simultaneously rotating position and momentum does not change the energy. Angular momentum provides the observables that express this symmetry and organise the many possible three-dimensional wavefunctions.

Orbital angular momentum is \(\hat{\mathbf L}=\hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf p}\). Its components obey \([L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z\) and cyclic permutations. The total magnitude squared \(\hat L^2\) commutes with each component. We can therefore specify eigenvalues of energy, \(L^2\) and one projection, conventionally \(L_z\), together. The respective angular eigenvalues are \(\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2\) and \(m\hbar\), with integer \(\ell\ge0\) and \(m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\). Spherical harmonics \(Y_{\ell m}\) represent those angular states, with probability density proportional to their squared magnitude.

Separate angular and radial motion

Write \(\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\). Volume in spherical coordinates contains a factor \(r^2\), so normalising the angular function leaves radial probability \(r^2|R(r)|^2dr\). Defining \(u(r)=rR(r)\) both simplifies the radial normalisation and removes the first derivative from the radial equation:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''+\left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u=Eu.\]

For ordinary bound atomic states regularity at the origin gives \(u(0)=0\) and normalisability gives decay at large radius. The angular momentum term penalises small radius when \(\ell>0\). Although it resembles the effective centrifugal energy of classical central motion, here its coefficient comes from the angular eigenvalue rather than an adjustable classical angular momentum.

Derive the lowest Coulomb state

For hydrogen set \(V=-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)=-C/r\) with \(C>0\). For \(\ell=0\) try \(R=Ae^{-r/a}\), hence \(u=Ar e^{-r/a}\). The second derivative satisfies \(u''/u=1/a^2-2/(ar)\). Substitute this ratio in the radial equation. The terms proportional to \(1/r\) cancel only when \(a=\hbar^2/(\mu C)\), leaving \(E_1=-\hbar^2/(2\mu a^2)\). This nodeless solution is the lowest bound state. Choosing another exponential length would leave a residual \(1/r\) term, giving a useful check on the derivation.

For hydrogen with a heavy proton approximation, \(a\simeq0.0529\) nm and \(E_1\simeq-13.6\) eV. The three-dimensional density of the ground state is largest at the origin. The probability per unit radial distance instead contains \(r^2\) and peaks at \(r=a\). A claim about the “most likely radius” must specify which measure is in use. Measuring a shell of equal thickness at a larger radius includes more volume.

Distinguish spectra from angular distributions

Solving the regular bound-state radial equation for general \(\ell\) gives \(E_n=-\mu C^2/(2\hbar^2n^2)\) with \(n=1,2,\ldots\) and \(0\le\ell\le n-1\). States with different \(m\) are degenerate by rotational symmetry. The additional degeneracy among different \(\ell\) at fixed \(n\) is a special property of the Coulomb potential. Corrections such as fine structure and external fields can separate lines that the present model puts at one energy.

The transition from \(n=3\) to \(n=2\) releases \(13.6(1/2^2-1/3^2)=1.89\) eV, giving wavelength \(hc/\Delta E\simeq656\) nm. The energy gap predicts the wavelength but not the strength of every possible transition. Electric dipole emission involves an angular matrix element of position, so it connects orbital angular momenta differing by one. An excited state can have the right energy gap while a particular dipole transition remains forbidden by symmetry. This is why both radial energies and angular labels belong in a spectroscopy record.

The reduced mass supplies a further spectroscopic test. Replacing a proton by a deuteron approximately doubles the nuclear mass while preserving its charge. Since \(\mu=m_e/[1+m_e/M]\), the electronic reduced mass increases by a relative amount close to \(m_e/(2m_p)=0.000272\). All gross transition frequencies rise by the same fraction, while wavelengths fall. A protium line at 656 nm should have a deuterium counterpart roughly 0.179 nm to its short-wavelength side. Resolving the two lines requires sufficient spectral resolution and a source with both isotopes. Small additional shifts reveal corrections beyond the point-nucleus, nonrelativistic model.

Couple orbital and spin angular momenta

The angular labels of a central potential organize an electron's spatial and spin states separately. A weak spin–orbit interaction can split levels without breaking overall rotational symmetry. Model it within a fixed radial and orbital manifold by \(H_{\rm so}=\lambda\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar^2\), with \(\lambda\) an energy representing the radial coupling average. The model assumes coupling small relative to separations from other radial or orbital terms. Let \(\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S\). The identity \(J^2=L^2+S^2+2\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\) expresses the interaction entirely through simultaneous angular eigenvalues,

\[\Delta E_j=\frac{\lambda}{2}[j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-s(s+1)] .\]

The allowed total values run from \(|\ell-s|\) to \(\ell+s\) in unit steps. Each \(j\) has \(2j+1\) possible magnetic projections. The interaction is a rotational scalar, so all projections of the same \(j\) remain degenerate here. The original labels \(m_\ell\) and \(m_s\) taken separately no longer label eigenstates in general, since terms \(L_+S_-\) and \(L_-S_+\) exchange projection while keeping their sum fixed.

For \(\ell=1\) and electron spin \(s=1/2\), the product space has three orbital choices and two spin choices, six states in all. The allowed \(j=3/2\) quartet shifts upward by \(\lambda/2\) and the \(j=1/2\) doublet downward by \(\lambda\) for positive coupling. Weighted over all six states the shifts sum to zero, giving a useful check on energy bookkeeping.

Reconstruct a split spectral term

Suppose the two energies in such a \(p\) term are separated by 4.50 meV and the fourfold component is the upper one. The model gives splitting \(3\lambda/2\), hence \(\lambda=3.00\) meV. The quartet lies 1.50 meV above the unsplit term and the doublet 3.00 meV below it. If the observed quartet energy is 2.00150 eV on a common reference, the inferred unsplit centre is 2.00000 eV and the doublet lies at 1.99700 eV. The corresponding difference in optical frequencies for transitions from a common lower level is \(4.50\,{\rm meV}/h=1.09\) THz.

Relative line intensities are generally not fixed solely by the quartet-to-doublet multiplicity, because lower-state populations and dipole matrix elements enter. An external magnetic field can additionally split different \(m_j\) values and eventually mix the angular couplings. In the special limit \(\ell=0\), there is only \(j=s\) and the shift vanishes identically. Observing a doublet from an apparently orbital-singlet term would therefore point to another interaction or a mistaken assignment.

A measurement of angular momentum projection can distinguish the coupling assignments independently of transition wavelengths. The completely aligned state with \(m_\ell=1\) and \(m_s=1/2\) has total projection \(3/2\) and can belong only to the \(j=3/2\) manifold. For smaller total projection a given \(j\) state generally contains more than one orbital–spin product. The small-field magnetic response then reflects the combined angular content rather than the separate labels of one arbitrarily chosen component. Preparing or identifying a stretched state provides a particularly direct check on which member of a fine-structure doublet is the quartet.

Chapter 5 · Perturbations, degeneracy and level repulsion

Calculate an energy correction, identify where nondegenerate perturbation theory fails, and resolve an avoided crossing by diagonalising a two-state Hamiltonian.

From first principles

Resolve an energy correction into virtual couplings

Take \(H=H_0+\lambda V\) with a known orthonormal spectrum \(H_0|n\rangle=E_n^0|n\rangle\). Assume the target level is isolated and the coupling weak relative to the relevant energy gaps. Write its perturbed state in intermediate normalization as \(|n\rangle+\lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle+\cdots\) with \(\langle n|n^{(1)}\rangle=0\). Insert this and the energy expansion in the eigenvalue equation. Terms at first order give

\[(H_0-E_n^0)|n^{(1)}\rangle=(E_n^{(1)}-V)|n\rangle .\]

Projection onto \(n\) gives \(E_n^{(1)}=V_{nn}\). Projection onto each \(m\ne n\) gives the amplitude admixed from that other state. At second order the projection back onto \(n\) therefore gives

\[\langle m|n^{(1)}\rangle=\frac{V_{mn}}{E_n^0-E_m^0},\]
\[E_n^{(2)}=\langle n|V|n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^0-E_m^0}.\]

Higher unperturbed levels contribute negatively to the ground-state energy, while lower levels shift an excited state upward. A vanishing denominator signals that nearby states must be diagonalized together before expanding the effect of more distant states.

Derivation and applications

Read a mixed eigenstate from the slope of its energy

A level diagram around an avoided crossing contains more information than the minimum gap. Its slopes can reveal how much of each underlying basis state is present. Consider two orthonormal states with Hamiltonian \(H=(\Delta/2)\sigma_z+g\sigma_x\), real constant coupling \(g\), and a tunable signed energy difference \(\Delta\). We call the state with \(\sigma_z=+1\) the positive basis state. For a normalized, nondegenerate eigenstate, differentiate \(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\) with respect to \(\Delta\). Left multiplication by \(\langle n|\) gives

\[\langle n|\partial_\Delta H|n\rangle+\langle n|H|\partial_\Delta n\rangle=\partial_\Delta E_n+E_n\langle n|\partial_\Delta n\rangle .\]

The last terms cancel by Hermiticity. Since \(\partial_\Delta H=\sigma_z/2\), the measured slope equals half the population difference. If \(P_+\) is the probability of the positive basis state in this eigenstate, normalization gives

\[\partial_\Delta E_n=\tfrac12\langle\sigma_z\rangle=P_+-\tfrac12 .\]

The characteristic equation \(E^2=\Delta^2/4+g^2\) supplies the eigenenergies. For the lower branch, evaluate its derivative explicitly:

\[E_-=-\tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2},\]
\[P_+^{(-)}=\tfrac12\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}}\right).\]

At zero detuning both basis states have equal weight. At large positive detuning the positive basis state belongs mainly to the upper branch. These are stationary-state probabilities at the chosen setting. Preparing that state by moving the setting additionally requires a sweep slow enough compared with the gap.

Worked example

Estimate the minority component of a spectroscopic state

For \(g=0.150\) meV and \(\Delta=0.400\) meV, the measured branch separation is \(0.500\) meV. The lower slope is \(-0.400\) meV per meV, so \(P_+^{(-)}=0.100\). A projective measurement resolving the original basis should yield about 100 positive outcomes in 1000 independent preparations of the lower state.

Second application

Infer a coupling without reaching the crossing

At a second setting a fitted lower-branch slope is \(-0.300\) while the energy gap is \(0.800\) meV. The measured slope requires \(\Delta/\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}=0.600\), giving \(\Delta=0.480\) meV. Squaring the gap then yields \(g=\tfrac12\sqrt{0.800^2-0.480^2}=0.320\) meV. The predicted minimum gap is \(0.640\) meV; a separate zero-detuning scan checks the inference.

Predict a weak response from a known spectrum

A tunable control can move two energy levels towards one another. Far apart, weak coupling shifts them only slightly. Near coincidence the observed spectrum can change substantially even if the same coupling is small on every other energy scale. Begin with a Hamiltonian \(\hat H_0\) with known orthonormal eigenstates \(|n\rangle\) and energies \(E_n^0\). A weak time-independent perturbation \(\lambda\hat V\) changes both energies and states, with \(\lambda\) a bookkeeping parameter eventually set to one.

Insert expansions \(E_n=E_n^0+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\ldots\) and \(|\psi_n\rangle=|n\rangle+\lambda|\delta n\rangle+\ldots\) into the eigenvalue equation. At first order \(\hat H_0|\delta n\rangle+\hat V|n\rangle=E_n^0|\delta n\rangle+E_n^{(1)}|n\rangle\). Projection onto \(\langle n|\) cancels the two terms containing the unperturbed Hamiltonian and gives \(E_n^{(1)}=\langle n|\hat V|n\rangle\). This shift is a weighted average of a spatial perturbation in the original eigenstate.

Follow the change in the state

Projection onto a different eigenstate \(\langle m|\) instead gives \(\langle m|\delta n\rangle=V_{mn}/(E_n^0-E_m^0)\). The dimensionless ratio of the coupling matrix element to the level separation controls the amount of mixing. Substitution of this corrected state at the next order gives

\[E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^0-E_m^0}.\]

A nondegenerate ground state has only higher-energy neighbours and therefore a nonpositive second-order shift. A vanishing diagonal element can make this second-order shift the leading effect. For example a symmetric bound state under a perturbation odd in position has zero first-order average. That fact alone does not establish that the state or its energy is unchanged.

As the energy separation approaches zero, the small-ratio condition fails. Apparent divergences signal that choosing one unperturbed vector as a unique starting state has become inappropriate. Within a degenerate subspace, diagonalise the perturbation first. Couplings to well-separated states outside the subspace can still be treated by ordinary perturbation theory if their matrix elements remain small compared with the external gaps.

Solve a pair exactly

Use two uncoupled levels \(-\Delta/2\) and \(+\Delta/2\), referenced to their midpoint, and take the off-diagonal coupling \(v\) real by a phase choice. The characteristic equation of the two-by-two Hamiltonian is \(E^2-(\Delta/2)^2-v^2=0\). Thus \(E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+v^2}\). At zero detuning the gap is \(2|v|\). The equal-weight combinations of the two basis states diagonalise the Hamiltonian, even when the coupling would have been negligible far from coincidence.

Expanding the positive root for \(|v|\ll|\Delta|\) gives \(|\Delta|/2+v^2/|\Delta|\) to leading correction; the lower level shifts down by the same amount. Their sum equals the original trace for every detuning. A measured change in the midpoint as well as the gap could therefore require a common diagonal shift in addition to this off-diagonal coupling.

Read coupling from an avoided crossing

Let \(\Delta=10.0\) meV and \(v=1.0\) meV. The unperturbed energies are \(\pm5.0\) meV and the exact energies are \(\pm5.099\) meV. The second-order prediction of a 0.100 meV shift is accurate to about 0.001 meV. At \(\Delta=1.0\) meV the exact energies are \(\pm1.118\) meV and the perturbative correction \(v^2/\Delta=1.0\) meV is already larger than the original positive energy. Diagonalising the pair remains well conditioned there.

A spectrometer measuring the smallest separation in a detuning sweep would infer \(|v|=1.0\) meV from a minimum gap of 2.0 meV. Level-dependent transition strengths can supply a separate test: as the eigenstates exchange their dominant basis character across the avoided crossing, a probe coupling mainly to one basis state will redistribute intensity between the two observed lines.

An alternative experimental strategy fits states well away from the mixing region first. There the slopes with respect to the control parameter reveal the approximate unperturbed levels. Extrapolating those lines locates the crossing that would occur if the coupling vanished. Measuring the two actual levels near that point then separately determines the energy origin and the magnitude of the off-diagonal interaction. A phase change of either basis state reverses the sign of a real coupling, leaving both energies unchanged. Its sign relative to another amplitude requires an interference measurement.

Sweep through an avoided crossing

A spectrum measured at a fixed control setting reveals a gap. To follow a state while turning the control, we must also know how quickly its instantaneous eigenvector rotates. Consider smooth \(H(t)\) and two nondegenerate eigenstates. Differentiate \(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\) and take the inner product with another eigenstate \(m\). The term containing \(\dot E_n\) vanishes by orthogonality, giving

\[\langle m|\dot n\rangle=\frac{\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle}{E_n-E_m}.\]

When the state is expanded in the instantaneous basis with its dynamical phases, this coupling drives the amplitude of \(m\). The relative phase oscillates at angular frequency \(|E_n-E_m|/\hbar\). A slowly changing coupling then averages out over many oscillations. The scale \(\eta=\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|/|E_m-E_n|^2\) compares basis rotation with that phase rate. For a smooth isolated sweep, keeping it small throughout is a useful adiabaticity test. The final probability also depends on the full drive and preparation.

Take \(H=\Delta(t)\sigma_z/2+g\sigma_x\) with fixed \(g>0\) and detuning increased linearly through zero. The gap is \(\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\). At the centre the eigenstates point along the \(x\) direction in spin space, so the off-diagonal matrix element of \(\dot H=(\dot\Delta/2)\sigma_z\) has magnitude \(|\dot\Delta|/2\). This is also where the sweep is most demanding:

\[\eta(0)=\frac{\hbar|\dot\Delta|}{8g^2}.\]

With \(g=0.100\,\mathrm{meV}\), \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{meV\,ps}\) and \(|\dot\Delta|=0.00100\,\mathrm{meV/ps}\), the minimum splitting is \(0.200\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\eta(0)=0.00823\). Starting sufficiently far away in the lower eigenstate, a slow sweep transfers the occupation to the opposite uncoupled basis state as the lower eigenvector exchanges character. Multiplying the rate by twenty gives \(\eta(0)=0.165\) and a weaker separation of time scales. At \(g=0\) the crossing is exact and this finite-gap argument breaks down.

Apply degenerate perturbation theory to hydrogen in an electric field

A nondegenerate atomic parity eigenstate has zero permanent electric dipole along \(z\). It is tempting to infer that a weak uniform field always shifts an energy only at second order. For hydrogen in the nonrelativistic Coulomb model, however, the \(2s\) and \(2p\) levels are degenerate. A field \(\mathcal E\) along \(z\) adds \(V=e\mathcal E z\) for an electron, with positive \(e\) denoting the elementary charge. The perturbation is odd under inversion. Diagonal parity-basis elements vanish, but an off-diagonal matrix element can survive between opposite parities.

Choose \(|2s\rangle\) and \(|2p,m=0\rangle\), since \(z=r\cos\theta\) preserves magnetic projection. With conventional real hydrogen phases, the angular integral \(\langle Y_{00}|\cos\theta|Y_{10}\rangle=1/\sqrt3\). Writing \(\rho=r/a_0\), the normalized radial functions are proportional to \((2-\rho)e^{-\rho/2}\) and \(\rho e^{-\rho/2}\). Including radial volume and the extra \(r\) from \(z\) gives

\[\int_0^\infty R_{20}R_{21}r^3dr=\frac{a_0}{8\sqrt3}\int_0^\infty(2-\rho)\rho^4 e^{-\rho}d\rho=-3\sqrt3\,a_0 .\]

Thus the off-diagonal position element is \(-3a_0\). Its sign depends on orbital phases, while the magnitude and energy shifts do not. The perturbation matrix in this degenerate subspace has zero diagonal and equal off-diagonal entries \(-3e\mathcal E a_0\). Its eigenvalues are \(\pm3e\mathcal E a_0\) with orthogonal equal superpositions of the two original orbitals. The remaining \(m=\pm1\) states have no first-order partners here and remain unshifted in this model.

Assess a resolvable Stark splitting

Let \(\mathcal E=2.00\times10^6\) V/m and \(a_0=5.29\times10^{-11}\) m. The field-induced voltage across one Bohr radius is \(1.058\times10^{-4}\) V, giving each mixed level an energy shift magnitude \(0.317\) meV. Their separation is \(0.635\) meV or \(0.154\) THz. This is far smaller than the electronvolt-scale separation from other principal shells, supporting restriction to the \(n=2\) manifold. The absolute laboratory field, line widths and finer internal splittings still set the domain where the ideal doublet picture is appropriate.

If the two unperturbed orbitals are split by a small residual gap \(\delta\), the new separation is \(\sqrt{\delta^2+4(3e\mathcal E a_0)^2}\). At extremely weak field the change from \(|\delta|\) is then quadratic, crossing to approximately linear splitting once the off-diagonal coupling dominates. Varying the field over this crossover can distinguish truly degenerate first-order splitting from a barely resolved avoided crossing without assigning a permanent dipole to either original parity orbital.

The slopes of the split energies also have an observable interpretation. By differentiating a normalized eigenvalue with respect to the applied field, the Hellmann–Feynman relation gives \(dE/d\mathcal E=e\langle z\rangle\) in the same sign convention. The two mixed states therefore have opposite mean electron displacements of magnitude \(3a_0\) in the degenerate first-order model. Their charge distributions are oriented along or against the applied field although neither original parity state had an orientation. Following a field reversal requires care about which eigenbranch or prepared state is being tracked when a much smaller unresolved zero-field splitting is present.

Measuring slopes on both sides of field reversal tests the branch assignment independently of any common instrumental drift in the energy reference.

Chapter 6 · Composite states and spin correlations

Predict joint and conditional spin measurements for two particles and distinguish an entangled state from a mixture with the same one-particle statistics.

From first principles

Build a joint spin probability from a two-particle amplitude

Two distinguishable spin-one-half measurements have a product basis \(|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle\). In a singlet the normalized state is \((|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Measure the first spin along \(z\) and the second along an axis tilted by \(\theta\) in the \(xz\) plane. A positive eigenstate along the new axis is

\[|+\theta\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|\uparrow\rangle+\sin(\theta/2)|\downarrow\rangle .\]

The first positive outcome selects the singlet's first product term. Its remaining second-particle amplitude along the tilted axis is the overlap with \(|\downarrow\rangle\). The Born rule for the joint projector now gives

\[P(+z,+\theta)=|\langle\uparrow,+\theta|\psi^-\rangle|^2=\tfrac12\sin^2(\theta/2)=\tfrac14(1-\cos\theta).\]

For the other tilted outcome the squared overlap is \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\). Adding the two joint outcomes gives \(P(+z)=1/2\). Dividing the joint result by this marginal yields the conditional probability for the partner. The same single-particle count occurs at all analyser angles even though the coincidence pattern changes.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the two-spin coincidence law from projectors

A pair prepared in a spin singlet has no preferred axis, yet coincidence counts change when one analyser rotates. Start with two spin-one-half particles in \(|\psi^-\rangle=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Ideal spin measurements along unit vectors \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\) have recorded outcomes \(s,t=\pm1\), in units of \(\hbar/2\). Neglect detector losses. Since the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) are \(\pm1\), its projector onto result \(s\) is

\[\Pi_s(\mathbf a)=\frac{I+s\,\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2},\]
\[P(s,t)=\langle\psi^-|\Pi_s(\mathbf a)\otimes\Pi_t(\mathbf b)|\psi^-\rangle .\]

Tracing over either particle gives \(I/2\), so terms linear in one spin have zero expectation. To evaluate the remaining product, choose both axes initially along \(z\). The singlet basis components always have opposite outcomes, giving \(\langle\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z\rangle=-1\). Rotational invariance of the singlet requires its two-index correlation matrix to be proportional to the identity. The \(zz\) result fixes the coefficient:

\[\langle\sigma_i\otimes\sigma_j\rangle=-\delta_{ij},\]
\[\langle(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\otimes(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\rangle=-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b .\]

Expand the product of projectors and substitute this correlation. For angle \(\theta\) between the analysers the full coincidence probabilities and their sum over the second analyser become

\[P(s,t)=\tfrac14(1-st\cos\theta),\]
\[\sum_tP(s,t)=\tfrac12 .\]

Parallel axes forbid equal outcomes. Perpendicular axes give four equally likely outcomes despite the pure preparation. Local counts alone reveal neither the relative angle nor the phase coherence of the joint state.

Worked example

Predict a coincidence histogram

With \(1200\) independently prepared pairs and a \(60.0^\circ\) angle, the equal-outcome cells each have probability \((1-0.5)/4=0.125\) and the opposite-outcome cells each have probability \(0.375\). The expected counts, ordered as \(++,+-,-+,--\), are \(150,450,450,150\). Given a positive result for the first analyser, the probability of a negative result at the second is \(450/(150+450)=0.750\).

Second application

Separate a singlet from a classical preparation

A source instead produces an equal incoherent mixture of the two product states anti-aligned along \(z\). Both sources are perfectly anti-correlated along that axis. Align both analysers along \(x\). Each product preparation now gives independent, equally probable outcomes, so \(P(\mathrm{equal})=0.500\). The singlet predicts zero equal outcomes. For 200 ideal pairs the predictions are 100 versus zero equal coincidences, while either local detector still reports each sign about half of the time.

Build the state space of a pair

A spin-½ measurement along a chosen axis yields two possible outcomes. Label its dimensionless results \(+1\) and \(-1\), corresponding to spin angular momenta \(\pm\hbar/2\). For two separately addressable particles A and B, a joint state belongs to the tensor product of their two spin spaces. In the \(z\) basis we need four product vectors: both up, A up and B down, A down and B up, or both down. A general normalised state contains four complex amplitudes whose squared magnitudes sum to one.

An uncorrelated pure preparation can be written \(|\psi_A\rangle\otimes|\psi_B\rangle\). Its product-structure forces a relation among the four amplitudes. For example the state \((|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\) has two nonzero amplitudes for opposite spins and zero amplitude for equal spins. No product of two individual pure states has that pattern. This entangled state is the spin singlet. Spin labels here refer to the internal states at the two measurement locations.

Project along the same and different axes

Measure both particles along \(z\). The two opposite outcomes have probability one half each, and the product of their dimensionless outcomes always equals \(-1\). Alice's result alone is random. Conditional on her obtaining \(+1\), the corresponding outcome for Bob along the same axis is definitely \(-1\). A classical mixture of the two opposite \(z\) product states with equal weights would give exactly the same statistics in this one measurement arrangement.

To distinguish the preparations, rotate Bob’s analyser through angle \(\theta\) in the \(xz\) plane while Alice continues to measure \(z\). A spin initially up along \(z\) has probability \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\) of being up along Bob’s axis and \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\) of being down along it. For the singlet, conditional on Alice's \(+1\) outcome, Bob starts in the opposite state. His probabilities are therefore interchanged. Summing over Alice's equally likely outcomes gives the joint correlation \(E(z,b)=-\cos\theta\). Its single-party marginals remain one half.

Find the coherence that an ensemble can hide

Rotational invariance of the singlet gives \(E(a,b)=-\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\) for any chosen axes. In particular if both measure \(x\), their results are again perfectly anticorrelated. Verify this directly by expressing \(|\uparrow_z\rangle=(|\uparrow_x\rangle+|\downarrow_x\rangle)/\sqrt2\) and \(|\downarrow_z\rangle=(|\uparrow_x\rangle-|\downarrow_x\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The two equal-\(x\) amplitudes cancel in the superposition.

In the classical mixture of the two opposite \(z\) states those amplitudes cannot cancel across different ensemble members. Each particle measured along \(x\) is individually random and the two \(x\) outcomes are independent conditional on either preparation. The mixture consequently has \(E(x,x)=0\). Measuring repeated pairs in two bases reveals an off-diagonal coherence invisible to either single-party measurement. Local outcome statistics alone stay random regardless of the distant analyser setting.

Predict counts and a Bell combination

At \(\theta=60°\) the singlet gives \(E=-1/2\). The probability of opposite outcomes is \((1-E)/2=0.75\). For 400 ideal detected pairs one therefore expects about 300 opposite and 100 equal outcomes, with ordinary statistical fluctuations. Rotating both analysers together preserves these fractions because only their relative angle matters for a singlet.

The distinction can be sharpened with four settings. In one plane choose Alice’s angles 0° and 90° and Bob’s 45° and −45°. Form \(S=E(a,b)+E(a,b')+E(a',b)-E(a',b')\). The three first correlations are \(-1/\sqrt2\) and the last is \(+1/\sqrt2\), giving \(|S|=2\sqrt2\). A model assigning local predetermined binary outcomes to all four settings gives for each emitted pair a sum \(A(B+B')+A'(B-B')\) of magnitude two; averaging bounds \(|S|\) by two if the sampled ensemble is independent of the choices of settings. A laboratory interpretation must also account for detection statistics and the sampling protocol. The calculation concerns joint correlations and grants no control over the individual random outcomes.

If one observer discards the other observer’s records, the local spin outcomes remain equally likely for each measurement axis. Sorting the same data into subsets according to the partner’s outcomes reveals the correlations. This sorting requires comparing the two records through an ordinary communication channel.

Classify a pure bipartite state by its Schmidt weights

The spin singlet has maximally mixed individual spins even though its joint state is pure. A partially entangled pair allows a continuous test of that distinction. Any pure state of two finite systems can be written as \(\sum_{ij}A_{ij}|i\rangle|j\rangle\). Tracing out the second system gives \(\rho_A=AA^\dagger\). Singular-value decomposition rotates the two local bases independently and writes the same state as \(\sum_k\sqrt{\lambda_k}|u_k\rangle|v_k\rangle\), where \(\lambda_k\ge0\) and their sum is one. The reduced states on both sides have the same nonzero eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\).

A pure product state has just one nonzero Schmidt weight. Two or more weights imply entanglement, because separate rotations of the local bases cannot remove singular values. The reduced purity is \(\sum_k\lambda_k^2\), and the entanglement entropy for a pure pair is \(-\sum_k\lambda_k\ln\lambda_k\). A nonzero reduced entropy describes uncertainty in local observations. It can occur jointly with zero entropy for the complete pure state. If the joint preparation is already mixed, reduced entropy alone is insufficient to quantify its entanglement.

Choose a measurement basis for the partner

Prepare \(|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.90}|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.10}|11\rangle\). Its local populations are 0.90 and 0.10, giving reduced purity \(0.90^2+0.10^2=0.820\) and entropy \(-0.90\ln0.90-0.10\ln0.10=0.325\) in natural-log units. Measuring the second spin in the computational basis prepares the first in \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) with those respective probabilities. Each branch has a definite computational outcome.

Now measure the partner in \(|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Both outcomes have probability one half. Conditional on the result, the first spin is in \(\sqrt{0.90}|0\rangle\pm\sqrt{0.10}|1\rangle\), already normalized. A subsequent plus-basis measurement on the first spin gives plus with probability \([1\pm2\sqrt{0.90\times0.10}]/2\), namely 0.800 or 0.200. Without receiving the partner's result the first observer averages these two values and sees 0.500, exactly as predicted by the unchanged reduced matrix.

As the smaller Schmidt weight tends to zero the state approaches a product and the two conditional plus probabilities coalesce. Equal weights give maximal local entropy \(\ln2\). A classical mixture of \(|00\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\) with the same 0.90 and 0.10 weights produces identical computational-basis statistics but gives 0.500 in both conditional plus measurements. The pair of rotated analysers therefore tests coherence between the alternatives rather than merely confirming their occupation probabilities.

Experiments verify the conditional prediction by collecting both detector outcomes and matching them trial by trial. If one sorts first-spin data without correctly identifying the partner from the same pair, the two 0.800 and 0.200 conditional probabilities both tend back toward the unconditional value of one half. This loss of contrast can thus result from timing or pairing errors even when each local detector has excellent single-spin fidelity. Repeating the computational-basis correlation measurement supplies an independent pairing check before interpreting the more delicate rotated-basis signal as a property of the prepared wavefunction.

Chapter 7 · Estimating a ground-state energy

Construct a variational upper bound, optimize the length of a trial orbital and evaluate what the bound says about its shape.

From first principles

Why minimizing a trial energy gives an upper bound

Take a self-adjoint Hamiltonian bounded below, with ground energy \(E_0\). Choose a nonzero trial state obeying the physical boundary conditions and having finite energy expectation. For a discrete spectrum, expand it in normalized eigenstates \(|n\rangle\). Orthonormality removes cross terms when the Hamiltonian acts on this expansion:

\[|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n\rangle,\]
\[\frac{\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}=\frac{\sum_n |c_n|^2E_n}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}.\]

Subtract \(E_0\). Each term now contains a nonnegative weight times a nonnegative excitation energy, giving

\[E[\psi]-E_0=\frac{\sum_n|c_n|^2(E_n-E_0)}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}\ge0 .\]

A continuum contribution gives the same inequality with an integral. Optimizing a trial length or mixing coefficient decreases the smallest accessible value of this quotient. Equality requires all weight to lie in the ground eigenspace; a low estimated energy alone does not specify which local features of the wave function are accurate. Checking a width, contact density or other independent observable tests the adequacy of the chosen trial family.

Derivation and applications

Optimize a superposition when the trial functions overlap

A single variational length can improve the size of a trial orbital, but it cannot reproduce every possible shape. A second trial function introduces a mixing coefficient. Frequently two convenient functions are individually normalized yet overlap. Let the linearly independent functions be \(\phi_1,\phi_2\), let \(H_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|H|\phi_j\rangle\), and define the overlap matrix \(S_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle\). In a trial superposition \(\psi=\sum_i c_i\phi_i\), normalization depends on the cross term:

\[\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=c^\dagger Sc,\]
\[E[c]=\frac{c^\dagger Hc}{c^\dagger Sc}.\]

To find stationary values, minimize \(c^\dagger Hc-E(c^\dagger Sc-1)\) with respect to the complex conjugates \(c_i^*\). Differentiation of each bilinear form removes a single row factor, giving two homogeneous equations for the same coefficients:

\[\sum_j(H_{ij}-ES_{ij})c_j=0,\]
\[\det(H-ES)=0.\]

Because \(S\) is positive definite, the quotient is well defined for every nonzero trial vector. Its smallest root is an upper bound to the exact ground energy. Adding an independent trial function cannot raise this optimum, since setting its coefficient to zero recovers the original trial state. For two equivalent sites with \(H_{11}=H_{22}=h\), \(H_{12}=H_{21}=v\) and real \(S_{12}=s\), add and subtract the rows to obtain

\[E_{\rm sym}=(h+v)/(1+s),\]
\[E_{\rm anti}=(h-v)/(1-s).\]

The signs label symmetry, not a universal ordering. The denominator records the actual norm of each combination. As \(|s|\to1\) one combination becomes nearly redundant; estimating its energy then becomes sensitive to small matrix-element errors.

Worked example

Estimate two trial energies from overlapping orbitals

Take \(h=3.00\) eV, \(v=-0.500\) eV and \(s=0.250\). The symmetric combination yields \(2.50/1.25=2.00\) eV; the antisymmetric combination gives \(3.50/0.750=4.67\) eV. The first improves the single-orbital bound by \(1.00\) eV. Normalization of its coefficient vector gives \(c_1=c_2=1/\sqrt{2(1+s)}=0.632\), even though the basis functions are individually normalized.

Second application

Change the energy reference to test an implementation

Shift the Hamiltonian by \(C I\) with \(C=2.00\) eV. In the overlapping basis this changes the matrix to \(H+CS\), because the matrix elements of the identity are \(S_{ij}\). The new diagonal is \(5.00\) eV and the off-diagonal is zero. The two roots become \(5.00/1.25=4.00\) and \(5.00/0.750=6.67\) eV. Both shifted by exactly \(2.00\) eV; leaving the off-diagonal unchanged would fail this energy-reference test.

A calculation that can be wrong in one direction

An exact time-independent Schrödinger equation can be difficult to solve even when the Hamiltonian is known. The variational principle allows a trial state to make a precise claim about the lowest energy. Suppose the Hamiltonian is self-adjoint and bounded below. Expand any normalized trial state of finite energy in its energy eigenbasis. For a discrete spectrum, with continuum terms replaced by integrals when needed,

\[\langle H\rangle_\psi=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n\ge E_0\sum_n|c_n|^2=E_0.\]

Lowering an expectation value inside the trial family improves the upper bound. Trial functions must obey the physical boundaries and give finite relevant energy integrals. A smooth function confined to the wrong symmetry sector can converge to the lowest state of that sector while missing the true ground state. To estimate an excited level one needs additional orthogonality or a suitable subspace principle.

Choose a length and compute two energies

As an example estimate a hydrogen-like electron bound to a fixed proton by \(V=-\kappa/r\), with \(\kappa=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\). The exact ground state has a cusp at the origin; deliberately choose the smooth, normalized Gaussian \(\psi=(\pi a^2)^{-3/4}\exp[-r^2/(2a^2)]\). The parameter \(a\) permits expansion and contraction but cannot repair the cusp or the large-radius shape. It therefore tests how much energy a simple length-scale argument can recover.

For a decaying state integration by parts expresses kinetic energy as \(\hbar^2\int|\nabla\psi|^2/(2m)\). Since \(\nabla\psi=-\mathbf r\psi/a^2\) and \(\langle r^2\rangle=3a^2/2\), the result is \(3\hbar^2/(4ma^2)\). For potential energy the radial measure \(4\pi r^2dr\) cancels one power of \(1/r\), leaving \(\langle 1/r\rangle=2/(\sqrt\pi a)\). Thus

\[E(a)=\frac{3\hbar^2}{4ma^2}-\frac{2\kappa}{\sqrt\pi a}.\]

As \(a\) becomes small, kinetic localization costs energy faster than attraction can reduce it. At very large \(a\) the state becomes weakly bound. These limiting trends guarantee an intermediate minimum.

Optimize and assess the answer

With \(a_0=\hbar^2/(m\kappa)\), differentiation gives \(a_*=(3\sqrt\pi/4)a_0=1.329a_0\) or \(0.0703\) nm. The minimized energy is \(-4m\kappa^2/(3\pi\hbar^2)\), about \(-11.55\) eV compared with the fixed-proton result \(-13.61\) eV. It lies above the exact energy by about 2.06 eV, as required. At the optimum the two terms satisfy \(2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle\). That scaling relation follows from extremization over a length and can hold even with an imperfect wavefunction.

Energy is stationary near an optimum, so a good energy estimate alone cannot certify other observables. For example, sampling the contact probability \(|\psi(0)|^2\) is sensitive to the cusp missing here. One can enlarge the basis using several Gaussian widths and diagonalize the Hamiltonian in their span. The old optimum remains an allowed choice, so the improved lowest eigenvalue can only fall or stay equal.

To see why the energy can be forgiving, resolve a normalized trial state as \(\sqrt{1-\eta^2}|0\rangle+\eta|1\rangle\) using two exact eigenstates. Its energy error is \(\eta^2(E_1-E_0)\), quadratic in the admixture. An observable with a nonzero off-diagonal matrix element between these levels can already change at first order in \(\eta\). Comparing several independent observables therefore tests more of the trial wavefunction than repeating the energy comparison.

Vary an effective nuclear charge for two electrons

Helium presents a variational problem in which the main missing physics can itself be identified. Fix a nucleus of charge \(+Ze\) and use atomic units, where \(\hbar=m_e=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=1\), length is measured in Bohr radii and energy in hartrees. Ignoring relativity, use \(H=\sum_{i=1}^2[-\nabla_i^2/2-Z/r_i]+1/r_{12}\). Try two identical normalized exponential orbitals \(\phi=(\zeta^3/\pi)^{1/2}e^{-\zeta r}\) with a spin singlet so that the complete electronic state is antisymmetric. The trial parameter \(\zeta\) lets both orbitals expand in response to repulsion.

For either electron the kinetic expectation is \(\zeta^2/2\) and \(\langle1/r\rangle=\zeta\). The two one-body terms together give \(\zeta^2-2Z\zeta\). To compute the interaction, spherical averaging the Coulomb kernel for the isotropic densities gives \(1/\max(r_1,r_2)\), the shell-potential result. Split the radial domain at \(r_1=r_2\) and double the part with \(r_1>r_2\). With radial probability \(P(r)=4\zeta^3r^2e^{-2\zeta r}\) this gives

\[\left\langle\frac1{r_{12}}\right\rangle=2\int_0^\infty\frac{P(r_1)}{r_1}\,dr_1\int_0^{r_1}P(r_2)\,dr_2=\frac{5\zeta}{8}.\]

For the last integration, set \(x=2\zeta r_1\). The inner integral is \(1-e^{-x}(1+x+x^2/2)\), so the remaining integral involves only exponential moments. The repulsion is positive and linear in \(\zeta\): squeezing both clouds together increases typical electrostatic repulsion while the faster quadratic kinetic cost eventually prevents collapse.

Estimate the first ionization energy of helium

For \(Z=2\), minimize \(E(\zeta)=\zeta^2-4\zeta+5\zeta/8\). Setting its derivative to zero gives \(\zeta_*=2-5/16=1.6875\) and \(E_{\rm trial}=-\zeta_*^2=-2.84766\) hartrees, or \(-77.49\) eV relative to a bare nucleus and two free electrons. An unscreened choice \(\zeta=2\) gives \(-2.750\) hartrees. Allowing the cloud to expand lowers the trial energy by about \(2.66\) eV.

The one remaining electron in He\(^+\) is a hydrogenic ion with exact fixed-nucleus energy \(-Z^2/2=-2.000\) hartrees in this model. The trial estimate for removing one electron is therefore \(0.84766\) hartree, or \(23.1\) eV. Because the final one-electron energy is exact while the neutral trial energy is an upper bound, this difference is a lower bound on the model's ionization energy. The trial state allows average screening but leaves the electrons statistically independent in position. Multiplying the product orbital by a factor depending explicitly on \(r_{12}\) can favour configurations where the electrons avoid one another and lower the bound further.

The effective size can be compared directly with the unscreened hydrogenic model. For one exponential orbital \(\langle r\rangle=3a_0/(2\zeta)\), so the optimum mean radius for either electron is \(0.889a_0\), compared with \(0.750a_0\) at \(\zeta=2\). The increased radius and reduced energy come from the same variation, but they have different error guarantees. Variational minimization bounds energy, while no analogous inequality requires this mean radius to be either above or below its exact value. Comparing the inferred radius with scattering or density data therefore probes trial-wavefunction quality beyond the energy test.

Diagnose the missing electron correlation locally

The optimum product state gives a useful upper bound yet treats the positions of the two electrons as statistically independent. A short-distance diagnostic follows by asking what happens when the electrons approach each other while remaining away from the nucleus. Introduce relative coordinate \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2\) and a centre coordinate. In atomic units the relative part of their kinetic Hamiltonian has the term \(-\nabla_r^2\), since the reduced mass is one half. The Coulomb repulsion is \(1/r\). For a singlet with nonzero amplitude at coincidence, expand its spherical average near \(r=0\) as \(\psi(r)=\psi(0)(1+cr+\cdots)\).

The radial Laplacian of the linear term is \(2c\psi(0)/r\). If the Schrödinger equation is to admit a finite eigenvalue without an uncancelled \(1/r\) term, the relative kinetic and repulsive singularities must cancel:

\[-2c\,\psi(0)/r+\psi(0)/r=0,\qquad c=\tfrac12 .\]

Thus the spherical average of the amplitude initially rises away from pair coincidence. The positive slope quantifies a correlation hole. A simple product of smooth one-electron orbitals has no such first-order slope at an ordinary coincident position. Optimizing its single length scale can reproduce average screening without satisfying this local condition.

For a separation of \(0.10a_0\), the leading cusp term predicts a relative amplitude roughly five percent above its coincident value at fixed electronic centre, before quadratic corrections. Squared probability density therefore rises by about ten percent to first order. This compares local densities, not the count per radial shell, which also contains the shell area factor. A trial factor \(1+r_{12}/(2a_0)\) supplies the required initial slope while leaving a separate orbital scale available for long-distance screening. Its potential to improve the energy is motivated by a local equation, rather than simply by the fact that adding another variational parameter cannot worsen a minimum.

Choose a variational length in a linear trap

Take \(H=-\tfrac12d^2/dx^2+|x|\) and a normalised trial amplitude proportional to \(\exp[-x^2/(2s^2)]\). Integration of the gradient and potential terms gives \(1/(4s^2)\) and \(s/\sqrt\pi\). Use their sum as a ground-state upper bound.

Chapter 8 · Semiclassical quantization

Use a local wavelength to construct the quantization rule for a smooth well and recognize where that construction breaks down.

From first principles

Build the semiclassical phase and the turning-point condition

In a classically allowed interval define \(p(x)=\sqrt{2m[E-V(x)]}>0\) and seek a one-dimensional stationary wave \(\psi=A(x)e^{iS(x)/\hbar}\). Assume \(p\) varies little over a local wavelength. Substitute into the Schrödinger equation and separate terms by powers of \(\hbar\). The leading real and next imaginary balances give

\[(S')^2=p^2,\]
\[2A'S'+AS''=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^2 S'=\text{constant}.\]

Thus the phase increment is \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\) and the travelling-wave amplitude varies as \(p^{-1/2}\). This approximation fails where \(p=0\). Near each smooth simple turning point the potential is locally linear; the decaying exterior Airy solution matches an interior sine with phase shift \(\pi/4\). The waves entering from the left and right must describe the same standing wave. Combining the two end phases gives

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\]
\[n=0,1,\ldots .\]

The offset comes from the two soft turning points. A hard wall imposes its own node condition and changes the quantization offset. The construction becomes more reliable when the state samples many local wavelengths inside the well.

Derivation and applications

Read a quantum level spacing from a classical clock

The semiclassical quantization rule uses the area swept in phase space. Its change with energy also carries information about time. Consider a smooth one-dimensional binding potential with two simple turning points \(x_\pm(E)\), mass \(m\), and many accessible levels. Write the half-cycle action using the positive momentum branch \(p(x,E)=\sqrt{2m[E-V(x)]}\):

\[I(E)=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x,E)\,dx,\]
\[I(E_n)\simeq\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12).\]

The endpoints move with energy, but their boundary contributions when differentiating the integral contain \(p(x_\pm)=0\). Inside the well \(\partial_Ep=m/p\). Its divergence near a simple turning point behaves as an inverse square root and is integrable. Using \(p/m=\dot x\) on the forward leg now gives

\[\frac{dI}{dE}=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{m}{p(x,E)}\,dx=\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{dx}{\dot x}=\frac{T_{\rm cl}(E)}{2}.\]

Two neighbouring quantized actions differ by \(\pi\hbar\). If the classical period varies little over a level spacing, linearizing the action about one level leads to

\[E_{n+1}-E_n\simeq\frac{2\pi\hbar}{T_{\rm cl}(E_n)}=\frac{h}{T_{\rm cl}(E_n)} .\]

Thus a densely spaced part of the spectrum is associated with a long classical excursion. For a harmonic well the period is constant and the result is exactly the known spacing; near a separatrix the rapidly varying period makes a one-step linearization less reliable. Only the period of a complete return to the starting phase-space point belongs in this relation.

Worked example

Estimate a frequency from adjacent lines

Consider an approximately harmonic vibrational well with nearby levels separated by \(2.50\) meV. With \(h=4.136\) meV ps, the inferred classical period is \(1.65\) ps and the ordinary oscillation frequency is \(0.604\) THz. A transit from the left turning point to the right should take about \(0.827\) ps. Equating this one-way transit with the period would overestimate the energy spacing by two.

Second application

Predict the scaling change for a quartic well

In \(V(x)=\lambda x^4\) the turning point is \(x_t=(E/\lambda)^{1/4}\). Substituting \(x=x_tu\) into \(T_{\rm cl}=2\int_{-x_t}^{x_t}m\,dx/p\) leaves an energy-independent integral and gives \(T_{\rm cl}\propto E^{-1/4}\). Therefore local level spacing grows as \(E^{1/4}\). At sixteen times the energy a spacing of \(0.180\) meV should become about \(0.360\) meV, assuming both regions contain enough levels for semiclassical counting. The quadratic trap would show no comparable growth.

A locally oscillating solution

Consider one-dimensional bound motion of mass \(m\) in a smoothly varying potential. In allowed regions the stationary equation is \(\psi''+p(x)^2\psi/\hbar^2=0\) with \(p=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}\). If the potential changes little over a local wavelength, a small interval resembles a free-particle region. Accumulating its local phase produces the action integral \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\). Yet a wave with changing local momentum must also change amplitude in order to conserve current.

Substitute \(\psi=A(x)\exp[iS(x)/\hbar]\). The leading real terms give \(S'^2=p^2\). The next imaginary terms give \(2A'S'+AS''=0\), or \((A^2S')'=0\). Therefore each travelling contribution has amplitude proportional to \(1/\sqrt p\). Equivalently a classical particle dwells longer where it is slow, and the smoothed semiclassical probability mirrors this dwell time. The neglected derivative terms are small away from turning points when \(\hbar|p'|\ll p^2\).

Match through a turning point

At an ordinary turning point \(V(x_t)=E\) with nonzero slope, the local wavelength diverges and the previous criterion fails. Linearizing \(V\) there gives the Airy equation. Its decaying forbidden-side solution continues to an oscillatory allowed-side standing wave with a phase offset of \(\pi/4\). Matching decaying exterior tails on both sides of a well bounded by two such turning points imposes

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots .\]

The offset encodes the two smooth reflections. An infinite hard wall instead imposes a node directly and has a different reflection phase. Applying this rule unchanged to a hard box would count its levels incorrectly. For slowly varying barriers the forbidden action also controls exponential attenuation, but the two-turning-point integral above determines the energies of a classically allowed well.

A trap with measurable spacings

For \(V=m\omega^2x^2/2\) let \(x_t=\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}\). Put \(x=x_t\sin u\) in the action integral. The integrand becomes \((2E/\omega)\cos^2u\,du\) between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), yielding \(\pi E/\omega\). The rule gives \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)\), exceptionally exact even for the oscillator’s lowest levels.

For a mechanical trap at frequency 100 kHz, \(E_4=4.5hf=2.98\times10^{-28}\) J, equivalent to \(21.6\) μK on division by \(k_B\). The spacing is \(hf\) or \(4.80\) μK. These temperatures express energy units; no equilibrium ensemble was assumed. As a transfer test consider \(V=gx^4\). Rescaling \(x=(E/g)^{1/4}y\) makes the action proportional to \(E^{3/4}\). Quantization gives \(E_n\propto(n+1/2)^{4/3}\), so successive spacings grow with excitation. At sufficiently high level number the action changes slowly over a wavelength, making the predicted trend more trustworthy than a low-level numerical prefactor.

An independent check on the energy spacing follows by differentiating the action with respect to energy. The integrand vanishes at the turning points, so their moving endpoints add no first-order term. Inside the integral \(\partial p/\partial E=m/p=1/v\). Consequently the derivative of the one-way action is the time for the classical particle to travel from one turning point to the other. Adjacent quantum levels at high excitation then have separation approximately \(h\) divided by the full classical period. Measuring a variation of level spacing with excitation is therefore also probing the dependence of the classical orbit period on energy.

Estimate tunnelling between two bound wells

A nearly symmetric double well can have pairs of quantum states concentrated mainly in classically separate regions. For one such pair take approximately orthogonal localized states \(|L\rangle,|R\rangle\) with equal isolated energy \(E_0\) and a small real tunnelling matrix element \(-J\). The sign of either localized basis state can be chosen so \(J>0\). The two-level Hamiltonian has symmetric and antisymmetric eigenstates with energies \(E_0-J\) and \(E_0+J\), respectively. A local preparation \(|L\rangle\) is a superposition of both and acquires a relative phase at rate \(2J/\hbar\).

On evolution the probability to be in the other well is \(P_R(t)=\sin^2(Jt/\hbar)\). The first complete transfer occurs at \(t_{\rm tr}=\pi\hbar/(2J)=h/(2\Delta E)\) with \(\Delta E=2J\). In a high smooth barrier the localized wave tails overlap weakly. A semiclassical estimate gives \(J\simeq J_0 e^{-\mathcal S/\hbar}\), with \(\mathcal S=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m[V(x)-E_0]}\,dx\) between inner turning points. This is an amplitude action. The through-barrier transmission probability of a scattering experiment instead contains twice that exponent. Confusing the two quantities gives an incorrect predicted change in the oscillation time.

Predict how a gate changes a tunnelling period

Suppose a symmetric electron double well shows a spectroscopic splitting \(\Delta E=40.0\) μeV. Its predicted first full transfer time is \(h/(2\Delta E)=51.7\) ps. Use the measured splitting to eliminate the unknown semiclassical prefactor. A gate modification adds a nearly flat forbidden segment of width \(0.300\) nm where \(V-E_0=0.150\) eV and otherwise changes the wells negligibly. Using \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.0381\) eV nm² gives decay wavenumber \(\kappa=\sqrt{0.150/0.0381}=1.98\) nm⁻¹. The added dimensionless action is 0.595.

If the prefactor and individual well frequencies remain similar, the new splitting is \(40.0e^{-0.595}=22.1\) μeV, and transfer time rises to \(93.6\) ps. Doubling the width change multiplies the splitting by the square of the original ratio, not by twice the original ratio. These estimates concern coherent dynamics before energy relaxation or phase noise destroys the relative phase.

A gate can also shift the two well bottoms differently. With detuning \(\delta\) the splitting is \(\sqrt{\delta^2+4J^2}\), while maximum transfer falls to \(4J^2/(\delta^2+4J^2)\). A larger measured splitting can therefore accompany poorer population transfer if symmetry is lost. Comparing spectroscopy and time-resolved populations distinguishes a changed tunnel coupling from an unintended potential offset.

The small tunnel coupling also sets a time criterion for disorder: a fluctuating left–right offset comparable with the splitting can produce substantial changes in both transfer amplitude and oscillation frequency. A static distribution of offsets across repeated preparations can wash out ensemble oscillations even when each individual preparation evolves coherently. Repeating the experiment after compensating the measured detuning separates this ensemble averaging from intrinsic energy relaxation. The semiclassical action comparison is most reliable when the two localized levels remain nearly resonant throughout the modification and no higher intrawell level enters the same small energy window.

Independent measurements of the individual well minima supply a practical detuning calibration.

Problem sheet 4 · Energy bounds and semiclassical actions

Construct a bound from a normalized trial state, compare two reflection conditions and infer a potential’s scaling from level spacings.

A polynomial trial state

In an infinite well \(0<x<L\), take \(\psi=Nx(L-x)\). Determine \(N\), compute its energy expectation and compare with the exact lowest level. Use \(\int_0^L x^2(L-x)^2dx=L^5/30\). What does the comparison test?

Show the variational calculation

Normalization gives \(N=\sqrt{30/L^5}\). Since the trial state vanishes at the walls, the kinetic expectation is \(\hbar^2\int|\psi'|^2dx/(2m)\). The derivative is \(N(L-2x)\), whose squared integral is \(N^2L^3/3=10/L^2\). Thus the bound is \(10\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\) and the exact ground energy \(\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\). The bound is \(10/\pi^2-1=1.32\%\) high. Correct endpoints and normalization yield a valid test even though the polynomial has a different shape from a sine.

One wall and one smooth turning point

Consider a particle with an infinite wall at \(x=0\) and \(V(x)=Fx\) for positive \(x\). Evaluate the one-way action to the turning point. Using \(\int_0^{x_t}p\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+3/4)\) derive the energy law and the ratio \(E_1/E_0\) in this approximation.

Show the mixed-boundary quantization

The turning point is \(x_t=E/F\). The integral is \(\int_0^{E/F}\sqrt{2m(E-Fx)}\,dx=2\sqrt{2m}E^{3/2}/(3F)\). Hence \(E_n=[3\pi\hbar F(n+3/4)/(2\sqrt{2m})]^{2/3}\), giving \(E_1/E_0=(7/3)^{2/3}=1.76\). The hard wall fixes a node while the smooth turning point contributes one Airy phase. The low-level ratio is approximate because semiclassical assumptions fail near the turning point and there are few oscillations in the allowed region.

Identify the stiffness of a potential

A symmetric smooth well behaves as \(V=g|x|^s\) and high-level energies scale approximately as \(n^{1.50}\). Derive the scaling for general \(s\), identify the exponent and predict how energies at fixed high \(n\) change if \(g\) is multiplied by four.

Show the action rescaling

Set \(x=(E/g)^{1/s}y\). The action becomes a constant times \(\sqrt{m}\,g^{-1/s}E^{1/2+1/s}\). Quantization gives \(E_n\propto g^{2/(s+2)}(n+1/2)^{2s/(s+2)}\) at fixed mass. Solving \(2s/(s+2)=1.50\) gives \(s=6\). A fourfold increase in \(g\) multiplies energies by \(4^{1/4}=\sqrt2\). This inference uses the spatial region sampled by the measured high levels; the central part of an actual well may follow a different law.

Chapter 9 · Transitions under a timed drive

Derive the line shape of a weak finite-duration drive and distinguish Fourier width from a system’s intrinsic lifetime.

From first principles

How the time profile of a drive becomes a transition amplitude

Assume an initially occupied eigenstate \(|i\rangle\) of a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H_0\), perturbed by a weak time-dependent operator \(V(t)\). Expand the state in its unperturbed energy eigenvectors and factor out their known phases. Inserting the expansion into the time-dependent Schrödinger equation cancels the unperturbed energy terms:

\[|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle,\]
\[i\hbar\dot c_f=\sum_n V_{fn}(t)e^{i(E_f-E_n)t/\hbar}c_n .\]

At first order the right side uses the initial amplitudes \(c_i=1,c_{n\ne i}=0\). Integrating from zero through pulse duration \(T\) gives

\[c_f^{(1)}(T)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^T V_{fi}(t)e^{i\omega_{fi}t}dt,\]
\[\omega_{fi}=(E_f-E_i)/\hbar .\]

A square nearly resonant sinusoidal drive has a slowly varying integrand proportional to \(e^{i\Delta t}\) plus a rapidly oscillating component. Keeping the slow term produces amplitude proportional to \(T\,\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta T/2)\) and probability proportional to its square. The approximation requires small population transfer; a longer resonant drive eventually requires coupled state amplitudes.

Derivation and applications

Why a strong resonant pulse stops increasing the transition probability

Perturbation theory predicts a probability growing as pulse duration squared on resonance. To follow the subsequent return of population, keep both amplitudes coupled. Consider an isolated pair of levels driven by a monochromatic field with a nearly resonant frequency. Use the rotating-wave approximation, justified when the detuning and coupling are both small compared with the carrier frequency. Let \(\Delta\) be the angular detuning and \(\Omega\) the resonant angular Rabi frequency. During a square pulse the rotating-frame Hamiltonian, up to an identity term, is

\[H_R=\tfrac{\hbar}{2}(\Omega\sigma_x+\Delta\sigma_z),\]
\[H_R^2=\tfrac{\hbar^2}{4}(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)I .\]

The square follows because the two Pauli matrices anticommute. In the exponential propagator the even powers of \(H_R\) therefore multiply the identity and the odd powers multiply \(H_R\). Sum the two Taylor series with \(\Omega_e=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\):

\[U(t)=I\cos(\Omega_e t/2)-i\,\frac{\Omega\sigma_x+\Delta\sigma_z}{\Omega_e}\sin(\Omega_e t/2).\]

Starting in the lower bare state, only \(\sigma_x\) contributes to the upper-state amplitude. Its squared magnitude is

\[P_e(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\,t}{2}\right).\]

On resonance a pulse area \(\Omega t=\pi\) transfers the entire population; twice that duration returns it to the lower state. At short times the leading term is \(\Omega^2t^2/4\), recovering perturbation theory. Detuning both speeds up the oscillation and reduces its attainable maximum. These two signatures help distinguish mistuning from a change in drive strength.

Worked example

Design a population inversion pulse

If \(\Omega/(2\pi)=2.00\) MHz, the resonant inversion time is \(\pi/\Omega=0.250\) microseconds. A \(0.125\) microsecond pulse gives \(P_e=0.500\). Holding the drive on for \(0.500\) microseconds instead gives zero in the ideal model. The source supplies and subsequently receives energy during a reversible oscillation.

Second application

Infer the mistuning from an incomplete oscillation

Suppose the same drive produces maxima of \(0.640\) and full population oscillations repeat every \(0.400\) microseconds. The period gives \(\Omega_e/(2\pi)=2.50\) MHz. The maximum gives \(\Omega/\Omega_e=\sqrt{0.640}=0.800\), recovering the calibrated \(2.00\) MHz drive. The detuning magnitude is \(\sqrt{2.50^2-2.00^2}=1.50\) MHz in ordinary-frequency units. A population trace alone leaves its sign undetermined.

How a time-dependent perturbation transfers population

For a time-independent Hamiltonian \(H_0\) the energy components of a state accumulate phases without changing populations. Let a weak applied perturbation \(V(t)\) act for a known interval. Prepare the unperturbed eigenstate \(|i\rangle\) at \(t=0\) and write \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle\). The interaction-picture amplitudes isolate changes beyond the free phases. Substitution into Schrödinger’s equation and projection onto \(|f\rangle\) give

\[i\hbar\dot c_f=\sum_n V_{fn}(t)e^{i\omega_{fn}t}c_n,\qquad \omega_{fn}=(E_f-E_n)/\hbar .\]

At first order retain \(c_i=1\) on the right-hand side. For \(f\ne i\) the resulting amplitude is the Fourier component of the perturbation at the transition frequency, \(c_f(T)=-(i/\hbar)\int_0^T V_{fi}(t)e^{i\omega_{fi}t}\,dt\). An energetically resonant drive still fails to populate a state if the coupling matrix element vanishes by symmetry. The operator describing the drive is as important as its frequency.

A rectangular pulse

Let \(V_{fi}(t)=V_{fi}\cos(\omega t)\) for \(0<t<T\), with an upward transition so \(\omega_{fi}>0\). Splitting the cosine into exponentials gives frequencies \(\omega_{fi}-\omega\) and \(\omega_{fi}+\omega\). Near resonance, for a pulse lasting many carrier cycles, the rapidly oscillating sum-frequency term contributes little. With detuning \(\Delta=\omega_{fi}-\omega\) the remaining integral gives

\[P_{i\to f}\simeq \frac{|V_{fi}|^2T^2}{4\hbar^2}\left[\frac{\sin(\Delta T/2)}{\Delta T/2}\right]^2 .\]

At exact resonance all contributions build with the same phase, so for a weak short pulse probability grows quadratically in time. The first zeros occur at \(|\Delta|=2\pi/T\), or ordinary-frequency detuning \(1/T\). The line shape measures coherent addition over the finite observation time. It can be broad even when the isolated energy levels have negligible natural width.

Calibrate a pulse

Take a 2.00 ms rectangular pulse with \(|V_{fi}|/\hbar=200\) rad/s. The resonant transition probability is \((200\times0.002/2)^2=0.0400\). At 250 Hz detuning the sinc argument is \(\pi/2\), leaving probability \(0.0162\). The first zeros lie 500 Hz on either side. Doubling pulse duration at the same coupling predicts four times the peak probability and half the first-zero spacing within the weak-drive approximation. A measurement can test both the area dependence and frequency scaling.

Once the predicted population is no longer small, depletion of the initial level removes the first-order approximation. For an isolated resonantly driven two-state system the rotating-wave solution is \(\sin^2(|V_{fi}|T/(2\hbar))\), describing coherent population transfer and return. Lifetime decay or field fluctuations introduce still other envelopes. Varying pulse duration at fixed drive strength distinguishes finite-time broadening from linewidths that persist as the pulse is lengthened.

The pulse envelope provides an additional independent control. Replacing the sudden rectangular turn-on and turn-off with smooth edges reduces spectral sidelobes because the Fourier transform of the smoother envelope falls faster away from resonance. Peak transfer at resonance, in the weak-drive regime, depends on the time integral of the drive envelope. Two pulses of different shapes can therefore have the same low-power resonant transition probability yet excite a nearby unwanted level with different probabilities. Specifying duration alone would be insufficient to reproduce such a measurement.

From a finite pulse to Fermi’s golden rule

An isolated resonant final state initially collects amplitude proportional to time. A dense set of final states can instead produce a nearly constant total transition rate. To see the change, let a weak drive connect one initial state to levels around energy \(E_\star=E_i+\hbar\omega\). Denote the matrix element of the resonant Fourier component by \(M\). For a cosine drive it contains the factor of one half. First-order time integration over a square pulse gives a phase times \((Mt/\hbar)\,\mathrm{sinc}[(E-E_\star)t/(2\hbar)]\), where \(\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\sin(u)/u\).

The different final states are orthogonal, so their probabilities add. If the density \(\rho(E)\) counts accessible final states per unit energy, and \(\rho\) and \(M\) vary slowly over the line width,

\[P(t)\simeq \frac{|M|^2t^2}{\hbar^2}\int \rho(E)\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{(E-E_\star)t}{2\hbar}\right)\,dE.\]

Set \(u=(E-E_\star)t/(2\hbar)\). Away from a band edge one can extend the integral across the line and use \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{sinc}^2u\,du=\pi\). One power of time cancels against the narrowing energy interval, leaving \(P(t)\simeq\Gamma t\) with \(\Gamma=2\pi |M|^2\rho(E_\star)/\hbar\). This intermediate-time result assumes many final levels within the line and little depletion of the initial state. For a small isolated system the long-time dynamics can resolve individual levels again.

As a numerical example, use \(M=0.500\,\mathrm{\mu eV}=0.000500\,\mathrm{meV}\), \(\rho=1000\,\mathrm{meV^{-1}}\) and \(\hbar=0.6582\,\mathrm{meV\,ps}\). The rate is \(0.00239\,\mathrm{ps^{-1}}\), or a transition probability of \(0.0239\) after \(10.0\,\mathrm{ps}\). The first zero lies \(2\pi\hbar/t=0.414\,\mathrm{meV}\) from resonance, spanning about 414 level spacings on each side for a locally uniform density. The small transition probability and dense spectrum are distinct checks on the approximation.

Separate phase accumulation from the drive pulses

A single spectroscopy pulse ties spectral resolution to its own duration. The Ramsey method separates two short coherent pulses by an interval of free evolution. Work with two isolated states \(|g\rangle,|e\rangle\) in the rotating frame of a stable reference. Approximate each pulse as short compared with the free interval and strong enough to rotate the Bloch vector by \(\pi/2\) about the same transverse axis. Ignore relaxation and pulse detuning during the rotations. Starting in \(|g\rangle\), the first pulse prepares \((|g\rangle-i|e\rangle)/\sqrt2\) for the chosen pulse phase.

Over free interval \(T\) the excited component gains relative phase \(e^{-i\Delta T}\), with \(\Delta=\omega_{eg}-\omega_{\rm ref}\). The second pulse mixes both paths to \(|e\rangle\). Using the rotation matrix \(R_x(\pi/2)=(I-i\sigma_x)/\sqrt2\) gives

\[c_e=-\frac i2(1+e^{-i\Delta T}),\qquad P_e=\frac{1+\cos(\Delta T)}2 .\]

A controllable phase on the second pulse shifts these fringes; on exact resonance two aligned pulses complete a \(\pi\) rotation and transfer all population. For a statistical mixture with the same half populations after pulse one, the two contributions to the final probability have no mutual phase and give one half regardless of delay. Thus the separated-pulse signal measures phase retained between pulses, even when no energy transfer takes place during most of the sequence.

Measure a small reference-frequency error

Take a free interval of 2.00 ms. Adjacent maxima occur at ordinary detunings separated by \(1/T=500\) Hz. The first minimum on either side occurs at 250 Hz. The separation of the two half-height points immediately surrounding resonance is \(1/(2T)=250\) Hz. These three figures refer to different linewidth or spacing conventions and should be identified before comparing experimental reports.

Set the second pulse phase so that the calibrated signal near zero detuning is \(P_e=[1+\sin(2\pi\delta\nu T)]/2\). A measured \(P_e=0.600\) gives \(\delta\nu=\arcsin(0.200)/(2\pi T)=16.0\) Hz on the branch nearest the reference. This setting is more sensitive to small signed frequency errors than the top of a fringe, where the first derivative vanishes. A first scan or a shorter free interval is needed to identify which repeated fringe is being tracked.

If the transverse coherence decays during free evolution, observed fringe amplitude is multiplied by the coherence factor, often approximated by \(e^{-T/T_2}\). Extending the delay narrows fringe spacing but eventually reduces contrast. Measuring two phase settings in quadrature can separate an actual phase shift from a loss of visibility due to decoherence or pulse-area errors.

The frequency sensitivity per detected particle can also be estimated. At half population an ensemble of \(N\) independent measurements has standard error \(1/(2\sqrt N)\) in the estimated excited fraction. On the central quadrature slope \(dP_e/d\delta\nu=\pi T\), giving frequency uncertainty approximately \(1/(2\pi T\sqrt N)\) for ideal contrast. With the 2.00 ms delay and ten thousand independent detections this is about 0.80 Hz for a single ensemble. Imperfect contrast reduces the slope, and an unknown phase offset adds a systematic error that collecting more particles alone cannot remove.

Separate detuning from shortened observation time

Start a two-level system in its ground state, apply a monochromatic drive of resonant Rabi frequency \(\Omega\), and neglect relaxation. In the rotating-wave approximation detuning \(\delta\) changes the oscillation frequency to \(\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}\) and reduces its maximum transfer.

Chapter 10 · Exchange and two-particle energies

Build allowed two-electron states and calculate how exchange symmetry changes the expectation of a repulsive interaction.

From first principles

Construct the allowed two-electron spatial wave functions

Consider identical electrons prepared from two orthonormal spatial orbitals \(a,b\). A product \(|ab\rangle\) labels which coordinate slot contains which orbital. Exchanging the slots maps it to \(|ba\rangle\); the same exchange squared is the identity, so its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). Form the normalized eigenvectors

\[\Phi_\pm=(|ab\rangle\pm|ba\rangle)/\sqrt2,\]
\[P_{12}\Phi_\pm=\pm\Phi_\pm .\]

The fermionic exchange postulate applies to the full state. A spatially symmetric pair therefore combines with an antisymmetric spin state, and a spatially antisymmetric pair with symmetric spin. For a symmetric interaction \(W\), expand its expectation in both components. The two diagonal terms coincide and the off-diagonal terms are complex conjugates, so

\[\langle\Phi_\pm|W|\Phi_\pm\rangle=J\pm K,\]
\[J=\langle ab|W|ab\rangle,\]
\[K=\operatorname{Re}\langle ab|W|ba\rangle .\]

The difference of \(2K\) is an interference effect in the two-particle amplitude. It can affect the spin-dependent spectrum even when \(W\) is itself independent of spin. If the orbitals cease to be orthogonal, their two-particle normalization must be recomputed before applying this expectation value.

Derivation and applications

Exchange a pair of particles prepared in overlapping orbitals

The familiar factor of \(1/\sqrt2\) for a two-particle superposition assumes orthogonal one-particle states. Localized orbitals centred near each other can overlap. Let \(a(x),b(x)\) be normalized orbitals and \(S=\langle a|b\rangle\). Temporarily label the two coordinate slots 1 and 2 to evaluate inner products. The overlap of \(|a(1)b(2)\rangle\) with its exchange is the product of two one-particle inner products:

\[\langle ab|ba\rangle=\langle a|b\rangle\langle b|a\rangle=|S|^2 .\]

Symmetrize or antisymmetrize the coordinate wave function and normalize only after including this overlap. For \(|S|<1\) the two allowed candidates are

\[\Psi_\pm(x_1,x_2)=\frac{a(x_1)b(x_2)\pm b(x_1)a(x_2)}{\sqrt{2(1\pm|S|^2)}} .\]

The appropriate spatial symmetry depends on the particles and their spin preparation. To connect the result to a measurable energy, consider a weak one-dimensional short-range interaction modelled by \(W=g\delta(x_1-x_2)\). First-order perturbation theory evaluates the probability density on the coincidence line. With \(D=\int|a(x)|^2|b(x)|^2dx\) it gives

\[\langle W\rangle_+=\frac{2gD}{1+|S|^2},\]
\[\langle W\rangle_-=0.\]

At equal coordinates the two exchanged terms have the same value, so the antisymmetric amplitude cancels exactly. This cancellation applies to an ideal contact interaction. A force with finite range can still sample nonzero amplitude on either side of the coincidence line. In the limit \(|S|=1\) the antisymmetric candidate itself disappears.

Worked example

Estimate the interaction splitting in separate traps

Two localized orbitals have overlap magnitude \(0.500\) and measured density overlap \(D=0.120\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}\). For a weak repulsion \(g=2.00\,\mathrm{meV\,\mu m}\) the symmetric spatial state acquires a shift \(2(2.00)(0.120)/1.25=0.384\) meV. The antisymmetric state has zero first-order contact shift. A distinguishable pair in the unsymmetrized product would give \(gD=0.240\) meV. All estimates assume orbital shapes can be held fixed as the interaction is turned on.

Second application

Put both particles in the same orbital

For two bosons occupying the same normalized orbital, \(S=1\) and \(\Psi_+=a(x_1)a(x_2)\). A box orbital of uniform density over \(L=5.00\) micrometres has \(D=1/L=0.200\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}\). At the same coupling the shift is \(g/L=0.400\) meV. Using the orthogonal-orbital normalization here would double this energy. Two spin-polarized identical fermions cannot prepare this shared-orbital state.

Exchange acts on the entire state

Two identical particles observed at different locations cannot be assigned enduring physical labels by a measurement of position alone. The operator \(P_{12}\) swaps all coordinates of the two particles, including spin. Since a second swap restores the original state, \(P_{12}^2=1\) and exchange eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). In three dimensions identical electrons are fermions, so their total state is antisymmetric under exchange. For a spin-independent Hamiltonian, spatial and spin factors can often be treated separately while enforcing the required product symmetry.

Take two normalized orthogonal spatial orbitals \(a\) and \(b\). Both products \(a(1)b(2)\) and \(b(1)a(2)\) represent the same occupied orbitals. Their symmetric and antisymmetric combinations are

\[\Phi_\pm=\frac{a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)}{\sqrt2}.\]

The spin singlet is antisymmetric, requiring \(\Phi_+\) for the spatial part. All three spin triplets are symmetric, requiring \(\Phi_-\). Two electrons can share one spatial orbital in a singlet because they occupy different spin orbitals. In contrast the antisymmetric spatial combination vanishes if one sets \(b=a\). The physical constraint applies to the full one-electron state.

Direct and exchange matrix elements

Suppose \(a,b\) are different eigenstates of a single-particle Hamiltonian with energies \(\epsilon_a,\epsilon_b\). For their two-particle sum both spatial combinations have energy \(\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b\). Now include a weak symmetric interaction \(W(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)\). Expanding \(\langle\Phi_\pm|W|\Phi_\pm\rangle\) gives two identical diagonal terms and two conjugate off-diagonal terms. Their combination is

\[\langle W\rangle_\pm=J\pm K,\qquad J=\langle ab|W|ab\rangle,\quad K=\mathrm{Re}\langle ab|W|ba\rangle .\]

For the Coulomb interaction \(K\) is nonnegative. The triplet spatial state has suppressed coincidence probability, since \(\Phi_-(\mathbf r,\mathbf r)=0\). A repulsive interaction can therefore cost less in the triplet than in the corresponding singlet. This splitting is produced by interference of exchanged amplitudes in the interaction expectation, even when the interaction does not directly depend on spin.

A measured splitting

For two orbitals suppose the independent-particle energy sum is \(-8.00\) eV, the direct integral is \(1.20\) eV and exchange is \(0.070\) eV. In the fixed-orbital estimate the singlet has energy \(-6.73\) eV and the triplet \(-6.87\) eV. The separation is \(2K=0.140\) eV, corresponding to frequency \(3.39\times10^{13}\) Hz. This energy difference need not produce a strong electric-dipole line between the two states. A spin-independent electric dipole preserves the orthogonality of singlet and triplet spin factors.

Orbitals localized far apart can still have appreciable direct electrostatic interaction because charge densities at different sites interact. The exchange integral also requires orbital overlap at a given coordinate and falls rapidly as overlap is removed. Holding sites apart while monitoring the singlet–triplet splitting can consequently distinguish the exchange contribution from the shared direct energy shift. Strong correlation or substantial orbital rearrangement would require a larger basis than the two fixed configurations used here.

Thermal population measurements require one more factor. With negligible magnetic field the three triplet spin projections share the same energy. At temperature well above the splitting, their combined population can approach three times that of the singlet. To extract an energy splitting from population ratios, include this multiplicity before taking a Boltzmann logarithm.

Interfere two identical bosons at a beam splitter

Exchange symmetry has measurable consequences even when there is no interaction energy. A balanced lossless optical beam splitter mixes two incident spatial modes \(a,b\) into outputs \(c,d\). Choose phases so creation operators transform as \(a^\dagger\to(c^\dagger+d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\) and \(b^\dagger\to(c^\dagger-d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\). The relative sign enforces orthogonality of the two output combinations, as required for a unitary transformation. Prepare one photon in each input with identical spectrum, time profile and polarization.

The input is \(a^\dagger b^\dagger|0\rangle\). Applying the two transformations yields terms for both photons leaving \(c\), both leaving \(d\), or one leaving each output. The two one-per-output terms have opposite signs. Boson creation operators commute, so they cancel exactly:

\[|\psi_{\rm out}\rangle=\frac{(c^\dagger)^2-(d^\dagger)^2}{2}|0\rangle=\frac{|2,0\rangle-|0,2\rangle}{\sqrt2}.\]

The alternatives that cancel are both photons transmitted and both photons reflected into the one-per-output event. Photon number is conserved, but ideally the coincidence probability vanishes. If the photons have orthogonal polarization or arrive at distinguishable times, the corresponding internal states retain information about the alternatives. Their one-per-output probabilities then add to one half. This is a two-particle interference measurement, distinct from ordinary interference fringes in a single detector intensity.

Predict a delay scan for Gaussian wave packets

Let normalized temporal field amplitudes be \(f(t)\) and \(g(t)\) and let their overlap be \(s=\int f^*(t)g(t)\,dt\). Retaining these internal labels while adding the two exchange amplitudes gives coincidence probability \(P_{\rm coin}=(1-|s|^2)/2\) for an ideal balanced splitter with one photon in each input. Choose otherwise identical transform-limited Gaussian pulses whose individual arrival-time intensity distributions have standard deviation \(\sigma_t\). Shifting one by delay \(\tau\) makes \(|s|^2=\exp[-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)]\), obtained by completing the square in the overlap integral.

Take \(\sigma_t=100\) fs. At \(\tau=200\) fs the expected coincidence probability is \((1-e^{-1})/2=0.316\), between zero for perfect overlap and 0.500 for widely separated pulses. For 10,000 incident pairs and ideal detection one expects about 3160 coincidences. At a delay \(2\sigma_t\sqrt{\ln2}=167\) fs the coincidence probability has recovered halfway from its minimum to the distant baseline. The full width between those two half-depth delays is 333 fs. Dark counts, multiple-pair generation or unequal beam-splitter transmission can raise the measured minimum even at zero delay. Recording the far-delay baseline separates overall detector efficiency from the normalized depth of the dip.

It is important here that indistinguishability includes every degree of freedom capable of preserving information about the two input alternatives. Two photons with the same central wavelength and similar arrival times may still have orthogonal polarizations or distinguishable frequency profiles. Including polarization overlap multiplies the temporal overlap by the appropriate inner product for separable polarization and time states. Rotating one linear polarization continuously therefore provides an independent control of coincidence depth, even with the delay held at zero. It can test whether residual coincidences come from distinguishability or from an unequal optical splitting ratio.

Chapter 11 · Density matrices and loss of phase

Distinguish a coherent superposition from a statistical mixture and reconstruct a dephasing rate from measurements in two bases.

From first principles

Turn a preparation ensemble into a density matrix

Suppose a preparation delivers normalized state \(|\psi_j\rangle\) with classical probability \(w_j\), where the weights sum to one. The Born probabilities for a measurement outcome with projector \(\Pi\) must be averaged over the unrecorded preparation. Use the identity \(\langle\psi_j|\Pi|\psi_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|\Pi)\) and linearity of the trace:

\[P(\Pi)=\sum_jw_j\langle\psi_j|\Pi|\psi_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi),\]
\[\rho=\sum_jw_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j| .\]

In an energy basis the diagonal entries determine energy populations. To measure coherence, project onto \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Multiplication and unit trace give

\[P(+)=\tfrac12(\rho_{00}+\rho_{11}+\rho_{01}+\rho_{10})=\tfrac12+\operatorname{Re}\rho_{01}.\]

Thus equal energy populations can correspond either to perfectly coherent superposition or to an incoherent mixture. For unitary individual evolutions \(\rho(t)=U\rho(0)U^\dagger\); random relative phases across the ensemble can suppress the average off-diagonal entry while preserving both populations. Tomography in a further phase-shifted basis accesses the imaginary component.

Derivation and applications

Separate reversible phase dispersion from irreversible decay with an echo

An ensemble of spins can lose average phase coherence even when every individual spin evolves unitarily. Start each spin in an equal-amplitude superposition. In a frame rotating at the reference frequency, assume each spin has a time-independent angular detuning \(\delta\) drawn from a Gaussian with zero mean and standard deviation \(\sigma\). Ignore energy relaxation during the measurement. Under free evolution, the two components accumulate a phase difference \(\delta t\). The normalized transverse coherence is the characteristic function of the detuning distribution:

\[C(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\delta}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp[-\delta^2/(2\sigma^2)]e^{-i\delta t}.\]

Completing the square in the Gaussian exponent or Fourier transforming the normalized Gaussian gives

\[C_{\rm free}(t)=e^{-\sigma^2t^2/2},\]
\[P_{+x}(t)=\tfrac12[1+\operatorname{Re}C_{\rm free}(t)].\]

Now apply an ideal instantaneous \(\pi\) rotation about \(x\) at time \(\tau\). It exchanges the two levels, reversing the sign of subsequent relative phase accumulation in the toggling frame. An individual spin accumulates \(\delta\tau-\delta(t-\tau)\) before readout at \(t\ge\tau\). Gaussian averaging yields

\[|C_{\rm echo}(t)|=e^{-\sigma^2(2\tau-t)^2/2}.\]

At \(t=2\tau\) the static phase dispersion refocuses for every detuning. A separate independent exponential homogeneous decay factor \(e^{-t/T_2}\) would survive in the echo amplitude. Detunings changing appreciably between the two halves also spoil the cancellation. The echo therefore measures which part of an apparent decay remembers its earlier phase.

Worked example

Predict recovery in a broadened sample

Take \(\sigma/(2\pi)=1.00\) kHz. At \(t=0.500\) ms the unrefocused coherence is \(e^{-\pi^2/2}=0.00719\), so \(P_{+x}=0.5036\). With a \(\pi\) pulse at \(0.250\) ms, the same static ensemble instead has ideal echo magnitude one at \(0.500\) ms. The two experiments distinguish rapid loss of ensemble contrast from loss of the individual phase information.

Second application

Infer a homogeneous decay and a timing tolerance

Suppose echoes at \(2.00\) and \(4.00\) ms have normalized magnitudes \(0.800\) and \(0.640\). Their ratio is \(0.800\), giving \(T_2=-2.00\,\mathrm{ms}/\ln0.800=8.96\) ms. If the readout is displaced by \(0.100\) ms from the true echo, static broadening with the previous \(\sigma\) reduces its contrast by another factor \(e^{-(2\pi\,0.100)^2/2}=0.821\). Timing and homogeneous decay enter separately in this inference.

Two preparations with identical energy populations

Prepare a two-level system with states \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). One source always prepares \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Another prepares \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) at random with equal probability. Measuring in the displayed basis gives identical frequencies for the two sources. After projection onto \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\), however, the first source always gives plus and the second gives plus on half the trials. An adequate description must store more than the populations seen in one basis.

Define \(\rho=\sum_jp_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|\) for a preparation ensemble. Probabilities follow from \(P(Q)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho Q)\) for a projector \(Q\). The trace is one, Hermiticity makes expectation values real and positivity ensures probabilities are nonnegative. The first preparation has all matrix entries equal to \(1/2\). The second has diagonal entries \(1/2\) and off-diagonal entries zero. Although a density matrix can be decomposed into ensembles in multiple ways, every measurement prediction depends only on the matrix itself.

Coherence can be lost to an unobserved system

Consider a pure joint state \((|0\rangle|e_0\rangle+|1\rangle|e_1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) of system and environment. Average over any complete environment basis, equivalently take the partial trace. The system retains equal populations, and its off-diagonal element is \(\rho_{01}=\langle e_1|e_0\rangle/2\). If the environment states become orthogonal, they carry enough information to distinguish the two alternatives and system-only interference disappears. The combined system can still be pure.

A simple dephasing model multiplies off-diagonal elements by \(e^{-t/T_2}\), leaving populations fixed. In a rotating frame that removes the known level splitting, initial plus preparation then gives

\[P_+(t)=\frac{1+e^{-t/T_2}}2,\qquad \mathrm{Tr}\,\rho^2=\frac{1+e^{-2t/T_2}}2 .\]

The first expression follows by projecting onto plus; the second is the sum of diagonal entries of \(\rho^2\). Purity ranges from one for a pure state to one half for the completely mixed two-level state. Population relaxation would change the diagonal entries too. Measuring both the energy basis and the interference basis helps decide whether the simpler model is adequate.

An interference record

Following a calibrated plus preparation, a measurement in the rotating frame gives \(P_+=0.700\) after 30.0 μs. The contrast is \(2P_+-1=0.400\), giving \(T_2=-30.0/\ln(0.400)=32.7\) μs. The inferred purity is \((1+0.400^2)/2=0.580\). At 60.0 μs an exponential model gives contrast \(0.160\) and \(P_+=0.580\). A different next value can signal nonexponential noise or a misestimated coherent phase.

A single interference quadrature cannot distinguish reduced visibility from uncompensated precession. To test the rotating frame assumption, vary the final projection phase and fit the full fringe. Its phase reports the coherent rotation; its amplitude reports visibility. Combining this with an energy-basis measurement determines the full Bloch vector and makes the purity estimate meaningful even when populations drift.

Purity estimates from finite counts can also be misleading when each fitted probability is treated independently. The reconstructed Bloch vector must have length at most one for a physical density operator. Longer vectors indicate sampling fluctuations or inconsistent calibrations. Increasing repetitions narrows counting uncertainty, while rotating a known reference state helps diagnose systematic errors among measurement axes.

Energy relaxation places a bound on phase memory

Pure dephasing leaves populations fixed. A two-level system coupled to a cold environment can also lose energy by relaxing from excited state \(|1\rangle\) to ground state \(|0\rangle\). Suppose the probability for an initially excited system to survive time \(t\) is \(s=e^{-t/T_1}\). A simple system–environment evolution sends \(|1,E_0\rangle\) to \(\sqrt s|1,E_0\rangle+\sqrt{1-s}|0,E_1\rangle\) and leaves \(|0,E_0\rangle\) unchanged. The two environment states represent absence or presence of a released excitation and are orthogonal.

For an initial superposition only its unrelaxed excited amplitude shares the original environment state with the ground amplitude. Partial tracing consequently multiplies the off-diagonal system element by \(\sqrt s\), while the excited-state probability is multiplied by \(s\). Additional independent Markovian phase noise can multiply the off-diagonal element by \(e^{-t/T_\phi}\). In a rotating frame the resulting evolution is

\[\rho_{11}(t)=e^{-t/T_1}\rho_{11}(0),\qquad \rho_{01}(t)=e^{-t/T_2}\rho_{01}(0),\qquad \frac1{T_2}=\frac1{2T_1}+\frac1{T_\phi}.\]

Thus even a perfectly stable transition frequency cannot make coherence last indefinitely when population relaxes. In this two-level model \(T_2\le2T_1\), with equality if there is no additional pure dephasing. At finite bath temperature upward jumps also occur and populations approach a nonzero equilibrium excitation. A multilevel environment or nonexponential noise can require a different fitting model.

Separate two decay mechanisms using independent preparations

First prepare the excited state and measure population decay; suppose it gives \(T_1=40.0\) μs. Prepare \(|+\rangle\) independently and fit fringe contrast to obtain \(T_2=50.0\) μs. The pure-dephasing rate is \(1/50.0-1/80.0=0.00750\) μs⁻¹, corresponding to \(T_\phi=133\) μs. At \(t=40.0\) μs after plus preparation, the excited population is \(e^{-1}/2=0.184\), and the real coherence in the calibrated rotating frame is \(e^{-0.8}/2=0.225\). Then \(P_+=1/2+\mathrm{Re}\rho_{01}=0.725\).

The reconstructed purity is \(\rho_{00}^2+\rho_{11}^2+2|\rho_{01}|^2=0.801\) to rounding. As time grows, coherence vanishes but the cold-bath model returns the system to the pure ground state, so purity ultimately rises back to one. This differs qualitatively from pure dephasing of an initial plus state, whose limiting purity is one half. An independently measured \(T_2>2T_1\) would call for checking the data and assumptions about temperature, state assignment, fit functions and time-dependent noise before assigning an unphysical negative pure-dephasing rate.

The two independent preparations can be checked against a single density matrix at an additional time. Starting in plus, a general measurement along a transverse direction gives a cosine fringe whose offset is one half regardless of the energy populations, while its amplitude measures the transverse coherence. A separate energy-basis measurement supplies the population imbalance. Together they determine the full Bloch-vector length, which should never exceed one. If independently fitted transverse and longitudinal decay curves imply a greater length at some time, the combined fitted model cannot represent a physical quantum state there even if each measured curve looks plausible in isolation.

Checking positivity across the fitted interval also constrains long extrapolations from records that cover only a small fraction of a relaxation time.

Chapter 12 · Confinement and spectral geometry

Map a two-dimensional boundary-value problem to quantum energies and determine which information an energy spectrum can reveal about a well.

From first principles

Derive a rectangular billiard spectrum from its boundary conditions

Take a free particle of mass \(m\) confined by infinite potential walls on the rectangle \(0<x<a\), \(0<y<b\). The potential is zero inside and the wave function vanishes at each edge. Use \(\psi=X(x)Y(y)\) in the stationary Schrödinger equation and divide by the product. A function of \(x\) plus a function of \(y\) equals a constant everywhere, so the contributions must separately be constant:

\[-X''/X=k_x^2,\]
\[-Y''/Y=k_y^2,\]
\[E=\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)/(2m).\]

The boundary at zero in each coordinate selects sine solutions. At the opposite walls those sines must vanish again, allowing only an integer number of half-wavelengths:

\[k_x=n\pi/a,\]
\[k_y=j\pi/b,\]
\[E_{nj}=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n^2}{a^2}+\frac{j^2}{b^2}\right),\]
\[n,j\ge1 .\]

A zero index would give the identically zero function here. Scaling both lengths by the same factor \(s\) reduces every energy by \(s^{-2}\) while preserving degeneracy patterns. For more complicated domains, direct separation is generally unavailable, but the same Dirichlet eigenvalue problem determines the allowed squared wave numbers.

Derivation and applications

How do the walls modify the leading state count?

The area term in a quantum billiard counts allowed wave vectors as though they were continuous. The finite perimeter removes a systematic number of states from this estimate. Take a rectangular two-dimensional domain of sides \(a,b\), an infinitely high wall on each side, and one spin species. The separated standing waves vanish at both ends; consequently their positive integer labels start at one. Let \(K\) be a cutoff in wave number. The exact count is a lattice-point problem:

\[N(K)=\#\{(n,m):n,m\ge1,\;(\pi n/a)^2+(\pi m/b)^2\le K^2\}.\]

For clarity mirror the quarter ellipse into all four quadrants in the integer \((n,m)\) plane. Every interior positive pair then contributes four points. A point on either axis belongs to none of the physical Dirichlet modes, and the origin also has to be removed. Denoting the full-ellipse count by \(N_{\rm full}\) gives the exact identity

\[4N=N_{\rm full}-2\lfloor aK/\pi\rfloor-2\lfloor bK/\pi\rfloor-1 .\]

A smooth local average at large \(K\) replaces the full-ellipse count by area \(\pi(aK/\pi)(bK/\pi)\) to leading order. Retain the leading subtractions from the axes, whose lengths grow linearly with \(K\). With physical area \(A=ab\) and perimeter \(P=2(a+b)\) this yields

\[\overline N(K)\simeq \frac{AK^2}{4\pi}-\frac{PK}{4\pi}.\]

The bar signifies a count smoothed over nearby thresholds. Fluctuations in exact lattice-point counts and constant corner contributions remain. This is a cumulative estimate for many states; it cannot resolve individual degeneracies. For a quadratic dispersion relate wave number to energy by \(E=\hbar^2 K^2/(2m^*)\) at effective mass \(m^*\).

Worked example

Compare the area estimate with exact enumeration

Take a square of side \(L\) with \(KL/\pi=5\). Positive pairs satisfy \(n^2+m^2\le25\). For \(n=1,2,3,4\) the allowed counts are \(4,4,4,3\), giving \(15\) in all. The area estimate alone gives \(25\pi/4=19.63\), while the perimeter subtracts \(5.00\), leaving \(14.63\). Agreement near this cutoff illustrates the systematic correction; it is not an exact equality.

Second application

Distinguish two billiards with the same area

At fixed area \(100\) nm², compare a \(10\times10\) nm square and a \(20\times5\) nm rectangle. Their perimeters are \(40\) and \(50\) nm. At \(K=5.00\) nm⁻¹ both leading area counts are \(198.9\). The wall terms reduce them to about \(183.0\) and \(179.0\) states. Thus the longer perimeter reduces the smooth count by an estimated 3.98 states at the same cutoff. Spin degeneracy would multiply both predictions and their difference by two.

OneSimulation laboratory · Turn spatial eigenvalues into confinement energies

A domain becomes part of the Hamiltonian

Constrain a nonrelativistic spinless particle of mass \(m\) to a flat region \(\Omega\) with perfectly impenetrable walls. There is no potential inside. The wavefunction vanishes on the boundary, and square-integrability fixes its overall norm. The stationary equation becomes \(-\nabla^2\psi=k^2\psi\) with \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\). Thus the Laplacian, together with its domain and boundary condition, determines the spectrum. An acoustic pressure mode at a rigid reflecting wall generally has a different boundary condition and cannot be transferred unchanged.

For a rectangle with sides \(a,b\), write \(\psi=X(x)Y(y)\). Separation and the four zero-value conditions give \(X\propto\sin(n_x\pi x/a)\), \(Y\propto\sin(n_y\pi y/b)\) with positive integers. The energies are proportional to \(n_x^2/a^2+n_y^2/b^2\). If \(a=b\), exchanged index pairs give a degeneracy when unequal. Mildly stretching one side splits such a pair because nodes along the stretched direction cost less gradient energy.

Scaling and counting states

Under uniform dilation \(\mathbf r=s\mathbf r'\) the gradient becomes \(\nabla_{\mathbf r}=s^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf r'}\). All eigenvalues of \(-\nabla^2\) and all energies fall by \(s^2\) in the denominator while nodal patterns simply stretch with the boundary. This result holds for any shape and any level of the empty hard-wall well. A potential with a fixed external length scale would add another term whose scaling requires separate analysis.

For highly excited modes, count the allowed points inside a wavenumber disk. In a large rectangle the spacing is \(\pi/a\) by \(\pi/b\), and only the positive quadrant contributes. Dividing its area by one state’s area gives

\[N(k)\simeq\frac{ab\,k^2}{4\pi}=\frac{A k^2}{4\pi}.\]

The leading counting law extends to smooth or piecewise smooth domains, and the Dirichlet boundary gives a subleading term \(-Pk/(4\pi)\) for perimeter \(P\) under the usual asymptotic conditions. It predicts the general growth of the level count. At small \(k\), discreteness and geometry dominate and an individual ground level cannot be inferred from area alone.

Test an inference about shape

For an electron in a 2.00 nm square, \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.03810\) eV nm² and the ground energy is \(0.03810\times2\pi^2/(2.00)^2=0.188\) eV. Doubling just one side gives \(0.03810\pi^2(1/4+1/16)=0.118\) eV. Doubling both gives \(0.0470\) eV. These outcomes distinguish directional stretching from uniform dilation.

Now compare the two irregular wells in the Dirichlet eigenmode experiment. With a physical length unit of 1 nm, their first dimensionless eigenvalues are both about 2.538, giving electron ground energies about \(0.0967\) eV. Predict their energies with a 2 nm unit, then verify the lowest stored level drops to \(0.0242\) eV for the rescaled well while its dimensionless eigenvalue stays fixed. Agreement of a few computed levels within numerical error alone cannot establish exact isospectrality. Known noncongruent isospectral domains show why even a full list of spectral lines need not uniquely specify a boundary.

The mass scaling offers a separate diagnostic on the conversion from spatial eigenvalues. Replacing the electron by a hypothetical particle of double its mass halves every mapped energy at fixed shape and physical size. No spatial eigenproblem has to be solved again because the mass occurs only in the conversion factor.

Open one direction and obtain a threshold spectrum

A closed rectangular well has discrete levels in both directions. A long conducting strip with hard side walls and open longitudinal ends has confinement in only one. Assume a nonrelativistic particle of effective mass \(m^*\), width \(W\) in \(y\) and unrestricted position \(x\), with zero interior potential. A separated solution is \(\psi=e^{ik_xx}\sin(n\pi y/W)\). The energy follows directly from the Laplacian,

\[E_n(k_x)=\frac{\hbar^2 n^2\pi^2}{2m^*W^2}+\frac{\hbar^2k_x^2}{2m^*}=E_n^\perp+\frac{\hbar^2k_x^2}{2m^*}.\]

Each transverse eigenfunction supplies a propagation channel only when the total energy exceeds its threshold \(E_n^\perp\). Below threshold \(k_x\) is imaginary and a corresponding component excited at an interface decays along the strip. The group velocity in an open channel is \(\hbar k_x/m^*\) and tends to zero at threshold.

For a normalization length \(L_x\), periodic boundary conditions place successive \(k_x\) values \(2\pi/L_x\) apart. Counting both propagation directions gives \(N_n/L_x=k_x/\pi\) below a chosen energy above threshold, per spin state. Differentiating with respect to energy yields a density of states per unit length proportional to \((E-E_n^\perp)^{-1/2}\). Slow longitudinal motion near an opening creates an enhanced number of states per energy interval. The ideal divergence is integrable: the total number added in a small energy window remains finite. Finite temperature, disorder and instrumental resolution round the singular feature.

Count channels in a semiconductor strip

Take \(W=20.0\) nm and \(m^*=0.0670m_e\). Then \(\hbar^2/(2m^*)=0.0381/0.0670=0.569\) eV nm². The first threshold is \(E_1^\perp=0.569\pi^2/(20.0)^2=14.0\) meV. The second is \(56.1\) meV and the third \(126\) meV. At 70.0 meV two transverse modes carry travelling waves. The second has kinetic energy in the longitudinal direction of \(13.9\) meV, giving \(k_x=\sqrt{0.0139/0.569}=0.156\) nm⁻¹. Its wavelength along the strip is \(2\pi/k_x=40.3\) nm, considerably longer than the width.

Reduce the width to 16.0 nm while keeping the reservoir energy fixed. Every transverse threshold rises by \((20/16)^2=1.5625\), so the second moves to \(87.7\) meV and closes. A wave launched from the original wide section may then reflect or populate an evanescent component at the narrowing. Resolving those reflection amplitudes requires matching fields at the interface. The threshold count alone predicts how many modes remain available far beyond it, furnishing a useful test before doing a full scattering calculation.

A threshold can be recognized without measuring absolute particle energy if the width is varied systematically. Plot an identified onset energy against inverse squared width. In a strip with a constant, spatially uniform band-edge offset the slopes are in the ratio of squared transverse quantum numbers while the intercept gives that common offset. A finite confining barrier permits wavefunction penetration beyond the nominal geometric boundary and lowers the transverse energy compared with a hard wall of the same width. Checking several mode onsets helps distinguish effective width changes from a shift of the entire interior potential.

Such comparisons should use the same effective mass throughout the fitted energy range.

Three independently prepared ensembles give probabilities for spin-up of 0.80 along x, 0.50 along y and 0.90 along z. Reconstruct the density matrix, decide whether the state is pure, and predict the result of an x measurement after a z measurement whose result is discarded. Worked solution

For each Pauli measurement the mean is p(+) − p(−) = 2p(+) − 1. The Bloch vector is (0.6, 0, 0.8). In the z basis the diagonal entries are 0.9 and 0.1 and both off-diagonal entries equal 0.3. Its trace is one and its determinant vanishes. Thus its eigenvalues are one and zero. A state vector is (√0.9, √0.1) up to global phase.

An unread projective z measurement removes the off-diagonal terms. The z probabilities stay fixed, while the subsequent x-up probability becomes 0.50. The new purity is 0.9² + 0.1² = 0.82. Selecting the z-up subset instead would leave a pure z-up state, which also gives 0.50 in x. Testing z again distinguishes selection from mixing.

With z-up probability 0.95 the reconstructed Bloch length squared would become 0.6² + 0.9² = 1.17. The nominal matrix has a negative eigenvalue. Finite-sample fluctuations or calibration differences can produce such estimates. A physical reconstruction fits a positive matrix to the original counts. Applied study

Connect confinement, quantised energies and measurement probabilities through wavefunctions.

What information does a quantum state contain?

Suppose an electron is confined to a narrow region and a sequence of energy measurements shows distinct lines. To interpret them, distinguish a state from an outcome. In one spatial dimension a pure state has a complex amplitude \(\psi(x,t)\) normalised so that \(\int|\psi|^2dx=1\). The Born rule gives the probability in an interval by integrating \(|\psi|^2\) there. Relative phases affect interference between amplitudes.

An observable is represented by a self-adjoint operator with suitable boundary conditions. The Hamiltonian for a nonrelativistic particle of mass \(m\) in a scalar potential \(V\) is \(\hat H=-(\hbar^2/2m)\partial_x^2+V\). It both sets the possible energy outcomes and generates the time evolution \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\hat H\psi\).

Derive a spectrum from confinement

Take an infinite square well on \(0<x<L\). The potential vanishes inside and hard walls impose \(\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0\). Separating \(\psi(x,t)=\phi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\) reduces the equation to \(-\phi''=k^2\phi\), \(E=\hbar^2 k^2/(2m)\). The left wall selects a sine. The right wall fixes \(k_n=n\pi/L\). The integer starts at one because the zero solution cannot be normalised. Integrating \(\sin^2\) over the well fixes the normalisation

\[\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2mL^2}.\]

Take an electron and \(L=1.00\) nm. With \(\hbar=1.05457\times10^{-34}\) J·s, \(m_e=9.10938\times10^{-31}\) kg and 1 eV \(=1.60218\times10^{-19}\) J, the ground energy is 0.376 eV and the second energy is 1.504 eV. The ground state is lowest in energy because it has the gentlest curvature compatible with both walls.

Separate energy measurements and time evolution

Prepare \(\psi=(\phi_1+\phi_2)/\sqrt2\). Orthogonality makes \(|\langle\phi_n|\psi\rangle|^2=1/2\) for each of these two energy outcomes. An ideal projective measurement giving \(E_1\) leaves the state in \(\phi_1\); an immediate repetition gives \(E_1\) with probability one. Measurements on an ensemble of fresh preparations give mean energy \((E_1+E_2)/2=0.940\) eV.

Before any measurement the two components evolve with different phases. For these real eigenfunctions the density contains the cross term \(\phi_1\phi_2\cos[(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar]\). A position detector can therefore observe a period \(h/(E_2-E_1)=3.67\) fs for the 1 nm well. Each energy probability remains constant. This is the distinction between changing local probability and conserved energy in a time-independent Hamiltonian.

Transfer the measurement rule to spin

A spin-½ atom prepared in the upper output of a Stern–Gerlach analyser along \(z\) enters another analyser oriented along \(x\). In the two-dimensional spin space, \(|+z\rangle=(|+x\rangle+|-x\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The second analyser therefore sends half of a fresh ensemble into each output. Retain only the upper \(x\) beam and measure along \(z\) again. Since \(|+x\rangle=(|+z\rangle+|-z\rangle)/\sqrt2\), half of that selected beam now enters the lower \(z\) output, or one quarter of the original flux. With a second \(z\) analyser directly after the first, this lower output would have been empty. A change of measurement basis has observable consequences for the subsequent statistics.

How much geometry survives in a spectrum?

Inside a two-dimensional hard-wall region the same reasoning gives \(-\nabla^2\phi_n=k_n^2\phi_n\) with \(\phi_n=0\) on its boundary. A membrane fixed along its edge obeys the same spatial equation with \(k_n^2=\rho_A\omega_n^2/\mathcal T\), where \(\rho_A\) is mass per area and \(\mathcal T\) tension per unit length. This correspondence gives a numerical way to find the quantum energy levels of a freely moving particle confined within corresponding walls. Two distinct polygons can have identical lists of these eigenvalues. Mode shapes and local probabilities can still differ. Rigid-wall acoustic pressure modes illustrate how changing the boundary condition changes the spectrum. Standing waves in a closed room.
