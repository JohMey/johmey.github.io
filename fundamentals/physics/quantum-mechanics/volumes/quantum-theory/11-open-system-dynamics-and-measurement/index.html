Derive loss of coherence from a larger unitary system

A qubit prepared in \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) interacts with an environment initially in \(|e\rangle\). A joint unitary correlates \(|0,e\rangle\to|0,e_0\rangle,\ |1,e\rangle\to|1,e_1\rangle\), with both environmental states normalised. The joint output is a pure state. Its qubit density matrix follows by tracing over a complete environment basis. Populations stay \(|\alpha|^2,|\beta|^2\). The off-diagonal entry is \(\alpha\beta^*\langle e_1|e_0\rangle\). Orthogonal environment records remove coherence from all local qubit observations; identical records leave its magnitude intact. Partial distinguishability gives a continuous range in between. Energy need not flow in this controlled dephasing model. An observed reduction in fringe contrast can arise from entanglement with unobserved degrees of freedom even when total evolution is unitary.

Consider instead amplitude loss from an excited qubit to an initially empty environment mode:

\[|0,0_e\rangle\mapsto |0,0_e\rangle,\qquad |1,0_e\rangle\mapsto\sqrt{1-p}|1,0_e\rangle+\sqrt p|0,1_e\rangle .\]

The two output states remain orthogonal and unit-normalised, so this map on the prepared environment subspace can be extended to a unitary on the full joint space. If environment state \(k\) is unobserved, sum the associated unnormalised alternatives in density-matrix form:

\[\rho'=\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger.\]

Here \(K_0=|0\rangle\langle0|+\sqrt{1-p}|1\rangle\langle1|\) and \(K_1=\sqrt p|0\rangle\langle1|\). Since \(\sum K_k^\dagger K_k=I\), trace is preserved. The excited population obeys \(\rho_{11}'=(1-p)\rho_{11}\) and the coherence \(\rho'_{01}=\sqrt{1-p}\rho_{01}\). Averaging state vectors before forming outer products would introduce interference between orthogonal environmental records that have already been traced out.

Take a controlled infinitesimal limit

In a memoryless approximation each small interval \(dt\) meets a fresh environment record with vanishing probability of a previously lost excitation returning. Let jump operators \(L_j\) have units \(1/\sqrt{\mathrm s}\), set \(K_j=\sqrt{dt}\,L_j\) for events and \(K_0=I-dt(iH/\hbar+\sum L_j^\dagger L_j/2)\). Expanding the completeness relation shows trace preservation through first order in \(dt\). Expand \(\sum K_k\rho K_k^\dagger-\rho\), divide by \(dt\) and keep the finite terms:

\[\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac i\hbar[H,\rho]+\sum_j\left(L_j\rho L_j^\dagger-\tfrac12 L_j^\dagger L_j\rho-\tfrac12 \rho L_j^\dagger L_j\right).\]

The first term of each dissipative bracket adds probability after an event and the anticommutator subtracts probability from the no-event branch. Their traces cancel. This construction is completely positive, meaning that the same local channel remains positive even when tensored with identity on an unused entangled ancilla. Positivity only on isolated input states would be too weak: transposition maps density matrices to density matrices on a single system, yet partial transposition can produce a negative eigenvalue when applied to half an entangled pair. The Lindblad form embodies the relevant stronger constraint for the assumed Markovian dynamics.

Independent loss and pure dephasing can be represented by \(L_1=\sqrt{\Gamma_1}|0\rangle\langle1|\),

\[L_\phi=\sqrt{\Gamma_\phi/2}\,\sigma_z.\]

The factor one half in the dephasing operator fixes our convention for \(\Gamma_\phi\). Direct substitution gives \(\dot\rho_{11}=-\Gamma_1\rho_{11}\), while \(\dot\rho_{01}\) acquires \(-(\Gamma_1/2+\Gamma_\phi)\rho_{01}\) in addition to its Hamiltonian phase. Thus

\[T_1=\Gamma_1^{-1},\]
\[T_2^{-1}=(2T_1)^{-1}+\Gamma_\phi .\]

At zero temperature with these mechanisms, \(T_2\le2T_1\). This upper bound is tied to definitions and assumptions. A broad ensemble with static detuning spread, a driven nonequilibrium sample or a refocused pulse sequence may yield an observed envelope with a different shape and timescale. Fitting every contrast decay with one exponential and interpreting that number as the homogeneous \(T_2\) can assign an unphysical dephasing rate.

Update a state when an individual event is recorded

If an ideal detector reports branch \(j\), the probability is \(p_j=\operatorname{tr}K_j\rho K_j^\dagger\), and the normalised conditional output is \(K_j\rho K_j^\dagger/p_j\). The conditional evolution depends on which environment observable is measured; summing outcomes gives the same physical unconditioned channel. In the loss model, detecting one emission places the qubit in its ground state. Absence of an emission also carries information. At finite duration \(t\), no emission acts by \(K_0=|0\rangle\langle0|+e^{-\Gamma_1t/2}|1\rangle\langle1|\) for the simple undriven zero-temperature case.

From initial \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\), at \(t=T_1\ln2\) the no-event probability is

\[(1+1/2)/2=3/4.\]

Conditional excited probability is

\[(1/2)/(1+1/2)=1/3.\]

With the event branch in the ground state, the unconditioned excited probability is \((3/4)(1/3)=1/4\), agreeing with exponential decay of its initial one half. Repeatedly renormalising only no-event trajectories and calling their mean the ensemble population would overestimate survival. If the event detector misses photons, “no click” groups genuine no-emission with undetected emissions and its conditional state is generally mixed.

Use heating and cooling to infer the reservoir

Allow upward and downward transitions at rates \(\Gamma_\uparrow,\Gamma_\downarrow\). Rate equations give

\[\dot p_1=\Gamma_\uparrow(1-p_1)-\Gamma_\downarrow p_1,\]
\[p_{1,\infty}=\frac{\Gamma_\uparrow}{\Gamma_\uparrow+\Gamma_\downarrow},\quad\]
\[p_1(t)=p_{1,\infty}+[p_1(0)-p_{1,\infty}]e^{-(\Gamma_\uparrow+\Gamma_\downarrow)t}.\]

Both upward and downward rates contribute half their sum to coherence decay. If the reservoir is in thermal equilibrium, detailed balance gives

\[\Gamma_\uparrow/\Gamma_\downarrow=e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}.\]

The population relaxation time measures their sum and equilibrium population measures their ratio, allowing a substantive check of a proposed bath temperature. The relation is a thermodynamic assumption in addition to the two-state rate model. A pumped bath can have the same measured relaxation time with an entirely different stationary population.

For a 5.00 GHz transition, \(\hbar\omega/k_B=h(5\,\mathrm{GHz})/k_B\simeq0.240\) K. A stationary excited fraction 0.10 would imply \(T=0.240/\ln9=0.109\) K under equilibrium. With population relaxation 20 μs the sum of rates is \(5.0\times10^4\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), giving 5000 upward and \(45\,000\) downward transitions per second for the respective source populations. Verified independent upward and downward preparations test more than fitting the decay of an initially excited state alone.

Discriminate static detuning, Markovian dephasing and coherent error

A Ramsey experiment prepares an equal superposition, allows free evolution for time \(t\), then converts relative phase to a population by a final analysis pulse. If the detuning \(\delta\) is fixed in each run but varies randomly between runs, then the ensemble coherence is multiplied by the characteristic function \(C(t)=\int p(\delta)e^{-i\delta t}d\delta\). Here \(\delta\) is an angular frequency. For a Gaussian detuning of mean \(\delta_0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\),

\[C=e^{-i\delta_0t}e^{-\sigma^2t^2/2}.\]

A uniform detuning offset shifts fringe phase without reducing the contrast of a fully coherent, identically repeated preparation. A Lorentzian distribution yields an exponential envelope which can resemble homogeneous Markovian dephasing in a simple time scan. The fitted functional form alone may therefore fail to identify the physical cause of lost contrast.

An ideal instantaneous \(\pi\) pulse about \(x\) halfway through free evolution reverses the effect of a static \(z\)-detuning on relative phase: \(e^{-i\delta t\sigma_z/4}\sigma_x e^{-i\delta t\sigma_z/4}=\sigma_x\), since \(\sigma_x\) anticommutes with \(\sigma_z\). All static offsets refocus at the echo. Independent white Markovian dephasing accumulated in each half is uncorrelated and cannot be undone in this way. The deterministic Hamiltonian error and a rapidly fluctuating environment can share a short Ramsey contrast while giving different echo contrasts. If the pulse itself has finite duration or an angle error, the algebraic cancellation is no longer exact; repeating at two pulse powers tests that particular assumption.

Take \(\sigma/2\pi=20.0\) kHz and negligible damping. At \(t=10.0\,\mu\mathrm s\) the free Ramsey contrast from Gaussian detuning alone is

\[e^{-(1.257)^2/2}=0.454.\]

An ideal echo returns unity. Add independent \(T_1=80\,\mu\mathrm s,\ T_\phi=50\,\mu\mathrm s\). The homogeneous coherence time is

\[[1/(160\,\mu{\rm s})+1/(50\,\mu{\rm s})]^{-1}=38.1\,\mu\mathrm s.\]

The expected echo contrast is now \(e^{-10/38.1}=0.769\) and the ordinary Ramsey contrast \(0.349\). A single fitted \(T_2^*\) cannot display these three separate timescales. Measuring populations, Ramsey fringes and echoes on consistent ensembles gives three complementary observables with independently interpretable limits.

For a time-dependent classical detuning \(\delta(t)\), pulse sequences choose a modulation \(y(t)=\pm1\) multiplying the noise phase. The phase error is

\[\phi=\int y(t)\delta(t)dt.\]

For stationary zero-mean Gaussian noise, \(\langle e^{-i\phi}\rangle=e^{-\langle\phi^2\rangle/2}\) and the variance is a double integral of the noise correlation weighted by \(y(t)y(t')\). Equivalently it is the spectral integral of the power density weighted by \(|\int y(t)e^{i\omega t}dt|^2\), with the normalisation set by the chosen two-sided Fourier convention. A single echo has zero weight at \(\omega=0\) because its two half integrals cancel, while keeping finite-frequency sensitivity. Families of pulse spacings can therefore probe noise timescales rather than simply fitting larger and larger numbers to a nominal coherence time.

Find the damped saturation curve from optical Bloch equations

A continuously driven qubit has an additional route to separate population and coherence relaxation. In a rotating frame let \(H=\hbar(\Omega\sigma_x+\Delta\sigma_z)/2\), with \(z=p_e-p_g\), equilibrium \(z_{\rm eq}=-1\) at zero temperature, and transverse damping \(1/T_2\). Choose a consistent Bloch basis so the commutator gives the cross product of \((\Omega,0,\Delta)\) with \((x,y,z)\). The equations are

\[\dot x=-\Delta y-x/T_2,\]
\[\dot y=\Delta x-\Omega z-y/T_2,\]
\[\dot z=\Omega y-(z-z_{\rm eq})/T_1 .\]

At steady state the first equation gives

\[x=-\Delta T_2 y.\]

The second then gives

\[y=-\Omega T_2 z/(1+\Delta^2T_2^2).\]

Inserting into the third gives

\[z=z_{\rm eq}/[1+\Omega^2T_1T_2/(1+\Delta^2T_2^2)].\]

Thus the excited fraction for \(z_{\rm eq}=-1\) is

\[p_{e,\infty}=\frac{\Omega^2T_1T_2}{2[1+\Delta^2T_2^2+\Omega^2T_1T_2]}.\]

A weak resonant drive gives a population proportional to its power, and a strong continuous drive saturates toward one half rather than preparing a permanently inverted state. Coherent \(\pi\)-pulse inversion is a transient unitary process; continuous steady balance with relaxation gives a different preparation. The full angular-frequency width at half of the on-resonance excitation is \(2\sqrt{1+\Omega^2T_1T_2}/T_2\), so increasing power broadens the line even at unchanged intrinsic \(T_2\).

Choose \(T_1=20\) μs, \(T_2=16\) μs and resonant \(\Omega/2\pi=10\) kHz. The saturation parameter is

\[s=\Omega^2 T_1T_2=1.26.\]

The stationary excited fraction is \(s/[2(1+s)]=0.279\), and the ordinary-frequency full width is

\[\sqrt{1+s}/(\pi T_2)=29.9\,\mathrm{kHz}.\]

At very low drive it would be 19.9 kHz. A measured linewidth that increases approximately as the square root of power at high drive can have this coherent saturation origin without any increase in the environment's pure-dephasing rate. Comparing the zero-power linewidth extrapolation with an independent time-domain \(T_2\) is a stronger test than fitting a single apparent line broadening.

Connect an individual event stream to an ensemble lifetime

The lifetime \(T_1\) of one excited qubit is a distribution of observed emission times across many trials. With perfect detection, no drive and initially excited state, survival \(S(t)=e^{-\Gamma t}\) implies waiting-time density \(w(t)=-dS/dt=\Gamma e^{-\Gamma t}\). The mean waiting time and standard deviation are both \(1/\Gamma\). A no-click trajectory stays in the excited state conditionally until its first emission when the initial preparation is certainly excited. Its unnormalised weight decays exponentially, but assigning every no-click trajectory a continuously decreasing normalised excitation would confuse conditional and unconditioned statements. For an initial superposition the absence record instead changes the normalised state as derived earlier.

Real acquisitions may stop each trial at a fixed deadline \(T_{\rm stop}\). Treating all no-click trials as though they emitted exactly at the deadline biases lifetime inference. For \(n\) observed emissions at times \(t_j\) and \(m\) censored trials with no emission through \(T_{\rm stop}\), the likelihood is proportional to \(\prod_{j=1}^n\Gamma e^{-\Gamma t_j}\prod_{k=1}^m e^{-\Gamma T_{\rm stop}}\). Optimising gives

\[\hat\Gamma=n/(\sum_j t_j+mT_{\rm stop}).\]

The numerator is actual events and the denominator all time observed at risk. For 40 detections totalling 480 μs and 10 additional censored runs each observed 30 μs, the estimate is \(40/780=0.0513\,\mu\mathrm s^{-1}\), or \(T_1\simeq19.5\,\mu\mathrm s\). Discarding the censored runs instead would report 12 μs from the mean detected delay. This statistical correction follows directly from the same physical survival law used for the quantum channel.

Detection inefficiency can cause a different data problem. In a single-excitation undriven trial, a missed decay leaves a qubit in its ground state and unable to emit again. The probability of no registered event up to \(t\) is \(1-\eta[1-e^{-\Gamma t}]\) at constant efficiency \(\eta\), with a nonzero late plateau \(1-\eta\). It is not \(e^{-\eta\Gamma t}\), which would describe thinning a renewable stationary Poisson event stream. Long-time survival of the recorded count and independent final-state readout can distinguish a missed emission from a genuinely long-lived excitation. This example shows why the detector model must specify whether events can repeat after one has been missed.

Audit a reconstructed Pauli noise channel

Suppose three independently prepared Bloch components appear to shrink by factors \(\lambda_x,\lambda_y,\lambda_z\) after a calibrated waiting time, with no displacement of the centre. A diagonal unital qubit channel can be written \(\mathcal E(\rho)=\sum_{\nu=0,x,y,z}p_\nu\sigma_\nu\rho\sigma_\nu\) for a physical Pauli mixture. Expanding its action on the three axes and inverting gives

\[p_0=(1+\lambda_x+\lambda_y+\lambda_z)/4,\]
\[p_x=(1+\lambda_x-\lambda_y-\lambda_z)/4,\]

with cyclic partners for \(p_y,p_z\). Complete positivity demands all four numbers be nonnegative. Checking each \(|\lambda_i|\le1\) only checks lengths along single Bloch axes and, for this diagonal map, positivity on a single qubit. It does not guarantee that the reconstructed operation is consistent on half of an entangled system.

For \(\lambda=(0.9,0.9,0.5)\), every axis appears to shrink, yet \(p_z=(1-0.9-0.9+0.5)/4=-0.075\). Thus the exact diagonal unital channel inferred from those numbers is not completely positive. Finite measurement noise, neglected affine offsets or changing preparation could be responsible. Compare the fit with uncertainty regions and a constrained process model; simply forcing each measured contraction coefficient into \([-1,1]\) cannot repair this violation. As a constructive control, \(\lambda=(0.8,0.7,0.6)\) gives \(p_0=0.775,\ p_x=0.125,\ p_y=0.075,\ p_z=0.025\), all valid and summing to one. A simulator using these four random Pauli operations on entangled inputs then supplies a direct check of the estimated channel beyond single-qubit positivity.

Prove when two different event descriptions give the same channel

A given unconditioned quantum operation does not determine a unique set of environmental records. Suppose a set of Kraus operators \(K_j\) describes a channel. If an environment detector resolves an alternative orthonormal basis related by unitary \(U=(u_{\alpha j})\), its new system operators are \(\tilde K_\alpha=\sum_j u_{\alpha j}K_j\). Summing the unnormalised conditional outputs gives

\[\sum_{\alpha,j,k}u_{\alpha j}u_{\alpha k}^*K_j\rho K_k^\dagger=\sum_jK_j\rho K_j^\dagger.\]

This calculation explains why environment-dependent state trajectories can be quite different while every unconditioned prediction on the system agrees. A simulator's choice of individual jump records only describes a particular monitoring scheme when the environment observable actually corresponds to those records.

Consider complete dephasing in the \(z\) basis. One representation is \(K_0=|0\rangle\langle0|,\ K_1=|1\rangle\langle1|\), an ideal path measurement with its result forgotten. Rotate the two environment labels by the Hadamard matrix to obtain

\[\tilde K_+=(I/\sqrt2),\ \tilde K_-=(\sigma_z/\sqrt2).\]

These describe equally likely identity or phase-flip operations. For input \(|+x\rangle\), the first recorded instrument conditionally gives \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\); the second gives \(|+x\rangle\) or \(|-x\rangle\). Both produce \(I/2\) if their outcome is ignored. For initial \(|0\rangle\) the first record is deterministic, while the second remains equally random and leaves the state unchanged up to a phase. Measuring only the system after discarding the labels cannot tell which set was used, but correlations with a retained environmental measurement can immediately tell them apart.

The same distinction is useful for a partially dephasing environment. If \(|0\rangle,|1\rangle\) become correlated with environment states of real overlap \(c\), the qubit's transverse Bloch vector contracts by \(c\). For \(-1\le c\le1\) a random phase-flip representation has probabilities \(p_I=(1+c)/2,\ p_Z=(1-c)/2\). For \(c<0\) a fringe phase reversal accompanies partial contraction; a reported positive “visibility” loses that sign. One can separate a deterministic phase rotation from the magnitude of random or entangling dephasing if the phase reference is maintained. Over independent identical intervals a real \(c\) multiplies repeatedly; the coherence after \(n\) steps is \(c^n\). If the same randomly chosen phase flip is instead held fixed throughout all intervals, successive errors are correlated and the even-step coherence can return to one. A single-step channel tomography cannot identify the environmental memory shared across steps.

Tell apart a memoryless phase-flip channel and a fixed hidden phase

Both devices individually implement \(p_Z=0.20\) over a calibrated interval for freshly prepared qubits. In device A the phase-flip draws at successive intervals are independent. In device B one draw is held fixed for both consecutive intervals. Predict \(+x\) readout after one and two intervals for an initial \(|+x\rangle\).

Derive and check the result

One interval leaves \(x=1-2p_Z=0.60\), so both devices give

\[P(+x)=0.80.\]

Independence in A gives \(x=0.60^2=0.36\) after two intervals, or \(P(+x)=0.68\). For B, two identical phase flips multiply to identity whether the hidden choice was identity or \(Z\), hence \(P(+x)=1\). In both devices the one-step process applied to an independently prepared ensemble is consistent with the measured completely positive map. Composing that map twice implicitly assumed fresh, independent environment conditions; a memory experiment tests exactly that assumption. Observing a revival thus need not imply an energy input, a negative probability or a nonunitary failure in the larger coupled system.

Distinguish dipole coherence from an excited-state population lifetime

Consider a single initially excited emitter in a memoryless zero-temperature environment with radiative population decay \(\Gamma\) and no additional dephasing. The probability density of emitting at time \(t\ge0\) is \(\Gamma e^{-\Gamma t}\). A normalised single-photon temporal mode consistent with this ideal undistinguished radiative channel is \(g(t)=\sqrt{\Gamma}\,e^{-\Gamma t/2}e^{-i\omega_0t}\) on \(t\ge0\). Its field amplitude decays twice as slowly as the population probability. Use the Fourier convention \(\tilde g(\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int g(t)e^{i\omega t}dt\). The half-line exponential integral gives

\[|\tilde g(\omega)|^2=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi[(\Gamma/2)^2+(\omega-\omega_0)^2]} .\]

It integrates to one in angular frequency. At a detuning of \(\Gamma/2\) the intensity has half its peak, so the full angular-frequency linewidth is \(\Gamma\), or \(\Gamma/(2\pi)\) in hertz. Using the population decay directly as a field amplitude would predict a linewidth twice too large and the wrong wave-packet envelope. The transform-limited result requires a single radiative mode in the sense of a pure emitted temporal state and absence of additional phase randomisation. Extra pure dephasing broadens the spectrum while population decay may remain unchanged.

For \(\Gamma^{-1}=10.0\) ns the ideal linewidth is 15.9 MHz. Add independent homogeneous pure dephasing at \(\Gamma_\phi=2.0\times10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The first-order dipole correlation decays at \(\Gamma/2+\Gamma_\phi\), giving a full line width \((\Gamma/2+\Gamma_\phi)/\pi=22.3\) MHz. The photon detection time distribution can still have 10 ns decay. A measured line twice its nominal transform-limited width might indicate pure dephasing, but spatially varying frequencies, slow source drift or spectral resolution can also broaden the observed distribution. A lifetime measurement alone does not calibrate those effects.

The acquisition window has an independently calculable consequence. If only photons arriving before time \(T\) are retained, their normalised temporal mode is a truncated exponential divided by \(\sqrt{1-e^{-\Gamma T}}\) in this ideal pure-emission model. Its Fourier amplitude gains factor \(1-e^{-[\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)]T}\) in the numerator. At \(T\ll1/\Gamma\) the shape approaches the squared sinc of a short rectangular temporal window. Selecting only early photons can thus broaden the spectrum even though the emitter and its environment are unchanged. A time gate, an energy filter and unconditioned counting describe different optical preparations and different uncertainty budgets.

Infer the transform-limited fraction from a linewidth and test the model

A homogeneous line has measured weak-drive full width 25.0 MHz and population lifetime 10.0 ns. Compute \(T_2\) and the implied pure dephasing rate assuming ideal Lorentzian broadening. Which second measurement would discriminate this inference from static sample inhomogeneity?

Derive and check the result

With angular half width \(1/T_2\), the frequency full width is \(1/(\pi T_2)\), so \(T_2=12.73\) ns. Subtracting \(1/(2T_1)=5.00\times10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) from \(1/T_2=7.85\times10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) gives

\[\Gamma_\phi=2.85\times10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.\]

The coherence relative to the radiative limit is

\[T_2/(2T_1)=0.637.\]

Compare a time-domain echo, a single-emitter linewidth or spectra over short acquisition segments with the ensemble line. A recoverable component of contrast or slowly wandering line centres would indicate a static or slow contribution instead of assigning every megahertz to homogeneous phase damping.

Recover a pure-dephasing rate and assess a conflicting fit

A population measurement gives

\[T_1=30.0\,\mu\mathrm s;\]

homogeneous Ramsey contrast gives

\[T_2=24.0\,\mu\mathrm s.\]

Find \(T_\phi\). What would a reported \(T_2=80\,\mu\mathrm s\) imply in the same model?

Work through the solution

Subtract \(1/(60\,\mu{\rm s})\) from \(1/(24\,\mu{\rm s})\) to get \(\Gamma_\phi=0.0250\,\mu{\rm s}^{-1}\), hence \(T_\phi=40.0\,\mu\mathrm s\). Replacing 24 by 80 yields a negative pure dephasing rate, incompatible with the specified independent time-homogeneous channels. Check the fitted envelope, uncertainties, thermally shifted equilibrium population and whether the two times were measured under comparable conditions. A negative fitted number is evidence about the model or data interpretation, not a physically negative random-event rate.

Calculate an inefficient no-click update

The initial excited probability is one half, the decay probability for an initially excited qubit during the window is 0.40, and a true emitted excitation is detected with efficiency 0.75. Find the probability of no click, its conditional excited fraction and the unconditioned population.

Work through the solution

A real emission occurs with probability \(0.5\times0.40=0.20\); a click is seen with probability 0.15, so no click has probability 0.85. Remaining excited and no click occurs with probability \(0.5(1-0.40)=0.30\). Conditional excitation is \(0.30/0.85=0.3529\); the unconditioned excitation is 0.30 since clicked branches are ground. For an initially coherent preparation the no-click off-diagonal term must also be updated by grouping the undetected-emission and true-no-emission Kraus outcomes. Simply replacing \(p\) by detector efficiency times \(p\) would predict the wrong physical excitation loss.

Diagnose a nonphysical time step and repair it

Apply one forward-Euler step to \(\dot p_1=-\Gamma p_1\) with initially excited state and \(\Gamma\Delta t=1.2\). Compute the prediction and an exact completely positive alternative. Would enforcing unit trace fix Euler's failure?

Work through the solution

Euler gives \(p_1=1-1.2=-0.2,\ p_0=1.2\), already with exact trace one. The finite-time amplitude-damping channel has \(p=1-e^{-1.2}=0.6988\), giving \(p_1=0.3012\). For this simple model the exact Kraus step can be applied repeatedly at arbitrary duration. In a driven model an operator-splitting scheme with unitary Hamiltonian substeps and completely positive damping substeps maintains positivity while introducing a controllable splitting error. Check convergence by halving the step and comparing with an independent observable. Renormalising trace or clipping a negative eigenvalue changes the numerical trajectory without diagnosing the original time-step error.
