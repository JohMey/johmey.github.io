From a finite pulse to a continuum transition rate

Switch a weak perturbation on for a measured duration. Its Fourier transform determines the amplitude of a discrete transition. Summing over a dense set of final states reveals where a time-independent transition rate comes from and where it fails.

Remove the known free evolution before approximating

Let \(H_0|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\) and \(H(t)=H_0+V(t)\). Expand \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle\). Substituting into the time-dependent Schrödinger equation cancels the free phase derivatives and leaves an exact equation \(i\hbar\dot c_f=\sum_n e^{i(E_f-E_n)t/\hbar} V_{fn}(t)c_n(t)\). Suppose the initial state is \(|i\rangle\) and the perturbation is appreciable only between \(0\) and \(T\). Iterating the integral equation once, replacing \(c_n(t)\) on the right by \(\delta_{ni}\), yields

\[c_f^{(1)}(T)=-\frac i\hbar\int_0^T e^{i\omega_{fi}t}V_{fi}(t)\,dt,\]
\[\omega_{fi}=(E_f-E_i)/\hbar .\]

For \(f\ne i\) the leading transition probability is \(|c_f^{(1)}|^2\). It is second order in the perturbation even though the amplitude is first order. Conservation of total probability to this order requires a second-order correction to the initial amplitude. Simply appending all positive final probabilities while keeping the initial probability exactly one would fail that test. The approximation is controlled when total probability removed from the initial state is small over the observation duration and higher-order pathways are negligible.

Let the resonant component of a drive have matrix element \(V_{fi}(t)=v e^{-i\omega t}\) for \(0<t<T\). For a real cosine perturbation the two exponentials each carry half the real peak amplitude, so \(v\) refers to just the near-resonant part. Define \(\Delta=\omega_{fi}-\omega\). Direct integration gives

\[P_f^{(1)}=\frac{|v|^2T^2}{\hbar^2}\operatorname{sinc}^2(\Delta T/2),\]
\[\operatorname{sinc}x=\sin x/x .\]

At resonance the amplitude grows linearly in time while its phase remains coherent. A detuned contribution winds around in the complex plane and largely cancels. The first zeros occur at ordinary detuning \(\pm1/T\) in hertz. The full width at half height is approximately \(0.886/T\) in ordinary frequency since \(\sin x/x=1/\sqrt2\) at \(x\simeq1.3916\). Doubling duration quadruples the line-centre probability for one final state but halves the linewidth, a distinction central to interpreting a continuum rate.

Sum over final states before taking a long-time limit

A large box provides a discrete set of final levels with spacing \(\delta E\); at fixed finite time many can lie inside the pulse's energy bandwidth. Let \(\rho(E)\) be the density of the accessible final states per joule, including degeneracy exactly once. Replace the sum by an integral with \(E_*=E_i+\hbar\omega\):

\[P_{\rm out}\simeq\frac{T^2}{\hbar^2}\int dE\,\rho(E)|v(E)|^2\operatorname{sinc}^2\left[\frac{(E-E_*)T}{2\hbar}\right].\]

If \(\rho|v|^2\) changes little across the dominant width and the line is well away from thresholds, take it outside the integral. Substitute \(u=(E-E_*)T/(2\hbar)\), and use \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\sin u/u)^2du=\pi\). The area of the squared Fourier transform grows linearly, giving

\[\Gamma=\frac{P_{\rm out}}T\simeq\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|v(E_*)|^2\rho(E_*).\]

The identity for the sinc area also follows by Parseval from the energy in the rectangular time window. This is Fermi's golden rule with \(\rho\) per energy. If a supplied density is per unit angular frequency,

\[\rho_\omega=\hbar\rho_E;\]

failing to change that measure introduces a factor of \(\hbar\). The units are a strong check: energy squared times states per energy divided by action is inverse seconds.

Several limits have been taken in a definite order. The perturbation lasts long enough to resolve the scale on which \(\rho|v|^2\) varies but short enough that many levels still fit in its bandwidth. A window of times must exist between a reservoir correlation time and a recurrence time of order \(2\pi\hbar/\delta E\). In addition \(\Gamma T\ll1\) for the first-order depletion argument. Taking \(T\to\infty\) at fixed finite box instead resolves individual levels and can produce coherent oscillations or recurrences. Extending the constant rate to a full exponential decay uses a further memoryless approximation and renewed weak transitions of the remaining population. A linear short-time fit by itself does not establish exponential dynamics at every time.

Keep matrix elements and density normalisation consistent

For illustration choose \(|v|=5.00\times10^{-6}\) eV and \(\rho=20.0\ \mathrm{eV^{-1}}\) for box-normalised accessible modes dense enough on the pulse scale, giving \(\Gamma\simeq4.77\times10^6\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) as a formal product. But the implied mean level spacing \(1/\rho=0.050\) eV corresponds to a recurrence scale of only \(8.27\times10^{-14}\) s. Using a nanosecond pulse with this literal finite density violates the continuum criterion, irrespective of how small \(\Gamma T\) looks. This numerical mismatch is a useful trap: reproducing a golden-rule number to many digits does not ensure the system supports irreversible decay in the chosen time range.

Now enlarge the quantisation volume by \(10^6\) for continuum-normalised physical coupling. The number of accessible states per energy grows by \(10^6\), while each squared matrix element shrinks by \(10^6\) for a localised initial state and individually unit-normalised extended final states. The same product gives the same \(\Gamma\), but spacing is now \(5.0\times10^{-8}\) eV and the recurrence scale 82.7 ns. At \(T=2.0\) ns, \(\Gamma T=0.00954\) and roughly \(h/(T\delta E)\simeq41\) spacings fit up to the first positive zero. Whether density and coupling are locally smooth on that bandwidth remains an additional condition. The scaling removes explicit box dependence from a physical rate while clarifying why direct sums at finite volume can require larger spaces.

Use phase space to predict the shape of a continuum edge

For free relative motion in three dimensions, count single scalar internal channels in a cubic periodic volume \(\mathcal V\). One state occupies volume \((2\pi)^3/\mathcal V\) in wave-vector space. The shell between \(k,k+dk\) thus contains \(\mathcal V4\pi k^2dk/(2\pi)^3\). From \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\) it follows that \(\rho(E)=\mathcal V m k/(2\pi^2\hbar^2)\), which vanishes like \(\sqrt E\) at threshold. In one dimension the corresponding free density diverges like \(1/\sqrt E\); the geometrical shell measure is different. A threshold suppression observed in one spectrum could come from available states, the coupling matrix element, or both.

If a local transition couples chiefly to a final orbital partial wave \(l\), its regular low-\(k\) free radial factor near the source scales as \(k^l\) when the potential is sufficiently short ranged and no threshold resonance changes the law. Squaring and multiplying by the three-dimensional density gives

\[\Gamma_l\propto k^{2l+1}\propto E^{l+1/2}.\]

For a \(p\)-wave channel doubling the excess energy increases this ideal rate by \(2^{3/2}=2.83\). Long-range Coulomb fields alter the final-state radial behaviour and can invalidate the short-range threshold power. Very close to any edge a finite pulse straddles energies where the density changes rapidly or vanishes. Replace the local golden-rule product by the finite-window integral to model the measured onset.

Compare pulse shapes at equal area and equal energy

The rectangular pulse is useful because its Fourier transform is elementary. Its abrupt start and end also generate sidelobes that can excite nearby unwanted transitions. For a general narrowband drive write its resonant matrix element \(v(t)e^{-i\omega t}\). First order gives

\[P_f=|\int v(t)e^{i\Delta t}dt|^2/\hbar^2.\]

At zero detuning only the complex pulse area \(\int v(t)dt\) matters. After summing over a flat continuum, Parseval gives \(\int P_f(\Delta)d\Delta=(2\pi/\hbar^2)\int|v(t)|^2dt\), so it is the temporal integral of squared coupling that controls total spectral weight. Two pulses with the same area can have different continuum probabilities; two with the same integrated intensity can have different resonant peak heights.

For a Gaussian envelope \(v(t)=v_0e^{-t^2/(2\tau^2)}\) on the full line, complete the square in its Fourier integral. The amplitude is \(v_0\sqrt{2\pi}\tau e^{-\Delta^2\tau^2/2}\), so the transition profile has no sidelobes and has full half-height angular width \(2\sqrt{\ln2}/\tau\). The time intensity is \(|v_0|^2e^{-t^2/\tau^2}\), with temporal full half-height width \(2\sqrt{\ln2}\tau\). Multiplying this by the ordinary-frequency full width gives

\[2\ln2/\pi=0.441.\]

Replacing an amplitude width by an intensity width changes that product by \(\sqrt2\); specifying the plotted quantity is necessary when comparing a spectrum with temporal photodetector data.

Take a Gaussian with \(\tau=0.50\) ms. Its spectral intensity full width is \(530\) Hz and pulse intensity full temporal width \(0.833\) ms. An unwanted line 1.0 kHz away receives a relative first-order probability \(e^{-(2\pi1000\tau)^2}\simeq5.17\times10^{-5}\). A nominally same-duration rectangular pulse may place an ideal zero near some precisely chosen detuning, but neighbouring frequencies generally see sidelobes. Which pulse gives smaller unwanted excitation depends on what quantity is fixed, where the extra lines lie and how much uncertainty there is in their positions. A shaped envelope can suppress spectral leakage without improving every possible resolution metric.

A pair of pulses illustrates how coherently adding amplitudes differs from broadening a single envelope. Let identical, short pulses of area \(A\) be separated by start-time difference \(T_s\), and let the second have a controlled drive phase \(\phi\). At a detuning small compared with each pulse bandwidth, the amplitude is proportional to \(A[1+e^{i(\Delta T_s+\phi)}]\). Its squared magnitude has fringes \(2|A|^2[1+\cos(\Delta T_s+\phi)]\) inside the broader single-pulse line. Their ordinary-frequency spacing \(1/T_s\) can be much smaller than the inverse individual pulse duration. Randomising the relative phase removes fringes while keeping the two single-pulse energies unchanged. The observed fine spectral structure is therefore a measurement of coherent time separation as well as energy difference.

Derive depletion and the energy shift from the same kernel

The first-order rate gives initial population loss in a useful time window. To see which extra assumptions allow exponential decay, retain a single initial discrete level coupled to many initially empty final levels with time-independent couplings \(v_f\) in a rotating frame. Let \(c_i(0)=1,\ c_f(0)=0\). In the interaction picture \(\dot c_f=-(i/\hbar)v_f e^{i\omega_{fi}t}c_i(t)\). Integrate this equation exactly in terms of the unknown \(c_i\) and insert the result in its partner equation for \(\dot c_i\). Assuming final levels do not couple directly to one another gives

\[\dot c_i(t)=-\int_0^t K(t-t')c_i(t')\,dt',\]
\[K(\tau)=\hbar^{-2}\sum_f |v_f|^2e^{-i\omega_{fi}\tau}.\]

No irreversibility has been assumed in this elimination. The previous discrete amplitudes are encoded in a memory kernel and can feed back into the initial level. At very short times \(c_i(t)\simeq1-K(0)t^2/2\) when the second moment is finite, yielding a quadratic survival loss. At long times a sharply structured or bounded continuum can produce nonexponential decay. A finite number of exactly known final levels evolves unitarily under the full Hamiltonian and can show recurrences; treating the memory as identically zero after any arbitrarily short interval would discard this possibility.

When the spectral coupling varies slowly over the line and extends across a sufficiently broad band, phase cancellation makes \(K(\tau)\) decay over a correlation time \(\tau_c\). For \(\tau_c\) short relative to changes in \(c_i\), write \(c_i(t-\tau)\approx c_i(t)\) inside its memory integral after the initial transient, and extend its upper limit to infinity. The regulated integral \(\int_0^\infty e^{-i\omega\tau-\eta\tau}d\tau=(\eta+i\omega)^{-1}\) tends to \(\pi\delta(\omega)-i\,\mathcal P(1/\omega)\) as a distribution. Its real part gives

\[\dot c_i=-\Gamma c_i/2+\cdots,\]

with the same golden-rule population rate \(\Gamma\). The imaginary part gives a coherent energy shift proportional to \(\mathcal P\sum_f |v_f|^2/(E_i-E_f)\), requiring the physical bandwidth and a consistent bare energy. This shows how an absorptive linewidth and a dispersive shift can emerge from different parts of the same elimination, without claiming a perfectly flat infinite continuum supplies a physically meaningful absolute shift.

For a symmetric model with constant spectral strength inside \(-W<\omega_{fi}<W\), the kernel is proportional to \(\sin(W\tau)/\tau\). Narrowing the band increases its memory time and can delay the onset of an approximately constant rate. Moving the discrete level close to a band edge also removes the cancellation between positive and negative detunings responsible for a vanishing symmetric shift. An experiment that varies the carrier across such an edge should fit the full finite-window spectrum before inferring a time-independent linewidth or level shift. A numerically cheap sum over known discrete final energies provides a check: double the spectral cutoff, halve level spacing and track the early quadratic regime separately from any intermediate exponential segment.

Ask whether frequent projective tests inhibit a transition

For an initial pure state with finite energy variance under a time-independent \(H\), expand the survival amplitude \(A(t)=\langle i|e^{-iHt/\hbar}|i\rangle\) to second order. The linear term \(-it\langle H\rangle/\hbar\) is a phase. Squaring gives

\[P_i(t)=1-(\Delta H)^2t^2/\hbar^2+O(t^3),\]

with still higher-order control depending on available moments. Define \(t_Z=\hbar/\Delta H\). In the model with one initial level coupled only off-diagonally to the final levels, \((\Delta H)^2=\sum_f|v_f|^2\), agreeing with the memory-kernel expansion. If the needed moments diverge in an idealised continuum, this short-time expansion requires a physical high-frequency regularisation.

Now make ideal instantaneous projective tests of whether the system is still in \(|i\rangle\) every \(\tau=T/N\), selecting only histories that have passed every test. Conditional on survival, each projection prepares exactly the same initial state. The joint survival probability is \(P_i(\tau)^N\simeq[1-(T/Nt_Z)^2]^N\to1\) as \(N\to\infty\) for fixed total duration \(T\). This is a mathematically precise limiting model of the quantum Zeno effect. It is not obtained by simply looking at a stored record more often after a single final measurement; the successive physical interventions reset the conditional state.

With a resonantly coupled two-state Hamiltonian \(H=v\sigma_x\), the survival against a single interval is \(\cos^2(v\tau/\hbar)\). Choose \(vT/\hbar=\pi/2\); unobserved survival at \(T\) is zero. For ten equally spaced perfect tests the joint survival is

\[\cos^{20}(\pi/20)=0.7805.\]

If the instrument has 2% independent loss per successful test, the recorded joint fraction falls to

\[0.7805(0.98)^{10}=0.638.\]

A finite-duration detector may also change \(H\) or broaden spectral coupling rather than implement an instantaneous projection. The ideal repeated-measurement result gives a comparison baseline, while predicted observed counts must include the physical instrument and conditioning.

Derive the sweep-rate scale for a transition through resonance

For a chirped weak drive let its instantaneous detuning from one discrete transition change linearly, \(\Delta(t)=\dot\Delta\,t\), crossing zero at the middle of a long pulse. The phase accumulated in the first-order integral is \(\int^t\Delta(t')dt'=\dot\Delta\,t^2/2\), not \(\Delta(t)t\). For approximately constant coupling \(v\) around the crossing and sufficiently far endpoints the oscillatory Gaussian integral gives \(|c_f^{(1)}|^2\simeq2\pi|v|^2/(\hbar^2|\dot\Delta|)\). A slower passage accumulates a larger resonant amplitude, but this weak-coupling formula cannot exceed probability one. At large transfer the full two-level avoided-crossing dynamics replaces it.

For \(|v|/\hbar=2\pi\times200\) Hz and an ordinary-frequency sweep \(d(\Delta/2\pi)/dt=10^8\) Hz/s, the estimate is \(0.0158\). Changing only the sign of the sweep gives the same first-order probability in this symmetric idealisation, while the phase of the transition amplitude changes. Coherently driving a second passage can convert that phase difference into an observable interference pattern. Missing endpoints, asymmetric pulse shaping and additional nearby lines can spoil the simple symmetry. Comparing both sweep directions and checking that the result scales as inverse speed over a low-transfer range are concrete tests of the stationary-phase approximation instead of assuming every apparent line crossing signifies complete adiabatic transfer.

Derive the spectral distinction between a phase-coherent pulse and stationary noise

A fluctuating perturbation is often characterised by a power spectral density rather than a prescribed pulse phase. Suppose \(V_{fi}(t)=d_{fi}F(t)\) with \(F\) a real classical, zero-mean stationary random process and the coupling weak enough for first-order amplitudes in each realisation. Averaging the modulus squared produces a double time integral involving the autocorrelation \(C_F(\tau)=\langle F(t+\tau)F(t)\rangle\):

\[\langle P_f(T)\rangle=\frac{|d_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\int_{-T}^{T}(T-|\tau|)C_F(\tau)e^{i\omega_{fi}\tau}\,d\tau.\]

The factor \(T-|\tau|\) counts the length of pairs of times in \([0,T]\) separated by \(\tau\). If \(T\) is long compared with the correlation time and depletion remains weak, divide by \(T\) to find rate \(|d_{fi}|^2S_F(\omega_{fi})/\hbar^2\), where \(S_F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty C_F(\tau)e^{i\omega\tau}d\tau\) is the two-sided angular-frequency spectrum. No sum over a continuum of final quantum states was involved here. A continuum of drive frequencies supplies a different physical route to an approximately linear transition probability of one resolved final state.

For exponentially correlated noise \(C_F(\tau)=F_{\rm rms}^2e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}\), integration gives

\[S_F(\omega)=2F_{\rm rms}^2\tau_c/[1+\omega^2\tau_c^2].\]

Fix \(F_{\rm rms}\) and vary correlation time. At a nonzero fixed transition frequency, extremely rapid fluctuations have little integrated spectral weight there because their fixed variance is spread over a very broad band. Very slow fluctuations concentrate their weight near zero frequency. Differentiating the spectral expression gives a maximum excitation rate at \(\tau_c=1/|\omega_{fi}|\). This nonmonotonic dependence cannot be inferred just by quoting the field's total rms amplitude. Unit conventions are also critical: changing to ordinary-frequency spectral densities and a one-sided instrument display requires the corresponding Jacobian and factor of two.

A real classical stationary process has an even spectrum \(S_F(-\omega)=S_F(\omega)\), so this model drives upward and downward transitions equally per available source population when matrix elements are conjugate. A quantum environment at thermal equilibrium can have unequal positive and negative frequency unsymmetrised spectra. Its absorption and emission probabilities encode detailed balance. Thus an observed difference between calibrated upward and downward rates cannot generally be explained by merely drawing samples from a real classical noise spectrum of the same symmetrised power. Distinguishing a driven classical source from an equilibrium quantum bath requires precise statements about which correlations enter the transition probability.

Infer a fluctuation timescale from a rate ratio

Two transitions with equal coupling strengths see the same exponentially correlated classical field at ordinary frequencies \(f\) and \(2f\). Their measured weak transition rates obey \(\Gamma(2f)/\Gamma(f)=0.40\). Infer the dimensionless correlation frequency and, for \(f=50.0\) kHz, the correlation time. What feature of the acquisition would invalidate this inference?

Derive and check the result

Let \(x=(2\pi f\tau_c)^2\). The ratio is \((1+x)/(1+4x)=0.40\), giving \(0.60=0.60x\) and \(x=1\). The correlation time is

\[1/(2\pi f)=3.18\,\mu\mathrm s.\]

At the lower frequency the noise spectrum lies at half its zero-frequency value; the higher line samples one fifth of that value. If the exposure time is comparable to or shorter than \(\tau_c\), the triangular window in the double integral still influences both rates and the infinite-time Lorentzian ratio is inadequate. Likewise unequal matrix elements or a finite observation band must be corrected separately. Measuring a third transition at another frequency supplies an independent test of the exponential-correlation model rather than simply determining its fitted timescale.

Measure a frequency width without confusing two line shapes

A rectangular pulse lasts 4.00 ms with resonant coupling \(|v|/\hbar=25.0\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Find the line-centre probability, separation of the first zeros and full half-height width in hertz.

Work through the solution

At resonance first-order probability is

\[(25\times0.004)^2=0.0100.\]

The first zeros are 250 Hz on either side, giving a 500 Hz separation. The full half-height width is

\[0.886/0.004=221.5\,\mathrm{Hz}.\]

A Lorentzian fit with its width interpreted as a spontaneous lifetime would mistake pulse broadening for decay. Doubling \(T\) while keeping coupling fixed is a diagnostic: the first-order peak would rise fourfold and the width halve. At large peak probability use the full two-level dynamics rather than extrapolating the quadratic indefinitely.

Verify the breakdown against exact two-level dynamics

Assume an isolated resonantly driven two-state system with rotating-frame Hamiltonian off-diagonal \(v\) and zero detuning. What replaces the first-order expression when \(T=\pi\hbar/(2|v|)\)? At which small pulse area is the fractional error approximately one percent?

Work through the solution

The resonant Hamiltonian squares to \(|v|^2I\), so the exact probability is \(\sin^2(|v|T/\hbar)\). At the stated pulse time it is unity while the first-order result is \((\pi/2)^2=2.47\), impossible as a probability. For pulse area \(a=|v|T/\hbar\),

\[\sin^2a=a^2-a^4/3+\cdots.\]

The relative discrepancy is approximately \(a^2/3\), reaching 1% at \(a\simeq0.173\). The pulse can already make a measurable few-percent transition while first-order theory remains reasonably accurate. A dense continuum requires checking the total across all levels as well.

Infer an accessible density from the observed time dependence

At long-enough but weak-depletion times an ensemble loses 2.0% population in 5.0 ns. The squared box matrix element is calibrated as \(2.5\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm{eV^2}\). Infer \(\rho\), estimate the recurrence scale and test whether this interpretation is internally consistent.

Work through the solution

The slope estimate is \(4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). With \(\hbar=6.5821\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{eV\,s}\) the inferred \(\rho=\Gamma\hbar/(2\pi|v|^2)=1.68\times10^7\,\mathrm{eV^{-1}}\). The corresponding spacing is \(5.97\times10^{-8}\) eV and recurrence scale \(h/\delta E\simeq69\) ns. A 5 ns observation fits within that scale, with about 14 spacings to the first zero. Two percent is compatible with weak depletion. Smoothness of the density and a single accessible channel still require independent spectral checks. Repeating the measurement at several durations reveals whether the inferred slope is stable.
