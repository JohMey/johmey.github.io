Two electrons, exchange splitting and the cost of localisation

Build a two-electron state that respects exchange, derive its charge and spin correlations, and then solve a two-site model exactly. The two examples reveal when a triplet is favoured and when an apparently weak hopping lowers the singlet instead.

Construct states before introducing a force

A two-electron wavefunction has two spatial arguments and two spin labels. The labels 1 and 2 identify argument slots; they do not attach persistent tags to individual identical electrons. Exchanging all position and spin variables must multiply an electronic state by minus one. This is the fermionic exchange postulate. It constrains which vectors describe electrons even when their Hamiltonian contains no spin-dependent term. A measurement with no access to the ordering of slots is represented by an observable symmetric under the same exchange.

Let \(a(\mathbf r),b(\mathbf r)\) be normalised orthogonal spatial orbitals. The symmetric and antisymmetric spatial combinations are \(\Phi_\pm=[a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)]/\sqrt2\). Combine \(\Phi_+\) with the spin singlet \((|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\), and \(\Phi_-\) with any of the three symmetric triplet spin states. For equal positions the antisymmetric spatial combination vanishes exactly. Its joint probability has an exchange hole at coincidence even for noninteracting same-spin fermions. When \(a=b\), the antisymmetric combination vanishes identically and only the symmetric spatial state with opposite spins in a singlet is available. Occupation of the same spatial mode by two electrons is thus compatible with the exclusion principle if the full spin orbitals differ.

For two distinct orthogonal modes, both \(\Phi_+\) and \(\Phi_-\) have the same one-particle number density \(|a(\mathbf r)|^2+|b(\mathbf r)|^2\), normalised to two electrons. To see this, integrate \(|\Phi_\pm(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)|^2\) over \(\mathbf r_2\) and multiply by two. Cross terms contain \(\langle a|b\rangle=0\). Coincidence and correlation measurements can therefore distinguish preparations that share their entire one-particle density. The spin-antisymmetric singlet can also give correlated opposite spin outcomes along any common measurement axis, while both individual spins are unpolarised. Position and spin comparisons probe different reduced descriptions of the same total state.

Separate the direct and exchange integrals

For an independent-orbital estimate take \(H=h(1)+h(2)+v(1,2)\) with \(h a=\epsilon_a a,\ h b=\epsilon_b b\), and a symmetric spin-independent repulsion. Expand the expectation of \(v\) in \(\Phi_\pm\). Diagonal terms give the same direct integral and off-diagonal terms are conjugates:

\[J_{ab}=\iint |a(1)|^2 v(1,2)|b(2)|^2\,d^3r_1d^3r_2,\qquad\]
\[K_{ab}=\iint a^*(1)b(1)v(1,2)b^*(2)a(2)\,d^3r_1d^3r_2 .\]

For Coulomb repulsion \(K_{ab}\) is nonnegative: it is the positive Coulomb quadratic form of the transition density \(a^*b\), seen for instance from its Fourier representation weighted by \(4\pi/k^2\). Orthonormality only makes the spatial integral of the transition density vanish, not its weighted self-interaction. The expectation energies are \(\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b+J_{ab}\pm K_{ab}\). In this fixed pair of spatial orbitals the triplet is lower by \(2K_{ab}\). If the orbitals have disjoint support the exchange integral vanishes, although the direct repulsion need not. A splitting caused by spatial symmetry does not require an extra microscopic spin–spin repulsive force.

Set \(J_{ab}=0.80\) eV and \(K_{ab}=0.070\) eV in an illustrative excited configuration. Its Coulomb shifts are 0.87 eV and 0.73 eV. A weighted average over all four spin states is

\[J-K/2=0.765\,\mathrm{eV}.\]

Optical line strengths generally do not populate those four spin states equally. The energy difference is a statement about this restricted configuration; another spatial occupation can place a singlet below the entire triplet manifold. Predicting a ground-state spin therefore requires admitting the competing occupations and the one-body and correlation energies they carry.

Allow hopping and double occupation

Now take two orthonormal localised orbitals \(L,R\) of equal one-body energy. A fermion hops between them at amplitude \(-t\), and placing opposite spins on the same site costs \(U>0\). Define \(H=-t\sum_\sigma(c^\dagger_{L\sigma}c_{R\sigma}+c^\dagger_{R\sigma}c_{L\sigma})+U\sum_i n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}\). This is a specified two-site Hubbard model, not a complete microscopic Coulomb Hamiltonian. Its direct intersite exchange integral is set to zero here, so its spin ordering need not follow the fixed-orbital calculation above. Position, spin and field units enter through the experimentally or computationally determined \(t,U\).

In the two-electron sector a state with two up spins must occupy one orbital on each site. Hopping would create two up spins in one orbital and therefore annihilates this state. Rotational symmetry implies the same energy zero for all three triplets. Define the singly occupied singlet \(|S\rangle\) and symmetric doubly occupied state \(|D_+\rangle=(|\uparrow\downarrow,0\rangle+|0,\uparrow\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\). A consistent fermionic ordering gives an off-diagonal \(-2t\) between them; each direction and spin contributes and the normalisations leave the factor two. Changing the phase of \(D_+\) reverses that matrix element but leaves the eigenvalues unchanged. The orthogonal antisymmetric doublon remains at energy \(U\). The relevant singlet block is

\[H_S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2t\\-2t&U\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_{\pm}=\frac{U\pm\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}}{2}.\]

The ground energy \(E_-<0\) for \(t\ne0\). At \(U\gg|t|\) expand the square root to obtain

\[E_-\simeq-4t^2/U+16t^4/U^3+\cdots.\]

The singlet–triplet splitting \(J_{\rm eff}=E_T-E_-\simeq4t^2/U\) can be represented in the singly occupied subspace by \(J_{\rm eff}(\mathbf S_L\cdot\mathbf S_R/\hbar^2-1/4)\). The singlet can lower its energy by virtual double occupation while spin symmetry blocks the triplets. The inferred spin coupling scales as the square of hopping and inversely with the energy cost. This mechanism is commonly called antiferromagnetic superexchange.

Determine a correlation without reading an eigenvector

The fraction of probability with double occupation in the ground state is \(\langle\partial H/\partial U\rangle\). Differentiating its energy gives

\[D=\tfrac12[1-U/\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}].\]

For positive large \(U\), \(D\simeq4t^2/U^2\). Comparing the derivative with the explicit eigenvector provides a useful independent test of signs and normalisations. From \(E s=-2t d\) one obtains

\[d/s=-E/(2t).\]

Normalising gives \(D=E^2/(4t^2+E^2)\), identical to the derivative expression for either lower-branch parameter value.

For \(t=10\) meV and \(U=100\) meV, \(\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}=107.70\) meV and the exact splitting is 3.85 meV. The leading 4.00 meV expression is about 3.9% high. Double occupation is only 3.58%, yet forbidding it at the outset would predict exactly zero splitting. A virtually occupied state can matter appreciably in a low-energy Hamiltonian even when a projective charge measurement rarely finds it occupied. At \(U=0\) the ground energy is \(-2|t|\), corresponding to two opposite spins in the bonding one-electron orbital; the double-occupation fraction is one half. The large-\(U\) expression diverges as \(U\to0\) because its perturbative premise disappears. The exact two-state matrix remains finite.

Derive an effective screening charge for a helium-like ground state

A two-electron atom provides a continuous-space counterpart to the two-site model. In atomic units set \(\hbar=m_e=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=1\) and take an infinitely heavy nucleus of charge \(Z\). The nonrelativistic Hamiltonian is

\[H=\sum_{i=1}^2[-\nabla_i^2/2-Z/r_i]+1/r_{12}.\]

The two one-electron ground orbitals may be identical in space because opposite spins can form an antisymmetric singlet. Use a trial product of two normalised hydrogenic functions \(u_\zeta(\mathbf r)=(\zeta^3/\pi)^{1/2}e^{-\zeta r}\) with a variable positive exponent \(\zeta\). This single parameter lets the spatial cloud expand in response to mutual repulsion. Choosing the exponent equal to \(Z\) without testing it would impose the noninteracting size on an interacting problem.

A single orbital gives \(\langle-\nabla^2/2\rangle=\zeta^2/2\) and \(\langle1/r\rangle=\zeta\), either by direct integration or by hydrogenic scaling. Two electrons thus contribute \(\zeta^2-2Z\zeta\) to the expectation. To derive the repulsion term, average \(1/|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|\) over two independent uniform angles at fixed radii. Gauss's shell result, or a direct change of variable in the angular integral, gives \(1/\max(r_1,r_2)\). The radial probability is

\[P(r)=4\zeta^3r^2e^{-2\zeta r}.\]

Symmetry between the two electron labels permits restricting one integral to \(r_2<r_1\), then doubling:

\[J=2\int_0^\infty \frac{P(r)}r\,dr\int_0^r P(s)\,ds,\]
\[\int_0^rP(s)ds=1-e^{-2\zeta r}(1+2\zeta r+2\zeta^2r^2).\]

The first part of the resulting \(r\) integral gives \(2\zeta\). The three exponential subtraction terms sum to \(11\zeta/8\), leaving \(J=5\zeta/8\). Their evaluation uses \(\int_0^\infty r^ne^{-a r}dr=n!/a^{n+1}\). Both the dependence linear in \(\zeta\) and the positive sign follow from scaling an inverse-distance repulsion; only the numerical coefficient needed the angular and radial integration. Replacing the expectation of \(1/r_{12}\) by \(1/\langle r_{12}\rangle\) would generally give a different coefficient because inverse distance is nonlinear.

Consequently \(E(\zeta)=\zeta^2-2(Z-5/16)\zeta\) is an upper bound on the ground energy. It minimises at \(\zeta_*=Z-5/16\), with \(E_{\rm var}=-(Z-5/16)^2\) hartree for positive \(\zeta_*\). For helium \(Z=2\), \(\zeta_*=1.6875\), giving \(-2.84766\) hartree, compared with \(-2.75\) hartree at \(\zeta=2\). The one-electron ion has energy \(-2\) hartree, so this variational value lies below the threshold for an electron plus the ion and demonstrates binding within the model. The trial ionisation energy \(0.84766\) hartree is a lower bound on the exact ionisation energy when that threshold is known exactly. An upper bound on the neutral energy gives a lower bound on the energy required to remove an electron.

The interpretation of \(5/16\) as a screening correction is limited. At short radii each electron feels nearly the full nuclear singularity, while far outside it sees the remaining net charge. One constant exponent cannot reproduce both limits and cannot describe how the two electron positions avoid one another at fixed one-particle radius. The product trial permits nonzero joint weight at electron coincidence. Multiplying by a factor involving \(r_{12}\) introduces genuine correlation beyond independent orbitals. Optimising a broader variational family containing this product can only lower or retain the variational energy, provided its integrals and normalisation are computed consistently. Monitoring energy together with the pair-separation distribution tests whether the enlargement captures the physics of repulsion rather than merely adjusting a one-body radius.

Infer a local two-electron boundary condition from the Coulomb singularity

Near coincidence of two unlike-spin electrons, the Coulomb repulsion diverges like \(1/r_{12}\). The wavefunction itself can remain finite at coincidence, but its derivative must cancel this singularity in the Schrödinger equation. Consider a fixed smooth centre coordinate and average the wavefunction over the direction of the interelectron separation \(r\), away from simultaneous nuclear coincidence. Assume an expansion \(\bar\Psi(r)=A+Br+\cdots\). In atomic units the reduced mass is one half, so the relative spherical kinetic operator is \(-[d^2/dr^2+(2/r)d/dr]\) for the locally \(s\)-wave part. Its leading singular term is \(-2B/r\). Adding \(\bar\Psi/r\) gives coefficient \(A-2B\) of \(1/r\). A finite energy eigenvalue requires this coefficient to vanish:

\[\left.\frac{d\bar\Psi}{dr}\right|_{0}=\frac12\,\bar\Psi(0).\]

The positive cusp slope means the spatial amplitude rises as the electrons move apart from contact, consistent with repulsion. The corresponding electron–nucleus cusp has opposite sign and coefficient set by the nuclear charge and reduced mass. In a same-spin state the scalar contact amplitude vanishes by antisymmetry; this \(s\)-wave formula becomes \(0=0\) and a leading higher-partial-wave analysis is needed. Forgetting either angular averaging or exchange symmetry can turn an instructive local condition into a false statement about every directional derivative of every wavefunction.

A product of independent \(1s\) orbitals is smooth in \(r_{12}\) at a generic fixed centre and has no required linear cusp in the spherical average. A trial factor \(1+r_{12}/2\) in atomic units can impose the first-order unlike-spin cusp while leaving additional variational freedom to model long-range behaviour. Merely enforcing the cusp does not solve the entire two-electron problem. It constrains a local ratio of derivative to value, while energy depends on a six-dimensional integral. In variational Monte Carlo one can diagnose this distinction through the local energy \(H\Psi/\Psi\) near coincidence. A cusp-mismatched trial may have a singular local energy even though the volume element \(r^2dr\) keeps its integrated average finite. Imposing the cusp can reduce variance and improve short-distance pair observables more dramatically than it changes the energy expectation.

Use one-body occupation measurements to detect missing correlations

A single determinant of orthonormal spin orbitals \(\chi_i\) has a one-particle reduced density matrix \(\gamma=\sum_{\rm occupied}|\chi_i\rangle\langle\chi_i|\), normalised to the electron number \(N_e\). Thus \(\gamma^2=\gamma\) and its eigenvalues are zero or one. This is an algebraic signature of a single pure Slater determinant in the spin-orbital convention. For a correlated pure fermion state, natural occupations can become fractional while remaining between zero and one and summing to \(N_e\). Ensemble mixing can produce fractional occupations too, so knowing whether the entire state is pure matters if one wants to interpret fractional one-particle populations as evidence of correlation.

Consider the normalised two-electron singlet \(|\Psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.8}\,|a\uparrow,a\downarrow\rangle+\sqrt{0.2}\,|b\uparrow,b\downarrow\rangle\), with each ket a determinant. A one-body operator changes at most one occupied spin orbital, so its matrix element between these two doubly different determinants vanishes. The four natural spin-orbital occupations are \(0.8,0.8,0.2,0.2\). Their sum is two while \(\operatorname{tr}\gamma^2=1.36\), smaller than the determinant value two. An incoherent mixture of the two determinants at the same weights has the same one-body matrix, although it no longer has their joint coherence. A two-body pair-transfer operator that converts both \(a\) electrons into \(b\) electrons, plus its adjoint, can reveal a relative phase between these branches. Thus a complete one-body spectrum still need not determine a two-body state.

The symmetric Hubbard dimer ground state has another direct relation between energy derivatives and its one-body coherence. Let \(T=\sum_\sigma(c^\dagger_{L\sigma}c_{R\sigma}+c^\dagger_{R\sigma}c_{L\sigma})\). The energy derivative gives

\[\langle T\rangle=-\partial E_-/\partial t=8t/\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}.\]

At \(U=0\) and \(t>0\), \(\langle T\rangle=2\) because both electrons occupy the bonding orbital. At large \(U/t\) this coherence approaches zero even though the exact finite-\(t\) ground state remains a total spin singlet. In the bonding/antibonding spatial basis, the sum of both populations is two and their difference is \(\langle T\rangle\). For the example \(U=100,\ t=10\) meV this gives spatial occupations roughly 1.371 and 0.629. Dividing by two gives the spin-orbital occupations for each spin in this singlet. Site populations alone are one on each site by symmetry at every \(U/t\); their apparent constancy completely misses this changing form of correlation.

Break site symmetry and measure the onset of charge transfer

Add a static site-energy imbalance with energies \(-\Delta/2\) on \(L\) and \(+\Delta/2\) on \(R\). The singly occupied configuration still has total site energy zero, while double occupations cost \(U-\Delta\) and \(U+\Delta\). For \(|t|\ll U-|\Delta|\) the singly occupied singlet gains the second-order shift \(-2t^2/(U-\Delta)-2t^2/(U+\Delta)\), since its matrix element to either double occupation has magnitude \(\sqrt2|t|\). The triplets remain blocked. The effective exchange splitting is \(4t^2U/(U^2-\Delta^2)\). At zero imbalance it reduces to the earlier \(4t^2/U\); small imbalance changes it quadratically, consistent with exchanging left and right sites.

For \(U=100,\ t=5,\ \Delta=40\) meV, the estimate gives \(1.19\) meV compared with \(1.00\) meV at zero bias. Increasing imbalance can make one virtual double occupation especially inexpensive, changing the gap before the mean site charge substantially relocates. Near \(\Delta=U\) the denominator vanishes and the perturbative model predicts a divergence. The true three-state singlet matrix instead has an avoided crossing of singly and doubly occupied configurations. A sweep through that region should retain both double occupations and compute site charge from \(-2\,\partial E/\partial\Delta\), with the sign matched to the specified imbalance. Here an apparent runaway effective spin coupling marks the failure of eliminating charge degrees of freedom, rather than an arbitrarily large spin energy in the actual finite Hamiltonian.

See exchange interference directly in a two-port experiment

A lossless balanced beam splitter for two orthonormal single-particle modes can be chosen to act on creation operators as \(a^\dagger\to(c^\dagger+d^\dagger)/\sqrt2,\ b^\dagger\to(c^\dagger-d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\). Bring two identical particles to the device, one per input, with wave-packet timing and internal states controlled. The input \(a^\dagger b^\dagger|0\rangle\) contains no explicit interaction. Nevertheless the coincidence statistics after the device depend on exchange because two paths can lead to the same unordered output configuration. This provides a way to test exchange far from the Coulomb energy calculations of the previous sections.

For identical spinless bosons multiply the two sums and use commuting creation operators. The cross terms cancel, leaving \([(c^\dagger)^2-(d^\dagger)^2]|0\rangle/2=(|2,0\rangle-|0,2\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Both particles leave the same port and coincidence probability vanishes. The \(\sqrt2\) in a normalised doubly occupied Fock state is essential. For identical fermions in the same internal state the repeated creation terms vanish and the cross terms add because \(d^\dagger c^\dagger=-c^\dagger d^\dagger\). The outgoing state has one particle in each port with unit probability. Neither result requires an attractive or repulsive force at the splitter. Their interpretation turns on whether the alternatives remain indistinguishable and on the symmetry of the total input.

Now give the two particles internal wave packets \(|u\rangle,|v\rangle\), which may encode time, frequency or spin, and suppose the detectors report output ports but do not resolve internal labels. One coincidence alternative puts \(u\) in \(c\) and \(v\) in \(d\); the other interchanges them. The interference cross term contains the product \(\langle u|v\rangle\langle v|u\rangle=|\langle u|v\rangle|^2\). At a balanced splitter the coincidence probability is \([1-|\langle u|v\rangle|^2]/2\) for initially independently prepared bosons and \([1+|\langle u|v\rangle|^2]/2\) for corresponding fermions. At zero overlap both reduce to one half, as expected for two distinguishable independent particles each taking a random output.

Take transform-limited Gaussian wavepacket intensities of common time variance \(\sigma_t^2\) with delay \(\tau\) between their centres and no carrier mismatch. Integrating their two normalised amplitude envelopes gives

\[|\langle u|v\rangle|^2=\exp[-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)].\]

For \(\sigma_t=0.50\) ps and delay 1.00 ps this is

\[e^{-1}=0.368.\]

The predicted bosonic coincidence fraction is 0.316 and the fermionic value 0.684. A narrow time gate can itself identify the particles differently at different delays and must be included in the counting model. Measuring the single-output rates together with coincidences tests splitter balance and detection bias before converting dip or peak contrast into an overlap estimate.

Infer distinguishability with an unbalanced splitter

A calibrated splitter has intensity transmittance \(T=0.60\), reflectance \(R=0.40\) and negligible loss. Two photons from separate inputs give coincidence probability 0.184 at equal central arrival time. Infer their internal squared overlap. Would equal-time arrival alone have guaranteed perfect bunching?

Derive and check the result

For indistinguishable paths the two coincidence amplitudes have opposite signs with magnitudes \(T,R\). A nonresolving detector gives

\[P_c=T^2+R^2-2TR|\langle u|v\rangle|^2.\]

Substitution yields squared overlap \((0.52-0.184)/0.48=0.70\). Even perfect internal overlap at this unbalanced splitter would leave \((T-R)^2=0.040\) coincidences. Equal arrival time controls only one possible distinguishing label; orthogonal polarisation or spectral mismatch can still reduce overlap. A far-delay reference should give \(T^2+R^2=0.52\) for distinguishable, otherwise comparable photons. This independent control helps separate exchange interference from lost counts or a miscalibrated splitter.

Tutorial · discriminate spatial exchange from virtual hopping

Normalise two nonorthogonal orbitals before predicting coincidences

Let \(a,b\) each have norm one but overlap \(s=0.30\). Find the normalisations of their symmetric and antisymmetric products. Does the antisymmetric state still vanish at common position?

Work through the solution

The inner product \(\langle ab|ba\rangle=|s|^2=0.09\). The normalisation denominators are

\[\sqrt{2(1+0.09)}=1.476,\]
\[\sqrt{2(1-0.09)}=1.349.\]

At identical positions the antisymmetric numerator cancels pointwise for every \(s\). When the orbitals become identical its norm tends to zero and there is no independent antisymmetric state of that pair. For nonzero overlap the one-body cross terms in the earlier density calculation no longer integrate away, so those equal-density formulas must be rederived. Orthonormalising local orbitals changes the parameters of a corresponding Hubbard model as well.

Infer a hopping amplitude from a measured singlet gap

A calibrated two-site sample has \(U=120\) meV and a singlet–triplet gap 5.0 meV. In the model above find \(t\) exactly, predict the double-occupation probability and compare with a large-\(U\) inference.

Work through the solution

Writing \(J=(\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}-U)/2\) gives \(4t^2=J(U+J)\), hence \(|t|=12.50\) meV. The large-\(U\) estimate \(\sqrt{JU}/2=12.25\) meV is close but measurably different. The double-occupation fraction is

\[\tfrac12[1-U/(U+2J)]=J/(U+2J)=0.03846.\]

Gap alone cannot distinguish signs of \(t\) in a two-site model because rephasing one orbital reverses the sign. Measuring charge fluctuations independently tests whether the observed spin gap can be explained by this same virtual process.

Predict coherent double-occupation oscillations

Start suddenly in the singly occupied singlet of the symmetric two-site model. Find the probability of observing either doubly occupied site after time \(\tau\) and its maximum at \(U=100,\ t=10\) meV. Predict the triplet control.

Work through the solution

Subtract \(U/2\) times identity from the two-dimensional Hamiltonian. The traceless matrix squares to \((U^2+16t^2)I/4\). Exponentiating gives

\[P_D(\tau)=16t^2\sin^2[\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}\,\tau/(2\hbar)]/(U^2+16t^2).\]

Its maximum is

\[1600/11600=0.138.\]

This is larger than the ground-state admixture because the sudden preparation is a coherent superposition of both energy eigenstates. A triplet evolves with no doubly occupied component in this ideal Hamiltonian. Observing double occupation after triplet preparation would test spin mixing, state-preparation fidelity or additional orbitals.
