From electron exchange to atomic shells and molecular binding

An orbital model can explain why a closed atomic shell is special and why a shared electron can bind two protons. Both predictions need an energy as well as a rule for counting states. We construct the energies and check them against their separated-particle limits.

Count states and find what changes in an atom with many electrons

For a hydrogenic principal number \(n\), allowed orbital quantum numbers run from \(0\) to \(n-1\). Each \(l\) permits \(2l+1\) magnetic projections. Two spin states accompany each orbital. An antisymmetric many-electron state vanishes if two occupied spin orbitals coincide, giving the shell capacity

\[N_n=2\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=2n^2 .\]

This is a counting bound. Hydrogen energies depend only on \(n\) in the nonrelativistic Coulomb problem. In a neutral many-electron atom a core screens the nucleus. An orbital that penetrates the core samples a larger net attraction. Self-consistent screening and exchange split subshells. A bare \(2n^2\) table cannot predict the order of filling across all atoms or the configurations of all transition metals.

For carbon, a closed \(1s^22s^2\) core leaves two electrons in \(2p\). For distinct spatial states \(a,b\), form normalised spatial combinations \((a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2))/\sqrt2\). A symmetric repulsive interaction gives \(E_\pm=E_0+J\pm K\) for orthogonal orbitals, with \(J\) the direct integral and \(K\) the exchange integral. The antisymmetric spatial branch pairs with a symmetric spin triplet and often lies lower in a degenerate open subshell. This gives a mechanism for Hund's first rule. Large orbital splittings can favour double occupation despite exchange.

Recall · Why does an inert shell show a spectroscopic jump?

Adding an electron above a completed low shell and removing one from that shell involve orbitals with different spatial extent and binding energy. Closed-shell counting also gives zero total orbital and spin angular momentum for the filled subshell. Actual ionisation energies still include relaxation of the remaining electrons.

Construct a molecular-ion energy curve in its own nonorthogonal basis

Fix two protons at separation \(R\) and work in atomic units. The nuclei move much more slowly than the electron. The electronic Hamiltonian is \(h=-\nabla^2/2-1/r_A-1/r_B\). For each fixed \(R\) the total Born–Oppenheimer energy includes the proton repulsion \(1/R\). Use two unit-charge hydrogenic \(1s\) basis orbitals \(\phi_A,\phi_B\). Their overlap matters whenever a bond might form.

\[S=e^{-R}(1+R+R^2/3),\]
\[J=-1/R+(1+1/R)e^{-2R},\]
\[K=-(1+R)e^{-R}.\]

Here \(J=\langle A|-1/r_B|A\rangle\) and \(K=\langle A|-1/r_A|B\rangle\). They follow by integrating the two decaying exponentials in the field of the other nucleus. For example, the spherical average of \(1/r_B\) on a sphere about \(A\) is \(1/\max(r_A,R)\). Splitting the \(J\) radial integral there gives the expression above. Using the isolated hydrogen equation acting on the ket now gives \(H_{AA}=-1/2+J\) and \(H_{AB}=-S/2+K\).

Vary the Rayleigh quotient with respect to the coefficients. The stationarity equation is \(Hc=E_{\rm el}Sc\) where the matrix on the right has unit diagonal and off-diagonal overlap \(S\). Reflection through the midpoint fixes the even and odd combinations and their normalisations:

\[\psi_\pm=\frac{\phi_A\pm\phi_B}{\sqrt{2(1\pm S)}},\]
\[U_\pm(R)=\frac{H_{AA}\pm H_{AB}}{1\pm S}+\frac1R.\]

As \(R\) grows, both curves tend to \(-1/2\) hartree, the energy of a neutral H atom and a distant proton. At \(R=2\) bohr the even branch is about \(-0.554\) hartree. Its energy below the dissociation threshold demonstrates binding even within this restricted trial space. The minimum of this curve determines a bond length only within the chosen basis and the fixed-nucleus approximation.

Try the molecular density between two nuclei

Move the nuclei and inspect how constructive and destructive interference change the electron density on their connecting line. The odd combination keeps a node at the centre. Compare the even-state energy with the separated-atom value of −0.5 hartree and locate a bound separation.

\[|\psi_\pm(x,0,0)|^2={\bigl(e^{-|x+R/2|}\pm e^{-|x-R/2|}\bigr)^2\over2\pi(1\pm S(R))}.\]

The orbitals are normalised in three dimensions and the plot is a line through that density. This is the same fixed two-orbital variational approximation as the OneSimulation notebook below. Nuclear motion and orbital relaxation are excluded. Use the notebook's eigenproblem to check the displayed total energies independently.

Run the downloaded notebook with OneSimulation, NumPy and Matplotlib installed. The output below was recorded with the displayed calculation.

"""Hydrogen molecular ion in a fixed pair of hydrogenic 1s orbitals; atomic units."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from onesim.numerics.linear.matrices.dense_linear_algebra import symmetric_eigenvalues

def total_energies(r):
    s = np.exp(-r) * (1 + r + r*r/3)
    j = -1/r + (1 + 1/r) * np.exp(-2*r)
    k = -(1 + r) * np.exp(-r)
    h = np.array([[-0.5+j, -0.5*s+k], [-0.5*s+k, -0.5+j]])
    metric = np.array([[1.0, s], [s, 1.0]])
    chol = np.linalg.cholesky(metric)
    left = np.linalg.solve(chol, h)
    orthogonal = np.linalg.solve(chol, left.T).T
    return symmetric_eigenvalues(orthogonal) + 1/r

distance = np.linspace(0.9, 8.0, 711)
bonding, antibonding = np.array([total_energies(r) for r in distance]).T
i = np.argmin(bonding)
print(f"Grid minimum R = {distance[i]:.3f} bohr")
print(f"Total energy = {bonding[i]:.6f} hartree")
print(f"Binding relative to H + proton = {(-0.5-bonding[i])*27.2114:.3f} eV")
plt.plot(distance, bonding, label="Even combination")
plt.plot(distance, antibonding, label="Odd combination")
plt.axhline(-0.5, linestyle=":", color="grey", label="Separated atom and ion")
plt.xlabel("Nuclear separation (bohr)")
plt.ylabel("Total energy (hartree)")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("physics-molecular-ion.png", dpi=160)

Add the second electron without assigning an electron to a nucleus

For neutral hydrogen include \(1/r_{12}\) in the electronic Hamiltonian. The separated neutral-atom energy is now \(-1\) hartree. With \(u=\phi_A(1)\phi_B(2)\) and \(v=\phi_B(1)\phi_A(2)\) the two assignments have overlap \(S^2\). Spatial symmetrisation gives the covalent Heitler–London trial states and their energies:

\[U_\pm(R)=\frac{\langle u|H_{\rm el}|u\rangle\pm\langle u|H_{\rm el}|v\rangle}{1\pm S^2}+\frac1R.\]

The plus state must carry the antisymmetric spin singlet. The off-diagonal matrix element includes electronic hopping and two-electron terms. Its sign and the overlap both enter the total energy. At large \(R\), \(S\to0\) and each product contains one atom at each site. In contrast, a doubly occupied bonding molecular orbital expanded in the site basis contains ionic configurations with both electrons on one site. Optimising the relative weights of covalent and ionic configurations improves the dissociation limit.

As a worked matrix test take \(R=2\), \(S^2=0.25\), \(H_{uu}=-1.48\) and \(H_{uv}=-0.45\) hartree. These are illustrative matrix elements, not tabulated hydrogen data. The singlet energy is \(-1.044\) hartree after nuclear repulsion. The triplet yields about \(-0.873\) hartree. Only the first trial value lies below the separated-atom threshold. A true equilibrium requires repeating the calculation over \(R\).

Tutorial on shell models and bonds

1 · Count the microstates of two electrons in a p subshell

Work through the solution

Six spin orbitals allow \(\binom62=15\) antisymmetric two-electron determinants. For two parallel spins choose two distinct spatial orbitals in \(\binom32=3\) ways for a fixed spin orientation. This counting alone provides no energy ordering.

2 · Diagnose a false bond from an omitted term

Work through the solution

If a calculation reports only \(E_{\rm el}\), decreasing \(R\) can lower it through attraction to both nuclei. The force on the separation comes from \(-d(E_{\rm el}+1/R)/dR\). Omitting repulsion gives a false energy minimum or a collapse at short distance.

3 · Estimate the vibrational frequency near a computed minimum

Work through the solution

Suppose \(U''(R_e)=0.10\) hartree/bohr² and the nuclear reduced mass is 918 electron masses. The angular frequency in atomic units is \(\sqrt{0.10/918}=0.0104\). In this harmonic model the vibrational energy spacing is 0.0104 hartree, or 0.284 eV. Replacing both protons by twice their mass divides the spacing by \(\sqrt2\).
