From two-path interference to the geometry of a quantum state

Two preparations give the same counts at one analyser. Place a second analyser in a rotated basis and the counts separate. From this experiment develop amplitudes, phase, projective measurement, mixed states and the geometry of distinguishability.

Begin with an experiment that can distinguish two preparations

A single photon enters a lossless interferometer and can take either an upper or a lower path. Think of the paths as two distinguishable spatial modes labelled \(0,1\), with equal travel times unless a phase shifter is inserted. Record one binary result from each freshly prepared photon. Our model predicts outcome frequencies over many repetitions. A preparation that always follows path 0 is represented by \(|0\rangle\), one that always follows path 1 by \(|1\rangle\). Orthonormality, \(\langle i|j\rangle=\delta_{ij}\), encodes perfect distinguishability by a path measurement. A general pure preparation has two complex amplitudes,

\[|\psi\rangle=a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle,\]

with \(|a|^2+|b|^2=1\). The probability rule \(P(0)=|a|^2\) and its partner for path 1 are physical postulates. Linear algebra lets us derive testable consequences of them; it does not derive probability from arithmetic alone.

Take preparation A with \(a=b=1/\sqrt2\). For preparation B choose either path with ordinary probabilities one half using a classical random switch. A detector placed directly in either arm records equal count rates for A and B. One shared probability distribution is therefore insufficient to identify the state. To discriminate the preparations, bring the paths together at a lossless recombiner. Choose its phase references so an incoming \(|0\rangle\) emerges as \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\), while \(|1\rangle\) emerges as \((|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The matrix \(H=2^{-1/2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) is unitary because its columns are orthonormal. Other beam-splitter conventions put factors of \(i\) on reflection; a consistent set of propagation phases yields the same observed fringes.

Insert a relative phase \(\phi\) on the lower path in preparation A. After recombination the amplitudes are \((1+e^{i\phi})/2\) and \((1-e^{i\phi})/2\). Multiplying each by its complex conjugate gives

\[P(0)=\frac{1+\cos\phi}{2},\]
\[P(1)=\frac{1-\cos\phi}{2}.\]

For preparation B each possible input produces equal exit probabilities. Averaging those probabilities leaves a constant one half at both ports for every phase. The contrast term results from multiplying the sum of amplitudes by its conjugate. Summing probabilities over alternatives is justified when the alternatives correspond to distinct preparations, or when a physical record distinguishes the two paths and that record is ignored. Confusing these operations predicts a fringe in the random-switch experiment. Counting both outputs checks normalisation independently of visibility, provided detector efficiencies have been calibrated.

At \(\phi=\pi/3\), 400 independently prepared photons from A have expected output-0 count 300 and binomial standard deviation \(\sqrt{400(3/4)(1/4)}=8.66\). Preparation B predicts 200 with standard deviation 10. Repeating the measurement at \(-\pi/3\) gives exactly the same probabilities: an analyser sensitive only to the cosine cannot determine the sign of the phase. Add a known \(\pi/2\) offset before one further run. It converts a sine into a cosine and removes that ambiguity. This simple experiment is a model of tomography, the recovery of state parameters from several distinct measurement settings.

Remove the phase that no ideal analyser can observe

Let a downstream analyser transmit normalised state \(|v\rangle\). Born's rule gives transmission probability \(|\langle v|\psi\rangle|^2\). Replacing the entire preparation by \(e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle\) multiplies the overlap by a phase but leaves its squared modulus invariant for every \(v\). Physical pure states are therefore rays. Relative phases survive because changing the phase of only one term is not multiplication of the whole vector by the same number. A basis vector itself may be assigned an arbitrary phase if its coordinates and every operator matrix are transformed consistently. Such a convention change cannot affect the phase measured relative to an actually inserted optical delay.

A unit vector in two complex dimensions has four real components. Normalisation removes one degree of freedom and the common phase removes another. Away from the coordinate poles a convenient parametrisation is

\[|\psi(\theta,\varphi)\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle,\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi .\]

Projecting onto \(|0\rangle\) gives \((1+\cos\theta)/2\). The corresponding point on the unit sphere has coordinates \(\mathbf r=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)\). Orthogonal states sit at opposite ends of a diameter. Their resulting angle in ordinary three-space is \(\pi\), while their angle defined from the Hilbert-space overlap is \(\pi/2\). This factor of two is why the spinor parametrisation contains half angles. At the poles \(\varphi\) drops out of all probabilities. Attempting to estimate an azimuth of an exactly path-localised preparation is a singular parametrisation, rather than a failure of the experiment.

Represent the analyser for the pure direction \(\mathbf n\) by the projector \(P_{\mathbf n}=|v\rangle\langle v|\). Projectors satisfy \(P^\dagger=P\) and \(P^2=P\). Let \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\) be the Pauli matrices with products \(\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k\). Then \(P_{\mathbf n}=(I+\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2\). Since each Pauli matrix has zero trace and \(\operatorname{tr}\sigma_i\sigma_j=2\delta_{ij}\),

\[\operatorname{tr}(P_{\mathbf r}P_{\mathbf n})=\frac{1+\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n}{2}=\cos^2(\gamma/2),\]

where \(\gamma\) is the spatial angle between the two directions. Thus transmission through a rotated analyser determines an inner product between real geometric directions, even though the original amplitude was complex. The vector for a path interferometer describes a two-dimensional mode space; rotating it need not mean physically rotating the entire apparatus in the laboratory.

Differentiate a probability from the state after a measurement

For an ideal projective measurement \(\{P_j\}\), mutually orthogonal projectors sum to the identity. The probability of result \(j\) in a pure state is \(\langle\psi|P_j|\psi\rangle\). Conditional on actually observing that result, the state for subsequent predictions in an ideal repeatable experiment is \(P_j|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{p_j}\), provided \(p_j>0\). Applying the same measurement immediately gives the same result with probability one, because \(P_j^2=P_j\). Measuring a different observable in between can change the answer. The formula for an ideal repeatable measurement is an assumption about the measuring device as well as about the observable. An absorbing photon counter ends the photon's trajectory, so a sequential model applies to a nondestructive analyser or to a filtered ensemble prepared in the transmitted state.

Prepare \(|0\rangle\) and then sequentially measure a direction tilted \(60^\circ\) from \(+z\), followed by \(z\). If one keeps only the positive tilted outcome, three quarters of the original ensemble survive the selection. Among those survivors the fraction with final \(+z\) is again three quarters. The joint probability of both positives is \(9/16\); reporting \(3/4\) for this joint event would discard the probability cost of selection. If all outcomes of the intermediate measurement are retained but unread, half is no longer the right default either. One must add over both paths of recorded outcomes. The final \(+z\) probability is

\[(3/4)^2+(1/4)^2=5/8.\]

Without the intermediate device it would be one. Removing data after a measurement and removing the measurement itself are experimentally different operations.

For a broad outcome corresponding to several basis states, a projector can preserve coherence inside the accepted subspace. Suppose a three-mode preparation is \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle)/\sqrt3\). A device that only asks whether the state is in the span of \(0,1\) accepts with probability \(2/3\) and ideally transmits \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). A device measuring the individual paths and later combining their labels into “accepted” yields the same acceptance probability but no surviving phase relation between 0 and 1 unless its record is coherently erased. A recombiner acting on the accepted ensemble distinguishes the implementations. An observable's probability table by itself does not specify every disturbance produced by a physical instrument.

Recover a state matrix from three families of counts

For a classical mixture of pure preparations \(|\psi_j\rangle\) with weights \(w_j\), average their individual probabilities. The result is \(\operatorname{tr}(\rho P)\) with \(\rho=\sum_jw_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|\). This matrix has trace one, is Hermitian and has nonnegative expectation on every vector. Changing the ensemble decomposition without changing \(\rho\) makes no difference to any measurement performed on the system alone. Preparation B is \(I/2\). A 50:50 mixture of the two opposite \(x\)-analysed states is the same matrix. Trying to infer which randomisation mechanism was used requires access to its preparation record.

Every Hermitian two-state density matrix of trace one has the form \(\rho=(I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2\). Diagonalise along \(\mathbf r\). The eigenvalues are \((1\pm|\mathbf r|)/2\). Positivity is therefore equivalent to \(|\mathbf r|\le1\). Interior points correspond to mixtures and surface points to pure states. Measuring \(\sigma_i\) gives \(p_i^+=(1+r_i)/2\), so three noncoplanar analyser directions suffice to reconstruct an otherwise unknown two-level state given enough identically prepared copies. Noncommutation places no obstacle to using independent copies for these settings.

For 1000 trials per direction take \(n_x^+=740,\ n_y^+=320,\ n_z^+=750\). The linear estimates are

\[r_x=0.48,\ r_y=-0.36,\ r_z=0.50.\]

Their length is

\[\sqrt{0.61}=0.7810.\]

The eigenvalues are 0.8905 and 0.1095. The probability for \(\mathbf n=(3/5,4/5,0)\) is \(1/2\) even though the state has strong coherence in the path basis. The setting is accidentally perpendicular to its transverse projection. A fourth such measurement supplies an out-of-sample check of state preparation and analyser calibration. If finite data instead give \(|\mathbf r|>1\), a negative estimated eigenvalue signals statistical inconsistency with exact physical probabilities. Fitting all binomial data under \(|\mathbf r|\le1\) retains a physical model. Clipping each Cartesian expectation separately fails to enforce the spherical constraint.

Recover a phase from a closed loop of projections

There is phase information that cannot be associated with any one point on the sphere. For three nonorthogonal preparations \(A,B,C\), the complex product \(G=\langle A|B\rangle\langle B|C\rangle\langle C|A\rangle\) is unchanged by independent phase changes of the three state vectors. The phase acquired in each overlap cancels from the complete product. A loop of conditional projections can therefore have an observable phase relative to a reference arm even if every intermediate selection probability depends only on a modulus. The probability of successfully traversing the filtered path also matters for the attainable interference contrast.

Take \(A=|0\rangle,\ B=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2,\ C=(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The overlaps are \(1/\sqrt2,(1+i)/2,1/\sqrt2\). Their product is \((1+i)/4\), giving phase \(+\pi/4\). Traversing the reversed overlap product gives its complex conjugate and changes the sign. The directed path in a laboratory must be matched carefully to the order of bra and ket in the adopted product. If the analysers actually project a state in the order \(A\to B\to C\to A\), the amplitude in the reference convention is \(\langle A|C\rangle\langle C|B\rangle\langle B|A\rangle=G^*\). A path with a zero overlap has zero surviving interference amplitude and an undefined phase. This condition prevents interpreting a phase jump at a dark output as a measured singular rotation of a physical state.

Design the best analyser for discriminating two known signals

Imagine a source chosen at random from two calibrated preparations \(\rho_0,\rho_1\) with known prior probabilities \(\eta_0,\eta_1\). One copy arrives; the task is to guess which preparation was used and minimise the probability of a wrong guess. This differs from estimating all unknown entries of a state matrix from many repetitions. For example, with highly unbalanced priors the best choice may be to guess the more common preparation after many different measured outcomes. A measurement along the direction connecting the two Bloch vectors is optimal for equal priors under the criterion derived below, but generally need not be a universal optimal analyser for other decisions.

Let a binary measurement have two positive effects \(E_0,E_1=I-E_0\), where the eigenvalues of \(E_0\) lie between zero and one. These positive-operator-valued effects give probabilities \(\operatorname{tr}(\rho E_j)\), extending projectors to include randomisation or coarse responses. Adopt the decision rule of reporting label \(j\) after measurement result \(j\). The average probability of success is then

\[P_{\rm good}=\eta_0\operatorname{tr}(E_0\rho_0)+\eta_1\operatorname{tr}[(I-E_0)\rho_1]=\eta_1+\operatorname{tr}(E_0\Delta),\]
\[\Delta=\eta_0\rho_0-\eta_1\rho_1 .\]

Diagonalise the Hermitian \(\Delta\). The trace depends only on diagonal elements of \(E_0\) in this basis. Each lies in \([0,1]\). To maximise the sum, take one for positive eigenvalues and zero for negative ones. The resulting projector is an allowed measurement, proving optimality rather than only providing an upper bound. Zero eigenvalues permit arbitrary treatment without changing average success. Since the sum of positive eigenvalues equals \([\|\Delta\|_1+\operatorname{tr}\Delta]/2\), substitution gives

\[P_{\rm good}^{\max}=(1+\|\Delta\|_1)/2.\]

The trace norm here is the sum of singular values; for this Hermitian matrix the singular values are absolute eigenvalues. This operational meaning explains why trace distance between preparations appears repeatedly when comparing quantum processes.

For two equally likely pure states of overlap magnitude \(c=|\langle\psi_0|\psi_1\rangle|\), use the two-dimensional span of the states. The eigenvalues of \(\Delta=(\rho_0-\rho_1)/2\) are \(\pm\sqrt{1-c^2}/2\). Consequently \(P_{\rm good}^{\max}=[1+\sqrt{1-c^2}]/2\). Orthogonal states can be perfectly distinguished. Identical preparations attain success one half, exactly the prior random-guess limit. For a concrete optical example let preparation 0 be \(|0\rangle\) and preparation 1 be \(\cos30^\circ|0\rangle+\sin30^\circ|1\rangle\). Their Hilbert-space overlap is \(\sqrt3/2\), while the Bloch vectors have a spatial separation angle of \(60^\circ\). The best success probability is \(3/4\). Measuring the path basis instead and reporting 0 whenever it transmits yields success \([1+\sin^230^\circ]/2=0.625\). The best analyser deliberately allows some errors on both sources to reduce the average error over the pair.

The physical realisation is simple in this example. The difference of Bloch vectors is \((-\sqrt3/2,0,1/2)\), already of unit length. Use that direction for the positive projector labelled 0. Its dot product with the preparation-0 vector is \(+1/2\), and with the preparation-1 vector is \(-1/2\), giving correct guess probability \(3/4\) for either source independently. The sign of this vector matters because it assigns the outcome label. Reversing the analyser axis and interchanging the decision labels leaves the same performance. An experimental confusion matrix recording each conditional outcome rate checks considerably more than an aggregate fraction of successful guesses, particularly if the priors in the actual source differ from those in the calibration trial.

If two independent copies of the same unknown signal can be stored and measured jointly, the pure-state overlap becomes \(c^2\). The optimum success rises to \([1+\sqrt{1-c^4}]/2\). For the previous optical example this is about 0.831. The calculation assumes both copies were identically prepared from one shared unknown label. It would be invalid if each copy came from a newly and independently drawn source label. Such a distinction arises naturally when comparing a two-photon coincidence experiment with two unrelated records in a database. Increasing the number of independent preparations for tomography and increasing copies available per individual discrimination decision are different resources.

Calibrate an imperfect path detector with its classical record intact

An ideal path measurement perfectly distinguishes the two modes and destroys their mutual coherence in an unread outcome. A tunable weaker measurement provides an intermediate experiment linking information and disturbance quantitatively. Suppose an instrument reports \(+\) or \(-\) with operators \(M_+=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{(1+d)/2},\sqrt{(1-d)/2})\) and \(M_-=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{(1-d)/2},\sqrt{(1+d)/2})\), in the path basis, with \(0\le d\le1\). Squaring and summing gives identity, so probabilities normalise for every input. Applied separately to the known \(0\) and \(1\) calibration sources, the \(+\) result occurs with probabilities \((1+d)/2\) and \((1-d)/2\); their difference measures \(d\). At zero this detector returns an independent coin flip; at one it reads the path without misclassification.

For an input with Bloch component \(z\), the result probability is

\[p_+=(1+dz)/2.\]

Conditional on that result, the path-0 and path-1 weights must be renormalised by \(p_+\). The new longitudinal component and transverse coherence satisfy

\[z'=\frac{z+d}{1+dz},\qquad (\rho_{01})'=\frac{\sqrt{1-d^2}}{1+dz}\,\rho_{01}.\]

If the outcome is unread, the map is the sum of both \(M_j\rho M_j^\dagger\). The populations are unchanged and the off-diagonal entry becomes \(\sqrt{1-d^2}\rho_{01}\). A balanced pure input consequently gives a recombiner fringe visibility \(\sqrt{1-d^2}\). This equality links this calibrated detector's ability to distinguish path preparations with the contrast left in a complementary measurement. It depends on the chosen instrument being a minimal diagonal measurement. A device with the same two outcome probabilities can also apply an uncontrolled relative phase, yielding a smaller visibility. The existence of such instruments is why probability effects alone do not determine post-measurement states.

Choose \(d=0.60\) and a balanced pure \(+x\) input. Either outcome has probability one half. Given \(+\), the new state has \(z'=0.60\) and \(x'=0.80\), so it is pure. The other branch has \(z'=-0.60,x'=0.80\). If the outcomes are ignored their average has \(\mathbf r=(0.80,0,0)\), which is mixed. Feeding each branch through a separate calibrated rotation can align both with the original \(+x\) direction, restoring full visibility when the input is known. This feed-forward preparation does not invert arbitrary unknown states through a closed deterministic quantum map; it makes use of the recorded result and knowledge of the preparation. A single fixed rotation cannot increase the purity of the unread mixture because unitaries preserve the eigenvalues of \(\rho\).

There is also a practically important calibration ambiguity. Suppose a perfect projective path detector is followed by a classical reporting error which flips the stored label with probability \((1-d)/2\). Its outcome rates on every input agree exactly with those of the weak instrument above. But its unrevealed physical interaction has already produced orthogonal environmental records of the true path; unread coherence is then zero for every value of the classical label reliability. Test the distinction by preparing a known balanced superposition, measuring with the candidate instrument and recombining its exits while retaining both count channels. Matching path-classification accuracy cannot substitute for this separate disturbance test. Conversely observing reduced visibility alone does not reveal whether useful path information is accessible in the recorded labels.

Count statistics when the analyser itself has a bias

Suppose the final binary detector has efficiency \(\epsilon_0\) at port 0 and \(\epsilon_1\) at port 1. If lost events are discarded and only a ratio of recorded clicks retained, the observed fraction is \(p_{\rm obs}=\epsilon_0p/[\epsilon_0p+\epsilon_1(1-p)]\). At a balanced state unequal efficiencies can imitate a longitudinal preparation bias. For \(p=1/2,\epsilon_0=0.90,\epsilon_1=0.75\), after selection the recorded port-0 fraction is

\[0.90/1.65=0.5455.\]

Treating it as an ideal probability would infer \(z=0.091\). Collecting arbitrarily many photons reduces the scatter about that biased result without reducing the bias itself.

A measured ratio from an independently characterised balanced reference can determine relative efficiency \(r=\epsilon_0/\epsilon_1\). In odds form \(p_{\rm obs}/(1-p_{\rm obs})=r\,p/(1-p)\). If the reference is unavailable but a known unitary exchanges the measurement ports, record a second measurement on the same preparation with that exchange. Its observed odds are \(r(1-p)/p\). Their product is \(r^2\); their ratio is \([p/(1-p)]^2\). Thus the two runs can separate relative gain and true probability, provided the gain and preparation remain stable across them. For observed odds 1.8 and 0.8 one obtains \(r=1.2\) and true odds 1.5, hence \(p=0.60\). Stability is testable by alternating the two settings across the acquisition instead of making one long run after the other. This class of self-calibrating measurement is useful far beyond a two-path interferometer; the proposed physical symmetry is built into both the inversion formula and its independent checks.

Separate component uncertainty from experimental readout resolution

A variance bound can be proved directly from Hilbert-space geometry without assuming a model of a particular measurement apparatus. For a normalised pure state set \(|u\rangle=(A-\langle A\rangle)|\psi\rangle,\ |v\rangle=(B-\langle B\rangle)|\psi\rangle\), for observables and states for which the required products exist. The squared norms are \((\Delta A)^2,(\Delta B)^2\). Cauchy–Schwarz bounds their product below by \(|\langle u|v\rangle|^2\). Separating the real and imaginary parts of the inner product gives

\[(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\ge\left|\frac{\langle[A,B]\rangle}{2i}\right|^2+\left|\frac12\langle\{\Delta A_{\rm op},\Delta B_{\rm op}\}\rangle\right|^2,\]

where \(\Delta A_{\rm op}=A-\langle A\rangle I\) denotes a centred operator rather than its scalar standard deviation. The real term is the squared symmetrised covariance. The commonly quoted commutator-only inequality drops this nonnegative contribution. The bound describes spreads across identical preparations; the electronics' error in reading a single outcome is a different quantity and enters an experimental analysis through the detection model.

For Pauli measurements \(A=\sigma_x,B=\sigma_y\), the elementary lower bound on the product of variances is \(r_z^2\), since \([\sigma_x,\sigma_y]=2i\sigma_z\). The full covariance is \(-r_xr_y\) because their anticommutator vanishes before centring. The actual product is \((1-r_x^2)(1-r_y^2)\). Subtracting the full lower bound leaves \(1-r_x^2-r_y^2-r_z^2\), directly testing the Hilbert-space positivity constraint on the Bloch vector. Every pure qubit saturates the full inequality for this pair, while a mixed interior state can give strict excess. A zero commutator expectation in a particular state does not prove that the two observables commute or can be jointly assigned precise values there. For \(|+x\rangle\) one variance is zero, while the other is maximal.

Decide whether an apparent uncertainty violation is an instrument problem

A device reconstruction reports \(r_x=0.80,\ r_y=0.60,\ r_z=0.20\). Test the commutator-only bound, the full covariance inequality and the eigenvalue positivity condition. Why is testing the weaker bound alone insufficient?

Derive and check the result

The measured components imply variances 0.36 and 0.64, with product 0.2304. The commutator-only bound is 0.04 and passes. The covariance-squared contribution is \(0.8^2\,0.6^2=0.2304\), so the full bound is 0.2704 and fails by 0.0400. Independently \(|\mathbf r|^2=1.04\) predicts a slightly negative eigenvalue. Finite count noise or inappropriate calibration can make separate empirical component estimates leave the physical Bloch ball even though every individual estimate lies in its allowed range. Check uncertainty regions jointly and, when fitting a physical state, retain measurement likelihoods and positivity. Satisfying one weaker inequality cannot certify that all estimated outcomes come from a common quantum preparation.

Infer visibility and a path-coherence phase

An equal-population path state gives normalised output-0 probabilities 0.80 with zero phase offset and 0.70 after applying \(+\pi/2\) to path 1. With the recombiner convention above, find the complex density matrix entry \(\rho_{01}\), its allowed magnitude, and the highest achievable output probability.

Work through the solution

The phase plate sends \(\rho_{01}\) to \(e^{-i\phi}\rho_{01}\), so \(p_0=1/2+\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\phi}\rho_{01})\). The observations give real part 0.30 and imaginary part 0.20. Its magnitude is 0.3606, below the positivity bound \(\sqrt{\rho_{00}\rho_{11}}=0.5\). The maximum exit probability is 0.8606, obtained when the phase offset cancels the argument of \(\rho_{01}\); here \(\phi=\arctan(2/3)=33.7^\circ\). The minimum is 0.1394. The usual fringe visibility \((p_{\max}-p_{\min})/(p_{\max}+p_{\min})\) is 0.721. The shortfall from unity in ideal equal-population data measures reduced path coherence. Unequal detection efficiencies or an unbalanced recombiner would need an independent calibration before drawing that conclusion from raw counts.

Is a succession of nearly parallel filters an efficient rotator?

Start in \(+z\). Place \(N\) ideal positive-channel filters whose directions rotate in equal steps from \(+z\) to \(-z\) along a great circle. Find the transmitted fraction and evaluate it for \(N=2,10\). Compare with applying a single final filter.

Work through the solution

Adjacent directions differ by \(\pi/N\), so each conditional transmission probability is \(\cos^2(\pi/2N)\). All accepted states end at \(-z\), with joint transmission \([\cos^2(\pi/2N)]^N\). For one filter overlap is zero. Two filters give \(1/4\), whereas ten give about 0.7805. Taking logarithms gives \(2N\ln\cos(\pi/2N)=-\pi^2/(4N)+O(N^{-3})\), tending to zero, so the ideal transmission tends to one. Each retained state has been prepared along the new axis by the filtering. Finite loss per filter eventually reverses the trend: multiplying by an efficiency \(0.99^N\) penalises arbitrarily large \(N\). Tracking both conditional and joint rates distinguishes this sequence from a unitary spin rotation.

Set an error bar on a prediction along a new direction

Use the three tomography runs above to predict \(p_+\) for \(\mathbf n=(3/5,4/5,0)\) with an approximate standard error. Test whether observing 535 positives in 1000 new trials is surprising at the level of a few standard deviations.

Work through the solution

For a component estimate \(r_i=2n_i^+/N_i-1\), the plug-in variance is \((1-r_i^2)/N_i\) for independent binomial trials. Independent settings give \(\operatorname{var}(\hat p)=\tfrac14[0.36(1-0.48^2)+0.64(1-0.36^2)]/1000=0.0002085\), or standard error 0.0144. The new fraction has sampling error about 0.0158. Comparing two independent estimates requires adding these variances, giving combined standard error about 0.0214. The discrepancy 0.035 is about 1.6 standard errors. Treating the reconstructed state as exactly known would overstate the evidence of miscalibration. For operational work near the Bloch-sphere boundary, a likelihood analysis handles correlations and positivity more accurately than this linear interior approximation.
