Read the angular momentum algebra as a statement about rotations

Rotate a prepared quantum state through a small angle \(\delta\theta\) about a unit laboratory direction \(\mathbf n\). A continuous norm-preserving transformation has form \(U=I-i\delta\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J/\hbar+O(\delta\theta^2)\), with Hermitian generators \(J_i\). Rotations about different axes need not commute. Comparing the commutator of two infinitesimal rotations with the rotation around the third axis gives

\[[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k.\]

The sum \(J^2=J_x^2+J_y^2+J_z^2\) commutes with each component. One can therefore label eigenstates of \(J^2\) and one chosen component \(J_z\) simultaneously, while generally leaving the other components uncertain.

Define \(J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y\). Their commutators with \(J_z\) are \(\pm\hbar J_\pm\); with \(J^2\) they vanish. If \(J_z|m\rangle=\hbar m|m\rangle\), the two ladder operations change \(m\) by one without changing total angular momentum. To determine their coefficients, use \(J_-J_+=J^2-J_z^2-\hbar J_z\) and \(J_+J_-=J^2-J_z^2+\hbar J_z\). Nonnegative squared norms bound the ladder above and below. Let \(j\) be its maximum magnetic quantum number. Vanishing raising norm there fixes the \(J^2\) eigenvalue at \(\hbar^2 j(j+1)\); vanishing lowering norm at the bottom gives

\[m_{\min}=-j.\]

The number of unit steps between the two endpoints is \(2j\), so \(j\) is an integer or half-integer. With a consistent phase convention

\[J_\pm|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|j,m\pm1\rangle .\]

Spatial orbital eigenfunctions on an ordinary single-valued scalar wavefunction carry integer orbital \(l\). Spin-\(\tfrac12\) has two states that cannot be modelled by adding another spatial coordinate angle to a scalar orbital. The operator algebra describes both. In \(|j,j\rangle\), \(J_z\) is definite but \(J_x,J_y\) fluctuate. Their variance sum is \(\hbar^2 j(j+1)-\hbar^2j^2=\hbar^2j\); rotational symmetry around \(z\) splits it evenly. Drawing a classical vector with precisely fixed components perpendicular to the axis would miss these fluctuations.

Construct a coupled basis from the stretched state

For independent angular momenta \(\mathbf L,\mathbf S\), the total generator is \(\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S\). Operators on different factors commute. In the product basis \(|m_l,m_s\rangle\), \(J_z\) is diagonal with eigenvalue \(\hbar(m_l+m_s)\). The Hamiltonian may instead depend on \(J^2\), for example through \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\). Then states with the same \(m_l+m_s\) can mix. Resolving that degeneracy requires a unitary change of basis, whose entries are Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

Take \(l=1,s=1/2\). The unique state \(m=3/2\) is \(|1,\uparrow\rangle\), so it must be \(|3/2,3/2\rangle\). Lower once with \(J_-=L_-+S_-\). The left side is \(\hbar\sqrt3|3/2,1/2\rangle\); the right side is \(\hbar(\sqrt2|0,\uparrow\rangle+|1,\downarrow\rangle)\). Normalising and constructing an orthogonal state in the same \(m\) plane gives

\[|3/2,1/2\rangle=\sqrt{2/3}|0,\uparrow\rangle+\sqrt{1/3}|1,\downarrow\rangle,\qquad\]
\[|1/2,1/2\rangle=\sqrt{1/3}|0,\uparrow\rangle-\sqrt{2/3}|1,\downarrow\rangle .\]

The sign of an entire coupled vector is conventional. The relative sign of its two components matters whenever a later measurement recombines amplitudes. Applying \(J_+\) to the second combination gives zero, which verifies the top of the \(j=1/2\) multiplet independently of orthogonality. Lowering produces four states for \(j=3/2\) and two for \(j=1/2\), exhausting the six-dimensional product space. Merely listing both possible \(j\)'s does not supply the amplitude with which a prepared product state belongs to each, which is what spectroscopy needs.

There is a counting derivation for arbitrary \(j_1,j_2\). Start with one stretched state at \(m=j_1+j_2\) and remove its entire ladder. The next highest unused \(m\) supplies the next multiplet and so on down to \(|j_1-j_2|\). The identity \(\sum_{j=|j_1-j_2|}^{j_1+j_2}(2j+1)=(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)\) checks completeness. Distinct coupling schemes for three angular momenta are different bases of a common space. An intermediate angular momentum may be a reliable state label when a corresponding interaction dominates, yet cease to be conserved if an external field favours another axis or coupling.

Derive magnetic and parity selection rules

Consider a weak optical field coupled by the electric dipole interaction \(-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E\), where \(\mathbf d\) transforms as a vector and has odd parity. Its spherical components relative to \(z\) are

\[d_0=d_z,\ d_{\pm1}=\mp(d_x\pm i d_y)/\sqrt2.\]

For a definite tensor component \([J_z,d_q]=q\hbar d_q\). Sandwiching the commutator between initial and final \(J_z\) eigenstates yields

\[(m_f-m_i-q)\langle f|d_q|i\rangle=0.\]

Thus nonzero amplitudes require

\[m_f=m_i+q.\]

Coupling \(j_i\) with a rank-one tensor also requires \(|j_i-1|\le j_f\le j_i+1\), and \(0\to0\) is excluded. Parity gives a separate test: under inversion the matrix element gains factor \(-\pi_f\pi_i\), so it vanishes for equal initial and final parity in a parity-symmetric Hamiltonian. For purely orbital motion with parity \((-1)^l\), the triangle condition plus odd parity gives

\[\Delta l=\pm1.\]

A direction label on a photon depends on propagation and polarisation conventions. Stating the driven operator component \(q\) is less ambiguous than calling a beam “right circular” without defining the viewing direction.

For a spin-independent dipole transition the spin factor is \(\langle m_s'|m_s\rangle\). Begin in \(l_i=0,m_l=0,m_s=+1/2\) and drive \(q=0\) to \(l_f=1,m_l=0\). In the product basis there is one equally normalised orbital amplitude \(D\). Its projections on the two coupled final levels have squared amplitudes \(2|D|^2/3\) and \(|D|^2/3\), giving a 2:1 line-strength ratio under equal field intensity, comparable detection and negligible population saturation. For \(q=+1\) the product state is \(|1,\uparrow\rangle\). The transition to final \(j=1/2\) vanishes because no \(m=3/2\) belongs to that multiplet. These statements predict relative angular factors. Actual count rates can also depend on frequencies, radial matrix elements, initial populations and how fluorescence is collected.

As an explicit orbital check,

\[Y_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi},\]
\[Y_{10}=\sqrt{3/(4\pi)}\cos\theta.\]

The angular factor of \(d_0\propto r\cos\theta\) is \(\int Y_{10}^*\cos\theta Y_{00}\,d\Omega=1/\sqrt3\), while for \(Y_{1,\pm1}\) the azimuthal integral vanishes. A radial integral can still vanish accidentally even when all angular tests permit the transition. Symmetry rules predict zeros robust to changes preserving that symmetry; a permitted channel has an amplitude that must still be calculated.

Separate atomic structure from analyser orientation with a reduced matrix element

The angular selection rule from the \(J_z\) commutator identifies which transitions are forced to zero but does not alone give their relative strengths. A rotationally covariant way to connect the allowed amplitudes is to view an irreducible tensor component \(T_q^{(k)}\) as carrying angular momentum rank \(k\). Under rotations its \(2k+1\) components transform among themselves with the same representation matrices as the magnetic substates of an angular momentum \(k\). Coupling \(T_q^{(k)}\) to an initial \(|jm\rangle\) therefore furnishes the same product representation as coupling \(|jm\rangle\) to \(|kq\rangle\). The component projecting onto a final \(j'\) must transform as \(j'\). When this representation occurs once in the product, its relative magnetic amplitudes are fixed by Clebsch–Gordan coefficients up to one common factor independent of \(m,q,m'\).

In one standard convention the Wigner–Eckart theorem writes

\[\langle\alpha' j'm'|T_q^{(k)}|\alpha jm\rangle=\frac{\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle}{\sqrt{2j'+1}}\langle\alpha'j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert\alpha j\rangle .\]

Additional labels \(\alpha,\alpha'\) specify the radial, spin or configuration information absent from the angular labels. Conventions placing a different square-root factor in the reduced element are equally common, so a table of reduced strengths should be checked before combining it with an external coefficient table. The theorem predicts ratios within a fixed \(j,j'\) multiplet. It does not remove differences in radial integrals between unrelated configurations, nor does it assert the reduced element is nonzero. This factorisation explains why angular data alone may fix a branching ratio while offering no absolute radiative lifetime.

For a vector operator connecting \(j\) to the same \(j\), there is an especially transparent example. Within a given irreducible \(j\) space, any vector operator has matrix elements proportional to the corresponding \(J_q\) matrix elements, provided additional degeneracies are separated. For the conventional spherical components \(J_0=J_z\), \(J_{+1}=-J_+/\sqrt2\), ask for the ratio of line strengths out of \(j=2,m=1\) into \(m'=2\) via \(q=+1\) and into \(m'=1\) via \(q=0\), with equal field amplitudes in the specified components. The squared matrix elements of \(J_q\) are \(\hbar^2[6-1(2)]/2=2\hbar^2\) and \(\hbar^2\), hence the ratio is two. The same numerical angular ratio can apply to any appropriate nonzero reduced vector coupling between these specified manifolds. For an electric dipole transition parity must still allow the initial and final configurations; using \(J_q\) here computes angular ratios and does not turn angular momentum itself into an electric dipole operator.

Summing over a complete set of final magnetic substates and all tensor components supplies a useful normalisation audit. For a fixed initial \(m\), rotational invariance of the scalar sum \(\sum_q T_q^\dagger P_{j'}T_q\) makes its diagonal expectation independent of \(m\). A detector with a preferred polarisation or direction samples only a subset and may exhibit strong dependence on \(m\). This difference is essential in interpreting “unpolarised” line strengths: an unpolarised pump that populates all \(m\) equally, an unanalysed detector that sums final polarisations, and a source with a definite \(m\) viewed along one axis are three different experimental ensembles.

Compare successive Stern–Gerlach measurements with addition of spin

Two spin-\(1/2\) particles provide a minimal addition experiment with no spatial angular wavefunction needed. The coupled triplet basis is \(|1,1\rangle=|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle\),

\[|1,0\rangle=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2,\]
\[|1,-1\rangle=|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle;\]

the singlet is \((|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2\). An apparatus measuring the total \(z\) component alone cannot distinguish the two \(m=0\) states. Measuring \(J^2\) along with \(J_z\) can. Their local one-spin reduced matrices are both \(I/2\), so even complete tomography of either spin alone fails to reveal the difference. The distinction lies in a joint correlation.

The singlet projector can be obtained as a polynomial in \(\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2\). That scalar has eigenvalues \(-3\hbar^2/4,+\hbar^2/4\), so \(P_s=I/4-\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2/\hbar^2\). Expressing \(\mathbf S_i=(\hbar/2)\boldsymbol\sigma_i\) gives

\[P_s=[I-\sum_i\sigma_i\otimes\sigma_i]/4.\]

If three independently prepared ensembles yield measured same-axis correlations \((-0.80,-0.78,-0.82)\), their inferred singlet fraction is

\[(1+2.40)/4=0.85.\]

This result uses all three axes. Perfect \(z\)-anticorrelation alone constrains the population to a two-dimensional \(m=0\) space but says nothing about the relative phase within it. If each measurement has uncertainty 0.03 and the estimates are independent, the resulting statistical uncertainty in singlet fraction is \(\sqrt3(0.03)/4=0.013\), before calibration uncertainties.

For a coherent initial product \(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle\), a Hamiltonian \(H=J_{\rm ex}\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2/\hbar^2\) evolves the singlet and triplet amplitudes with distinct phases. Writing the initial state as \((|1,0\rangle+|0,0\rangle)/\sqrt2\), the probability to find spins reversed later is \(\sin^2(J_{\rm ex}t/(2\hbar))\). The total \(m\) remains exactly zero at all times. If \(J_{\rm ex}/h=5.00\) MHz, the first full reversal occurs at \(t=1/(2\times5\,\mathrm{MHz})=100\) ns. An incoherent mixture with the same singlet and triplet populations has a constant reversal probability one half. This calculation makes the signs in the coupled basis observable and distinguishes a true exchange evolution from random spin flips inferred solely from individual populations.

Rotate the quantisation axis without changing the atomic Hamiltonian

A transverse magnetic or polarisation analyser can interrogate a state prepared relative to \(z\) along a different laboratory axis. For spin \(1/2\), \(e^{-i\theta S_y/\hbar}=\cos(\theta/2)I-i\sin(\theta/2)\sigma_y\), from \(\sigma_y^2=I\). To obtain the rotation probabilities for a spin-one state without memorising a rotation matrix, represent its stretched state as the symmetric product of two up spinors. Under a common rotation each spinor transforms as \(\cos(\theta/2)|\uparrow\rangle+\sin(\theta/2)|\downarrow\rangle\). Expanding the two-spin product gives the triplet amplitudes \(\cos^2(\theta/2),\sqrt2\cos(\theta/2)\sin(\theta/2),\sin^2(\theta/2)\) for the three \(m\) values. This construction exploits the representation and applies to any angular momentum one, regardless of its microscopic origin.

At \(\theta=\pi/2\), a stretched \(m=1\) state projected on the rotated axis gives probabilities \((1/4,1/2,1/4)\). The expectation of its rotated component vanishes, but outcomes \(+\hbar\) and \(-\hbar\) still occur and the variance is \(\hbar^2/2\). A source prepared in \(m=0\) along \(z\) gives a different test. Rotate its symmetrised up–down form on both spins and project on \(m=0\); the amplitude is \(\cos\theta\) and the probability \(\cos^2\theta\). At a right angle the “zero” outcome is absent, although the mean along either axis is zero. A single mean value cannot distinguish alignment from an unpolarised mixture. Measuring the full resolved substate distribution, or its quadrupole moment, can.

The general stretched state \(|j,j\rangle\) can be represented by \(2j\) symmetrised spinors all up. Its rotated \(m\) outcome distribution is binomial:

\[P(m)=\binom{2j}{j-m}\cos^{2(j+m)}(\theta/2)\sin^{2(j-m)}(\theta/2).\]

Its mean angular momentum component is \(\hbar j\cos\theta\), with variance \(\hbar^2(j/2)\sin^2\theta\). At fixed nonzero \(\cos\theta\), the relative fluctuation falls like \(j^{-1/2}\) as \(j\) grows. This supplies a precise sense in which a large stretched state can approximate a classical oriented angular momentum while retaining quantum projection noise. A state with the same large \(j\) but \(m=0\) need not resemble an oriented classical arrow. The representation dimension alone does not determine the suitability of a classical approximation.

Distinguish an angularly forbidden line from coherent cancellation

Suppose two degenerate lower magnetic states \(|g_1\rangle,|g_2\rangle\) both couple to one excited state \(|e\rangle\) under controlled coherent drives. In a rotating-frame model the raising part is \(|e\rangle(\Omega_1\langle g_1|+\Omega_2\langle g_2|)\hbar/2\), with the lowering term its adjoint. A normalised superposition proportional to \(\Omega_2|g_1\rangle-\Omega_1|g_2\rangle\) has zero excitation amplitude because the two terms cancel. Each angular channel can be individually allowed and strong, yet this particular coherent combination is dark. Its orthogonal bright state is proportional to \(\Omega_1^*|g_1\rangle+\Omega_2^*|g_2\rangle\) and has effective Rabi coupling \(\sqrt{|\Omega_1|^2+|\Omega_2|^2}\).

For equal real drives and an initially balanced antisymmetric superposition, the transition ideally vanishes. A randomly prepared mixture of the same two basis states populates the bright and dark subspaces equally. If a resonant pulse transfers the bright component completely, that mixture produces 50% excitation. Changing the relative optical phase changes which superposition is dark but does not change the selection rule for either individual transition. Preparing a calibrated ground-state phase and scanning the drive phase can therefore distinguish coherent cancellation from a forbidden angular matrix element or a disconnected drive channel. Two-photon detuning, magnetic splitting of the lower states and dephasing can spoil the destructive interference even if every angular coefficient is exactly known.

Predict which perturbation can make a dark transition visible

Electric dipole selection rules use the approximation that the driving field is essentially uniform across the atom. Expand a monochromatic plane wave \(e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}=1+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r+\cdots\). For a source of size \(a\) the next spatial term is suppressed in amplitude by a factor of order \(ka\) when \(ka\ll1\). Consistently expanding the light–matter Hamiltonian yields higher electric multipoles and magnetic couplings with their own symmetry. The electric quadrupole is a rank-two even-parity tensor. Thus it can connect same-parity states with \(|j_i-2|\le j_f\le j_i+2\). A magnetic dipole is a rank-one operator of even spatial parity, permitting some same-parity transitions denied to an electric dipole. A line forbidden to one specified multipole need not vanish in all experimental conditions.

Take a model atom with a lower \(j=0\) even state and an upper \(j=2\) even state. Electric dipole fails both parity and angular-momentum tests. Magnetic dipole has the right parity but still cannot supply total angular rank two. Electric quadrupole has the correct parity and satisfies the triangle condition; its actual reduced matrix element and the applied field gradient still determine strength. In a \(j=0\to j'=2\) transition with \(m_i=0\), each spherical quadrupole component \(q\) excites only \(m_f=q\). A simple claim that changing circular to linear polarisation opens an electric dipole line in this field-free atom would be inconsistent with both global selection rules. A static field that mixes opposite parity or different angular momentum can change the states themselves and thereby modify that conclusion.

For a 600 nm wavelength and characteristic radius \(a=0.10\) nm,

\[ka=2\pi a/\lambda=1.05\times10^{-3}.\]

The squared geometric factor is about \(1.1\times10^{-6}\) relative to a typical allowed electric dipole strength in comparable units, before distinct radial and angular factors. This is an order estimate, not a quantitative branching ratio for two arbitrary lines. In particular, dividing a finite quadrupole amplitude by a strictly zero dipole amplitude of the same ideal transition is meaningless. Compare with a separate reference line whose dipole matrix element and detection efficiencies are known if a rate ratio is needed.

An independent way to brighten an otherwise forbidden line is mixing through a perturbation \(W\). Suppose an upper state \(|u\rangle\) has zero electric dipole amplitude from \(|g\rangle\), while a nearby state \(|v\rangle\) allows amplitude \(D\). If \(|\langle v|W|u\rangle|\ll|E_u-E_v|\), the perturbed upper state contains coefficient \(\epsilon=\langle v|W|u\rangle/(E_u-E_v)\) of \(|v\rangle\), and its borrowed dipole intensity scales as \(|\epsilon|^2|D|^2\). Where \(W\) is proportional to a reversible static field, borrowed intensity typically begins quadratically in that field for a previously dark isolated line. A native higher-multipole contribution may already be present at zero field and can interfere if it feeds the same final channel with coherent angular dependence.

Use polarisation and bias sweeps to identify borrowed intensity

In a two-state model the bright level is 2.00 GHz away and a perturbation mixes it with the dark state at off-diagonal \(W/h=20\) MHz. Predict relative borrowed intensity, assess the leading approximation, and identify an independent spectroscopic consequence.

Derive and check the result

The small parameter is

\[20/2000=0.010.\]

The leading borrowed intensity is \(10^{-4}\) of the corresponding bright reference, with corrections of relative order \(10^{-4}\) in the ideal two-level mixing expansion. The dark state's energy shifts by approximately \(W^2/(E_u-E_v)\), whose magnitude expressed as ordinary frequency is

\[20^2/2000=0.20\,\mathrm{MHz}.\]

Its sign is away from the neighbouring bright level. Measuring this level repulsion together with the same polarisation pattern as the borrowed bright transition tests the mixing explanation. Changing the perturbing field by a factor of two should increase both the leading borrowed intensity and the magnitude of the second-order level shift by a factor of four while the coupling remains weak. A field-independent residual or a different polarisation dependence motivates examining other multipoles or additional states.

Tutorial · infer the state that a spectrum actually selects

Measure the orbital label after total angular momentum preparation

Prepare \(|j=1/2,m=1/2\rangle\) in the coupled \(p\) manifold. Predict \(m_l\) outcomes, the spin expectation and the sum \(\langle L_z+S_z\rangle\). Is either product component a state with definite \(J^2\)?

Work through the solution

The probabilities are \(1/3\) for \(m_l=0\) with spin up, \(2/3\) for \(m_l=1\) with spin down. Thus \(\langle L_z\rangle=2\hbar/3\), \(\langle S_z\rangle=-\hbar/6\) and the sum is \(\hbar/2\). The product vectors in this two-dimensional plane are each superpositions of both \(j\)'s, as can be checked by inverting the two orthogonal combinations. A joint measurement of orbital and spin labels can therefore disturb a previously exact \(J^2\). The total \(m\) remains fixed in every outcome, stronger than merely having the correct mean.

Determine a spin–orbit interval without an angular integral

Within \(l=1,s=1/2\), take \(H'=A\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar^2\) with \(A/h=8\) GHz. Find level shifts, degeneracies and their degeneracy-weighted centre. Check the result from a product-basis trace.

Work through the solution

Use \(2\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=J^2-L^2-S^2\). For \(j=3/2\) the factor is \(+1/2\); for \(j=1/2\) it is \(-1\). Shifts are \(+4\) and \(-8\) GHz, with degeneracies four and two; their interval is 12 GHz and their weighted centre is zero. The product-basis trace of each \(L_iS_i\) is \(\operatorname{tr}L_i\,\operatorname{tr}S_i=0\), which predicts that centre independently. If a measured centroid is weighted by optical intensity instead of counting every substate once, it need not equal this trace centre.

Distinguish resolved lines from a coherent pulse

A short \(q=0\) pulse prepares the product final state \(|0,\uparrow\rangle\) above. If its spectrum covers both \(j\) levels coherently, find the later return probability to that same product state for a level angular-frequency splitting \(\Delta\). What would an incoherent population mixture give?

Work through the solution

The return amplitude is \((2/3)e^{-iE_{3/2}t/\hbar}+(1/3)e^{-iE_{1/2}t/\hbar}\). Its squared modulus is \(5/9+(4/9)\cos\Delta t\), between \(1/9\) and 1. The corresponding incoherent mixture gives a constant \(5/9\). At \(t=0\) both constructions share the same energy-measurement probabilities, yet only the coherent preparation returns with certainty to the original product state. Temporal resolution, controlled pulse phase and bandwidth sufficient to cover both lines are part of the proposal. If the emitted or absorbed field records distinguishable frequencies, the observed beat can be suppressed.
