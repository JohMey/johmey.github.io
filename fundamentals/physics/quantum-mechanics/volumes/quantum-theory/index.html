Fundamentals / Physics / Quantum mechanics

Quantum states, dynamics and measurement

Build the connection between preparation, time evolution and measurement. Develop the algebra, approximations and scattering theory needed to predict a spectrum or a set of count rates.

    Contents

    1. From two-path interference to the geometry of a quantum state

      Two preparations give the same counts at one analyser. Place a second analyser in a rotated basis and the counts separate. From this experiment develop amplitudes, phase, projective measurement, mixed states and the geometry of distinguishability.

    2. What a momentum eigenstate means in a finite experiment

      A detector never counts a perfectly sharp momentum eigenket. Derive continuum normalisation from a finite box, change the spectral measure from momentum to energy and predict the full evolution of a normalisable Gaussian packet.

    3. Why an oscillator can move without its packet spreading

      Start with the measured mass and curvature of a potential. Factor its Hamiltonian, prove the energy ladder, construct a freely moving Gaussian from number states and find when squeezing or anharmonicity changes that prediction.

    4. Adding angular momentum and predicting which lines can appear

      A three-component orbital wavefunction combines with a two-component spin. Derive the allowed total angular momenta, construct their amplitudes explicitly and predict both forbidden transitions and relative strengths in a polarisation-resolved spectrum.

    5. From a Coulomb orbit to the fine-structure multiplet

      Separate the hydrogenic Schrödinger equation, identify the origin of its large degeneracy and evaluate the three dominant relativistic corrections. The cancellation among them predicts an equality that spectroscopy can then test.

    6. When a magnetic field changes the quantum numbers of an atom

      Weak-field Zeeman slopes label total-angular-momentum multiplets. At stronger fields those slopes curve and intensities migrate. Solve an entire orbital-plus-spin manifold and infer coupling constants from measurable line positions and polarisation.

    7. Two electrons, exchange splitting and the cost of localisation

      Build a two-electron state that respects exchange, derive its charge and spin correlations, and then solve a two-site model exactly. The two examples reveal when a triplet is favoured and when an apparently weak hopping lowers the singlet instead.

    8. From a finite pulse to a continuum transition rate

      Switch a weak perturbation on for a measured duration. Its Fourier transform determines the amplitude of a discrete transition. Summing over a dense set of final states reveals where a time-independent transition rate comes from and where it fails.

    9. Inferring a short-range interaction from elastic scattering

      A beam of neutral particles encounters a weak, localised static potential. Derive differential cross section from current, extract phase shifts by radial matching and compare exact low-energy scattering with the Born approximation and its failure near a bound state.

    10. Distinguishing entanglement from ordinary shared randomness

      Two distant analysers sometimes agree even when individual outcomes look random. Derive which correlations shared classical information can produce, compute the quantum prediction and identify what a finite table of coincidence counts can establish.

    11. How an unobserved environment changes a quantum state

      Model a qubit and a two-state environment by a joint unitary. Derive channels, the continuous-time master equation and the relation between longitudinal and transverse relaxation. A recorded event and an unread detector produce different conditional predictions.

    12. How a turning point supplies the missing semiclassical phase

      Far from a turning point the wavelength follows the classical momentum. At the turning point that approximation becomes singular. Derive the Airy connection, quantise a smooth well, distinguish a hard wall and estimate a tunnelling exponent with a defensible error check.

    13. Measure geometric phase from three spin overlaps

      A numerical eigensolver is free to choose a new complex phase for every eigenvector. A product of three measured overlaps removes these arbitrary choices. Relate its argument to a spherical triangle, then see why a vanishing overlap destroys phase information.

    14. From electron exchange to atomic shells and molecular binding

      An orbital model can explain why a closed atomic shell is special and why a shared electron can bind two protons. Both predictions need an energy as well as a rule for counting states. We construct the energies and check them against their separated-particle limits.

    15. The scattering integral equation and successive Born terms

      A weak potential often produces an angular pattern resembling its Fourier transform. What controls that approximation? A particle can propagate between two interactions with the same potential. Accounting for that propagation builds an integral equation and supplies a calculable second term.

    16. Spontaneous radiation, stimulated transitions and thermal detailed balance

      A coherent laser can drive Rabi oscillations in two atomic levels. A large cavity filled with thermal radiation presents many modes with random phases. In this limit one can infer the ratios of absorption, stimulated emission and spontaneous emission coefficients by requiring the observed equilibrium to be independent of the atoms used to probe it.

    17. Which properties of an unknown spin can an instrument copy?

      A device calibrated with spin up and down can write two perfectly distinguishable records. Asking what happens to a superposition reveals a limit on information extraction and explains why state tomography needs an ensemble.
