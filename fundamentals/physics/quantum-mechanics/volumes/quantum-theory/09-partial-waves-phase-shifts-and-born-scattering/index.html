Inferring a short-range interaction from elastic scattering

A beam of neutral particles encounters a weak, localised static potential. Derive differential cross section from current, extract phase shifts by radial matching and compare exact low-energy scattering with the Born approximation and its failure near a bound state.

Define an observable from incident and outgoing current

Consider elastic scattering from a real, short-range, spherically symmetric potential in three dimensions. In a two-body problem use the relative coordinate and reduced mass \(m\); here the target may simply be a prescribed static potential. At a large distance the stationary solution for a plane wave incident along \(z\) has the form \(\psi\simeq e^{ikz}+f(\theta)e^{ikr}/r\). The amplitude \(f\) has dimensions of length in this convention. Incident current density is \(\hbar k/m\) for unit plane-wave amplitude. The outgoing spherical component has radial current density \(\hbar k|f(\theta)|^2/(mr^2)\) to leading order. Multiplying by detector area \(r^2d\Omega\) and dividing by incident flux gives

\[d\sigma/d\Omega=|f(\theta)|^2.\]

Normalised wave packets and finite detectors supply a physical limiting interpretation for the plane waves.

The incident and scattered components also interfere. Away from a vanishingly narrow forward region the far-field detector resolves the outgoing scattered beam in this derivation. Integrated forward interference accounts for removed incident flux and leads to the optical theorem. Dropping the cross term everywhere and then demanding total current conservation would incorrectly add a stream of scattered particles without depleting the forward beam. For different outgoing and incident wave numbers an additional ratio of group velocities enters the differential flux; the simple squared amplitude here assumes elastic kinematics.

Match a regular radial solution to free waves

Expand a plane wave in Legendre polynomials as \(e^{ikz}=\sum_l(2l+1)i^l j_l(kr)P_l(\cos\theta)\). Outside a short-range potential the reduced radial function in each partial wave is a combination of spherical Bessel and Neumann solutions, with large-distance behaviour proportional to \(\sin(kr-l\pi/2+\delta_l)\). Fix the incoming part equal to that in the incident plane wave. Then the ratio by which the outgoing coefficient is changed is \(S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}\). For a real one-channel Hamiltonian conservation of each partial wave's radial current gives \(|S_l|=1\), so \(\delta_l\) is real modulo \(\pi\). It is the change in radial phase at the same energy, not a separate potential-dependent energy shift of the free particles far from the target.

Separating the excess outgoing part gives

\[f(\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^\infty\frac{2l+1}{k}e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta),\qquad\]
\[\sigma_{\rm el}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l .\]

The second formula follows from orthogonality of Legendre polynomials in the angular integral. At \(\theta=0\), \(P_l(1)=1\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l)=\sin^2\delta_l\), so \(\sigma_{\rm el}=4\pi\operatorname{Im}f(0)/k\) in this purely elastic model. The differential intensity at some other angle can result from cancellations between waves, even though their contributions to the integrated cross section add separately. A narrow angular detector cannot replace full angular integration without a model of the partial waves.

Derive and interpret a scattering length

Take an attractive spherical well \(V=-V_0\) for \(r<R\), zero outside. At zero incident energy the regular \(s\)-wave reduced solution inside is \(u=A\sin(\kappa r)\),

\[\kappa=\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar.\]

Outside \(u=B(r-a)\); extrapolating this line to zero defines the scattering length \(a\). Matching the logarithmic derivative at \(R\) gives \(\kappa\cot(\kappa R)=1/(R-a)\), or

\[a=R\left[1-\frac{\tan u}{u}\right],\]
\[u=\kappa R .\]

A regular zero-energy extrapolation node can lie outside the range of the potential. It is an intercept characterising the exterior solution and need not mark an actual hard surface. For weak attraction \(u\ll1\) one finds \(a/R=-u^2/3-2u^4/15+\cdots\), so \(a\) is negative. As \(u\) crosses \(\pi/2\) the first \(s\)-wave bound state appears at threshold and \(a\) diverges and changes sign. A large positive scattering length can indicate a shallow bound state for a short-range attraction, whereas a hard repulsive sphere also has positive length. Its sign alone cannot reconstruct an arbitrary potential.

At nonzero \(k\), regular interior solution has wave number \(q=\sqrt{k^2+\kappa^2}\). Match \(q\cot(qR)=k\cot(kR+\delta_0)\) to compute an exact phase modulo \(\pi\). For \(kR\ll1\) and away from singular length scales, the effective range expansion \(k\cot\delta_0=-1/a+r_e k^2/2+\cdots\) describes the first energy dependence. Writing \(f_0=1/(k\cot\delta_0-ik)\) retains unitarity automatically. Neglecting the effective range gives

\[\sigma_0=4\pi a^2/(1+k^2a^2).\]

The zero-energy result \(4\pi a^2\) is justified only if \(k|a|\ll1\) as well as range effects being small. Taking \(a\) to infinity at fixed \(k\) gives the finite bound \(4\pi/k^2\) for one partial wave. The apparent infinite cross section at the threshold arose from interchanging limits.

Turn a potential into a first-order Fourier amplitude

The outgoing free Green function obeys \((\nabla^2+k^2)e^{ikr}/r=-4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf r)\). Rearrange Schrödinger's equation into a free wave plus an integral of this kernel times \(V\psi\). At large observation distance \(|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\simeq r-\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r'\). Replacing the wave inside the integral by the incident plane wave gives the first Born amplitude

\[f_B(\mathbf k',\mathbf k)=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int e^{-i(\mathbf k'-\mathbf k)\cdot\mathbf r'}V(\mathbf r')\,d^3r',\]
\[|\mathbf k'-\mathbf k|=2k\sin(\theta/2).\]

For the square well the angular integral is \(4\pi\sin(Qr)/(Qr)\),

\[Q=|\mathbf k'-\mathbf k|.\]

Integrating \(r^2dr\) gives

\[f_B=(2mV_0/\hbar^2)[\sin(QR)-QR\cos(QR)]/Q^3.\]

At \(Q\to0\) the bracket is \((QR)^3/3+\cdots\), so \(a_B=-f_B(0)=-u^2R/3\). This reproduces the first weak-well term but misses the bound-state pole entirely. For a real central potential \(f_B(0)\) is real. Its first-order imaginary part is zero, while the integrated Born cross section is already second order in \(V\). The forward imaginary contribution needed by the optical theorem first appears at that same second order. Applying the theorem as though a first-order amplitude were exact would mix orders inconsistently.

Extract a phase numerically without fitting an arbitrary sinusoid

For a finite-range real potential, one of the most useful laboratory calculations integrates each radial equation from its regular origin to a matching radius \(r_m\) outside the interaction. Use the reduced radial functions \(\hat j_l(z)=zj_l(z)\) and \(\hat n_l(z)=zn_l(z)\); their large-\(z\) limits are \(\sin(z-l\pi/2)\) and \(-\cos(z-l\pi/2)\). The exterior solution can be written \(u=A[\hat j_l(kr)\cos\delta_l-\hat n_l(kr)\sin\delta_l]\). The interior ODE supplies \(u_m,u'_m\) up to an arbitrary common normalisation. Form the matching determinants rather than estimating a phase by reading one oscillation maximum from a plot.

Taking the radial derivative and eliminating \(A\) gives

\[\tan\delta_l=\frac{k\hat j_l'(kr_m)u_m-\hat j_l(kr_m)u'_m}{k\hat n_l'(kr_m)u_m-\hat n_l(kr_m)u'_m},\]

where primes on the hatted functions differentiate their dimensionless argument. Both numerator and denominator remain finite if \(u_m\) happens to lie close to a node; a direct logarithmic derivative \(u'_m/u_m\) can instead amplify roundoff severely. Use the two-argument arctangent before reducing the result modulo \(\pi\). A reported phase can jump by \(\pi\) under a continuously varying energy while \(\sin^2\delta_l\) and \(S_l\) remain continuous. Unwrap branches by checking neighbouring energies together with the desired physical reference, especially when estimating an energy derivative of the phase.

For \(l=0,\hat j_0=\sin z,\hat n_0=-\cos z\), choose a simple controlled test: a hard sphere of radius \(R\) imposes \(u(R)=0\). With \(u_m=0\) at \(r_m=R\), the ratio reduces to \(-\tan kR\), giving \(\delta_0=-kR\pmod\pi\). At \(kR=0.40\), the exact contribution is \(4\pi R^2[\sin(0.40)/0.40]^2=0.9478(4\pi R^2)\), while the zero-energy length estimate gives \(4\pi R^2\). The finite-\(k\) effective-range expansion of the hard sphere follows from \(k\cot\delta_0=-k\cot kR=-1/R+k^2R/3+\cdots\), so

\[a=R,\ r_e=2R/3.\]

Comparing this term with the previous denominator retaining \(a\) alone explains why their leading corrections at finite \(k\) need not agree.

A general radial solver should separately vary the first finite radius, step size, matching radius and maximum partial wave \(l_{\max}\). Near a nonsingular origin the regular solution behaves up to normalisation as \(r^{l+1}\); initialize both value and derivative with this local solution or a more accurate series. For Coulomb or more singular long-range interactions the exterior wavefunctions must be changed rather than matching to free Bessel functions at an arbitrary finite radius. Relative error near a zero of differential intensity can be meaningless as a convergence measure. Report absolute angular error there along with integrated current, compare several scattering directions and retain the exact free result \(V=0\) as a phase-zero test.

Relate a rapidly varying phase to a wave-packet time delay

A narrow incident energy packet accumulates an extra phase from an elastic partial wave relative to free propagation. The \(S\)-matrix phase is \(2\delta_l(E)\). In a stationary-phase arrival-time calculation the derivative of phase with respect to energy shifts the envelope peak, giving the Wigner time delay \(\tau_l=2\hbar\,d\delta_l/dE\) when the potential varies slowly across the packet bandwidth and the outgoing branch is well resolved. A derivative of the observable scattering intensity is not enough; \(\sin^2\delta\) loses the branch and sign information required for a delay. Causality and reference distances must be treated with care, particularly when a finite detector counts a sum of waves or a long-range potential changes even the asymptotic reference.

A narrow isolated resonance can be represented approximately by a slowly varying background plus a phase with \(\cot\delta_{\rm res}=(E_r-E)/(\Gamma/2)\). Choose the continuous branch increasing through \(\pi/2\). Then

\[\sin^2\delta_{\rm res}=(\Gamma/2)^2/[(E-E_r)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2],\]
\[d\delta_{\rm res}/dE=(\Gamma/2)/[(E-E_r)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2].\]

With nearly constant \(k\) and zero background the partial cross section has full energy width \(\Gamma\) and its peak reaches the unitarity limit. The associated peak delay in this convention is \(4\hbar/\Gamma\), whereas the population lifetime inferred from an isolated pole with amplitude \(e^{-\Gamma t/(2\hbar)}\) is \(\hbar/\Gamma\). Both involve the same width, but comparing them as if they were identical measurements would introduce a factor of four.

For \(\Gamma=0.10\) meV and \(\hbar=0.6582\ \mathrm{meV\,ps}\), the pole lifetime is 6.58 ps and the peak partial-wave delay is 26.3 ps in this model. A finite incoming energy spread comparable to 0.10 meV samples a changing phase derivative; it can distort the shape of the outgoing packet and invalidate the simple peak-delay interpretation. A background phase also causes interference: \(\sin^2(\delta_{\rm bg}+\delta_{\rm res})\) may exhibit an asymmetric feature or a dip while the resonant phase still evolves by almost \(\pi\). Observing the peak position of a single angular intensity curve is therefore a poor standalone estimator of the resonance energy when background amplitudes are appreciable.

Symmetrise the scattering amplitude for identical particles

If two identical particles emerge into opposite directions in their centre-of-mass frame, detecting one at \(\theta\) and the other at \(\pi-\theta\) does not assign unique incoming particle labels. For a definite spatial symmetry the indistinguishable alternatives interfere. For spinless identical bosons the amplitude is \(f(\theta)+f(\pi-\theta)\); for a spatially antisymmetric channel it is \(f(\theta)-f(\pi-\theta)\). A differential cross section defined as the number of unordered final pairs per incident flux can be integrated over one hemisphere, or over the whole sphere with a factor \(1/2\). If an instrument instead records the count of individual outgoing particles over all angles it uses a different event convention. State the convention before quoting integrated enhancement or suppression factors.

Since \(P_l(-\cos\theta)=(-1)^lP_l(\cos\theta)\), the symmetric combination retains only even \(l\) and the antisymmetric one only odd \(l\). At very low energy short-range \(s\)-wave scattering is typically dominant. For spinless identical bosons with \(f\simeq-a\), the unordered-pair total becomes

\[2\pi|2a|^2=8\pi a^2;\]

for distinguishable particles it is \(4\pi a^2\). Fully spin-polarised identical fermions instead have antisymmetric spatial motion and no \(s\)-wave channel. Low-energy collisions can be suppressed without a repulsive force. If internal spin states differ or are mixed, the allowed symmetric and antisymmetric spin fractions must be tracked before assigning a measured cross section.

For two unpolarised spin-\(1/2\) identical fermions with equal independent spin populations and a spin-independent potential, the spin singlet fraction is \(1/4\) and permits symmetric spatial motion; the triplet fraction \(3/4\) requires antisymmetric spatial motion. Under the stated unordered-pair convention the low-energy total from the \(s\)-wave singlet is \(2\pi a^2\) after spin averaging. Preparing a particular unlike-spin product and tagging the two final spin species changes the counting experiment, and one should compute its spin-resolved amplitudes rather than reusing the unpolarised factor. A detector that can label internal states can distinguish alternatives that a spin-insensitive detector combines.

See which potential features low-energy data can determine

At low momentum a finite set of parameters such as \(a,r_e\) summarises scattering over a narrow energy band. This does not usually identify a complete potential \(V(r)\). An illustrative degeneracy is already visible within spherical square wells: \(\tan u/u\) has poles and multiple branches, so different well strengths at the same range can give the same scattering length while supporting different numbers of bound states. A single very-low-energy total cross section loses even the sign of that length. Including a measured bound-state count, phase-sensitive interference or higher partial waves can remove some of these ambiguities. The intended parameterisation and the measured energy interval define the inverse problem.

To extract \(a,r_e\) from phase-sensitive \(s\)-wave data, plot \(Y=k\cot\delta_0\) against \(X=k^2\). In the effective-range window a straight-line fit has intercept \(-1/a\) and slope \(r_e/2\). Suppose values in nm units are \((X,Y)=(0.010,-0.190),(0.040,-0.160),(0.090,-0.110)\). They lie on \(Y=-0.200+1.00X\), giving \(a=5.00\) nm and \(r_e=2.00\) nm. Repeat at a fourth moderately larger \(k\) to test curvature from higher terms, then repeat the fit omitting that point. A near-zero intercept makes the extracted \(a\) highly sensitive to measurement noise even if individual phase points are accurate. Reporting the intercept and its uncertainty directly can be more informative than a huge indistinct scattering length with asymmetric error bars.

Derive an effective range from a zero-energy solution

The scattering length captures the intercept of a zero-energy exterior \(s\)-wave. A second coefficient can be related to how the interior wave differs from that extrapolated straight line. For a finite-range real potential choose the dimensionless zero-energy radial solution \(u_0(r)\to1-r/a\) outside the range. A convenient finite-\(k\) exterior normalisation is \(u_k=\cos kr+\cot\delta_0\,\sin kr\); its zero-energy limit gives the same straight line when \(k\cot\delta_0\to-1/a\). Two regular radial equations at energies \(0,\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\) give a Wronskian derivative \(d(u_0u_k'-u_0'u_k)/dr=-k^2u_0u_k\). Integrate to a radius beyond the potential. The origin contribution vanishes for regular solutions.

Expand the exterior wave using \(k\cot\delta_0=-1/a+r_e k^2/2+\cdots\). To order \(k^2\) it is \(u_k=v+k^2w\), with \(v=1-r/a\) and \(w=-r^2/2+r^3/(6a)+r_e r/2\). In the exterior Wronskian \(v w'-v'w\) simplifies to \(r_e/2-\int_0^r(1-s/a)^2ds\). Equating to the integrated differential equation gives Bethe's effective range identity

\[r_e=2\int_0^\infty\left[\left(1-\frac r a\right)^2-u_0(r)^2\right]dr .\]

The integrand vanishes identically outside the true range, so the formal infinite limit is harmless. Both solutions must use the specified equal exterior normalisation; rescaling one independently would leave a divergent difference rather than a material coefficient. The proof also shows why a single scattering length cannot fix an effective range: the former fixes only the exterior line, while \(r_e\) measures an integral of the true interior probability relative to its extrapolation.

For a hard sphere of radius \(R=a\), set \(u_0=0\) inside. The integral gives \(2\int_0^R(1-r/R)^2dr=2R/3\), agreeing with the direct expansion of \(-k\cot kR\). For the attractive square well inside radius \(R\), write \(u_0=(1-R/a)\sin(\kappa r)/\sin(\kappa R)\). The integral of its square uses \(\int_0^R\sin^2(\kappa r)dr=R/2-\sin(2\kappa R)/(4\kappa)\). Combining with the scattering-length matching condition reduces the result to \(r_e=R-R^3/(3a^2)-1/(\kappa^2 a)\). This expression should not be evaluated at exactly zero scattering length using naive division; the adopted expansion and exterior normalisation then have to be reconsidered. Close to a diverging length in this finite well one instead finds \(r_e\to R\), a natural range scale even while \(|a|\) is much larger.

Distinguish two short-range interactions from their energy-dependent phases

A hard sphere and a different finite-range well happen to have equal scattering length \(a=5.0\) nm but effective ranges \(3.33\) and \(5.00\) nm. Predict the difference of \(k\cot\delta_0\) at \(k=0.10\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\). Which measured uncertainty would resolve it at about three standard deviations?

Derive and check the result

The shift between the two predictions is

\[\Delta(k\cot\delta)=\tfrac12(5.00-3.33)(0.10)^2=0.00835\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}.\]

Their values are approximately \(-0.18335\) and \(-0.1750\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\). If the theoretical curves and common length are accurately specified, a measurement uncertainty below roughly \(0.0028\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) would distinguish their difference at three standard deviations. If comparing two independent equally uncertain experimental values, require each to be below \(0.00835/(3\sqrt2)=0.00197\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\). The expansion error from fourth and higher powers of \(k\) must also be smaller than the target difference. Repeating at multiple wave numbers tests that the observed difference scales as \(k^2\) before treating it as evidence for a distinct effective range.

Read a spatial length scale from an angular diffraction envelope

For a weak smooth isotropic potential \(V(r)=V_g\exp[-r^2/(2R^2)]\), the first Born amplitude follows from three one-dimensional Gaussian Fourier integrals. In the convention used above,

\[f_B(Q)=-\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{mV_gR^3}{\hbar^2}\exp(-Q^2R^2/2).\]

Its differential intensity relative to the forward value is \(\exp(-Q^2R^2)\), with \(Q=2k\sin(\theta/2)\). Forming this ratio cancels the unknown scalar prefactor and fixes the product \(kR\) from the angular width. In contrast, a single absolute forward intensity measures \(|V_g|^2R^6\) and cannot separate radius and strength. Neither intensity-only measurement fixes the sign of \(V_g\) at this order. Thus even in an exactly specified two-parameter family, different angular data have different inverse information.

Suppose \(k=0.40\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) and the intensity at \(60^\circ\) is half that at zero angle. Then \(Q=k\) at \(60^\circ\) and \(R=\sqrt{\ln2}/k=2.08\) nm. At \(90^\circ\), \(Q=\sqrt2k\), so the predicted ratio is \(1/4\). A third angle tests the Gaussian shape rather than merely refitting its radius. An unmodelled isotropic background would lift the ratio at large angle and bias the inferred radius low if one took logarithms of raw intensity without fitting that background. Use counts divided by incident flux, detector solid angle and efficiency before fitting. Differences of measurements at several wave numbers can check whether the same spatial \(R\) accounts for all angular envelopes.

The integrated cross section can also be evaluated without numerical quadrature. Since \(Q^2R^2=2(kR)^2(1-\cos\theta)\), integrate over \(u=\cos\theta\) to obtain

\[\sigma_B=2\pi|f_B(0)|^2[1-e^{-4(kR)^2}]/[2(kR)^2].\]

At \(kR\to0\) the ratio of the exponential difference to \(2(kR)^2\) tends to two, yielding the isotropic \(4\pi|f_B(0)|^2\). At \(kR\gg1\) the angular envelope narrows and the integrated cross section falls roughly as \(1/(kR)^2\) relative to forward intensity. This is a geometrical focusing of the weak-scattering distribution and does not imply that the physical range of the potential changes with incident energy.

Test first Born consistency after fitting a length

For the Gaussian model a fitted low-energy length magnitude is \(0.15R\). Infer the dimensionless strength \(\eta=m|V_g|R^2/\hbar^2\) predicted by the Born formula. How would a comparison at a second field preparation test whether the amplitude fit is still perturbative?

Derive and check the result

The model gives \(|a_B|/R=\sqrt{2\pi}\eta\), hence \(\eta=0.0598\). This is small compared with unity, a useful first consistency check for weak low-energy scattering by a smooth single-scale potential. It does not rule out other interactions or state-dependent channels absent from this parametric model. If a controlled preparation doubles the potential amplitude without changing range, first Born predicts four times the differential intensity at every angle and the same normalised angular envelope. Failure of either scaling after calibration can reveal higher-order scattering or a changing spatial form. The test also illustrates why fitting an angular width alone is insufficient to establish that the Born approximation is controlled.

Tutorial · decide which approximation the data support

Compare exact and perturbative lengths at two well strengths

For \(R=2.00\) nm, compute \(a\) and \(a_B\) for \(u=0.50\) and \(1.40\). At \(k=0.020\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\), determine whether \(4\pi a^2\) is a good low-energy cross-section estimate.

Work through the solution

At \(u=0.50\), \(a/R=1-\tan(0.50)/0.50=-0.0926\), giving \(-0.185\) nm versus \(-0.167\) nm in Born approximation. Here \(kR=0.040\) and \(k|a|\simeq0.0037\). At \(u=1.40\), \(\tan1.40\simeq5.798\), giving \(a=-6.28\) nm against \(a_B=-1.31\) nm. Now \(k|a|\simeq0.126\); the leading unitarity denominator alone reduces \(4\pi a^2\) by about 1.6%, before effective-range effects. Weak range resolution and weak scattering are separate criteria. A particle can have wavelength much larger than the target while Born approximation performs poorly.

Reconstruct interference between two partial waves

Only \(l=0,1\) contribute, with \(\delta_0=\pi/4,\delta_1=-\pi/6\). Find \(k^2 d\sigma/d\Omega\) forward and backward and \(k^2\sigma_{\rm el}\). Verify the optical theorem with the forward value.

Work through the solution

In units of \(1/k\), the \(s\) coefficient is \((1+i)/2\), and the \(p\) coefficient multiplying \(\cos\theta\) is \(-3\sqrt3/4+3i/4\). Forward amplitude is roughly \(-0.799+1.25i\), giving dimensionless intensity 2.20. Backward it is \(1.799-0.25i\), giving 3.30. The integrated cross section is

\[4\pi[1/2+3(1/4)]=5\pi.\]

The same value follows from the forward imaginary part \(4\pi(1.25)=5\pi\), whereas the forward intensity 2.20 cannot itself be substituted in the optical theorem. Phase-sensitive information survives in angular asymmetry.

Determine what a single low-energy cross section cannot tell you

An elastic isotropic sample gives \(\sigma=400\pi\ \mathrm{nm^2}\) at sufficiently low \(k\). Infer the scattering length magnitude and propose a sign-sensitive check. What changes at \(k=0.1\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\)?

Work through the solution

The zero-energy relation gives \(|a|=10\) nm. The integrated cross section alone admits both signs, and the finite-\(k\) expression with zero effective range still depends only on \(a^2\). Interference with a known reference scattering amplitude, a measured phase shift, or independent evidence for a shallow bound state together with range constraints can supply additional information. At the specified finite wave number \(k|a|=1\) the predicted cross section is \(200\pi\ \mathrm{nm^2}\) when the effective range remains negligible. Fitting it with the zero-energy relation would return \(7.07\) nm and obscure the energy dependence.
