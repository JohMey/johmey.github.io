Choose the Hamiltonian appropriate to a resolved orbital manifold

Suppose an isolated atomic \(p\) manifold with \(l=1,s=1/2\) is sufficiently far from other orbital or principal levels to treat only its six substates. Take \(z\) along a uniform external magnetic field \(B\). Work to first order in the electronic Zeeman operator and model spin–orbit coupling by the constant \(A>0\). With \(b=\mu_B B\) and electron spin factor approximated by two,

\[H=\frac A{\hbar^2}\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S+\frac b\hbar(L_z+2S_z).\]

Both \(A\) and \(b\) are energies. The sign of the second term is positive for the electron because magnetic moment is opposite to angular momentum and field energy is minus moment dot field. Here \(\mu_B=e\hbar/(2m_e)>0\). The orbital manifold approximation requires magnetic splittings small compared with gaps to omitted levels. Neglected terms can include the difference of the actual spin factor from two, hyperfine interactions and the diamagnetic \(B^2\) term. An “intermediate field” in this discussion means \(b\) comparable to \(A\); it need not exceed any other electronic binding scale.

At zero field the coupled levels \(j=1/2,3/2\) have energies \(-A,+A/2\). At nonzero field the projection \(m=m_l+m_s\) is still conserved because \([H,J_z]=0\). For intermediate fields \(J^2\) need not commute with the Zeeman term. Consequently the six-state problem reduces to two one-dimensional and two two-dimensional invariant blocks, even when the label \(j\) loses its exact meaning. Following a state by magnetic quantum number and overlap with a neighbouring field value is generally more reliable than sorting all computed energies from low to high. Crossings of different \(m\) can reorder a sorted list without exchanging the underlying states.

Derive the Landé slope from angular momentum projection

In a state with definite \(j,l,s,m\), expectation of any vector operator proportional in direction to \(\mathbf J\) within its irreducible multiplet can be obtained from its scalar projection on \(\mathbf J\). For spin,

\[\frac{\langle S_z\rangle}{\hbar}=m\,\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)} .\]

One way to obtain the projection numerator is \(2\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf J=J^2+S^2-L^2\). Since \(L_z+2S_z=J_z+S_z\), the energy slope for weak fields is \(dE/db=g_jm\) with \(g_j=1+[j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)]/[2j(j+1)]\). A state \(j=0\) has vanishing linear vector moment and should be treated separately instead of dividing by zero. Within the \(p\) manifold the \(j=1/2\) factor is \(2/3\) and the \(j=3/2\) factor \(4/3\). For \(m=+1/2\) these correspond to slopes \(1/3\) and \(2/3\) in energy units of \(b\). Their sum is exactly one, a trace constraint that remains valid for the sum of the two eigenvalues in that block at every field.

If \(A/h=8.00\) GHz as in the previous angular exercise, the zero-field interval is 12.00 GHz. At \(B=20.0\) mT, \(b/h\simeq0.280\) GHz using \(\mu_B/h=13.996\) GHz/T. The two positive-\(m\) shifts to linear order are \(+0.0933\) and \(+0.1866\) GHz. Actual optical frequencies measure final minus initial energies; an initial \(s_{1/2}\) level has its own Zeeman slope. Omitting it is a common reason a slope inferred from a line scan seems inconsistent with the expected Landé factor.

Diagonalise the mixed levels across the entire crossover

In the \(m=+1/2\) product basis \((|0,\uparrow\rangle,|1,\downarrow\rangle)\), expand \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=L_zS_z+(L_+S_-+L_-S_+)/2\). The diagonal spin–orbit elements are \(0,-A/2\); the off-diagonal element is \(A/\sqrt2\). The Zeeman term is diagonal with entries \(b,0\). Hence

\[H_+(b)=\begin{pmatrix}b&A/\sqrt2\\ A/\sqrt2&-A/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_\pm(b)=\frac{b-A/2}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(b+A/2)^2+2A^2}.\]

At \(b=0\) the square root is \(3A/2\), recovering the coupled levels. For the \(m=-1/2\) basis \((|0,\downarrow\rangle,|-1,\uparrow\rangle)\), replace \(b\) by \(-b\). The stretched levels \(|1,\uparrow\rangle\) and \(|-1,\downarrow\rangle\) remain product states at every field with energies \(A/2\pm2b\). Counting all levels and their traces provides an independent check: spin–orbit and Zeeman each have zero trace in the full six-dimensional space. At zero field both slopes in the \(+\) block are finite and positive. At large positive \(b\) the upper slope tends to one and the lower to zero, approaching their uncoupled product labels.

In the negative-\(m\) block the two uncoupled energies would cross at \(b=A/2\). The coupling opens a minimum gap \(\sqrt2A\). Within the model, levels of the same \(m\) avoid one another whenever this matrix element is nonzero. Crossings between different \(m\) can remain exact because the uniform \(z\)-directed Hamiltonian does not mix them. A tilted perturbation could turn some of these into avoided crossings. Such symmetry dependence matters when trying to infer a coupling from the smallest visible distance between any two lines.

Obtain an eigenvector weight from an energy derivative

Suppose an initial \(s\) state has spin up. A weak \(q=0\) electric-dipole drive prepares the product basis state \(|0,\uparrow\rangle\) in the \(m=+1/2\) block. Its squared overlap with an exact eigenstate determines the angular part of that optical line's strength. Rather than computing a mixing angle with a branch ambiguity, differentiate the exact energies. Since \(\partial H_+/\partial b=|0,\uparrow\rangle\langle0,\uparrow|\), the eigenvalue derivative equals precisely that population:

\[w_\pm=\frac{dE_\pm}{db}=\frac12\left[1\pm\frac{b+A/2}{\sqrt{(b+A/2)^2+2A^2}}\right].\]

The weights sum to one as required by completeness. At zero field they are \(2/3\) and \(1/3\). At large positive field the upper line takes almost all this polarisation strength. In the negative block an analogous derivative with respect to \(-b\) gives the population of \(|0,\downarrow\rangle\). At its avoided crossing each eigenstate has equal weights and the two corresponding \(q=0\) intensities are equal for a selected initial spin down. The same experiment can test slopes measured from frequencies and overlaps measured through intensities, provided detection and radial coupling are common to both lines.

Set \(b=A\) in the positive block. The level separation becomes \(A\sqrt{4.25}=2.06155A\), while the upper product weight is \(0.8638\). A naive extrapolation of the weak-field \(g_jm\) slopes would give an interval \(1.5A+A/3=1.833A\). The discrepancy is governed by the spin–orbit mixing scale rather than failure of the magnetic Hamiltonian itself. If the frequency analyser resolves only a single intensity-weighted centroid, completeness still constrains the first moment:

\[\sum_\pm w_\pm E_\pm=\langle0,\uparrow|H_+|0,\uparrow\rangle=b.\]

Thus the centroid of unresolved lines in this ideal selected excitation can move linearly even while each constituent bends.

Resolve a second angular-momentum hierarchy with the same algebra

The \(p\)-manifold example coupled orbital and electronic spin. In an \(s\)-state atom the dominant remaining weak angular coupling may be between an electronic \(J=1/2\) and a nuclear \(I=1/2\). This is a different scale and a different physical pair of degrees of freedom. To illustrate how a state label changes with field, take

\[H=\frac{A_h}{\hbar^2}\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J+\frac B\hbar(g_J\mu_B J_z-g_I\mu_N I_z)\]

with positive hyperfine constant \(A_h\) and a positive model nuclear \(g_I\). The minus before the nuclear-magneton term reflects the assumed positive nuclear magnetic moment. Actual sign and \(g\)-factor depend on the isotope. Here both \(A_h\) and the products of magnetic moments and field have energy units. At zero field the electronic and nuclear angular momenta combine to \(F=0,1\). Their scalar-product shifts are \(-3A_h/4\) and \(+A_h/4\), respectively. A rotation of the entire coupled system leaves this zero-field Hamiltonian invariant, but independent rotations of just one constituent generally do not.

Within \(m_F=0\) order the product basis by electronic magnetic sign as \(|\uparrow_J,\downarrow_I\rangle,|\downarrow_J,\uparrow_I\rangle\). Both diagonal hyperfine matrix elements are \(-A_h/4\), off-diagonal exchange is \(A_h/2\), and field changes the diagonal difference by \(\delta=(g_J\mu_B+g_I\mu_N)B\). Hence the central pair of energies is

\[E_\pm=-A_h/4\pm\tfrac12\sqrt{A_h^2+\delta^2}.\]

The two stretched \(m_F=\pm1\) levels sit at \(+A_h/4\pm(g_J\mu_B-g_I\mu_N)B/2\). Their slopes contain a difference of electronic and nuclear moments, while the central splitting depends on their sum. This sign distinction can be determined directly by comparing the relevant product states: in the anti-aligned pair the sign change reverses one angular momentum along with its opposite magnetic sign. A sign convention imported from electron spin alone would give an erroneous nuclear contribution.

The transition between the two central states has gap \(\sqrt{A_h^2+\delta^2}\) and zero linear slope at \(B=0\). Its quadratic shift is \(\delta^2/(2A_h)\) for \(|\delta|\ll A_h\). Measuring at a point with zero first derivative reduces sensitivity to slow field fluctuations without eliminating it. Suppose \(A_h/h=1.00\) GHz and \((g_J\mu_B+g_I\mu_N)/h=28.0\) GHz/T in an illustrative species model. At a 1.0 mT bias the central gap rises by about 0.392 MHz. A 1 μT field error at that bias gives a first-order frequency error of about 784 Hz using the derivative of the quadratic expansion. At zero mean bias, fluctuations with rms 1 μT instead give an average quadratic shift about 0.392 Hz. Stabilising the field mean and reducing its variance address different terms in the measured spectral position.

The magnetic drive operator for a small oscillating field along \(z\) is itself proportional in this block to \(\sigma_z\). At zero bias the hyperfine eigenstates are the symmetric and antisymmetric superpositions, so this drive couples them off-diagonally despite the central transition frequency being stationary against a small static bias. The diagonal derivative and the off-diagonal transition matrix element are distinct quantities. At large bias the eigenstates approach product states and the same longitudinal drive becomes mostly diagonal, reducing its ability to flip between them. Thus a transition can have both a vanishing first-order shift and a nonzero resonant driving strength, consistent with the same Hamiltonian. Measuring both properties probes the field dependence of eigenvectors as well as eigenvalues.

Infer a field distribution from widths and centres

Spectra of spatially extended samples often contain a distribution of static magnetic fields. Consider a single weak-field transition with frequency \(\nu(B)=\nu_0+sB\), an intrinsic Gaussian line standard deviation \(w_0\), and a sample field distributed approximately Gaussian with mean \(B_0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_B\). A sum over independent source regions convolves the intrinsic line with the frequency distribution generated by \(sB\). The observed centre is \(\nu_0+sB_0\), and variance is \(w_0^2+s^2\sigma_B^2\). A single linewidth cannot determine both an intrinsic width and a field inhomogeneity. Two lines with different known magnetic slopes or one calibrated zero-slope reference can separate the contributions.

For example two transitions with slopes 7.0 and 14.0 GHz/T have measured standard deviations 0.250 and 0.400 MHz. Subtract the squared widths to eliminate \(w_0^2\):

\[\sigma_B^2=\frac{(0.400)^2-(0.250)^2}{(14000)^2-(7000)^2}\ \mathrm{T^2},\]

using MHz/T in the denominator. The result is \(\sigma_B=25.8\,\mu\mathrm T\), and the inferred intrinsic width is

\[\sqrt{0.250^2-(7000\sigma_B)^2}=0.173\,\mathrm{MHz}.\]

A third line with slope near zero supplies an out-of-sample check. If lines have significantly different lifetimes or power broadening, the assumption of a common \(w_0\) should be relaxed; otherwise one can spuriously infer a magnetic gradient or even a negative intrinsic variance.

For a curved avoided-crossing line expand \(\nu(B)\simeq\nu(B_0)+s(B-B_0)+q(B-B_0)^2/2\). A symmetric distribution shifts the measured mean by \(q\sigma_B^2/2\) even when the mean field is known exactly. Near a stationary point the leading field broadening can be strongly asymmetric because a squared Gaussian variable has a one-sided distribution around its minimum. Fitting a symmetric line at such a point can shift its apparent peak or centre according to the fitting convention. This effect is especially relevant when locating a minimum gap from spectra at several nominal fields. A model integrating intensities over a measured spatial field distribution can be better constrained than independent fits of Gaussian centres, because all lines share the same underlying field profile.

An independent geometric test tilts the weak sensing light relative to the magnetic axis. For equal populations, a truly scalar average over all polarisations and emitted directions is insensitive to this coordinate change, but a selected \(q\) transition changes strength according to the decomposition of the laboratory polarisation. If a peak attributed to frequency splitting mainly changes its fitted centre while unresolved component intensities redistribute, some of its apparent shift may be an intensity-weighted centroid effect. Record spectra at two orthogonal polarisations, fit the same physical energy parameters and allow the predicted angular strengths to vary. A common energy correction that survives the intensity change carries a different interpretation from a change in the mixture of unresolved substates.

Predict state transfer during a field sweep

The negative-\(m\) avoided crossing of the \(p\) manifold also poses a time-dependent question. Does a changing field keep the atom in the instantaneous energy eigenstate, or in approximately the same product state? In the local two-state description subtract the trace and write \(H=(\Delta(t)/2)\sigma_z+g\sigma_x\), where the difference of diabatic diagonal energies is \(\Delta\) and the minimal adiabatic gap is \(2|g|\). Introduce a mixing angle \(\tan(2\vartheta)=2g/\Delta\), chosen continuously through the crossing. Differentiating at fixed \(g\) gives

\[|\dot\vartheta|=|g\dot\Delta|/(\Delta^2+4g^2).\]

The moving eigenbasis itself couples the two instantaneous energy states with an energy scale \(\hbar|\dot\vartheta|\).

At closest approach the order-of-magnitude adiabatic criterion becomes \(\hbar|\dot\Delta|/(8g^2)\ll1\). A slow sweep keeps occupation on an energy branch, which changes its product character across the avoided crossing. A fast sweep can leave the original product state approximately unchanged, moving population from one instantaneous energy branch to the other. Both descriptions refer to actual state transfer; the basis in which “remaining in the same state” is assessed is part of the observable. The criterion here follows directly by comparing the nonadiabatic derivative coupling with the instantaneous level separation. It estimates a regime but does not by itself give a transition probability.

For a strictly linear detuning passing from large negative to large positive values with constant \(g\), the Landau–Zener result gives the diabatic survival probability \(P_D=\exp[-2\pi g^2/(\hbar|\dot\Delta|)]\). Its applicability requires asymptotic start and end detunings well outside the coupling region, coherent passage and the indicated linearity over the crossing. In ordinary-frequency units take \(g/h=20\) kHz and \(|d(\Delta/h)/dt|=1.0\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Hz/s}\). The exponent is \(4\pi^2(2.0\times10^4)^2/10^{10}=1.58\), giving \(P_D\simeq0.206\). Doubling the sweep rate halves the exponent and raises survival to about 0.454. Confusing angular with ordinary frequency in this conversion loses a factor \(2\pi\), large enough to change the predicted population qualitatively.

A double sweep forms a useful control. On entering and later leaving the avoided crossing, the two amplitudes propagated along different energy branches can accumulate different phases and interfere. Treating the two passages as independent classical jumps would miss oscillations versus waiting time between them. Strong dephasing or averaging over a broad field distribution can remove the oscillations while retaining the one-pass populations. Distinguish these mechanisms by calibrating single-passage transfer first and then varying the waiting time over several periods at multiple sweep speeds. This extends a static magnetospectroscopy model to a specified dynamical experiment without inferring the full time dependence from a static level diagram alone.

Test the orientation of a magnetic sensor from resolved spin populations

The scalar energy spectrum of an isotropic spin-\(1/2\) Zeeman Hamiltonian measures field magnitude. A transition energy by itself cannot determine the signed orientation of that field. Let \(H=\hbar\gamma\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2\), with signed angular gyromagnetic ratio \(\gamma\) defined in the experiment. Its eigenvalues are \(\pm\hbar|\gamma||\mathbf B|/2\). If instead one prepares spin up along a known laboratory \(z\) direction and observes its population after evolution time \(t\), the rotation axis appears in the dynamics. Exponentiating \(U=\cos(\omega t/2)I-i\sin(\omega t/2)\hat{\mathbf B}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) for a consistent choice of \(\omega,\hat{\mathbf B}\) gives

\[P_{\downarrow z}(t)=(1-\hat B_z^2)\sin^2(\gamma|\mathbf B|t/2).\]

The oscillation frequency gives magnitude and its contrast gives the squared transverse fraction. It is insensitive to whether the field points slightly above or below a perpendicular plane. Incomplete preparation or pulse contrast can also reduce the observed oscillation amplitude. A calibration with a known transverse field tests that ambiguity before assigning every missing contrast to geometric tilt.

Suppose a resolved precession has angular frequency corresponding to 0.50 mT and a calibrated flip contrast of 0.36 for initial \(+z\). Then \(\sqrt{B_x^2+B_y^2}=0.30\) mT and \(|B_z|=0.40\) mT. Repeating for an initial \(+x\) state with calibrated contrast 0.64 yields \(|B_x|=0.30\) mT and thus \(B_y=0\) for these numbers. Neither experiment identifies the signs of \(B_x\) and \(B_z\). To resolve them one can measure an early-time transverse quadrature with a phase reference. In Bloch notation \(\dot{\mathbf r}=\gamma\mathbf B\times\mathbf r\), so the initially zero slope \(\dot r_y=-\gamma B_x\) for \(+z\) is sign sensitive. This derivative is independent of \(B_z\) at the initial instant, giving a direct check complementary to the energy splitting.

A second strategy adds a calibrated bias \(\mathbf b_0\) in opposite directions and examines squared frequencies. Since \(|\mathbf B+\mathbf b_0|^2-|\mathbf B-\mathbf b_0|^2=4\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf b_0\), the difference isolates a signed projection even when the zero-field splitting reveals only magnitude. For a 0.20 mT known bias along \(z\) and a 0.40 mT signed \(z\) component the squared-field difference should be \(0.32\,\mathrm{mT}^2\). Alternating the two bias signs across the measurement reduces slow field drift. A quadratic diamagnetic response, anisotropic \(g\) tensor or an uncalibrated bias axis can break this simple inversion; repeating in three independent directions tests the vector model and reveals which assumptions are being used.

Separate an anisotropic magnetic response from an unknown field direction

A crystal-fixed effective spin has diagonal \(g=\operatorname{diag}(2.0,2.0,2.4)\). Derive the transition energy for a field rotated in the \(x,z\) plane by angle \(\theta\) from \(z\), and explain what an isotropic fit would infer.

Derive and check the result

The Hamiltonian is \(\mu_B\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(g\mathbf B)/2\). The splitting is \(\mu_B B\sqrt{2.0^2\sin^2\theta+2.4^2\cos^2\theta}\). At \(45^\circ\) the effective \(g\) is \(\sqrt{4.88}=2.209\); interpreting the measured splitting with constant \(g=2\) would overestimate field magnitude by about 10.5%. The spin also precesses around the effective axis \(g\mathbf B\), whose polar angle satisfies

\[\tan\theta_{\rm eff}=(2.0/2.4)\tan\theta.\]

Measuring contrast from a calibrated spin preparation and rotating a field of known magnitude can therefore distinguish material anisotropy from a simple error in magnet alignment. One scalar spectral line at an unknown orientation cannot separate these effects.

Infer the coupling from an anticrossing

The two negative-\(m\) lines exhibit a minimum frequency separation of 11.31 GHz at 0.286 T. Infer \(A/h\) from each measurement. What calibration would be needed for a comparison of absolute optical line frequencies?

Work through the solution

Minimum gap divided by \(h\) equals \(\sqrt2 A/h\), giving \(A/h=8.00\) GHz. The crossing condition \(b=A/2\) gives

\[A/h=2(13.996)(0.286)=8.01\,\mathrm{GHz}.\]

Their agreement checks field calibration independently of a common frequency reference. Both final states must be viewed from the same initial state when using their frequency difference. Comparing absolute optical frequencies also requires the zero-field transition energy and the field-dependent initial-level shift. Two lines originating in different initial states would have a field-dependent separation even if their final levels had a fixed interval.

Extract the polarisation weight using a finite difference

Near \(b=A\), estimate the slope of the upper positive-\(m\) level using its exact values at \(b/A=0.95,1.05\). Compare with the exact weight and predict the second line's strength.

Work through the solution

In units of \(A\) the upper level is \((t-0.5+\sqrt{(t+0.5)^2+2})/2\) with \(t=b/A\). Subtracting the values and dividing by 0.10 gives about 0.864. The analytical derivative is

\[[1+1.5/\sqrt{4.25}]/2=0.8638.\]

The second line carries 0.1362 of the total angular strength. Halving the centred step should reduce the derivative's truncation error by about four for this smooth curve. In an experiment frequency noise per point is instead amplified as inverse step. Compare several field steps to establish a practical slope uncertainty.

Use an unresolved centroid to detect a calibration problem

At \(b=A\) a weak \(q=0\) experiment driven from a selected spin-up preparation records both positive-\(m\) final states. Its excitation-weighted centroid after restoring the initial-state energy is \(0.78A\). Could the exact two-level mixing alone produce that centroid?

Work through the solution

The sum rule requires \(b=A\). Direct calculation gives energies approximately \(1.2808A\) and \(-0.7808A\); weights 0.8638 and 0.1362 give \(1.000A\) within rounding. In this model mixing alone cannot explain \(0.78A\). A missing weak line, saturation, polarisation contamination, different collected fluorescence efficiencies or a mistaken correction for the initial state can shift a measured centroid. Compare both individual integrated line areas at low intensity and reverse the field with the corresponding spin preparation. These tests use distinct symmetry and strength predictions rather than interpreting one centroid as a complete spectrum.
