Why an oscillator can move without its packet spreading

Start with the measured mass and curvature of a potential. Factor its Hamiltonian, prove the energy ladder, construct a freely moving Gaussian from number states and find when squeezing or anharmonicity changes that prediction.

Use the equilibrium length and momentum scales

Near a stable minimum a smooth one-dimensional potential has expansion \(V(x)=V(0)+m\omega^2x^2/2+O(x^3)\) after the origin is shifted to the minimum. Choose the arbitrary zero \(V(0)=0\). The fitted curvature is \(m\omega^2\), not \(\omega\) itself; measuring both the oscillation frequency and restoring slope separately identifies the effective mass. The classical energy ellipse becomes a circle if position and momentum are rescaled consistently. Define \(X=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x\) and \(P=p/\sqrt{m\hbar\omega}\), so \([X,P]=i\) and \(H=\hbar\omega(X^2+P^2)/2\). Since position and momentum fail to commute, multiplying two linear factors must retain their order.

Set \(a=(X+iP)/\sqrt2\) and \(a^\dagger=(X-iP)/\sqrt2\). Then

\[a^\dagger a=(X^2+P^2+i[X,P])/2=(X^2+P^2-1)/2.\]

Thus \(H=\hbar\omega(N+1/2)\), \(N=a^\dagger a\) and \([a,a^\dagger]=1\). For every normalised state in the relevant domain \(\langle N\rangle=\|a|\psi\rangle\|^2\ge0\). The additive half quantum is an energy contribution that survives in a state with vanishing classical means of \(x,p\); discarding the commutator would lose it. Transition frequencies within an ideal ladder measure energy differences, so those frequencies alone cannot determine an absolute zero of energy.

Prove discreteness and construct the ground state

Using the product rule for commutators gives

\[[N,a]=-a,\]
\[[N,a^\dagger]=a^\dagger.\]

If a normalisable energy eigenstate has \(N\) eigenvalue \(\nu\), application of \(a\) either annihilates it or yields an eigenstate of eigenvalue \(\nu-1\) and norm squared \(\nu\). Repeated lowering cannot yield a state of negative norm squared. For noninteger nonnegative \(\nu\) the chain would reach \(0<\nu'<1\) with nonzero image at a negative eigenvalue. Therefore the chain must terminate at an integer step in an annihilated ground state. This argument uses the bound oscillator's spectrum and states in the ladder operators' domains. Existence and completeness can also be checked by solving the one-dimensional differential equation with decaying boundary conditions.

In coordinate representation \(P=-i\,d/dX\), so \(a\psi_0=0\) reduces to

\[d\psi_0/dX=-X\psi_0.\]

Its square-integrable solution is

\[\psi_0(X)=\pi^{-1/4} e^{-X^2/2}.\]

Normalised excited states follow by \(|n\rangle=(a^\dagger)^n|0\rangle/\sqrt{n!}\). Orthogonality follows from the self-adjoint number operator with distinct eigenvalues. The coordinates in metres have ground variance \(\sigma_x^2=\hbar/(2m\omega)\), momentum variance \(\sigma_p^2=m\hbar\omega/2\), and zero symmetrised covariance. The geometric ground-state width is thus fixed by mass and trap stiffness. A semiclassical estimate minimising \(\hbar^2/(8m\sigma_x^2)+m\omega^2\sigma_x^2/2\) gives \(E_{\min}=\hbar\omega/2\), because the Gaussian saturates the uncertainty inequality used in that estimate.

In a number state both \(\langle x\rangle\) and \(\langle p\rangle\) vanish. Their fluctuations do not. Substitute \(x=\sigma_x(a+a^\dagger)\). Terms \(a^2,(a^\dagger)^2\) change the occupation by two and have zero diagonal element, whereas \(aa^\dagger\) and \(a^\dagger a\) contribute \(n+1\) and \(n\). Hence \(\langle x^2\rangle_n=(2n+1)\sigma_x^2\); similarly \(\langle p^2\rangle_n=(2n+1)\sigma_p^2\). The mean kinetic and potential energies each equal half the total. Measuring a nonzero rms displacement does not imply that the oscillator centre is undergoing a coherent motion.

Find a state whose centre follows the classical trajectory

Search for a normalised eigenstate of \(a\) with complex eigenvalue \(\alpha\). It is permissible for this non-Hermitian operator to have complex eigenvalues; they label states rather than directly observable outcomes. Expand \(|\alpha\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n\rangle\). Matching every number-state coefficient yields

\[c_{n+1}=\alpha c_n/\sqrt{n+1}.\]

Using the exponential series to normalise gives

\[|\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle,\]
\[P(n)=e^{-|\alpha|^2}\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}.\]

The mean and variance of occupation are both \(|\alpha|^2\). Different coherent states are nonorthogonal: summing the overlap yields

\[|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2=e^{-|\alpha-\beta|^2}.\]

At displacement difference one in this dimensionless complex plane the overlap probability is still \(e^{-1}=0.368\). A measurement that distinguishes all such nearby coherent preparations perfectly from one copy would contradict this overlap.

Each coefficient acquires \(e^{-i(n+1/2)\omega t}\). Take the common zero-point phase outside the sum, leaving exactly the same expansion with \(\alpha(t)=\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\). Hence \(\langle x(t)\rangle=2\sigma_x\operatorname{Re}\alpha(t)\) and \(\langle p(t)\rangle=2\sigma_p\operatorname{Im}\alpha(t)\). The centred operator \(a-\alpha\) annihilates the state, so its centred quadrature variances match those of the ground state. The packet moves on the classical phase-space ellipse without spreading. In a free particle the quadratic momentum phase causes spreading; in the bound oscillator equally spaced energy levels preserve this particular relative-phase structure.

For a vibrational mode with \(m=2.00\times10^{-25}\) kg and \(\omega=2\pi\times100\) kHz, \(\sigma_x=20.5\) nm. Prepare \(\alpha=1.5i\). Initially the average position is zero and the momentum is positive; a quarter period later the centre reaches \(3\sigma_x=61.5\) nm. The mean occupation is 2.25 and the ground probability is 0.1054. This ensemble has a nonzero ground-state probability even though its position oscillation is clearly resolved by repeated measurements. A pure number state of average energy near this value could give similar rms positions but would show no time-dependent centre.

Background · Which measurement distinguishes two oscillators of equal mean energy?

Compare the first excited number state with a coherent state of amplitude \(\alpha=1\). Both have mean occupation one and hence the same mean energy \(3\hbar\omega/2\). For a number state the expectation of the lowering operator vanishes because it connects orthogonal levels. For a coherent state its expectation equals \(\alpha\) and evolves with the oscillator phase.

\[\langle x(t)\rangle_{\rm coh}=\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{m\omega}}\,\mathrm{Re}(\alpha e^{-i\omega t}),\]
\[\langle x(t)\rangle_{n=1}=0.\]

Synchronous position measurements on freshly prepared states distinguish the moving centroid from the stationary one. A repeated energy measurement also separates the preparations. The number state always gives one quantum, whereas the coherent state gives a Poisson distribution with mean and variance both equal to one. A mean energy by itself omits both distinctions.

Rotate an uncertainty ellipse and test a nonideal trap

A sudden frequency change provides an experimentally meaningful way to generate a state with unequal quadrature fluctuations. Prepare the ground state at frequency \(\omega_i\), then instantaneously change to \(\omega_f\). The state in the position representation is continuous through an ideal sudden change. Relative to the final oscillator,

\[\langle X_f^2\rangle=\omega_f/(2\omega_i),\]
\[\langle P_f^2\rangle=\omega_i/(2\omega_f);\]

their product remains \(1/4\). Their sum exceeds one unless the two frequencies coincide. Thus the state is a squeezed vacuum for the new trap, with mean occupation \(\tfrac14(\omega_f/\omega_i+\omega_i/\omega_f-2)\). Unlike a coherent displacement its centre remains zero. Measuring a variance as the ellipse rotates distinguishes these preparations even if their average energies match.

For \(\omega_f=2\omega_i\) the mean occupation is 0.125. Since \(X_f(t)=X_f(0)\cos\omega_ft+P_f(0)\sin\omega_ft\), variance oscillates between 1 and 0.25 in these units with angular frequency \(2\omega_f\). The ground variance for the final trap would be 0.5, so part of the cycle lies below it. Momentum variance rises correspondingly, and at intermediate angles a nonzero covariance is needed in the uncertainty determinant. Abrupt changes also inject work via the time-dependent Hamiltonian. Energy measured before and after the sudden change need not agree even though the state vector at the switching instant is unchanged.

Real traps eventually depart from exact harmonic confinement. Write a model diagonal spectrum \(E_n/\hbar=\omega(n+1/2)+\chi n(n-1)/2\) for weak amplitude-dependent spacings over the occupied range. Coherent-state sums give

\[\langle a(t)\rangle=\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\exp[|\alpha|^2(e^{-i\chi t}-1)].\]

Its magnitude is \(|\alpha|\exp[-|\alpha|^2(1-\cos\chi t)]\). The collapse of the centre can occur under strictly unitary evolution while the number probabilities stay fixed. Their phases reassemble at \(2\pi/|\chi|\) in this ideal spectrum. Irreversible noise and uncontrolled fluctuations can suppress a measured revival; loss of the mean alone does not diagnose dissipation. A position trace and a number-resolved distribution probe complementary parts of the dynamics.

Factor a measured displacement into force history and oscillator response

A linear force couples to position,

\[H(t)=\hbar\omega(N+1/2)-F(t)x.\]

Even if the force time series is irregular, this driven oscillator is exactly soluble within the harmonic approximation. In the Heisenberg picture use \(\dot a=(i/\hbar)[H,a]\). Since \([x,a]=-\sigma_x\), the equation is

\[\dot a=-i\omega a+i\sigma_x F(t)/\hbar.\]

Multiply by \(e^{i\omega t}\) and integrate:

\[a(t)=e^{-i\omega t}\left[a(0)+\frac{i\sigma_x}{\hbar}\int_0^t F(t')e^{i\omega t'}dt'\right].\]

A force therefore displaces the rotating oscillator according to one complex Fourier component of its history. Its phase determines which final quadrature changes. The centred operator still evolves as in free oscillation. Thus a known linear drive displaces a ground state into a coherent state without squeezing it. The same formula shifts a thermal state without removing its thermal fluctuations. The statement about pure coherence requires knowing the initial state as well as measuring the final displacement.

Take a constant force \(F_0\) switched on for time \(T\) then off. For an initially stationary ground state the final amplitude, measured at switch-off in the original oscillator basis, is \(\alpha_F=F_0\sigma_x[1-e^{-i\omega T}]/(\hbar\omega)\). The corresponding mean position is \(F_0(1-\cos\omega T)/(m\omega^2)\), agreeing with the classical step response of a mass on a spring. Its excitation number is \(4(F_0\sigma_x/\hbar\omega)^2\sin^2(\omega T/2)\). A constant force applied for exactly a full period creates zero residual excitation at switch-off even though the oscillator moved substantially during the pulse. Motion of the trap centre and persistent excitation after the trap returns must be experimentally distinguished.

For \(m=2.00\times10^{-25}\) kg and \(100\) kHz as above, let \(F_0=2.00\times10^{-21}\) N. Its static equilibrium displacement is

\[F_0/(m\omega^2)=25.3\,\mathrm{nm}.\]

A half-period pulse leaves the mean at 50.7 nm with zero mean momentum and \(|\alpha|=50.7/(2\times20.5)\simeq1.24\). Its mean added occupation is roughly 1.53. The elastic energy \(m\omega^2\langle x\rangle^2/2\) at switch-off matches \(\hbar\omega|\alpha|^2\), independently of any counting argument. This check subtracts the unchanged zero-point energy. Using the total energy including zero point in the comparison would incorrectly attribute a half quantum to the drive even when the applied force vanishes.

The sudden impulse limit provides another independent test. Let a short pulse deliver total impulse \(J=\int Fdt\) over a time much shorter than \(1/\omega\). The amplitude increment is approximately \(i\sigma_xJ/\hbar\) in the appropriate rotating phase. Since \(2\sigma_p\sigma_x=\hbar\), the mean momentum changes by \(J\). For a smooth pulse spread around one period the same total impulse can produce a much smaller residual excitation because the force acts at different phases of the oscillator. A force sensor based on final occupation measures the squared magnitude of a windowed resonant integral. Reconstructing a complete time-dependent force from that single energy measurement is generally impossible. Varying the drive start time or measuring both final quadratures gives additional information.

Use operator ordering to predict a phase with zero final displacement

Define the displacement operator \(D(\alpha)=\exp(\alpha a^\dagger-\alpha^* a)\). Its generator is anti-Hermitian, so it is unitary. The commutator of \(\alpha a^\dagger-\alpha^*a\) with \(a\) is a constant. Differentiating \(D^\dagger(s\alpha)aD(s\alpha)\) with respect to \(s\) and integrating gives

\[D^\dagger(\alpha)aD(\alpha)=a+\alpha.\]

Consequently \(D(\alpha)|0\rangle\) is an annihilation eigenstate of eigenvalue \(\alpha\), giving an independent physical construction of the number expansion. The normal-order factorisation \(D=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}e^{\alpha a^\dagger}e^{-\alpha^*a}\) follows from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula because the relevant commutator is a scalar. Acting on the ground state recovers the Poisson coefficients directly. Omitting the scalar prefactor gives a state with the wrong norm.

Two displacements do not commute as operators despite their effects on the mean phase-space position adding in either order. Their scalar commutator gives

\[D(\alpha)D(\beta)=e^{(\alpha\beta^*-\alpha^*\beta)/2}D(\alpha+\beta).\]

The product around a closed displacement loop is

\[D(\alpha)D(\beta)D(-\alpha)D(-\beta)=e^{\alpha\beta^*-\alpha^*\beta}I.\]

For \(\alpha=1,\ \beta=i/2\) the phase is \(-1\) rad in this written operator order. Reversing the physical order conjugates the phase. The oscillator returns to its initial phase-space position and, apart from this phase, to its initial state. In a single isolated preparation the common phase is invisible. To observe it, place the oscillator interaction under coherent control of one branch of a separate two-level reference, recombine the reference and compare the branch that went around the loop with the branch that did not. Surviving coherence of the reference is essential. The same result can be described as an oriented area in the \(\operatorname{Re}\alpha,\operatorname{Im}\alpha\) plane and ultimately follows from \([x,p]=i\hbar\).

It is useful to trace the actual motion before interpreting a claimed geometric phase. Operators act from right to left on kets, so plotting a particular rectangular loop in time can correspond to the conjugate of a product written left to right. A lab comparison should specify the four pulse phases in chronological order and the ordinary free rotation occurring between them. In the rotating frame the displacement vectors are simple complex increments; in the laboratory frame the oscillator itself continually advances in phase. An uncompensated delay can leave a residual displacement rather than a closed loop. The overlap of that residual displaced ground state with the reference branch is \(e^{-|\Delta\alpha|^2/2}\) in amplitude, reducing observed fringe contrast as well as shifting its phase.

Measure displaced parity to distinguish a number state from a mixture

The number parity operator \(\Pi=(-1)^N\) gives \(+1\) for even and \(-1\) for odd occupation. It implements the coordinate reflection \(x\to-x\) for the harmonic oscillator. A position-space Wigner distribution may be sampled by displacing the state and measuring its parity. In the complex phase-space convention used here define \(W(\beta)=(2/\pi)\operatorname{tr}[D(-\beta)\rho D^\dagger(-\beta)\Pi]\); it integrates to one in \(d^2\beta\). At \(\beta=0\) it is just twice the even-minus-odd probability divided by \(\pi\). Its sign is determined by observable population statistics, but its value at a point is not the probability that a particle occupies a sharp simultaneous position and momentum.

For a coherent input \(|\alpha\rangle\), displacement produces \(|\alpha-\beta\rangle\) up to a phase. Summing the even-minus-odd Poisson occupations gives \(e^{-2|\alpha-\beta|^2}\). Hence \(W(\beta)=2e^{-2|\alpha-\beta|^2}/\pi\), a positive Gaussian. A first excited number state instead has \(W(0)=-2/\pi\). Mix that state with ground-state population,

\[\rho=p|1\rangle\langle1|+(1-p)|0\rangle\langle0|.\]

Then \(W(0)=(2/\pi)(1-2p)\), negative only when \(p>1/2\). An observed lack of negativity at one displacement does not imply a Gaussian state or a globally classical description. It may simply mean that the probe is examining a positive region or that losses have mixed the odd preparation with the ground state.

Suppose an experimental detector distinguishes only “zero excitations” from “one or more”. A measured nonzero fraction of 0.80 does not determine parity without further information: all those events might contain one excitation or a substantial even population might be present. Number resolution or a separate calibrated parity mapping is needed. If only ground and first excited population are independently established, an observed \(p_1=0.70\pm0.02\) gives \(W(0)=-0.255\pm0.025\) in these dimensionless units. Propagating that uncertainty requires the assumption about higher occupations. Combining a displacement scan with number-resolved or parity-sensitive data therefore tests both the preparation and the adequacy of a finite oscillator basis.

Recover a thermal occupation from two independent observables

For an oscillator in thermal equilibrium at inverse temperature \(\beta_T\), the occupation probabilities are \(P_n=(1-q)q^n\) with \(q=e^{-\beta_T\hbar\omega}\). The geometric series gives \(\bar n=q/(1-q)\) and variance \(\bar n(\bar n+1)\). Unlike a coherent state with the same mean occupation, the number distribution is broader and the free mean displacement is zero. The symmetrised position variance is \((2\bar n+1)\sigma_x^2\). A variance measurement alone cannot distinguish a thermal distribution from an incoherent nonthermal mixture with the same mean energy. Resolving more than the variance probes the full probability law.

For example, at \(\bar n=2\) thermal equilibrium predicts ground occupation \(1/3\), number variance 6 and parity \(1/(2\bar n+1)=1/5\). A coherent state with \(|\alpha|^2=2\) gives ground probability 0.1353, variance 2 and parity \(e^{-4}=0.0183\). A uniform randomisation of the coherent phase removes the mean displacement but leaves these Poisson number statistics unchanged. It can therefore resemble the thermal state on a quadrature average while differing sharply in ground population and parity. A displaced thermal preparation adds a classical centre motion on top of the thermal fluctuations; fitting only its energy would confuse displacement with temperature. Centre, centred variance and at least one independent number statistic make a coherent experimental set of observables for these physically different preparations.

Recover individual transition probabilities after a sudden trap change

The sudden-frequency experiment predicted mean occupation from the second moments. Number-resolved probabilities provide a stronger test. The initial ground-state wavefunction is even, and changing the trap curvature about the same centre preserves parity. The initial state therefore overlaps only even eigenstates of the final trap. A measured odd population beyond readout uncertainty diagnoses a displaced minimum, parity-breaking drive or imperfect initial preparation. The vanishing of odd occupation is stronger than knowing only the mean and variance of a quadrature.

Write the old annihilator in terms of the new operators,

\[a_i=\cosh r\,a_f-\sinh r\,a_f^\dagger,\]

with \(r=\frac12\ln(\omega_f/\omega_i)\) for this choice of sign. This follows directly by expressing both in the same \(x,p\) and comparing coefficients. The relative sign can change under a different convention for defining the squeeze parameter but all probabilities below are invariant. The old vacuum obeys \((a_f-\tanh r\,a_f^\dagger)|0_i\rangle=0\). In the final number basis \(c_n=\langle n_f|0_i\rangle\), the coefficient of \(|n\rangle\) gives

\[\sqrt{n+1}c_{n+1}=\tanh r\sqrt n\,c_{n-1}.\]

The \(n=0\) equation forces \(c_1=0\), and iteration gives

\[c_{2j}=c_0\frac{\sqrt{(2j)!}}{2^j j!}(\tanh r)^j,\]
\[c_{2j+1}=0.\]

To normalise, use the central-binomial series \(\sum_j\binom{2j}{j}q^j/4^j=(1-q)^{-1/2}\) at \(q=\tanh^2r\). Thus

\[|c_0|^2=1/\cosh r.\]

A separate coordinate-space check follows by integrating the overlap of two Gaussian ground states, giving \(P_0=2\sqrt{\omega_i\omega_f}/(\omega_i+\omega_f)\). This agrees with \(1/\cosh r\), independently of the operator recursion. Differentiating the normalisation-generating series gives \(\bar n=\sinh^2r\), agreeing with the energy inferred from the sudden change of the Hamiltonian.

For a doubling of frequency, \(\tanh r=1/3,\ \cosh r=3/(2\sqrt2)\), so

\[P_0=0.9428,\ P_2=P_0/18=0.05238,\]
\[P_4=P_0(3/8)(1/81)=0.00436.\]

Higher even levels contain the remaining probability. At a mean excitation of 0.125, a coherent state would instead have \(P_1=0.1103\). Even imperfect number resolution discriminating odd versus even can strongly distinguish a pure curvature quench from a coherent displacement with equal final energy. A finite switching time can reduce excitation if the oscillator tracks the moving ground state adiabatically. A shift of trap centre during a nominal curvature change adds a displacement and can populate odd levels. Measure the mean and parity along with the energy estimate before inferring a squeeze strength solely from a single occupation number.

Separate temperature from a parity-preserving quench

Before a sudden centred frequency doubling, a calibrated trap has thermal mean occupation 0.10. Predict the fraction of odd-number results after the quench and its final mean occupation. How might an experiment mistakenly interpret the odd results?

Derive and check the result

In the initial thermal ensemble parity expectation is

\[1/(2\bar n+1)=1/1.2=0.8333.\]

Parity commutes with both centred trap Hamiltonians, and measuring even or odd final occupation measures that same parity operator. The odds fraction is therefore \((1-0.8333)/2=0.0833\), unchanged by the quench. The final energy fluctuations scale with the initial quadrature variances multiplied by \(2\bar n+1\), giving \(\bar n_f+1/2=(\bar n_i+1/2)(\omega_f/\omega_i+\omega_i/\omega_f)/2=0.75\). Thus \(\bar n_f=0.25\). Assuming a perfectly cold initial state might turn the observed odd fraction into a false diagnosis of trap displacement. Measure initial parity and post-quench parity independently to isolate processes that actually break the symmetry. The increase of mean occupation alongside unchanged parity is the predicted signature of a centred quench.

Tutorial · analyse a state from amplitude and fluctuation data

Test two preparations with the same mean energy

Take a coherent state with \(|\alpha|^2=3\) and number state \(|3\rangle\). Compare energies, number variance, ground probability and the largest possible mean displacement.

Work through the solution

Both have \(\langle H\rangle=3.5\hbar\omega\). The coherent number variance is 3 while the number-state variance is zero. Ground probabilities are \(e^{-3}=0.0498\) and zero. By selecting a suitable oscillator phase, the coherent state reaches \(2\sqrt3\sigma_x\); the number state has zero centre at every time. An energy average alone leaves these preparations indistinguishable. The event of measuring exactly three quanta has probability \(e^{-3}3^3/3!=0.224\) in the coherent case and unity in the number case. Repeated shots allow this comparison without assuming a single shot reveals the input state.

Estimate numerical truncation before trusting a simulated coherent state

Expand a coherent state with \(\alpha=2\) in number states \(0,\ldots,8\). Is the missing probability negligible at a desired accuracy of one part in a thousand?

Work through the solution

Generate probabilities successively from \(p_0=e^{-4}\), \(p_{n+1}=4p_n/(n+1)\); summing through 8 gives 0.97864, leaving 0.02136 above the cutoff. Renormalising the retained vector hides this discarded probability from a norm check and distorts quantities weighted by \(n\). Through \(n=11\) the tail is about 0.00092, just below the specified probability tolerance. Energy moments require a separate tail weighted by \(n\); a probability bound alone can be inadequate for them. Vary the cutoff while monitoring both number statistics and the centre motion to separate truncation artefacts from real dephasing.

Infer the coherent displacement and a squeezing test

At a known phase, measurements give

\[\langle x\rangle=1.2\sigma_x,\ \langle p\rangle=-1.6\sigma_p.\]

Infer the coherent amplitude consistent with those means, then say what must still be measured to confirm a coherent preparation.

Work through the solution

The means imply \(\operatorname{Re}\alpha=0.60,\ \operatorname{Im}\alpha=-0.80\), so \(|\alpha|=1\) and the putative coherent mean occupation is one. They do not fix the variances. A displaced thermal or squeezed state can produce the same means with different fluctuations. Measure the variance along independent quadratures, including a rotated axis to infer covariance. In an ideal normalised state the expectation of the positive operator \((a-\alpha)^\dagger(a-\alpha)\) equals \(\langle N\rangle-|\alpha|^2\). If it vanishes exactly, the support is in the kernel of \(a-\alpha\), giving the coherent state. With finite uncertainty a near-zero value instead bounds extra excitation in the displaced frame.
