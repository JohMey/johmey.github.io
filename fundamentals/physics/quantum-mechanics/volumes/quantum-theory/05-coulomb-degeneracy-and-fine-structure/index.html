From a Coulomb orbit to the fine-structure multiplet

Separate the hydrogenic Schrödinger equation, identify the origin of its large degeneracy and evaluate the three dominant relativistic corrections. The cancellation among them predicts an equality that spectroscopy can then test.

Separate centre-of-mass and relative motion

A light particle of mass \(m_e\) and charge \(-e\) interacts with a pointlike nucleus \(+Ze\) of mass \(M\). Use centre of mass \(\mathbf R=(m_e\mathbf r_e+M\mathbf r_N)/(m_e+M)\) and separation \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_N\). Expanding the sum of the two kinetic energies cancels all cross terms and gives a free centre-of-mass kinetic energy plus relative energy \(p^2/(2\mu)\), with reduced mass \(\mu=m_eM/(m_e+M)\). The relative potential is \(-k/r,\ k=Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\). Its rotational symmetry implies \([H,L_i]=0\). Use a spherical harmonic \(Y_{lm}\) and write \(\psi=u(r)Y_{lm}/r\), so the probability in a thin radial shell is \(|u(r)|^2dr\) for angular harmonics of unit norm.

Substituting in the Laplacian separates the radial equation

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''+\left[\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}-\frac{k}r\right]u=Eu .\]

For the regular three-dimensional solution \(u(0)=0\) and a bound state also decays at infinity. The centrifugal term is an effective energy associated with angular variation, not a separate force added by hand. For \(E<0\) define \(\kappa=\sqrt{-2\mu E}/\hbar,\ \rho=2\kappa r\), and \(a=\hbar^2/(\mu k)\). The small-radius behaviour of \(u\) is \(r^{l+1}\); the large-radius tail contains \(e^{-\kappa r}\). Pull both out by \(u=\rho^{l+1}e^{-\rho/2}v(\rho)\). Straight differentiation leaves

\[\rho v''+(2l+2-\rho)v'+(1/\kappa a-l-1)v=0 .\]

For \(v=\sum c_j\rho^j\), coefficient matching gives

\[c_{j+1}/c_j=(j+l+1-1/\kappa a)/[(j+1)(j+2l+2)].\]

The regular series must terminate to avoid an exponential asymptotic part that defeats the decaying prefactor. Thus \(1/(\kappa a)=n_r+l+1=n\), and \(E_n=-\mu k^2/(2\hbar^2 n^2)\). The normalised radial polynomials are associated Laguerre functions. This gives \(l=0,\ldots,n-1\) and \(\sum_l(2l+1)=n^2\) states before including spin. Rotational symmetry guarantees the degeneracy in \(m\), but a different central potential can split levels of different \(l\). The additional equality is specific to the Coulomb potential.

Calculate the radial moments needed for a correction

To isolate the leading relativistic corrections now take an infinitely heavy nucleus and set \(m=m_e\), \(a=\hbar^2/(mk)\). Including reduced mass in the leading energy is straightforward; a complete recoil correction to the fine structure requires additional interactions. A useful identity is \(\langle1/r\rangle=1/(an^2)\), obtained by differentiating \(E_n\) with respect to \(k\). The derivative of an eigenvalue equals the expectation of \(\partial H/\partial k=-1/r\) for the normalised eigenstate, which follows by differentiating \(H|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle\) and cancelling terms using its adjoint. The same method applied to the radial centrifugal parameter yields \(\langle r^{-2}\rangle=1/[a^2n^3(l+1/2)]\) at fixed radial node count. Treat \(l\) as a continuously varied radial parameter during differentiation, then evaluate at its physical integer value.

For \(l>0\), the stationary radial force identity \(\langle dV_{\rm eff}/dr\rangle=0\) with vanishing endpoint term gives

\[\langle r^{-3}\rangle=1/[a^3n^3 l(l+1/2)(l+1)].\]

Here \(V_{\rm eff}\) is the potential in the radial equation. The step uses sufficient behaviour of \(u,u'\) at the origin. For \(l=0\), the integral of \(r^{-3}\) diverges in an \(s\) state and the neglected endpoint term need not vanish. Substituting \(l=0\) formally into this expression would therefore be invalid. The contact density of an \(s\) state needed below is \(|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2=1/(\pi a^3 n^3)\), while states with \(l>0\) vanish at the origin.

Track the kinetic, spin–orbit and contact terms separately

The small parameter is \(Z\alpha=k/(\hbar c)\), with \(\alpha\) the fine-structure constant for \(Z=1\). Expand the relativistic dispersion about small momentum after subtracting rest energy: \(p^2/(2m)-p^4/(8m^3c^2)+\cdots\). For an energy eigenfunction \(p^2\psi=2m(E_n+k/r)\psi\), so \(\langle p^4\rangle=4m^2\langle(E_n+k/r)^2\rangle\) follows by taking the squared norm of \(p^2\psi\). Squaring the two operators as if \(p^2\) and \(1/r\) commute is unnecessary and would not be an operator identity. Inserting the first two radial moments gives

\[\Delta E_{\rm kin}=-C_n\left[\frac1{l+1/2}-\frac3{4n}\right],\]
\[C_n=\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}.\]

The Pauli expansion of the Dirac Hamiltonian adds \(H_{\rm so}=(1/[2m^2c^2r])V'(r)\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\) and the Darwin contact \(H_D=\hbar^2\nabla^2 V/(8m^2c^2)\). These are effective interactions through order \((Z\alpha)^4\); a purely nonrelativistic scalar equation does not generate the spin or contact term. For \(V=-k/r\), \(V'=k/r^2\) and \(\nabla^2 V=4\pi k\delta^3(\mathbf r)\). Thus the contact shifts only \(s\) states by \(+C_n\). For \(l>0\) insert the third inverse radial moment and the angular identity \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=\hbar^2[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-3/4]/2\). For the branch \(j=l+1/2\) the bracket is \(l\); for \(j=l-1/2\) it is \(-(l+1)\). Reducing fractions then gives

\[\Delta E_{\rm so}=C_n[1/(l+1/2)-1/(j+1/2)].\]

For \(l=0\) the spin–orbit operator vanishes and the Darwin term supplies the required difference. The sum in either case is

\[\Delta E_{nj}=-C_n\left[\frac1{j+1/2}-\frac3{4n}\right].\]

At fixed \(n\) the answer depends on \(j\) but no longer separately on \(l\). The cancellation cannot be inferred by merely attaching a phenomenological \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\) splitting to a nonrelativistic \(p\) state. In particular it predicts identical energies for \(2s_{1/2}\) and \(2p_{1/2}\) in this approximation, while \(2p_{3/2}\) lies higher. Radiative, recoil and finite-nuclear-size contributions can resolve some of these residual equalities. An experimentally resolved residual is evidence that the specified Hamiltonian omitted a physical term; it does not remove the cancellation inside that Hamiltonian.

Compare a small energy correction with a resolvable line

For hydrogen with \(mc^2\simeq511\,000\) eV and \(\alpha\simeq1/137.04\), the \(2p_{3/2}\)–\(2p_{1/2}\) interval is \(C_2/2\simeq4.53\times10^{-5}\) eV or \(10.95\) GHz. Compare with the leading \(n=2\) binding energy 3.40 eV. A small correction to total binding can give a prominent splitting when a measurement subtracts nearly equal large energies. Resolving it in a Fourier-limited time-domain experiment requires a coherent observation time at least of order the inverse frequency difference, here around 0.09 ns. Actual linewidths, lifetimes and pulse shapes affect how two spectral components can be distinguished.

A useful numerical audit is to fit the radial spectrum with three successive physical models. First solve the nonrelativistic radial boundary problem with the correct near-origin power and check that 2s and 2p converge toward the same energy. Then evaluate expectation values of the three corrections on those numerical states. Finally combine terms. Converged binding energies alone do not guarantee a converged contact density, which samples one spatial endpoint. A coarse radial grid can imitate an anomalous splitting between states which the analytical correction predicts to be equal. Check the origin slope of \(u_{n0}\), not only its norm or far tail.

Find where the electron is most likely to be detected

Confusion between spatial density and radial probability can lead to very different-looking descriptions of the same atom. For a hydrogenic ground state the normalised position-space wavefunction is \(\psi_{100}=(\pi a^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a}\). Its density is largest at the centre, but a thin spherical shell at radius \(r\) contains probability \(P(r)dr=4r^2 e^{-2r/a}dr/a^3\). The shell volume tends to zero at the origin. Differentiating \(\ln P\) gives \(2/r-2/a=0\), so the most likely shell radius is \(a\). The mean radius \(\int rP(r)dr=3a/2\) is different again because the shell distribution has a long tail. Neither number alone gives the probability that a finite spatial detector clicks.

Integrating the shell probability between zero and \(R\) gives

\[\Pr(r<R)=1-e^{-2R/a}\left(1+\frac{2R}a+\frac{2R^2}{a^2}\right).\]

At the most likely shell radius \(R=a\), the probability inside is \(1-5e^{-2}=0.3233\), substantially less than one half. At \(R=2a\) it is

\[1-13e^{-4}=0.7619.\]

Taylor expansion at a small radius \(R\ll a\) gives \(4(R/a)^3/3\), equal to the ball volume times central density to leading order. This is the local scaling underlying finite-nucleus corrections to a contact-sensitive observable. Using the most likely radius as though the electron followed a thin circular orbit would give none of these cumulative probabilities, even though the same Coulomb length scale appears in the Bohr model.

For \(2s\) and \(2p\), both states have principal index two and the same leading energy. The \(2s\) radial amplitude is proportional to \((2-r/a)e^{-r/(2a)}\); its node at \(2a\) separates regions of opposite amplitude sign. The \(2p\) radial amplitude is proportional to \(r e^{-r/(2a)}\), with no positive-radius radial node. At short distance, the probability inside \(R\) scales as \(R^3\) for a nonzero \(s\) amplitude and as \(R^5\) for the \(p\) wave. A sufficiently localised weak perturbation near the nucleus can therefore split the nonrelativistic degeneracy even if it is rotationally symmetric. Rotational symmetry still protects equal shifts within the \(m\) multiplet. A radial perturbation and a field selecting a direction leave different patterns of protected degeneracy.

Diagonalise a degeneracy before expanding a weak electric field

The \(n=2\) Coulomb degeneracy creates a striking response to a weak uniform electric field along \(z\). In the purely nonrelativistic model with spin ignored the relevant spatial states are \(2s,\ 2p_0,\ 2p_{\pm1}\). The perturbation for an electron in electric field \(\mathcal E\hat z\) can be written \(e\mathcal E z\) up to an additive constant. Each unperturbed parity eigenstate has zero diagonal expectation of this odd operator. Applying ordinary nondegenerate first-order perturbation theory separately to all four states would therefore predict zero for every shift. But first-order perturbation theory in a degenerate manifold requires diagonalising the projected perturbation, including off-diagonal amplitudes.

The \(z=r\cos\theta\) operator has \(q=0\) and changes parity, so within \(n=2\) it couples only \(2s\) and \(2p_0\). Its angular matrix element between \(Y_{00}\) and \(Y_{10}\) is \(1/\sqrt3\). In a conventional phase convention the radial functions are \(R_{20}=(2-r/a)e^{-r/(2a)}/(2\sqrt2\,a^{3/2})\) and \(R_{21}=(r/a)e^{-r/(2a)}/(2\sqrt6\,a^{3/2})\). The remaining radial integral is \(\int r^3R_{20}R_{21}\,dr\). Setting \(s=r/a\) gives

\[a(2\cdot4!-5!)/(8\sqrt3)=-3\sqrt3a.\]

Multiplying the angular factor gives \(-3a\). Rephasing one basis state would change this sign but leave all level shifts invariant.

The projected matrix has zero diagonal and off-diagonal \(-3ea\mathcal E\). Its symmetric and antisymmetric combinations have linear shifts \(\mp3ea\mathcal E\), while \(2p_{\pm1}\) remain unshifted to this order. The new states have definite first-order dipoles along opposite directions even though their zero-field basis states had zero dipoles. For \(a=5.29\times10^{-11}\) m and \(\mathcal E=1000\) V/m, an individual linear shift has magnitude \(1.59\times10^{-7}\) eV, corresponding to 38.4 MHz. This field is weak compared with the gross level separation, but whether the degenerate result is accurate in a real atom also depends on much smaller fine, hyperfine and radiative splittings. “Weak field” must specify the comparison energy.

A more precise two-state model retains a zero-field splitting \(\Delta\) between the coupled states. After subtracting their mean the Hamiltonian is \((\Delta/2)\sigma_z+d\mathcal E\sigma_x\), with \(d=3ea\) in magnitude. The energies are \(\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+d^2\mathcal E^2}\). At \(d|\mathcal E|\ll|\Delta|\) the shift from each initial level is quadratic, \(\pm d^2\mathcal E^2/|\Delta|\), while at \(d|\mathcal E|\gg|\Delta|\) the slopes approach the linear-degenerate prediction. An observed crossover distinguishes a small unresolved internal splitting from a strictly zero projected dipole matrix element. Tracking energies over both positive and negative fields also tests for a stray dc field shifting the centre of the response.

Estimate a finite-size correction from the potential itself

The contact term derived from the Pauli expansion is not the same physical effect as giving the nucleus a finite charge radius. A simple electrostatic model separates the two scalings. Replace a point nucleus by a uniformly charged sphere of radius \(R_N\). Inside it the electron potential becomes

\[V_{\rm sph}(r)=-k[3-(r/R_N)^2]/(2R_N);\]

outside it is unchanged at \(-k/r\). The perturbation \(\delta V=k[1/r-3/(2R_N)+r^2/(2R_N^3)]\) is positive for interior points and zero at the surface. The smoothed potential is less attractive than the point law near the centre.

For an \(s\) state and \(R_N\ll a\), the unperturbed density varies little across the tiny sphere. First-order perturbation theory therefore gives

\[\delta E\simeq4\pi k|\psi(0)|^2\int_0^{R_N}\left(r-\frac{3r^2}{2R_N}+\frac{r^4}{2R_N^3}\right)dr\]
\[\frac{2\pi}{5}\,k|\psi(0)|^2R_N^2 .\]

The first two radial integrals cancel and leave the \(R_N^2/10\) contribution before multiplication by \(4\pi\). Equivalently write \(\delta E=(2\pi/3)k|\psi(0)|^2\langle r_N^2\rangle\), since the uniform sphere has mean-square charge radius \(3R_N^2/5\). The result is a positive shift, decreasing binding. Corrections to the constant-density approximation involve additional powers of \(R_N/a\). A different microscopic charge distribution changes higher moments, while the leading small-size shift depends on its mean-square charge radius.

For a model \(R_N=1.0\) fm and the ordinary electronic hydrogen length \(a=5.29\times10^{-11}\) m, \(R_N/a=1.89\times10^{-5}\). For the ground state compare with \(|E_1|=k/(2a)\) and \(|\psi(0)|^2=1/(\pi a^3)\). The relative shift is \((4/5)(R_N/a)^2=2.86\times10^{-10}\), equivalent to roughly \(3.9\times10^{-9}\) eV. This illustrative uniform charge radius is input data of the model; do not equate it directly to a measured rms radius. For a heavier orbiting lepton a shorter Bohr radius increases the fractional sensitivity strongly. A complete precision spectrum would additionally include radiative and recoil effects and a realistic nuclear response. The present derivation isolates how one local perturbation scales and why \(s\) levels are especially sensitive.

Use dilation to audit stationary Coulomb energies numerically

A variational scaling argument gives a powerful independent check on a computed wavefunction. For any normalised trial state \(\psi\), define the normalised rescaling \(\psi_\lambda(\mathbf r)=\lambda^{3/2}\psi(\lambda\mathbf r)\). The Coulomb potential is homogeneous of degree minus one, and the gradient acquires a factor \(\lambda\). Its energy expectation is therefore

\[E(\lambda)=\lambda^2\langle T\rangle+\lambda\langle V\rangle.\]

For an exact stationary bound state the energy functional is stationary with respect to norm-preserving variations, giving \(2\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle=0\) at \(\lambda=1\). Hence \(\langle T\rangle=-E,\ \langle V\rangle=2E\), consistent with the independently derived inverse-radius mean. The argument requires appropriate normalisation and boundary behaviour so the scaled trial function is allowed.

Suppose a numerical \(1s\) reconstruction yields \(\langle T\rangle=13.2\) eV and \(\langle V\rangle=-26.7\) eV. Its energy is \(-13.5\) eV, yet its virial residual is \(-0.3\) eV. Along the scale family the minimum occurs at \(\lambda_*=-\langle V\rangle/(2\langle T\rangle)=1.0114\), a slightly more concentrated state. The resulting energy reduction is only about 0.0017 eV, much smaller than the change in individual kinetic and potential terms. A small total-energy error can coexist with a noticeably incorrect radial scale because first-order energy errors cancel near a stationary solution. Monitoring separate expectation values, the virial residual and a contact-sensitive observable provides a more informative test of a numerical atomic calculation than reporting the total binding energy alone.

Appraise a finite-grid Coulomb spectrum from convergence and degeneracy

To compute the radial Schrödinger eigenvalues directly, work in dimensionless radius \(s=r/a\), energy unit \(\hbar^2/(2ma^2)\), and write \([-d^2/ds^2+l(l+1)/s^2-2/s]u=\varepsilon u\). On a uniform grid \(s_j=jh\), use \(u_0=0\) and a far boundary \(u_{N+1}=0\). The diagonal approximation is \(2/h^2+l(l+1)/s_j^2-2/s_j\) and its neighbours are \(-1/h^2\). All potential terms are evaluated at positive interior grid points. Inserting an arbitrary finite value for \(1/s\) at the origin and treating it as an ordinary interior point changes the boundary approximation. A tridiagonal symmetric eigensolver returns orthogonal numerical vectors; physical radial norm is \(h\sum_j|u_j|^2\), so normalise appropriately before reconstructing spatial densities.

For \(l=0\) on an infinite grid the lowest mode permits an unusually informative exact lattice check. Try \(u_j=j q^j\) for \(0<q<1\). The finite-difference kinetic row divided by this trial value is \([2-q-q^{-1}]/h^2-(q-q^{-1})/(jh^2)\). Adding potential \(-2/(jh)\), the \(1/j\) coefficient vanishes when \(q^{-1}-q=2h\), or \(q=\sqrt{1+h^2}-h\). The eigenvalue is then

\[\varepsilon_h=[2-2\sqrt{1+h^2}]/h^2=-2/[1+\sqrt{1+h^2}].\]

It tends to the exact continuum ground value \(-1\) as \(-1+h^2/4+O(h^4)\). This analytic discrete answer separates algebraic solver correctness from discretisation error for the exact same potential and boundary treatment.

At \(h=0.20\) the infinite-grid ground value is approximately \(-0.99020\); at \(h=0.10\) it is \(-0.99751\). Their errors differ by nearly a factor of four as predicted. A finite far wall shifts both energies further; repeat first at fixed \(h\) and increasing boundary radius to isolate that effect. An excited \(n=2\) state has a more extended tail and requires a larger radius than the ground state for comparable truncation bias. Solving \(l=0\) and \(l=1\) on a small common box can yield a spurious nonrelativistic 2s–2p splitting. Check separately that both energies tend to \(-1/4\) before interpreting a much smaller physical fine splitting. Subtracting independently computed energies amplifies absolute numerical biases even if each number appears visually close to its expected value.

The distinctive near-origin behaviour supplies a second check. Since \(\psi_{100}(0)\) is proportional to the slope of the reduced radial function at zero, a first forward difference \(u_1/h\) typically incurs a first-order error even when the energy error is second order. For the lattice shape above \(u_j\propto j q^j\), the first two positive grid values can be used to estimate the endpoint derivative by \((4u_1-u_2)/(2h)\). Test its convergence under the physical radial normalisation. For a contact perturbation, squaring an endpoint derivative doubles its small relative error to leading order. This is why an atomic calculation should include an observable-specific refinement criterion in addition to an energy tolerance.

Use an eigenvalue benchmark to separate numerical and physical shifts

A report on the infinite-grid hydrogenic ground state quotes energy \(-0.99750\) at \(h=0.10\) and attributes the difference from \(-1\) to a short-range interaction. Predict the lattice-only change on refinement to \(h=0.05\), and propose a test that could reveal a real interaction independently.

Derive and check the result

The lattice expression gives \(-2/[1+\sqrt{1.0025}]\simeq-0.999376\) at 0.05, so the discretisation alone accounts for the original difference at the stated accuracy. A local physical correction with fixed continuum strength should converge toward a fixed nonzero shift as \(h\to0\), while the lattice bias vanishes approximately as \(h^2/4\). Compute control and perturbed energies on exactly the same grids and compare their difference under refinement, with consistent treatment of the perturbation's physical range. An unresolved delta perturbation represented as a fixed diagonal value on one grid point changes its effective continuum strength upon refinement and would produce another artificial shift. Cross-check with the first-order expectation of a resolved physical potential or with the independently converged contact density.

Use an isotope pair to cancel a common transition-energy reference

High-precision spectroscopy often obtains its most reliable insight from combinations of frequencies rather than absolute energies. Consider two hydrogenic model isotopes with the same nuclear charge, different reduced masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) and different mean-square charge radii \(R_1^2,R_2^2\). For a specified transition the leading point-Coulomb frequency can be written \(C\mu\), since its \(n\)-dependent dimensionless numbers are fixed. A local nuclear-size perturbation shifts each participating \(s\) level by a factor proportional to \(|\psi_{ns}(0)|^2 R_i^2\propto\mu_i^3 R_i^2/n^3\), while a \(p\) level receives a much smaller finite-size correction in the same limit. The different powers of reduced mass and radial quantum number permit differences of transitions to cancel selected terms.

For illustration compare a 1s–2s optical frequency with a 1s–2p frequency within one model isotope, assuming the fine, radiative and differing upper-state structures have been independently accounted for in the desired order. Subtracting these frequencies cancels the entire common lower 1s energy and the gross equal 2s/2p Coulomb energy, leaving the upper 2s–2p differences. If instead one sought the large 1s contact contribution, subtracting these same two lines would remove precisely the effect of interest. An apparently convenient common reference can therefore eliminate physical signal as well as noise. Write the coefficient of each contributing level explicitly before selecting a frequency combination.

Suppose two independently fitted line frequencies \(\nu_a,\nu_b\) each have a 10 MHz common reference error and independent residual standard errors of 0.30 and 0.40 MHz. The covariance from the shared reference is \(100\,\mathrm{MHz}^2\). The difference has variance \(0.30^2+0.40^2=0.25\,\mathrm{MHz}^2\), hence 0.50 MHz standard error. Treating both total errors as independent instead would suggest roughly 14 MHz. Conversely, an error in a calibration scale proportional to frequency leaves a residual proportional to the frequency difference; its cancellation is only approximate. Reporting the provenance of uncertainties and their covariance can matter more than recording an extra digit of each absolute transition.

Recover a contact shift from two excited-state probes

In a diagnostic model a local perturbation gives an unknown positive 1s shift \(D\), an \(ns\) shift \(D/n^3\), and zero shifts of \(p\) levels. Relative to a matched reference, how do 1s–2s, 1s–2p and 1s–3s frequencies change? Which experimental difference removes the unknown common lower-state shift yet recovers \(D\)?

Derive and check the result

The changes are \((-7D/8)/h,\ -D/h,\ (-26D/27)/h\), respectively. Subtracting the 1s–2p shift from the 1s–2s shift gives \(D/(8h)\), since the common lower-state contribution cancels. For a measured difference of \(0.90\pm0.05\) MHz the implied \(D/h=7.2\pm0.4\) MHz. The 1s–3s shift should then be \(-6.93\) MHz in this stipulated model. If unavailable corrections also differ among the upper states, that value is not an absolute nuclear-radius extraction. The third line gives an independent test of the \(1/n^3\) contact scaling before attributing all measured differences to the single parameter \(D\).

Tutorial · measure degeneracy through independent calculations

Verify two inverse moments directly in the \(2p\) state

Use \(R_{21}(r)=r e^{-r/(2a)}/(2\sqrt6\,a^{5/2})\) and normalised \(Y_{1m}\). Evaluate \(\langle r^{-2}\rangle,\langle r^{-3}\rangle\) without parameter differentiation.

Work through the solution

The radial measure is \(r^2dr\), so after inserting \(R\) the general moment is \((24a^5)^{-1}\int_0^\infty r^{4-q}e^{-r/a}dr\) for \(r^{-q}\). For \(q=2\) the integral is \(2a^3\), giving \(1/(12a^2)\). For \(q=3\) it is \(a^2\), giving \(1/(24a^3)\). Both agree with the formulas for \(n=2,l=1\). Dropping the radial Jacobian would change both powers and could falsely suggest singular behaviour. The result is independent of \(m\) because the operator is rotationally scalar.

Calculate the complete \(n=2\) shifts in units of \(C_2\)

Resolve the kinetic, spin–orbit and Darwin contributions for \(2s_{1/2}\), \(2p_{1/2}\) and \(2p_{3/2}\). Which comparison isolates the effect of the spin–orbit term cleanly at this order?

Work through the solution

For \(s\), kinetic gives \(-[2-3/8]=-13/8\) and Darwin \(+1\), summing to \(-5/8\). For both \(p\) states the kinetic value is

\[-2/3+3/8=-7/24.\]

The spin–orbit corrections are \(2/3-1=-1/3\) for \(j=1/2\) and \(2/3-1/2=1/6\) for \(j=3/2\), giving \(-5/8\) and \(-1/8\). Subtracting the two \(p\) levels removes their shared kinetic term and zero contact term. Their interval is \(C_2/2\). Comparing 2s with either \(p\) instead involves two different radial wavefunctions and does not isolate the same correction.

Separate a reduced-mass shift from fine structure

Consider two otherwise identical hydrogenic nuclei with \(M/m=1836\) and 3672. Predict the ratio of gross transition frequencies and compare its fractional change with the order of a fine-structure correction. Which common interpretation would conflate them?

Work through the solution

Gross transition frequencies scale as reduced mass \(\mu=m/(1+m/M)\). The heavier-to-lighter ratio is \((1+1/1836)/(1+1/3672)=1.000272\) approximately. The order of a relative fine-structure correction for \(Z=1\) is \(\alpha^2\simeq5.3\times10^{-5}\) with state-dependent coefficients. A change of isotope can shift a gross line by more than a particular resolved fine splitting, even with identical nuclear charge. Centroid measurements across isotopes and intervals within one multiplet are distinct observables. Recoil and nuclear magnetism require further inputs for precision predictions.
