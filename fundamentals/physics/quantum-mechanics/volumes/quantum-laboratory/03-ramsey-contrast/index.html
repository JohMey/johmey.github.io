Recover a transition frequency from two separated pulses

A two-level system is driven by two short pulses with a free evolution interval between them. Determine why a population measurement carries phase information. Use a scan at several delays to separate a constant detuning, intrinsic coherence loss and trial-to-trial frequency variation.

Follow the amplitudes through both pulses

Use basis \(|g\rangle,|e\rangle\). Let the first pulse prepare \((|g\rangle+|e\rangle)/\sqrt2\) in the frame of the applied oscillator. A detuning \(\Delta\) in radians per second accumulates a relative phase \(\Delta\tau\) in free time \(\tau\). Immediately before the second pulse each state has population one half; measuring population here cannot reveal detuning. Choose a second pulse equivalent to projecting onto \((|g\rangle+e^{-i\phi}|e\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Then one output population is

\[P(\tau,\phi)=\tfrac12[1+\cos(\Delta\tau-\phi)].\]

The sign of the phase follows our definitions of detuning and the reference pulse. Shifting the reference phase makes it possible to check that sign. A measurement using only \(\phi=0\) cannot distinguish \(\Delta\) from \(-\Delta\). Coherence is the off-diagonal density-matrix element that the second pulse converts into an observable population difference.

Derive two envelopes with different origins

A homogeneous decay time \(T_2\) multiplies the off-diagonal element by \(e^{-\tau/T_2}\). If instead the detuning of each experimental shot is constant during that shot and drawn from a Gaussian distribution of mean \(\bar\Delta\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_\Delta\), average the complex phase, not its absolute value. Completing the square in the Gaussian integral gives

\[\langle e^{i\Delta\tau}\rangle=e^{i\bar\Delta\tau}e^{-\sigma_\Delta^2\tau^2/2}.\]

With both independent mechanisms the measured contrast is \(C(\tau)=\exp[-\tau/T_2-\sigma_\Delta^2\tau^2/2]\). One mechanism gives an initial logarithmic slope, the other adds curvature. A single low contrast measured at just one delay cannot establish which one caused it.

Infer parameters from quadratures and a frequency scan

Record the \(\phi=0,\pi/2\) outputs, then form \(X=2P_0-1=C\cos(\bar\Delta\tau)\) and \(Y=2P_{\pi/2}-1=C\sin(\bar\Delta\tau)\). Their length estimates contrast and their angle estimates accumulated phase modulo \(2\pi\). Suppose at \(0.50\,\mathrm{ms}\) the readings are 0.500 and 0.850. Then \(X=0,\ Y=0.700\), and the phase is \(\pi/2\) modulo \(2\pi\). With an independent bound \(|\bar\Delta/2\pi|<1\,\mathrm{kHz}\), the implied cyclic detuning is \(+500\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Longer delays resolve fine frequency differences at the cost of phase ambiguity and declining contrast. Use a shorter-delay record to unwrap a longer one.

At a contrast of 0.700 the relation \(-\ln(0.700)=\tau/T_2+\sigma_\Delta^2\tau^2/2\) supplies one equation for two unknowns. Several delays, with independent error bars, are needed to identify the linear and quadratic terms. At very low signal the length \(\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\) has a positive noise bias. Fitting the two directly measured quadratures can be preferable to fitting the length.

What is the fringe spacing in a scan of cyclic detuning at fixed delay of 0.80 ms?

Work through the solution

One full phase cycle needs \(\delta f\,\tau=1\). The spacing is

\[1/0.00080=1250\,\mathrm{Hz}.\]

The angular-frequency spacing is \(2\pi\) times larger.

A fixed delay gives

\[P_0=P_{\pi/2}=0.5.\]

Does that show a phase of 45 degrees?

Work through the solution

Both centred quadratures are zero, so the contrast is unresolved and the angle is undefined. A phase of 45 degrees with finite contrast gives equal, nonzero centred quadratures.

Explain how a refocusing pulse halfway through the evolution helps identify quasistatic detuning noise.

Work through the solution

The pulse exchanges the phase-accumulating states, reversing phase winding for a detuning that stays constant through both halves. Random variations during the interval need not cancel. Recovering contrast in the refocused sequence supports a slowly varying contribution to the original envelope.
