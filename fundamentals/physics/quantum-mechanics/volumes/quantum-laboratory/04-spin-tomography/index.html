When three perfectly legal spin measurements imply an impossible state

Translate detector counts into three components

Write \(\rho=(I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2\). For measurement along a unit direction \(\mathbf n\), the probability of spin up is

\[p_+=(1+\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)/2.\]

With axes \(x,y,z\), count \(k_j\) positive outcomes in \(N_j\) trials. Linear inversion gives

\[r_j=2k_j/N_j-1.\]

The trace condition is already built into this parametrisation. Positivity remains a separate physical requirement. Using the Pauli product identity, \((\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2=|\mathbf r|^2I\), the eigenvalues are \((1\pm|\mathbf r|)/2\). They form legitimate probabilities only for \(|\mathbf r|\le1\).

Each axis can report a frequency in \([0,1]\) even when the three reconstructed components have length greater than one. This need not imply a broken detector. The axes are measured on different sets of particles and can fluctuate in mutually inconsistent directions. A state that slowly drifts between the measurements, however, can produce deviations that persist as more particles are counted. Interleaving the settings can test that explanation.

Reconstruct a specific estimate

In 25 shots per axis an experiment obtains positive counts \(23,19,19\). The reconstructed components are \(0.84,0.52,0.52\), with norm about 1.1165. The smaller eigenvalue is approximately −0.058. A fourth analyser set opposite the inferred Bloch direction would therefore receive a predicted negative probability from this linear model. That prediction exposes the issue independently of any particular plotting convention.

A simple constrained estimate minimises Euclidean distance from the measured vector to the closed unit ball. If the vector lies inside, the minimiser is the measured vector. Outside, a Lagrange multiplier or elementary projection shows the minimiser is the vector divided by its length. One can now predict valid probabilities for every analyser direction. The result minimises a specified quadratic loss; it need not maximise the likelihood of the original counts.

Why a likelihood fit weights measurements differently

For independent binomial trials, the log likelihood up to constants is \(\sum_j[k_j\ln(1+r_j)+(N_j-k_j)\ln(1-r_j)]\). The positive physical state constraint couples the components. Differentiating each term gives a score \([2k_j-N_j-N_jr_j]/(1-r_j^2)\). Thus the cost of moving a component away from its unconstrained estimate depends on both its count and its location. A radial Euclidean projection ignores these different weights. Doubling only \(N_z\) while leaving its relative count unchanged preserves the inversion vector but changes the evidence for retaining its \(z\) component. A fit using likelihood can account for that evidence.

The shape of the confidence region is a further question. An unconstrained Gaussian ellipse may extend beyond physical states near the surface. A physical point estimate does not by itself specify an uncertainty interval; a measurement report should include both the estimator and the count data or a stated statistical model.

Evaluate a new measurement using the two estimates

Fix the first count at 23 of 25. Vary the positive counts on \(y,z\), then scan a test analyser in the plane through the estimated direction and an orthogonal axis. The graph plots probability versus angle from the raw vector. Radial projection preserves that direction. Predict which orientation first gives an impossible probability when the estimate moves outside the ball. A truly independent fourth measurement near that direction is more informative about the failure than a repeated plot of the three fitted counts.

All three directions are measured on 100 independent systems and the counts are 50, 50 and 50. Does this prove that every specimen was prepared in a mixed state?

Work through the solution

The estimate is \(I/2\) as a description of the measured ensemble. Many preparations have the same density operator, including equally likely pure states pointing along opposite directions. The counts do not identify one particular preparation history.

A data analysis repairs a negative eigenvalue by setting it to zero while retaining the original positive eigenvalue unchanged. Which density-matrix condition now fails?

Work through the solution

The trace was initially one. Removing a negative number increases that trace above one. A valid construction must enforce positivity and normalisation together. Projection onto the state ball automatically gives both for a two-level system.

Why is it incorrect to send a single qubit successively through three projective analysers and use the resulting counts in this inversion?

Work through the solution

A projective measurement changes the state entering the next analyser. The likelihood above assumes independent preparations of a common input state. Sequential data instead require the conditional measurement operations and their ordering.
