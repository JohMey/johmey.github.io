Change an oscillator’s isotope

A bond with unchanged stiffness has reduced mass \(m\) and adjacent vibrational spacing 40 meV. An isotopic substitution doubles its reduced mass. Find the new spacing and the ratio of the ground-state position widths. Does measuring an overtone alone establish the isotope change?

Separate the effects of mass and frequency

Since \(\omega=\sqrt{k/m}\) the spacing falls by \(1/\sqrt2\) to 28.3 meV. The width \(\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}\) scales as \(m^{-1/4}\) for constant stiffness, giving a ratio \(2^{-1/4}=0.841\). A single overtone may arise from anharmonicity or a nonlinear dipole coupling. Isotope identification requires enough independent information to distinguish those effects from a change in mass or stiffness.

Different definitions of a likely radius

For hydrogen’s normalised 1s spatial density \(e^{-2r/a}/(\pi a^3)\), find the radius of largest shell probability and the probability inside a sphere of radius \(a\). Explain why the probability to find the electron at one specified mathematical point is zero.

Integrate using the spherical measure

Multiplying by \(4\pi r^2\) gives radial density \(4r^2e^{-2r/a}/a^3\). Its nonzero maximum occurs at \(r=a\) by differentiation. With \(u=2r/a\), the integral to \(a\) is

\[\tfrac12\int_0^2u^2e^{-u}du=1-5e^{-2}=0.323.\]

Finite probability requires integration over a finite region. A maximum of the spatial density at one point is a statement about probabilities per unit volume, whereas a single point has zero volume.

Compare hydrogen and a one-electron ion

Neglect reduced-mass differences. For a helium nucleus with charge \(+2e\) and one electron, derive the changes relative to hydrogen in ground-state length, binding energy and the wavelength of the \(n=3\) to \(n=2\) transition. Count the orbital states of the upper level before spin is included.

Rescale the Coulomb attraction

Replacing \(C\) by \(2C\) gives \(a\propto1/C\), so the length halves to about 0.0265 nm. Binding energy scales as \(C^2\) and rises to 54.4 eV. The transition energy is four times larger, so its wavelength falls from about 656 to 164 nm. For \(n=3\), \(\ell=0,1,2\) contribute \(1+3+5=9\) orbital states. The degeneracy count does not imply that all of their dipole transitions have the same intensity or are allowed.
