Driven transitions and exchange symmetry

Interpret pulse spectra, apply a parity rule, and distinguish orbital occupation from complete fermion-state occupation.

Read the pulse duration

A rectangular weak pulse gives first spectral zeros 2.00 kHz apart across resonance. The on-resonance transfer probability is 0.0100. Find the pulse duration, coupling \(|V_{fi}|/\hbar\) and probability halfway from the peak to the first zero.

Show the spectral calibration

The first zeros are at \(\pm1/T\), giving \(2/T=2000\) Hz or \(T=1.00\) ms. Resonance gives

\[|V_{fi}|/\hbar=2\sqrt{P}/T=200\,\mathrm{rad/s}.\]

Halfway to a zero the argument is \(\pi/2\), giving \(0.0100(4/\pi^2)=0.00405\). These numbers assume a stable transition, a nearly resonant carrier and sufficiently many cycles for the counter-rotating term to average away.

A forbidden line and an allowed one

A one-dimensional well is symmetric about zero. An initial even ground orbital is driven by \(V(t)=Fx\cos\omega t\). Determine which parity of final state can be reached to first order. Compare to driving with \(Kx^2\cos\omega t\) and explain why increasing pulse length fails to enable the forbidden first-order integral.

Show the matrix-element symmetry

For an even final state the product \(\psi_f^*x\psi_i\) is odd and its integral over symmetric limits vanishes. Only odd final parity can have a nonzero dipole matrix element. With \(x^2\) the operator is even, so even final parity is allowed and odd parity excluded. Pulse duration changes the frequency integral multiplying the spatial matrix element. It cannot remove a spatial zero. A broken potential symmetry, a different driving operator or a higher-order transition can change an observed selection rule.

Extract an exchange integral

Two electrons occupy distinct orbitals. Measurements relative to the same energy reference give singlet energy 1.36 eV and triplet energy 1.24 eV. In the fixed-orbital model determine \(K\) and the common nonexchange contribution. Would three electrons fit into the same spatial orbital in this nonrelativistic model?

Show the state counting

The energies are \(E_{\rm common}\pm K\), with the plus for the singlet, so \(K=(1.36-1.24)/2=0.060\) eV and \(E_{\rm common}=1.30\) eV. The shared energy includes the single-particle sum and direct interaction and cannot be separated by this splitting alone. One spatial orbital affords two orthogonal electron spin states, up and down. Three occupied spin orbitals drawn from this two-dimensional space give a vanishing antisymmetric state, so a third electron must use a different spatial orbital.

Compare two continua competing for one initial state

Suppose two orthogonal channels have \((M,\rho)=(1.00\,\mathrm{\mu eV},500\,\mathrm{meV^{-1}})\) and \((0.500\,\mathrm{\mu eV},1500\,\mathrm{meV^{-1}})\). Find the total rate and the fraction of transitions into each channel. At \(5.00\,\mathrm{ps}\), estimate how often the initial state has made either transition.

Add rates and normalize branching fractions

In meV units the products \(|M|^2\rho\) are \(0.000500\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(0.000375\,\mathrm{meV}\). Multiplication by \(2\pi/\hbar\) gives \(0.00477\) and \(0.00358\,\mathrm{ps^{-1}}\), hence \(\Gamma_{\rm total}=0.00835\,\mathrm{ps^{-1}}\). The branching fractions are \(4/7\) and \(3/7\). Multiplying the total rate by \(5.00\,\mathrm{ps}\) gives a probability of \(0.0418\) to leading order. Larger state density partly offsets the smaller amplitude in the second channel.
