How a pair of neutral oscillators acquires an attractive interaction

Two neutral atoms in their ground states have zero permanent electric dipoles. A molecule nevertheless feels a long-range attraction to another neutral molecule. Treat one coordinate of each atom as a quantised oscillator. Solve the coupled pair twice and decide which parts of the result would survive in three dimensions.

First make a prediction at fixed separation

Two equal masses \(m\) have displacements \(x_1,x_2\) about fixed positive centres a distance \(R\) apart. In isolation both have angular frequency \(\omega_0\). The two induced electric moments are proportional to the displacements. The leading electrostatic interaction has energy \(g x_1x_2/R^3\), with orientation and charges absorbed into \(g\). Explain why a classical point resting at each minimum gains no energy, then ask what part of the Hamiltonian a quantum ground state would sample.

Work through the solution

Write \(k=m\omega_0^2\) and \(\lambda=g/(kR^3)\). Along \(x_2=0\) the potential rises as \(kx_1^2/2\). Along the diagonals \(x_1=\pm x_2\) it rises at different rates. For \(|\lambda|<1\) both slopes of restoring force are positive and the minimum remains at the origin with classical potential zero. The quantum oscillators have finite widths at zero temperature. Their ground state depends on both diagonal stiffnesses. A shift in that energy can occur even if the average position of each mass stays zero.

Diagonalise the complete Hamiltonian

Starting from kinetic energy \((p_1^2+p_2^2)/(2m)\), introduce normal coordinates and their conjugate momenta. Find both frequencies and the ground-state shift. Estimate it at \(\lambda=0.40\), in units of \(\hbar\omega_0\), sufficiently accurately to distinguish the exact result from its leading approximation.

Work through the solution

Use \(x_\pm=(x_1\pm x_2)/\sqrt2\) and the identical orthogonal transformation on momenta. Orthogonality preserves the kinetic energy and the canonical commutators. The product in the interaction is \((x_+^2-x_-^2)/2\). The two uncoupled Hamiltonians have spring constants \(k(1\pm\lambda)\). Each costs half a quantum in its own ground state. Subtract the energy of the two separated oscillators before comparing separations.

\[\omega_\pm=\omega_0\sqrt{1\pm\lambda},\]
\[\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar\omega_0}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\lambda}+\sqrt{1-\lambda}}2-1.\]

At 0.40 the two frequency ratios are 1.18322 and 0.77460. Their mean lies 0.02109 below one. The frequency that decreases loses more than the other gains because a square root is concave. Changing the sign of the coupling exchanges the normal coordinates and leaves the energy unchanged. This symmetry already excludes odd powers of the interaction.

Recover the same force from virtual transitions

Calculate the first two perturbative corrections in the basis of the isolated pair. Identify the intermediate state and its energy denominator. Obtain a power law for the force with the sign convention that increasing \(R\) is positive.

Work through the solution

Let \(x_0=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega_0)}\). Acting on an isolated ground state, \(x\) creates one quantum with amplitude \(x_0\). The expectation of the interaction in \(|00\rangle\) vanishes because each coordinate has zero mean. One action of its product leaves only \(|11\rangle\). The matrix element is \(k\lambda x_0^2=\lambda\hbar\omega_0/2\), and the excited state lies two quanta above the ground state. The denominator \(E_{00}-E_{11}\) is therefore negative.

\[\Delta E^{(2)}=-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{8}\lambda^2=-\frac{C_6}{R^6},\]
\[C_6=\frac{\hbar\omega_0g^2}{8k^2},\]
\[F_R=-\frac{6C_6}{R^7}.\]

At the specified coupling the quadratic approximation gives −0.02000. Expanding each square root and adding yields the next correction \(-5\lambda^4/128\). At 0.40 that term is −0.00100. The formal expansion parameter is a stiffness ratio. Calling the interaction weak requires a separation large enough to make this ratio small, even if each microscopic charge is individually large.

What would distinguish the power law in data?

The downloadable OneSimulation model assembles the two-by-two stiffness matrix and computes its modes with a contour eigensolver. Taking square roots and summing their zero-point energies reproduces the plotted exact curve. The two separate frequencies in the CSV provide a stronger check than their sum.

Use separation units \(R_0\) and set the coupling there to 0.40. Before opening the graph, predict the factor by which the leading energy shift changes at \(2R_0\). Is the corresponding factor the same for force? Then change the coupling at the reference separation. Compare the exact and perturbative curves where the soft frequency approaches zero.

Work through the solution

Doubling the separation divides the coupling by eight. The leading energy falls in magnitude by \(8^2=64\), and the force by 128. For the reference coupling the exact shifts at \(R_0\) and \(2R_0\) have magnitudes about 0.02109 and 0.000313 in units of one quantum. The ratio of exact energies is about 67.4. A fit near strong coupling therefore gives a separation exponent steeper than six. Measuring it does not establish a new long-distance law. Repeat farther apart and check whether the estimate approaches six.

A mode becoming soft signals the breakdown of the quadratic model. At small separation real atoms overlap and exchange matters. At distances comparable with a transition wavelength, electromagnetic propagation time matters. This two-coordinate electrostatic model predicts neither limit. Extending it to three dimensions also requires carrying the dipole orientation tensor through the intermediate states. The coefficient found here is specific to the stated geometry.
