Hollow out a charged sphere

An insulating sphere of radius \(2a\) contains uniform charge density \(\rho\) except in a concentric empty cavity of radius \(a\). Derive the radial field in all three regions. Match it at the two boundaries and calculate the potential difference between the cavity and the outer surface for \(\rho=1.0\) µC/m³ and \(a=0.10\) m.

Enclose the remaining volume charge

In the cavity the concentric Gaussian sphere encloses zero charge and spherical symmetry gives zero field. In the charged material the enclosed charge is \(4\pi\rho(r^3-a^3)/3\), so \(E_r=\rho(r-a^3/r^2)/(3\epsilon_0)\). Outside it becomes \(7\rho a^3/(3\epsilon_0 r^2)\). The two expressions agree at \(2a\) and the material field tends to zero at \(a\). Integrating outward gives \(V(a)-V(2a)=\rho a^2/(3\epsilon_0)\), about 376 V. An off-centre cavity would break the symmetry used in this proof.

Place a grounded plane below a charge

A positive 4.0 nC charge sits 50 mm above a large grounded plate. Find the force magnitude and the radius collecting 80% of the negative induced charge. How do both change if the charge doubles at fixed height?

Use the image and integrate the surface charge

The point charge interacts with an image of magnitude 4.0 nC at separation 0.100 m. Its attractive force is \(kq^2/(2a)^2=14.4\) µN. The fraction within a disk is \(1-a/\sqrt{S^2+a^2}\). Requiring 0.8 gives

\[S=\sqrt{24}\,a=0.245\,\mathrm{m}.\]

Doubling the charge leaves the fractional distribution and this disk radius unchanged, doubles the density at every point and quadruples the force. The comparatively large disk helps explain why finite plates can distort measurements of total induced charge.

Recover a potential in a charge-free gap

Two coaxial conducting cylinders of radii 5 and 20 mm are held at 100 and 0 V in vacuum. Neglect end effects. Solve Laplace’s equation in the gap, predict the reading at 10 mm, and find how far from the inner wall a probe must move to see half the inner-wall field.

Integrate the radial Laplacian

With cylindrical symmetry Laplace’s equation is \((rV')'=0\). One integration gives \(rV'=\text{constant}\) and the second gives a logarithm. Matching the wall potentials yields \(V=100\ln(20\,\mathrm{mm}/r)/\ln4\) V and \(E_r=100/(r\ln4)\) V/m with \(r\) in metres. At 10 mm the potential is 50 V. Since the field is proportional to \(1/r\), moving from 5 to 10 mm, a distance of 5 mm, halves the field. The halfway-potential position is the geometric mean of the two radii.
