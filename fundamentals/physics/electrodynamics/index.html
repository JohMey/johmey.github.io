Applied study

Derive the fields of a coaxial cable and follow how electromagnetic energy travels along it.

Where does energy travel in a cable?

A coaxial cable contains two conductors separated by an insulator. Current travels along the metal surfaces. To locate the energy delivered to a load, find the electric and magnetic fields in the space between them. Consider concentric, perfectly conducting cylinders of radii \(a\) and \(b\), separated by a uniform lossless dielectric of permittivity \(\epsilon\) and permeability \(\mu\). Treat a straight, uniform section far from connectors.

Place charge per unit length \(+q'\) on the inner conductor and \(-q'\) on the facing shield. A cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\), with \(a<r<b\), gives \(2\pi rD_r=q'\) per unit length. Integrating \(E_r=D_r/\epsilon\) from inner to outer conductor gives \(V=q'\ln(b/a)/(2\pi\epsilon)\). Thus \(C'=q'/V=2\pi\epsilon/\ln(b/a)\) is capacitance per metre. More charge fits at the same voltage when the dielectric permittivity rises. Fields and voltage at a dielectric interface.

Find the second energy store

Let a current \(I\) flow along the inner conductor and return on the shield. Ampère’s law gives a circumferential magnetic field \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi r)\). Integrate its energy density \(\mu H_\phi^2/2\) over the annular cross-section. The resulting magnetic energy per metre is \(\mu I^2\ln(b/a)/(4\pi)\). Equating this to \(L'I^2/2\) gives \(L'=\mu\ln(b/a)/(2\pi)\). The product \(L'C'=\mu\epsilon\) is independent of the two radii for this homogeneous line. Magnetic fields from steady currents.

Couple the fields into a travelling wave

A slice of cable of length \(dz\) gains charge when more current enters than leaves. Its charge is \(C'V\,dz\), giving \(\partial_z I=-C'\partial_t V\). The changing magnetic flux gives the complementary voltage drop \(\partial_z V=-L'\partial_t I\) by Faraday’s law. Differentiate once more to obtain \(\partial_{zz}V=L'C'\partial_{tt}V\). For a waveform travelling in the positive \(z\) direction, these equations require

\[v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}},\qquad \frac{V}{I}=Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}.\]

Here \(Z_0\) is the ratio for a single travelling wave. The radial electric field crossed with the circumferential magnetic field gives axial Poynting flux. Indeed \(\int_a^b E_r H_\phi\,2\pi r\,dr=VI\). The surrounding dielectric carries this electromagnetic power in the ideal model.

What crosses the gap in a charging capacitor?

A changing electric field can also generate a magnetic field where there are no moving free charges. Charge a parallel-plate capacitor through a wire at steady current \(I\). A loop encircling the wire bounds a surface cut by the wire, but also a deformed surface passing between the plates. A consistent magnetic circulation for both surfaces requires the Maxwell term in Ampère’s law, \(\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{\rm free}+d\int\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf A/dt\). Between wide circular plates the total electric displacement flux is the plate charge, so its derivative is exactly \(I\).

Take plate radius \(R=5.0\) cm and \(I=1.0\) A. Inside the gap, neglect fringing and assume slow charging, so that \(D\) is uniform over the plate area. A centred loop of radius \(r=2.5\) cm encloses the fraction \(r^2/R^2=1/4\) of the changing flux. The magnetic flux density there is \(B=\mu_0 I r/(2\pi R^2)=2.0\) µT. A loop just outside the plate radius encloses the full displacement current. Charge conservation, the electric field and the magnetic circulation thus give the same current whether the chosen surface cuts a wire or a dielectric.

A short pulse in polyethylene

Use \(a=0.450\) mm, \(b=1.475\) mm, \(\epsilon=2.25\epsilon_0\) and \(\mu=\mu_0\). The logarithm of the radius ratio is 1.187. The line constants are 105.44 pF/m and 237.43 nH/m. They give \(Z_0=47.45\) Ω and a propagation speed of \(2.00\times10^8\) m/s. A 1 V forward pulse has current \(1/47.45=21.1\) mA and instantaneous power 21.1 mW while its flat top passes a cross-section. It reaches the end of a 10 m line about 50 ns after launch.

Exercises on transmission and reflection

1. Change voltage without changing geometry

Double the amplitude of the forward pulse. Compare electric energy per metre, magnetic energy per metre and transmitted power with their original values.

Work out the energy factors

\(I=V/Z_0\) doubles. Thus the two energies \(C'V^2/2\) and \(L'I^2/2\) both quadruple, and \(VI\) also quadruples. Their ratio is unity for a travelling wave because \(L'/Z_0^2=C'\). The pulse speed stays fixed since neither line constant changed.

2. Match the load

Terminate the cable in \(R_L=2Z_0\). At the load the forward and backward voltages add, while their currents have opposite propagation signs. Find the reflected fraction of incident power.

Apply the boundary condition

Write \(V_++V_-=R_L(V_+-V_-)/Z_0\). This gives \(V_-/V_+=(R_L-Z_0)/(R_L+Z_0)=1/3\). Each wave’s power magnitude is proportional to its voltage squared, so \(1/9\) returns and \(8/9\) is absorbed. For \(R_L=Z_0\), the boundary condition accepts the incident wave without a reflected wave.
