    Read the subject in depth

    Start with electrostatics if this is your first field theory course. Each volume develops its arguments in sequence, with worked problems and links to experiments.

    • Electrostatic field theory and measurement

      Learn to infer a field from charges, potentials or boundary measurements. Build the mathematics of flux, uniqueness and Green functions as each problem requires it.

    • Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

      Begin with a steady current and follow what changes when a source, a conductor or a field moves. Track energy and momentum with explicit system boundaries.

    • Waves, radiation and relativity

      Follow a disturbance from its source to a measurement. Work through polarisation, dispersion, scattering and changes of observer using measurable quantities.

    • Fields and the response of matter

      Connect electric and magnetic measurements to relaxation times, carrier motion, thermal fluctuations and collective order. Use frequency, temperature and geometry to distinguish competing explanations.

    • Electromechanics, optics and measurement

      Follow the field into an instrument. Predict which mechanical displacement, temperature change or optical signal can be observed and determine what can be learned from it.

    • Numerical field laboratory

      Turn a prediction into a computational experiment. Derive a reference, solve the field problem, challenge the model with a diagnostic and interpret the residual.

    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Charges and currents specify sources. Use symmetry and boundary conditions to turn their integral laws into field predictions, then ask how changing flux produces a voltage around a loop.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Combine the electric and magnetic energy balances to follow energy through space. A time-dependent source can radiate. Far-field expansions and interface conditions determine which parts of a source remain observable at a distance.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Conductivity, walls and material interfaces change how a field propagates. Follow a signal through a guide and along a transmission line, then integrate the field stress to find the force on matter.

    Chapters

    1. Charge, potential and field energy

      Reconstruct both the local electric field and the total energy of a charged body from the same continuous charge distribution.

    2. Conductors and boundary-value problems

      Use the uniqueness of the potential to replace a grounded conductor by an image charge and then recover its induced surface charge.

    3. Magnetic flux and material response

      Predict how a narrow air gap changes a toroidal coil’s flux, stored energy and inductance from Ampère’s law and the magnetic boundary conditions.

    4. Induction in moving circuits

      Separate transformer and motional electromotive forces, then use the mechanical and electrical power balances to check a moving conductor.

    5. Electromagnetic waves and energy flux

      Derive the electromagnetic energy balance and use it to infer field amplitudes and transmitted energy from a measured wave intensity.

    6. Radiation from a small electric dipole

      Relate a time-dependent electric dipole moment to its radiation pattern, total power and the conditions under which a far-field detector measures them.

    7. Multipole fields

      Predict the leading far field of a localized charge distribution and use symmetry to decide how rapidly it decays.

    8. Polarization of a dielectric sphere

      Match electrostatic fields at a curved interface and infer a material’s induced dipole from its exterior potential.

    9. Charge relaxation and magnetic diffusion

      Distinguish two conductor timescales and estimate how deeply an alternating magnetic field penetrates a solid.

    10. Waves in a hollow guide

      Derive a rectangular guide’s cutoff and use its dispersion relation to distinguish phase delay from signal transit time.

    11. Pulses on a transmission line

      Use charge and flux conservation to predict the first voltages seen when a pulse meets a mismatched load.

    12. Force carried by a field

      Compute electrical pressure from a local stress balance and reconcile two energy calculations for a moving capacitor plate.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. Local field laws at points, sheets and holes

      Derive divergence and curl from what a probe encloses. Track a point charge through a regularisation and work out when a potential can be defined over an entire region.

    2. Building a potential from point responses

      Solve one source problem exactly, reuse it for a distributed charge, and find what an electrode measures without solving for its surface charge point by point.

    3. How far does an electrode pattern reach?

      Solve Laplace’s equation in a strip, read decay lengths straight from boundary data and decide when a truncated series can predict an interior measurement.

    4. Spherical harmonics as measurements of boundary voltage

      Reconstruct interior and exterior potentials from an angular voltage scan. Use orthogonality to isolate a dipole or quadrupole and quantify how rapidly fine angular structure disappears.

    5. From a measured current to its magnetic field

      Construct the magnetic field of a steady source, calculate a coil pair’s uniform region and extract the first magnetic moment that survives at a large distance.

    6. How microscopic dipoles produce macroscopic charge

      Derive the volume and surface charges of a polarised body, determine the fields in layers and calculate which electrical work a capacitance measurement includes.

    7. How a magnet produces an exterior field

      Translate microscopic loop moments into volume and surface currents. Derive a shape-dependent internal field and learn which magnetic response a coil measurement can recover.

    8. Where momentum waits between source and receiver

      Derive the electromagnetic momentum balance, trace light pressure through a finite pulse, and account for the angular impulse when a magnetic flux decays.

    9. What an interface does to phase, field and power

      Match fields at an optical surface, derive reflection for each polarisation and follow energy when the refracted wave becomes evanescent.

    10. Why dispersion and absorption belong together

      Obtain complex permittivity from a damped charge, relate its imaginary part to heating and distinguish wave crests, envelopes and a causal change in a signal.

    11. Potentials, gauge freedom and the arrival of a signal

      When a source changes at noon, which part of its history can affect a detector at twelve o’clock and one nanosecond? The answer emerges from Maxwell’s equations once we specify both the source and the boundary condition in time.

    12. A moving charge, its retarded position and its radiation

      A moving source moves while its field travels. Finding its position on the past light cone supplies an extra factor in its potential and explains why an accelerated charge can carry energy to arbitrarily large distances.

    13. Changing observers and classifying electromagnetic fields

      Can an observer move so that a measured electric field disappears? Two numbers constructed from the fields settle the question before a velocity is chosen. The moving observer's force measurements explain why the classification works.

    14. From local symmetry to Maxwell’s equations

      Some electromagnetic equations describe the geometry of a potential. Others describe its response to charge. Tensor notation identifies the distinction precisely, and a variational argument shows why the source must obey a local conservation law.

    15. Cavity eigenmodes, measured linewidth and ring-down

      A cavity has both a pitch and a decay time. One is set predominantly by the field pattern and the boundaries. The other measures how much stored energy escapes per cycle. A spatial perturbation can reveal where a particular mode stores its electric energy.

    16. Why a small transparent sphere scatters light

      A particle can redistribute light without consuming it. The calculation starts with an electrostatic boundary-value problem and ends with power crossing a faraway sphere. Connecting the two limits predicts a wavelength dependence that can be tested with colour.

    17. When a circuit element must be treated as a field

      Can a short pair of conductors be called a capacitor? At low frequencies it stores energy almost electrostatically. As propagation time becomes important, the same open conductors support standing waves and their input current no longer grows proportionally to frequency.

    18. From wavefronts to rays, focusing and caustics

      A ray is a prediction extracted from the phase of a wave. Following its amplitude at the same time reveals the point at which a ray calculation loses its validity. A gently graded transparent material gives a solvable example of both effects.

    19. Radiation reaction and the energy stored near a charge

      A charge can radiate at an instant when a proposed frictional force would do no work. To account for the energy one needs three measurements: mechanical work on the charge, power leaving in radiation and a reversible change in its nearby field.

    20. Diffraction from an aperture of finite width

      Geometrical optics predicts a sharp edge and an infinitely narrow ideal focus. A scalar diffraction integral describes how contributions with different phase interfere. A slit gives an exact far-field reference against which to test an oscillatory numerical integral.

    21. Energy, momentum and a charged particle’s worldline

      A field may turn the momentum, change its magnitude, or do both. Beginning with a measurable clock carried by the particle gives a force law that remains well behaved even when increasing momentum produces little visible change in speed.

    22. Capacitance as a matrix of electrostatic energies

      Three conductors occupy a grounded metal enclosure. Charging one can induce charge on another even when its voltage stays fixed. To predict a measurement we must specify which conductors have fixed voltage, which are isolated and what defines the reference potential.

    23. How a conductor admits a field step and a sinusoid

      A time-varying magnetic field enters a conducting slab from its surface. A suddenly imposed constant field continues to penetrate with elapsed time. A sinusoid establishes a persistent depth profile. Both behaviours follow from the same initial–boundary value equation.

    24. Static screening and collective charge oscillations

      In a neutral ion–electron gas a small positive probe draws an excess of electrons. A displaced electron fluid also experiences a restoring electric field and can oscillate. The equilibrium screening length and the collective frequency involve the same density but describe different experiments.

    25. Reconstructing a partially polarised electromagnetic wave

      A detector averages enormous numbers of optical cycles. Its horizontal and vertical readings may be equal for a circularly polarised wave, a diagonal linearly polarised wave, or unpolarised light. Which additional intensities distinguish these preparations?

    26. Recovering a vector field from divergence, curl and boundary data

      A numerical field solver returns three components at every point. Divergence measures sources and curl measures circulation, yet neither measurement detects a spatially uniform offset. Helmholtz decomposition makes precise what can be recovered, how to compute it, and what the boundary must supply.

    27. A ring, a disk and an electric-field maximum in empty space

      Gauss’s law gives the flux through a closed surface. For a finite circular source the field varies across that surface. Adding the fields of actual charge elements predicts both the near field and a surprising axial maximum when the centre is removed.

    28. The same metal sphere connected to ground or electrically isolated

      A grounded sphere can draw charge through a wire. An isolated one can only rearrange what is already present. Both are equipotentials, but the second problem needs a different image system and gives a different force at long range.

    29. How a circular wall selects Bessel modes

      A voltage pattern is imposed on the end of a long grounded tube. Its penetration length depends on the tube radius through a number that looks unfamiliar in a Cartesian Fourier series. Deriving that number also explains the proper radial weight for projecting data.

    30. Forces and torques from a matrix of flux linkages

      Two measurements made while holding current fixed and flux fixed can produce different torque curves. They agree on the torque of the same physical state. A derivation from electrical work identifies precisely what must be held fixed when differentiating.

    31. Where current and energy go in a charging capacitor

      A wire carries current to the centre of a plate, yet charge appears across its face and the energy increases in the gap. Following that current over differently chosen surfaces gives a spatial test of charge conservation and an independent power budget.

    32. A lossy half-space seen through its surface impedance

      An absorbing medium transmits a complex field and converts electrical work into heat. Matching one complex number at its surface predicts reflection. Integrating the heating through its depth provides an independent check that is especially revealing near the crossover between dielectric and conductor.

    33. Two polarisations moving through a uniaxial crystal

      A crystal can send two pulses in different directions even when their wavefronts have the same normal. The distinction emerges by applying the divergence condition to electric displacement and measuring energy flow with the Poynting vector.

    34. Radiation when the electric dipole cancels

      Two optical dipoles can cancel in the long-wavelength electric-dipole approximation and still radiate. Expanding their source phase shows which moments survive, where the remaining light goes, and how a small asymmetry restores a different angular pattern.

    35. Four contacts on a conductor of finite thickness

      A voltmeter scarcely draws any current, yet its reading depends on how the current from a separate pair of leads spreads inside the specimen. Derive the conversion from a four-terminal reading to resistivity. Decide from the geometry whether the result describes a bulk material or a conducting sheet.

    Applied study

    Derive the fields of a coaxial cable and follow how electromagnetic energy travels along it.

    Where does energy travel in a cable?

    A coaxial cable contains two conductors separated by an insulator. Current travels along the metal surfaces. To locate the energy delivered to a load, find the electric and magnetic fields in the space between them. Consider concentric, perfectly conducting cylinders of radii \(a\) and \(b\), separated by a uniform lossless dielectric of permittivity \(\epsilon\) and permeability \(\mu\). Treat a straight, uniform section far from connectors.

    Place charge per unit length \(+q'\) on the inner conductor and \(-q'\) on the facing shield. A cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\), with \(a<r<b\), gives \(2\pi rD_r=q'\) per unit length. Integrating \(E_r=D_r/\epsilon\) from inner to outer conductor gives \(V=q'\ln(b/a)/(2\pi\epsilon)\). Thus \(C'=q'/V=2\pi\epsilon/\ln(b/a)\) is capacitance per metre. More charge fits at the same voltage when the dielectric permittivity rises. Fields and voltage at a dielectric interface.

    Find the second energy store

    Let a current \(I\) flow along the inner conductor and return on the shield. Ampère’s law gives a circumferential magnetic field \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi r)\). Integrate its energy density \(\mu H_\phi^2/2\) over the annular cross-section. The resulting magnetic energy per metre is \(\mu I^2\ln(b/a)/(4\pi)\). Equating this to \(L'I^2/2\) gives \(L'=\mu\ln(b/a)/(2\pi)\). The product \(L'C'=\mu\epsilon\) is independent of the two radii for this homogeneous line. Magnetic fields from steady currents.

    Couple the fields into a travelling wave

    A slice of cable of length \(dz\) gains charge when more current enters than leaves. Its charge is \(C'V\,dz\), giving \(\partial_z I=-C'\partial_t V\). The changing magnetic flux gives the complementary voltage drop \(\partial_z V=-L'\partial_t I\) by Faraday’s law. Differentiate once more to obtain

    \[\partial_{zz}V=L'C'\partial_{tt}V.\]

    For a waveform travelling in the positive \(z\) direction, these equations require

    \[v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}},\]
    \[\frac{V}{I}=Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}.\]

    Here \(Z_0\) is the ratio for a single travelling wave. The radial electric field crossed with the circumferential magnetic field gives axial Poynting flux. Indeed \(\int_a^b E_r H_\phi\,2\pi r\,dr=VI\). The surrounding dielectric carries this electromagnetic power in the ideal model.

    What crosses the gap in a charging capacitor?

    A changing electric field can also generate a magnetic field where there are no moving free charges. Charge a parallel-plate capacitor through a wire at steady current \(I\). A loop encircling the wire bounds a surface cut by the wire, but also a deformed surface passing between the plates. A consistent magnetic circulation for both surfaces requires the Maxwell term in Ampère’s law,

    \[\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{\rm free}+d\int\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf A/dt.\]

    Between wide circular plates the total electric displacement flux is the plate charge, so its derivative is exactly \(I\).

    Take plate radius \(R=5.0\) cm and \(I=1.0\) A. Inside the gap, neglect fringing and assume slow charging, so that \(D\) is uniform over the plate area. A centred loop of radius \(r=2.5\) cm encloses the fraction \(r^2/R^2=1/4\) of the changing flux. The magnetic flux density there is \(B=\mu_0 I r/(2\pi R^2)=2.0\) µT. A loop just outside the plate radius encloses the full displacement current. Charge conservation, the electric field and the magnetic circulation thus give the same current whether the chosen surface cuts a wire or a dielectric.

    A short pulse in polyethylene

    Use \(a=0.450\) mm, \(b=1.475\) mm,

    \[\epsilon=2.25\epsilon_0,\]
    \[\mu=\mu_0.\]

    The logarithm of the radius ratio is 1.187. The line constants are 105.44 pF/m and 237.43 nH/m. They give \(Z_0=47.45\) Ω and a propagation speed of \(2.00\times10^8\) m/s. A 1 V forward pulse has current \(1/47.45=21.1\) mA and instantaneous power 21.1 mW while its flat top passes a cross-section. It reaches the end of a 10 m line about 50 ns after launch.

    Exercises on transmission and reflection

    1. Change voltage without changing geometry

    Double the amplitude of the forward pulse. Compare electric energy per metre, magnetic energy per metre and transmitted power with their original values.

    Work out the energy factors

    \(I=V/Z_0\) doubles. Thus the two energies \(C'V^2/2\) and \(L'I^2/2\) both quadruple, and \(VI\) also quadruples. Their ratio is unity for a travelling wave because \(L'/Z_0^2=C'\). The pulse speed stays fixed since neither line constant changed.

    2. Match the load

    Terminate the cable in \(R_L=2Z_0\). At the load the forward and backward voltages add, while their currents have opposite propagation signs. Find the reflected fraction of incident power.

    Apply the boundary condition

    Write \(V_++V_-=R_L(V_+-V_-)/Z_0\). This gives

    \[V_-/V_+=(R_L-Z_0)/(R_L+Z_0)=1/3.\]

    Each wave’s power magnitude is proportional to its voltage squared, so \(1/9\) returns and \(8/9\) is absorbed. For \(R_L=Z_0\), the boundary condition accepts the incident wave without a reflected wave.
