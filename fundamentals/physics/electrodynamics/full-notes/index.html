Chapter 1 · Charge, potential and field energy

Reconstruct both the local electric field and the total energy of a charged body from the same continuous charge distribution.

Enclose the source before integrating the field

Imagine an insulating sphere charged uniformly throughout its volume. A probe outside it measures the field of a point charge, yet that representation fails at its centre. Our first objective is to see how a local charge distribution determines which measurements can distinguish the two sources. Use electrostatics in vacuum, with a sphere of radius \(R\) and total free charge \(Q\). A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium would distribute its excess charge differently.

Gauss’s law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is the enclosed charge divided by \(\epsilon_0\). Flux is the integral of the component normal to the surface. Pulling the field magnitude outside that integral requires symmetry in addition to the law itself. For this sphere rotational symmetry forces the field to be radial and of equal magnitude everywhere on a concentric spherical surface. The test surface can pass through the charged material, since it is a geometrical construction and does not insert a conductor.

Match the interior and exterior solutions

The uniform density is \(\rho=3Q/(4\pi R^3)\). At a radius \(r<R\) the enclosed charge is \(Q(r/R)^3\). At \(r>R\) the surface encloses all of \(Q\). Substitution in \(4\pi r^2 E_r=Q_{\rm encl}/\epsilon_0\) gives an interior field \(E_r=Qr/(4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3)\) and an exterior field \(E_r=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). The field vanishes at the centre and is continuous at the surface. A finite change in volume density changes the slope there. A true surface sheet of charge could instead create a finite jump in the normal field.

Since a static field has zero curl, a scalar potential can be chosen such that \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\). Take \(V(\infty)=0\) and integrate the radial field inward. The exterior potential equals \(Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)\). To reach an interior point, continue the integral from the surface with the interior field. This gives

\[V(r)=\frac{Q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left(3-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right),\qquad 0\le r\le R.\]

Matching at the surface supplies the additive constant which an interior integration alone cannot fix. A zero electric field at the centre means that the slope of the potential vanishes there. Its value need not vanish.

Assemble the sphere and account for the work

Build the charge by adding successive spherical shells while holding the final density fixed. When the assembly reaches radius \(r\), its charge is \(q(r)=Q(r/R)^3\). Bringing a small additional shell \(dq=3Qr^2dr/R^3\) from infinity costs \(dW=q(r)dq/(4\pi\epsilon_0 r)\). Integration gives \(W=3Q^2/(20\pi\epsilon_0 R)\). This positive work is stored in the electrostatic configuration. The shell’s interaction with itself is higher order in the infinitesimal added charge.

A complementary description uses the energy density \(\epsilon_0 E^2/2\). Integration over the exterior alone gives \(Q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0R)\), which is five sixths of the total. The remainder resides inside the charge distribution. A thin spherical conducting shell of equal radius and charge produces the same outside field, yet it has no interior field in its empty cavity. Its total energy is therefore lower.

Interpret three probe readings

For \(R=0.10\) m and \(Q=2.0\) nC, the surface field is \(1.80\times10^3\) V/m. At half the radius the field halves to about 899 V/m. At twice the radius it is roughly 449 V/m. The surface potential is 180 V, while the centre is near 270 V. Their difference predicts the work per unit charge required to carry a small positive test charge from surface to centre. A sufficiently large test charge would disturb the source distribution and violate the assumed measurement model.

The assembly energy is only \(2.16\times10^{-7}\) J. Doubling \(Q\) multiplies the field and potential by two but multiplies the stored energy by four. A comparison of fields outside two spherical samples probes their total charge. A measurement of assembly work at fixed radius can reveal how that charge was distributed inside them.

If the charge is reduced gradually while its spatial shape is preserved, the surface voltage is proportional to total charge. The incremental work needed to assemble the next element therefore grows with charge already present. Integrating that growth explains the familiar factor of one half in electrostatic self-energy expressions.

Insert a dielectric into a spherical capacitor

An enclosed-charge construction also works when different radial regions store different electric energies. Put a conducting sphere of radius \(a\) inside a concentric thin conducting shell of radius \(b\). Give the inner sphere free charge \(+Q\) and the outer shell \(-Q\). Fill \(a<r<c\) with a linear dielectric of permittivity \(\epsilon_1\) and \(c<r<b\) with \(\epsilon_2\). There is no free charge at the interface. Assume the shells and both materials share a common centre so that a spherical Gaussian surface has uniform radial displacement.

Gauss's law for free charge gives \(D_r=Q/(4\pi r^2)\) throughout the gap. The electric field in material \(i\) is \(D_r/\epsilon_i\). Thus the normal field can jump at \(c\) even though normal displacement is continuous. To predict the measured voltage, integrate the radial field outward from \(a\) to \(b\); the positive quantity obtained is the inner voltage minus the outer voltage. This yields

\[\frac1C=\frac{V_a-V_b}{Q}=\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac{1/a-1/c}{\epsilon_1}+\frac{1/c-1/b}{\epsilon_2}\right].\]

The sum is naturally a sum of inverse capacitances since the same flux passes through both layers and their voltage drops add. Replacing the two dielectric constants by a simple average would weight every radial thickness equally, despite the field being stronger near the small inner sphere. Setting the two permittivities equal cancels the arbitrary interface location, a useful check on the integration.

Decide where a small amount of dielectric matters most

Choose \(a=10.0\) mm, \(c=20.0\) mm, \(b=40.0\) mm, \(\epsilon_1=4\epsilon_0\) and \(\epsilon_2=\epsilon_0\). The radial contributions after factoring out \(1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\) are \((100-50)/4=12.5\) m⁻¹ and \(50-25=25.0\) m⁻¹. Hence \(C=4\pi\epsilon_0/(37.5\,{\rm m}^{-1})=2.97\) pF. A charge of \(0.300\) nC produces about 101 V, one third across the inner material and two thirds across the outer one. An all-vacuum device of the same radii has \(C=1.48\) pF.

For a further check integrate local energy density \(u=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D/2\) over each layer. The integration element \(4\pi r^2dr\) reduces the energy integral to \(Q^2(1/r_{\rm in}-1/r_{\rm out})/(8\pi\epsilon_i)\). The two contributions are again in the ratio one to two, summing to \(Q^2/(2C)=15.2\) nJ. Moving dielectric towards the inner conductor usually has a larger effect per unit volume because the undisturbed energy density is higher there. Near metal contact surfaces roughness or air pockets can make precisely this region differ from its intended permittivity.

At the interface voltage is continuous but radial field just inside the outer material is four times its value just inside the inner material for these permittivities. A finite jump in voltage would imply an infinitely strong field over zero thickness, absent any imposed dipole sheet. Inserting these interfacial fields into the constitutive law gives identical normal displacement from either side. This is a more local test than comparing the terminal capacitance: a calculation can accidentally reproduce a similar integrated voltage while placing the field discontinuity on the wrong side of the boundary.

Chapter 2 · Conductors and boundary-value problems

Use the uniqueness of the potential to replace a grounded conductor by an image charge and then recover its induced surface charge.

Trade unknown surface charge for known potential

A point charge held above a large grounded metal plane attracts charge towards the region beneath it. The induced surface density is nonuniform and initially unknown. Direct integration of Coulomb’s law would therefore introduce the very quantity one wants to determine. In a boundary-value formulation the potential of the metal is known instead. Assume a perfectly conducting plane at \(z=0\), vacuum above it and a positive point charge \(q\) at \(z=a>0\). Grounding fixes the plane at zero potential and allows charge to enter or leave it.

Away from the point source the potential obeys Laplace’s equation, and at the charge its singular behaviour is the Coulomb potential. These properties with the boundary values determine a unique solution. To see why, subtract two candidate solutions with the same charges and Dirichlet boundary conditions. Their difference \(w\) has zero Laplacian and vanishes on the boundary. Integrating \(\nabla\cdot(w\nabla w)=|\nabla w|^2+w\nabla^2w\) over the domain gives zero net surface term, including the limit at infinity for appropriately decaying fields. Hence the integral of \(|\nabla w|^2\) vanishes and \(w=0\) throughout.

Construct the image

Put a fictitious charge \(-q\) at \(z=-a\). At any point on the plane its distance equals the distance to the actual charge, so the two potentials cancel pointwise. In the upper half-space the fictitious source lies outside the solution domain. The combined potential has the correct single singularity, is zero on the metal and decays at infinity. By uniqueness it is the upper-half-space solution. With \(s\) the horizontal distance from the projection of the charge,

\[V(s,z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2+(z-a)^2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2+(z+a)^2}}\right].\]

It is important to keep the domain in view. The expression continued below \(z=0\) describes an auxiliary mathematical problem. Inside the real conductor electrostatic equilibrium gives a constant potential and zero field. The fictitious charge is a way to reproduce the valid exterior data, rather than a literal localisation of the induced charge.

Recover the induced charge and force

The field just outside a conducting surface has no tangential component. Its outward normal component is \(\sigma_s/\epsilon_0\) because the field inside is zero. Differentiate the potential with respect to height and set \(z=0^+\) to obtain \(\sigma_s(s)=-qa/[2\pi(s^2+a^2)^{3/2}]\). Integrating over rings \(2\pi s\,ds\) from zero to infinity gives total induced charge \(-q\). A nearby grounded conductor connected to a reservoir can supply that total charge even though its potential remains fixed.

At the position of the real charge its own singular field is excluded when calculating force. The induced field equals that of the image at distance \(2a\), so the attraction has magnitude \(q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a^2)\). Integrating the force from infinity gives interaction energy \(-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a)\). Simply multiplying the image potential at the charge by \(q\) would double the magnitude because the induced distribution adjusts as the charge moves. The work integral automatically includes the changing response.

Quantify the localisation

Take \(q=1.0\) nC and \(a=20\) mm. The force magnitude is \(5.62\) µN. The surface density directly beneath the charge is \(-q/(2\pi a^2)=-0.398\) µC/m². Within a disk of radius \(S\) the integral gives \(-q[1-a/\sqrt{S^2+a^2}]\). Setting the fraction in brackets equal to one half yields \(S=\sqrt3\,a=34.6\) mm. The density peaks directly below the charge, but a disk wider than its height is needed to collect half the total.

Bring the charge twice as close while keeping \(q\) fixed. Both the peak density and the attraction quadruple, while the radius containing half the induced charge halves. This spatial prediction is sensitive to finite plate size. A real plate with a radius comparable to \(a\) cannot reproduce the infinite-plane surface distribution far from the centre, even when the potential at its surface is tightly controlled.

Only the half-space accessible to the physical charge enters the field-energy comparison. Extending the mathematical solution through the grounded boundary introduces a second singular source whose mechanical motion has no counterpart in the laboratory. This is especially important when computing energy by moving the real charge toward the plate.

How a patterned electrode is smoothed by distance

A grounded plane is one boundary condition. Another laboratory problem specifies a different potential at each position along a plane, using many narrow electrodes separated by small insulating gaps. Model the ideal limit by a prescribed function \(V(x,0)\) in vacuum occupying \(z>0\), uniform along \(y\). Away from charges electrostatic potential obeys \(\partial_x^2V+\partial_z^2V=0\). First impose \(V(x,0)=V_k\cos(kx)\) with \(k>0\) and require decay at large \(z\). Separability suggests \(V(x,z)=V_k\cos(kx)Z(z)\). Then \(Z''-k^2 Z=0\). Discard the growing exponential and set \(Z(0)=1\).

\[V(x,z)=V_k e^{-kz}\cos(kx),\qquad E_z=kV_ke^{-kz}\cos(kx).\]

Each sinusoidal boundary component is transmitted with a factor \(e^{-kz}\). By linearity a general periodic pattern decomposes into such harmonics, each decaying according to its own wavenumber. The constant component fixes the voltage reference or background field separately. Smoothing with height follows directly from Laplace's equation in a source-free half-space. It places a physical limit on how well a distant probe can resolve narrow electrode features even before instrument noise is considered.

Predict a non-sinusoidal surface measurement

Let a fabricated pattern have period \(d=4.00\) mm with a fundamental voltage amplitude of 2.00 V and a third harmonic of 0.600 V, both cosine waves with maxima at \(x=0\). At height \(z=0.500\) mm the attenuation exponents are \((2\pi/d)z=0.785\) and \(3(2\pi/d)z=2.356\). Hence the voltage amplitudes there are \(2.00e^{-0.785}=0.912\) V and \(0.600e^{-2.356}=0.0569\) V. The third-to-first amplitude ratio has fallen from 0.300 at the metal to 0.0624.

A probe sensitive instead to the normal field differentiates in \(z\) and multiplies each voltage component by its wavenumber. At the same height the fundamental contributes \(1.43\times10^3\) V/m and the third harmonic contributes \(268\) V/m at the shared maximum. Reporting a field ratio as though it were a voltage ratio would therefore exaggerate the surviving high spatial frequencies by a factor of three. Inverting the voltage law to recover the fabrication pattern multiplies measurement noise by \(e^{kz}\). Repeating a scan at two known heights allows a direct test: the ratio for each Fourier component should follow its own exponential independently of the unknown electrode voltage. A ground plane placed nearby on the other side would change the far boundary condition and require a different transfer factor.

For the same apparatus, suppose detector noise permits relative voltage harmonics down to one percent of their surface amplitudes. A harmonic remains individually detectable only if \(e^{-kz}>0.01\). At a height of 0.500 mm this requires spatial wavelength greater than \(2\pi z/\ln100=0.682\) mm at that noise level. The limit is a measurement criterion, not a strict cutoff in Laplace's equation: every finite wavelength has a nonzero transfer at finite height. An improved signal-to-noise ratio extends the accessible range only logarithmically. Reducing the probe height can therefore improve recovery more effectively than merely accumulating many additional scans.

Which electrode details reach a remote probe?

Set the potential on an infinite plane to a spatial sinusoid of wavelength \(L/n\), with no free charge above it. Separation in Laplace’s equation gives an exponentially decaying height envelope \(e^{-2\pi n y/L}\) for the bounded solution.

Problem sheet 1 · Fields from charge and metal boundaries

Test the symmetry and boundary-value arguments of Lectures 1 and 2 by predicting fields, energies and induced charges.

Hollow out a charged sphere

An insulating sphere of radius \(2a\) contains uniform charge density \(\rho\) except in a concentric empty cavity of radius \(a\). Derive the radial field in all three regions. Match it at the two boundaries and calculate the potential difference between the cavity and the outer surface for \(\rho=1.0\) µC/m³ and \(a=0.10\) m.

Enclose the remaining volume charge

In the cavity the concentric Gaussian sphere encloses zero charge and spherical symmetry gives zero field. In the charged material the enclosed charge is \(4\pi\rho(r^3-a^3)/3\), so \(E_r=\rho(r-a^3/r^2)/(3\epsilon_0)\). Outside it becomes \(7\rho a^3/(3\epsilon_0 r^2)\). The two expressions agree at \(2a\) and the material field tends to zero at \(a\). Integrating outward gives \(V(a)-V(2a)=\rho a^2/(3\epsilon_0)\), about 376 V. An off-centre cavity would break the symmetry used in this proof.

Place a grounded plane below a charge

A positive 4.0 nC charge sits 50 mm above a large grounded plate. Find the force magnitude and the radius collecting 80% of the negative induced charge. How do both change if the charge doubles at fixed height?

Use the image and integrate the surface charge

The point charge interacts with an image of magnitude 4.0 nC at separation 0.100 m. Its attractive force is \(kq^2/(2a)^2=14.4\) µN. The fraction within a disk is \(1-a/\sqrt{S^2+a^2}\). Requiring 0.8 gives \(S=\sqrt{24}\,a=0.245\) m. Doubling the charge leaves the fractional distribution and this disk radius unchanged, doubles the density at every point and quadruples the force. The comparatively large disk helps explain why finite plates can distort measurements of total induced charge.

Recover a potential in a charge-free gap

Two coaxial conducting cylinders of radii 5 and 20 mm are held at 100 and 0 V in vacuum. Neglect end effects. Solve Laplace’s equation in the gap, predict the reading at 10 mm, and find how far from the inner wall a probe must move to see half the inner-wall field.

Integrate the radial Laplacian

With cylindrical symmetry Laplace’s equation is \((rV')'=0\). One integration gives \(rV'=\text{constant}\) and the second gives a logarithm. Matching the wall potentials yields \(V=100\ln(20\,\mathrm{mm}/r)/\ln4\) V and \(E_r=100/(r\ln4)\) V/m with \(r\) in metres. At 10 mm the potential is 50 V. Since the field is proportional to \(1/r\), moving from 5 to 10 mm, a distance of 5 mm, halves the field. The halfway-potential position is the geometric mean of the two radii.

Chapter 3 · Magnetic flux and material response

Predict how a narrow air gap changes a toroidal coil’s flux, stored energy and inductance from Ampère’s law and the magnetic boundary conditions.

Specify the path taken by the field

A toroidal winding wraps a ring of magnetic material. Large permeability can guide flux around the ring. Cutting a small gap into it can greatly reduce inductance. To understand this change we need to distinguish the field produced by free current from the magnetic response of the material. Write \(\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M)\), where \(\mathbf M\) is magnetic moment per unit volume. For a linear isotropic material, \(\mathbf B=\mu_0\mu_r\mathbf H\). The approximation of a single constant \(\mu_r\) requires operation below strong saturation and negligible hysteresis over the field excursion.

Treat the cross-section as narrow compared with the mean radius and assume tightly distributed turns. Let the material path length be \(\ell_c\), gap length \(g\) and common area \(A\). Neglect stray flux and fringing as a first approximation when the gap is short compared with the transverse dimensions. These choices reduce a spatially varying field to one representative flux through each cross-section. They do not imply equal values of \(H\) in the two materials.

Calculate a coil field without an assumed magnetic path

Ampère's circulation law simplifies when symmetry makes the field uniform along a useful closed path. For a circular wire loop in free space, symmetry does not supply such a path through its aperture. In vacuum a steady thin-wire current instead contributes according to Biot–Savart, \(d\mathbf B=(\mu_0 I/4\pi)d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R/R^3\), where \(\mathbf R\) points from the wire element to the observation point. The formula assumes a complete steady circuit. For a laboratory coil the distant return leads should contribute little to the region being observed.

Take a circular loop of radius \(a\) and observe on its axis at coordinate \(z\). All wire elements share distance \(\sqrt{a^2+z^2}\). Their transverse contributions cancel in opposite pairs. The axial component of \(d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R\) is \(a\,d\ell\), and \(\oint d\ell=2\pi a\). For \(N\) tightly spaced turns the sum yields

\[B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0NIa^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.\]

A 50-turn coil of radius 40.0 mm carrying 0.800 A produces 0.628 mT at the centre. One radius away the ratio is \(2^{-3/2}=0.354\), giving 0.222 mT. Far along the axis the field falls as the inverse cube and is proportional to the magnetic moment \(NI\pi a^2\). Such a falloff is absent from a lumped magnetic circuit with a single prescribed uniform field. Which model is appropriate depends on whether a core guides most of the flux or the winding produces a field through open space.

Apply the two circulation and flux laws

Magnetic flux continuity follows from \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). With negligible leakage, the same \(\Phi=BA\) crosses the core and the gap. Ampère’s law around the ring gives \(H_c\ell_c+H_g g=NI\) for \(N\) turns carrying current \(I\). Substitute \(H_c=B/(\mu_0\mu_r)\) and \(H_g=B/\mu_0\) to find

\[B=\frac{\mu_0NI}{g+\ell_c/\mu_r},\qquad \Phi=\frac{NI}{\ell_c/(\mu_0\mu_r A)+g/(\mu_0 A)}.\]

Each denominator contribution in the flux expression is called a reluctance. The sum follows from the distinct magnetic potential drops along one flux path. A gap as short as \(\ell_c/\mu_r\) accounts for half the total circulation even though it occupies only a small fraction of the length. For a ring of appreciable radial width, the ungapped toroidal field varies as \(1/r\) and flux should be integrated over the cross-section.

Link field energy to an electrical measurement

A coil’s flux linkage is \(N\Phi\) when each turn links the same flux. For a fixed, linear geometry \(L=N\Phi/I\). The voltage needed to raise the current is \(v=L\,dI/dt\) in the passive sign convention, omitting winding resistance. The power delivered to the magnetic field is \(vi\). Integrating as the current grows gives stored energy \(W=LI^2/2\). Equivalently, integrating \(B^2/(2\mu)\) over each region apportions this energy between the gap and the core.

Since the same \(B\) passes through both regions but their permeabilities differ, a unit volume of gap stores \(\mu_r\) times the energy of the same volume of core in this model. The ratio of total gap energy to total core energy is \(g\mu_r/\ell_c\). If permeability depends on field, a small-signal inductance measured around a bias is the derivative \(d(N\Phi)/dI\). It can differ from the ratio \(N\Phi/I\) inferred from a single point.

Estimate a coil with a machined gap

Choose \(N=100\), \(I=0.40\) A, \(\ell_c=0.20\) m, \(\mu_r=2000\), \(A=1.0\) cm² and \(g=0.50\) mm. The effective length in the field denominator is \(0.50+0.10=0.60\) mm. Thus \(B=0.0838\) T, \(\Phi=8.38\) µWb and \(L=2.09\) mH. The gap contributes five sixths of the circulation and stores five sixths of the \(0.168\) mJ total energy.

Closing the gap at the same current would predict \(0.503\) T from the linear law. The calculation itself supplies a reason to consult the real material curve before accepting that number. A measured inductance that departs from the prediction may reflect core saturation, imperfect contact at the joint or fringing that changes the effective gap area. Measuring a small current sweep at several gap widths helps distinguish material response from the geometry of a single fixed gap.

The current-to-flux ratio and the incremental response need not agree near magnetic saturation. If \(\lambda\) is the linked flux, a slow voltage measurement detects \(d\lambda/dt=(d\lambda/dI)\dot I\), so small AC perturbations about a DC operating point report the local slope. In contrast, taking a single ratio \(\lambda/I\) effectively joins that point to the origin. The two are equal in the linear magnetic-circuit model derived above. Deliberately sweeping the bias provides an operational way to find where that model stops describing the core.

Two windings sharing a flux path

A second coil wound on the same magnetic core allows the flux model to predict mutual induction. Choose both positive winding directions so currents create flux around the core in the same sense. In a linear core of reluctance \(\mathcal R\) with negligible leakage, the magnetic flux is \(\Phi=(N_1I_1+N_2I_2)/\mathcal R\). Each turn links that same flux. Multiplying by the number of turns in winding 1 or 2 gives flux linkages \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\), hence

\[\lambda_1=L_1I_1+MI_2,\qquad \lambda_2=MI_1+L_2I_2,\qquad M=\frac{N_1N_2}{\mathcal R},\quad L_i=\frac{N_i^2}{\mathcal R}.\]

Equality of the two mutual coefficients expresses reciprocity in this linear, stationary arrangement. With imperfect flux linkage one often writes \(M=k_c\sqrt{L_1L_2}\). The stored energy \(W=(L_1I_1^2+2MI_1I_2+L_2I_2^2)/2\) must be nonnegative for arbitrary currents, requiring \(|M|\le\sqrt{L_1L_2}\). In the perfectly coupled limit opposing ampere-turns can cancel the common core flux. Leakage and winding resistance then control the small residual voltages seen in real instruments.

Open the secondary so that \(I_2=0\). Faraday's law in the passive terminal-voltage convention gives \(v_i=d\lambda_i/dt=N_i\,d\Phi/dt\). Their voltage ratio is the turns ratio, regardless of sinusoidal amplitude, provided winding drops are negligible. The input current still has to build the necessary flux. Inferring it requires the primary magnetizing inductance, which the turns ratio alone cannot supply.

Set a sinusoidal operating point

Use \(N_1=400\), \(N_2=100\), core area \(A=4.00\) cm² and linear reluctance \(\mathcal R=1.00\times10^6\) A/Wb. Then \(L_1=0.160\) H, \(L_2=0.0100\) H and \(M=0.0400\) H. Drive the primary with 8.00 V RMS at 100 Hz with secondary open. Integrating the sinusoidal voltage gives peak flux \(\Phi_{\rm pk}=\sqrt2V_{\rm rms}/(2\pi fN_1)=45.0\) μWb. The predicted peak flux density is \(0.113\) T, secondary voltage \(2.00\) V RMS and magnetizing current \(V_{\rm rms}/(2\pi fL_1)=79.6\) mA RMS.

At unchanged voltage but half the frequency, voltage ratio is unchanged while peak flux density and current double in this model. A measured current waveform that develops sharp peaks at low frequency can thus signal core saturation before any dramatic change in the nominal turns ratio. A fixed DC terminal voltage on an ideal resistance-free winding would instead keep changing the flux in time, outside the assumed sinusoidal operating condition.

A short electrical pulse can determine the sense of the windings before a sinusoidal test. Mark one terminal on each coil and increase primary current through the first marked terminal. The sign of the measured open-secondary pulse with respect to its marked terminal identifies whether the two positive winding conventions reinforce or oppose one another. Reversing one winding changes the sign of the mutual coefficient and of that pulse but leaves both self-inductances and the magnitude of the voltage ratio unchanged. A sign convention overlooked here becomes important when combining two coils in series, because their mutual induced voltages can either add or subtract.

Winding resistance also introduces voltage even at steady current, so a four-wire resistance measurement helps separate ohmic voltage from linkage changes during a slow pulse.

When does an air gap dominate a magnetic circuit?

A 0.25 m core with uniform cross-section carries 100 ampere-turns. In the linear, negligible-leakage model, the core and air gap share the same \(B\). Their magnetomotive drops add, giving \(B=\mu_0NI/(\ell/\mu_r+g)\).

Chapter 4 · Induction in moving circuits

Separate transformer and motional electromotive forces, then use the mechanical and electrical power balances to check a moving conductor.

Follow a charge around a circuit

A conducting rod slides on two rails in a static magnetic field. Although a flux meter sees the enclosed flux changing, a laboratory observer finds no time-varying applied magnetic field. The measured voltage comes from the force on charges carried with the moving rod. In a stationary loop beside an AC coil the situation is different: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field with nonzero circulation. A consistent treatment includes both mechanisms.

Choose a positive direction around a thin material circuit. The associated surface normal follows the right-hand rule. If a segment moves at velocity \(\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\), the electromotive force is the integral of \((\mathbf E+\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) around it. In the quasistatic circuit description, the drift motion along a thin wire adds no tangential magnetic force. The integral has units of work per charge. Its sign fixes the direction of the current for a passive resistance.

Recover the same fields from different potentials

Zero magnetic divergence permits \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\) in a contractible region, such as the interior of a ball. Combining this with Faraday's law yields \(\nabla\times(\mathbf E+\partial_t\mathbf A)=0\) and hence \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\). The scalar potential alone cannot represent a nonzero electric circulation around a stationary loop. Still, individual values of the potentials are not unique. For any smooth single-valued \(\chi\), take \(\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi\) and \(\phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi\). The curl of a gradient vanishes, and the two new derivative terms in \(\mathbf E'\) cancel, so both fields and the Lorentz force stay fixed.

Inside a long ideal solenoid assume \(\mathbf B=B(t)\mathbf e_z\) and axial symmetry. Choose \(\phi=0\) and \(\mathbf A=B(-y,x,0)/2\). The resulting azimuthal electric field has magnitude \(r|\dot B|/2\). Another allowed choice uses \(\chi=Bxy/2\), which gives \(\mathbf A'=(0,Bx,0)\) and \(\phi'=-\dot Bxy/2\). Computing only \(-\partial_t\mathbf A'\) would misleadingly give a field pointing along the \(y\) axis everywhere. Including the scalar gradient restores \((\dot By,-\dot Bx,0)/2\).

At \(x=0.0400\) m, \(y=0.0300\) m and \(\dot B=0.500\) T/s both calculations give \((0.00750,-0.0100,0)\) V/m. A circle of radius 50.0 mm encloses a flux growing at \(3.93\) mWb/s and has circulation \(-3.93\) mV in the positive sense around \(z\). The measured loop emf is gauge independent. A voltage measured along a specified open path in a changing magnetic field also depends on the routing of its leads.

Differentiate the linked flux

Let the loop boundary move during a short interval. Its magnetic flux changes both because \(\partial_t\mathbf B\) changes across the original surface and because the perimeter sweeps a thin ribbon. Faraday’s field equation relates the first contribution to \(-\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\). The oriented ribbon gives the second contribution \(-\oint(\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\). Adding them gives \(\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt\). The flux rule holds here because the moving contour is the circuit being followed.

Take a rectangular loop in the horizontal plane with uniform field \(B\) upwards. Its right side is a rod of length \(\ell\), moving outwards at speed \(v\) so the upward flux increases. Positive circulation seen from above is counterclockwise. The induced electromotive force is therefore negative and the current is clockwise. Equivalently, on the moving rod \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\) points down the page. This local force gives the same sign as the global flux law.

Close the energy balance

For negligible self-inductance and total resistance \(R\), the magnitude is \(I=B\ell v/R\). A current-carrying rod experiences force \(\mathbf F=I\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B\), opposite to its velocity. Constant-speed operation needs a pulling force \(B\ell I\). Multiplying by speed gives \(P_{\rm mech}=B\ell Iv=I^2R\). The magnetic interaction redirects the transfer. The external pull supplies the energy dissipated as heat.

If the wire has appreciable self-inductance \(L\), its own flux changes when current changes. With constant applied speed the magnitude equation becomes \(L\,dI/dt+RI=B\ell v\) after the initial step in velocity. The current builds with time constant \(L/R\). At each instant the mechanical power balances Joule heating plus \(d(LI^2/2)/dt\). Predicting an immediate full drag force at the moment of starting would neglect the energy required to establish current.

Compare two measurements

Let \(B=0.40\) T, \(\ell=0.25\) m, \(v=3.0\) m/s and \(R=0.60\) Ω. The open-circuit emf magnitude is 0.300 V. Closing the circuit gives steady current 0.500 A and drag force 0.0500 N. Mechanical power and electrical heating both equal 0.150 W. A voltmeter with large internal resistance measures nearly the open-circuit value but draws little current, so it produces little braking. The induced voltage alone does not establish how much energy is transferred.

With \(L=0.12\) H the time constant is 0.20 s. At that time after closing, \(I=0.500(1-e^{-1})=0.316\) A. The mechanical power is 0.0948 W, of which 0.0599 W heats the resistor. The remaining 0.0349 W increases magnetic field energy. If the pulling force is removed entirely, speed can no longer be prescribed; the mechanical inertia must enter an additional equation. Under those conditions a single exponential computed at constant speed describes only part of the response.

A simple reversal checks all sign conventions in the experiment. Reverse the external magnetic field while keeping the prescribed velocity fixed. The induced voltage changes sign, and so does the steady current in a passive resistor. Their product in the electrical power is unchanged. The mechanical force depends on the product of current and magnetic field and therefore still opposes motion. The same circuit can operate as a motor if an independent supply forces current against its generator direction. The supply then contributes to the energy balance as a separate external port.

A changing flux can slow a moving magnet

Consider a small conducting loop of resistance \(R\) and inductance \(L\) fixed beside the path of an external magnet. Let \(x\) be the magnet's displacement and \(\Phi(x)\) its flux through the loop in a specified positive direction. Its motion supplies an electromotive force \(-\Phi'(x)\dot x\). Over a small region approximate the gradient by the constant \(K=\Phi'(x)\) and take the loop's self-inductance to be independent of position. The current equation is \(L\dot I+RI=-K\dot x\). It follows that a static magnet produces no sustained current in a resistive loop, even if its absolute magnetic flux is large.

Multiply the equation by \(I\). Inductive energy changes at rate \(d(LI^2/2)/dt\), and heat appears at rate \(RI^2\). Together these equal \(-KI\dot x\). Mechanical power supplied by the loop to the magnet is therefore \(KI\dot x\), giving electromagnetic force \(F=KI\). At sufficiently low frequency the inductive term is negligible and \(F\simeq-(K^2/R)\dot x\). A magnetic interaction has become an effective viscous damper through the combined action of induction and resistance.

For harmonic velocity with complex amplitude \(u\), the force amplitude is \(-K^2u/(R+i\omega L)\). Its component opposing velocity has effective damping \(c_{\rm em}=K^2R/(R^2+\omega^2L^2)\). The imaginary component returns stored energy during part of each cycle. Equating cycle-mean mechanical loss to mean resistor heating gives another way to verify which component really dissipates.

Compare motion below and above the electrical corner

Near a chosen working point let \(K=0.200\) Wb/m, \(R=2.00\) Ω, \(L=20.0\) mH and peak speed \(0.100\) m/s. The corner frequency in angular units is \(R/L=100\) rad/s. At \(\omega=10.0\) rad/s the induced emf amplitude is \(0.0200\) V and current amplitude is \(0.0200/\sqrt{2.00^2+0.200^2}=9.95\) mA. The average heat is \(RI_{\rm pk}^2/2=99.0\) μW. The low-frequency approximation gives \(K^2/R=0.0200\) N s/m and predicts \(100\) μW, sufficiently close here.

At \(200\) rad/s the same velocity amplitude gives current \(4.47\) mA and heat \(20.0\) μW. The smaller loss despite unchanged peak speed comes from a greater fraction of the emf supporting inductive voltage. At fixed nonzero frequency the limit \(R\to0\) eliminates dissipation while retaining induced current. The apparent divergence of the low-frequency approximation as resistance tends to zero is resolved because its assumption \(\omega L\ll R\) then fails. Measuring both force phase and heating provides independent tests of this simple localized flux model.

The approximation of constant flux gradient can itself be checked experimentally. Translate the magnet slowly through several known positions while measuring open-circuit emf and speed. Dividing the signed emf by the signed speed estimates the local gradient, without needing an absolute calibration of the magnet’s total flux. If the gradient changes sign across the motion, the induced current can have a distorted waveform even under perfectly sinusoidal displacement. The small-amplitude force response derived here should then be measured about a fixed mean position, or the full position-dependent gradient should be retained in the coupled electrical and mechanical equations.

Determine a gap from an inductance measurement

A 200-turn toroidal coil has cross-section 80 mm², core length 0.16 m and relative permeability 1600. A low-current test gives \(L=8.0\) mH. Infer the gap and determine the fraction of magnetic energy held there. State one reason this inference can misestimate the physical gap.

Convert the inductance to an effective length

For uniform area and negligible leakage, \(L=\mu_0N^2A/(g+\ell_c/\mu_r)\). The inferred effective length is \(0.503\) mm, of which 0.100 mm is contributed by the core. The gap is about 0.403 mm and the energy fraction is \(0.403/0.503=0.801\). Fringing gives the gap an effective area larger than the core area. Variations in actual incremental permeability would likewise bias a gap determined solely from an inductance reading.

Drop a sliding rod under gravity

Two vertical rails and their conducting top link form a closed circuit through a sliding rod of mass 0.020 kg and length 0.25 m. A uniform 0.50 T field is normal to the loop. The total resistance is 0.10 Ω. Derive the speed from rest, find the terminal speed and compare electrical power with gravitational power there. Neglect inductance and friction.

Solve the coupled force and current laws

The current magnitude is \(B\ell v/R\), giving upward drag \(B^2\ell^2v/R\). Thus \(m\dot v=mg-B^2\ell^2v/R\), with solution \(v=v_t(1-e^{-t/\tau})\), \(v_t=mgR/(B^2\ell^2)=1.26\) m/s and \(\tau=mR/(B^2\ell^2)=0.128\) s. At terminal speed the current is 1.57 A and heating is 0.246 W, matching \(mgv_t\). Changing resistance changes the terminal speed; the mechanically available power per second therefore also changes.

Drive a stationary loop with a changing field

A one-turn circular loop of area 0.012 m² and resistance 3.0 Ω lies normal to \(B(t)=0.080\cos(2\pi 50t)\) T. Find the peak emf, mean resistive power and total charge passing a reference point during a half-cycle from maximum positive to maximum negative flux. What changes if a second identical turn is added in series?

Distinguish derivative and integrated flux

The peak emf is \(A B_0\omega=0.302\) V. With negligible inductance, mean power is \(\mathcal E_0^2/(2R)=15.2\) mW. Integrating \(I=-\dot\Phi/R\) gives signed transported charge \(2AB_0/R=0.640\) mC in the selected positive direction. Two identical series turns double both the emf and the resistance. Current and half-cycle charge remain the same, while total mean power doubles. This assumes the same field threads each turn.

Choose the separation of a uniform-field coil pair

Place two identical coaxial coils at \(z=\pm d/2\) with the same current orientation. Use the single-coil axial field to choose \(d\) so that both the first and second derivatives vanish at the midpoint. Find its field for 100 turns per coil, radius 0.100 m and current 0.500 A.

Differentiate the axial field twice

Reflection symmetry makes the first derivative zero for every spacing. A single factor \((a^2+z^2)^{-3/2}\) has second derivative \(3(4z^2-a^2)/(a^2+z^2)^{7/2}\). Both coils contribute zero curvature at the midpoint if \(d/2=a/2\), hence \(d=a\). Adding their fields gives \(B(0)=(4/5)^{3/2}\mu_0NI/a=0.450\) mT. The first surviving midpoint variation is then fourth order in displacement.

In a region of zero physical fields take \(\chi=sxt\) with \(s=0.300\) V/m. Transform from zero potentials, recover the fields, and determine the false acceleration inferred for a charge-to-mass ratio of \(2.00\) C/kg if the transformed scalar potential is forgotten.

Chapter 5 · Electromagnetic waves and energy flux

Derive the electromagnetic energy balance and use it to infer field amplitudes and transmitted energy from a measured wave intensity.

Charge conservation requires a changing field term

Ampère’s magnetostatic law relates magnetic circulation to current. A surface passing through a wire detects a conduction current, whereas a second spanning surface between the plates of a charging capacitor may intercept none. The evolving electric field supplies the missing current. In vacuum, \(\nabla\times\mathbf B/\mu_0=\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E\). Taking the divergence of both sides and using Gauss’s electric law gives \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho=0\), the continuity equation for charge.

Together with Faraday’s law and the two divergence laws, this term permits waves even in a region containing no material charges. In source-free vacuum take the curl of \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B\). Use \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E\) and substitute the Maxwell–Ampère law. The result is \(\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_{tt}\mathbf E\). The coefficient sets \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}\).

Read a propagating field

Take a sinusoidal electric field \(\mathbf E=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf x}\). The wave equation gives \(\omega=ck\), and Faraday’s law fixes \(\mathbf B=(E_0/c)\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf y}\). The divergence equations require these plane-wave fields to be transverse. Their relative orientation selects the positive direction of travel. Flipping the sign of the magnetic field alone with the same electric waveform would reverse the local energy flow but would violate Faraday’s law for this chosen propagation phase.

Wave speed and field amplitude answer different questions. A stronger sinusoidal source increases the energy in a given volume without changing its speed through linear vacuum. Changing to a lossless homogeneous nondispersive material changes the propagation speed to \(1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\) and the impedance relating electric and magnetic fields to \(\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}\). Near a source, the simple wave relation between amplitudes need not hold.

Identify work and transport locally

Dot the Maxwell–Ampère equation with \(\mathbf E\) and Faraday’s law with \(\mathbf B/\mu_0\). Use the identity \(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\) to combine them. Rearranging gives Poynting’s theorem

\[\partial_t u+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E,\qquad u=\frac{\epsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0},\quad \mathbf S=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{\mu_0}.\]

Integrated over a volume, the equation says that net incoming electromagnetic power increases stored field energy or performs work on matter. In a vacuum plane wave the electric and magnetic energy densities are equal and \(\mathbf S=cu\,\hat{\mathbf z}\). In a standing wave counterpropagating energy flows can cancel on average while local fields and stored energy remain large. A detector of heating and a point probe of electric amplitude need not then respond in the same way.

Convert intensity into field and pulse energy

A broad, normally incident vacuum wave has mean intensity 2.0 W/m². For a sinusoid the cycle mean of \(\cos^2\) is one half. Therefore \(\langle S\rangle=E_0^2/(2Z_{\rm vac})\) with \(Z_{\rm vac}=\mu_0c\simeq377\) Ω. The electric peak is 38.8 V/m and the magnetic peak is \(1.30\times10^{-7}\) T. Using the RMS electric field instead gives \(\langle S\rangle=E_{\rm rms}^2/Z_{\rm vac}\). Mixing a peak-valued field with that RMS formula would overestimate the power by a factor of two.

An absorbing disk of area 0.010 m² intercepts a 3.0 ms pulse with this mean intensity throughout its flat top. Neglect edge effects and switching intervals. The energy received is \(2.0(0.010)(0.0030)=60\) µJ. In the lossless TEM line, the same flux principle becomes the circuit power \(VI\). The coaxial-line experiment reports \(Z_0=47.45\) Ω for the baseline cross-section. A 1 V RMS forward sinusoid there carries about 21.1 mW. A 1 V peak sinusoid carries half that mean power, which gives a direct check on the convention used by a voltage probe.

For a travelling plane-wave pulse, integrate the instantaneous Poynting flux over a plane normal to propagation and over the time during which the pulse crosses. The result is the total transported energy. Equivalently integrate field energy density throughout the pulse volume at one instant. The length of the pulse is its speed multiplied by its duration, so the two constructions agree. This provides a check when a detector reports time-averaged irradiance but an independent instrument reports energy per pulse; the averaging interval and illuminated area enter explicitly.

Which observer can see a purely magnetic field?

A charged particle responds to \(q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\) in one inertial frame. A frame moving alongside the particle initially sees no magnetic part of the force on it, so the assignment of a field as electric or magnetic cannot be frame independent. For a frame moving at velocity \(\mathbf u\) relative to the laboratory, let parallel and perpendicular denote components relative to \(\mathbf u\). The parallel fields remain unchanged. Transforming the electromagnetic field tensor gives the perpendicular components in SI units,

\[\mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E_\perp+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B),\qquad \mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf B_\perp-\mathbf u\times\mathbf E/c^2),\quad \gamma=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.\]

For crossed fields \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0\), choose \(\mathbf u=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). The vector triple product then gives \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B=-\mathbf E\). If \(E<cB\), this is an admissible subluminal boost and the moving observer sees \(\mathbf E'=0\). The remaining magnetic field has magnitude \(\sqrt{B^2-E^2/c^2}\). A charge initially moving at \(\mathbf u\) in the laboratory has zero Lorentz force, in agreement with a stationary charge in the new frame.

Let \(\mathbf E=6.00\) MV/m along \(x\) and \(\mathbf B=0.0500\) T along \(z\). The required velocity is \(1.20\times10^8\) m/s along negative \(y\), about \(0.400c\). The moving frame measures \(B'=0.0458\) T. At \(E=cB\) the required speed would be \(c\) and no inertial rest frame eliminates either field. This is the relation for a vacuum plane wave. Its electromagnetic field cannot be removed by trying to travel beside the wave.

Locate the energy entering a resistor

A steady resistor has constant fields and still receives a continuous supply of energy. Model a long straight cylindrical wire of radius \(a\), conductivity \(\sigma\) and resistive length \(\ell\). A DC current \(I\) flows in the positive axial direction. Assume a uniform current density, negligible end effects and a coaxial return sufficiently far away that the magnetic field immediately outside the wire has cylindrical symmetry. The internal axial electric field is \(E_z=I/(\sigma\pi a^2)\). Continuity of tangential electric field gives the same axial value immediately outside its lateral surface.

Ampère's law fixes the azimuthal magnetic field there at \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi a)\). Since \(\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_\phi=-\mathbf e_r\), the Poynting flux points radially inward. Multiplying its magnitude by the lateral area gives

\[P_{\rm in}=E_zH_\phi(2\pi a\ell)=\frac{I^2\ell}{\sigma\pi a^2}=I^2R .\]

The result is the familiar circuit power but obtained from fields on the surrounding surface. It accords with the local energy theorem: field energy within the chosen volume is steady while the inward flux supplies the material work \(\int\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\,dV\). A radial electrostatic field can also be present because of surface charge establishing a voltage relative to the return. It produces longitudinal Poynting transport in the gap. Its direction and magnitude depend on the wider circuit, whereas the inward component above accounts for the resistive loss in the selected segment.

Audit the flux at different radii

Use \(\ell=1.00\) m, \(a=0.500\) mm, \(\sigma=1.00\times10^6\) S/m and \(I=1.00\) A. The cross-sectional area is \(7.85\times10^{-7}\) m² and the resistance is \(1.273\) Ω. The axial electric field is \(1.273\) V/m and the surface magnetic field is \(318\) A/m. Their product gives an inward energy flux density of \(405\) W/m². Integrating over \(2\pi a\ell=0.00314\) m² yields \(1.27\) W, matching the measured electrical input.

Now evaluate the field on an imaginary cylinder of radius \(r=a/2\) inside the wire. Uniform current density encloses only \(I/4\). Its magnetic field is half the surface value, and its area is also halved. The integrated inward power is therefore one quarter, \(0.318\) W. It supplies precisely the heat produced in one quarter of the wire volume. As \(r\) tends to zero the local inward flux vanishes with \(r\) while volumetric heating density remains finite. Taking the divergence in cylindrical coordinates reconciles these limits. A skin-effect current profile at high frequency would invalidate the uniform-field calculation and redistribute both heating and energy input toward the surface.

Measured current and terminal voltage determine the inward energy rate without measuring either field directly, but they cannot locate the path of the incoming energy elsewhere in the circuit. To calculate that path one can choose a coaxial geometry with known conductor and return potentials. The radial field in the dielectric then combines with the azimuthal magnetic field to give longitudinal flux toward the load. Approaching the resistive segment, part of this flux turns inward as demonstrated above. Choosing a different return path may change the exterior pattern substantially while preserving the same resistance and the same total power budget.

Carry energy from source to load through a coaxial gap

The same energy theorem closes the circuit picture if the wire is the inner conductor of a coaxial delivery line. In the uniform, low-loss part take inner radius \(a\), return-conductor inner radius \(b\), potential difference \(V\) and equal opposing currents of magnitude \(I\). A cylindrical Gaussian surface gives radial field \(E_r=V/[r\ln(b/a)]\) in the dielectric. Ampère's law gives \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi r)\). The axial Poynting flux density is therefore proportional to \(1/r^2\). Integrate over the dielectric annulus,

\[P_z=\int_a^b E_rH_\phi\,2\pi r\,dr=\frac{VI}{\ln(b/a)}\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r}=VI.\]

The familiar terminal formula has emerged by integrating power moving through the dielectric. It remains compatible with power crossing the lateral surface into a resistor at the line's end. The outer return conductor also matters: it sets the radial electric field geometry and provides the return current defining where the external magnetic field cancels in the coaxial model.

Take \(V=10.0\) V, \(I=0.200\) A, \(a=1.00\) mm and \(b=4.00\) mm. Delivered power is 2.00 W. The fraction flowing between \(a\) and any intermediate radius \(r\) is \(\ln(r/a)/\ln(b/a)\). At \(r=2.00\) mm this is one half. The outer part of the gap has greater cross-sectional area yet carries the other half because flux density falls outward. If the inner conductor develops finite resistance per length \(R'\) and leakage is negligible, a small segment loses \(I^2R'\,dz\) of longitudinal power. The longitudinal difference equals its radial inward flux, providing a local field interpretation of the ordinary circuit voltage drop.

Chapter 6 · Radiation from a small electric dipole

Relate a time-dependent electric dipole moment to its radiation pattern, total power and the conditions under which a far-field detector measures them.

From potentials to a distant detector

An alternating current in a short conductor moves positive and negative charge relative to one another. A current probe at the source and a field probe well outside it measure different aspects of the same charge motion. Consider a localised neutral source of size \(d\), oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\) in vacuum. Assume \(d\ll\lambda=2\pi c/\omega\) so that phase changes across the source are small. The leading electric multipole is its dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho(\mathbf r',t)d^3r'\).

A signal emitted at source time \(t_r=t-r/c\) reaches an observation point a distance \(r\) away at time \(t\). Keeping the leading distant term of the retarded vector potential gives \(\mathbf A\simeq\mu_0\int\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t_r)d^3r'/(4\pi r)\). By charge continuity the current integral equals \(\dot{\mathbf p}\). One sees this by differentiating the integral defining a component of \(\mathbf p\), substituting \(-\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J\) for \(\partial_t\rho\) and integrating by parts. No surface term remains for a localised source whose current vanishes at the enclosing boundary.

Extract the transverse radiation field

Curling the potential differentiates both \(1/r\) and the retarded time. The former produces a term falling as \(1/r^2\). Differentiating \(t_r\) produces a term proportional to \(\ddot{\mathbf p}/(cr)\), which dominates sufficiently far away. The resulting magnetic field is transverse to the direction of observation. The radiative electric field can be written as the transverse vector \(\hat{\mathbf r}\times(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}(t_r))/(4\pi\epsilon_0 c^2r)\). The magnetic field is \(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf E/c\).

Take \(\mathbf p=p_0\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf z}\). The electric amplitude is proportional to \(\omega^2p_0\sin\theta/r\), where \(\theta\) is the angle to the dipole axis. Along the axis no transverse component survives. In the equatorial plane the radiation is strongest. These conclusions concern the energy-carrying far field. The electrostatic dipole field and the induction field decay with higher inverse powers of radius and may dominate a probe whose distance is small on the wavelength scale.

Integrate over a sphere

Use the plane-wave expression for the cycle-averaged Poynting flux locally in the far zone. Multiplying by \(r^2\) gives the power per unit solid angle. Integration uses \(d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\) and \(\int \sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3\). For a peak dipole moment the result is

\[\frac{d\langle P\rangle}{d\Omega}=\frac{\omega^4 p_0^2}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}\sin^2\theta,\qquad \langle P\rangle=\frac{\omega^4p_0^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.\]

The sphere has more area at larger radius while the intensity falls as \(r^{-2}\), so total power is independent of the chosen far-field radius. At fixed dipole moment, doubling frequency increases the radiated power sixteenfold. If the experiment instead fixes the amplitude of current through a short element, the resulting dipole moment falls with increasing frequency because current is the time derivative of charge. The change in what is controlled matters.

Check an angular scan

A calibrated small dipole radiates 3.0 mW in total. At \(r=10\) m in its far zone, the equatorial mean intensity is \(3\langle P\rangle/(8\pi r^2)=3.58\) µW/m². At 30° from the axis it is one quarter of that value, 0.895 µW/m². Treating the source as isotropic would conceal both the axial null and the enhanced equatorial intensity. A dipole probe receiving the field must also be correctly polarised to measure the predicted amplitude.

Move a correctly oriented equatorial probe from 10 to 20 m. Its measured electric amplitude should halve and its measured power per area should fall by four. Failure of this scaling could indicate reflections, inadequate distance from the source or a directional response in the probe. Combining this distance test with an angular scan distinguishes a radiation measurement from a mapping of the near electrostatic field.

An oscillating electric dipole with fixed orientation emits linearly polarized radiation at a generic far-field point. The electric field points along the projection of the dipole axis onto the plane transverse to the line of sight. Turning a linear receiving antenna through a right angle can therefore remove its signal even at a location of maximum emitted intensity. To distinguish antenna misalignment from a source-pattern null, repeat the scan with two orthogonal receiving orientations and add the received powers.

Radiation from a driven bound charge

A dipole need not be fed by a wire. An incident optical field can drive a bound charge, which reradiates part of the incident power. Model a charge of magnitude \(q\) and effective mass \(m\) attached to a harmonic restoring force \(-m\omega_0^2x\). Expose it to a spatially uniform sinusoidal electric field of peak amplitude \(E_0\) at angular frequency \(\omega\). Uniformity here requires the oscillator size to be small compared with the wavelength. Include linear damping rate \(\gamma\) through the term \(m\gamma\dot x\). Steady harmonic response gives a dipole amplitude

\[|p|=\frac{q^2E_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}} .\]

The radiated power of a sinusoidal electric dipole with peak moment \(p\) is \(\omega^4|p|^2/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)\). Divide by the incident plane-wave intensity \(\epsilon_0c E_0^2/2\) to obtain a scattering cross section. Well below resonance, where the restoring term dominates, define the static polarizability \(\alpha_0=q^2/(m\omega_0^2)\) in SI units. Then

\[\sigma_{\rm sc}\simeq \frac{k^4}{6\pi}\left|\frac{\alpha_0}{\epsilon_0}\right|^2,\qquad k=\omega/c.\]

The strong fourth-power dependence originates in the dipole's radiating acceleration. The amplitude of the induced moment varies little in the low-frequency regime, but twice differentiating it in time produces a field amplitude proportional to \(\omega^2\). Squaring that field gives the fourth power. Near an internal resonance the polarizability itself varies rapidly and this simple colour dependence no longer applies.

Predict the ratio and magnitude of scattered light

Suppose a small isotropic object has \(\alpha_0/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=1.00\times10^{-30}\) m³, and both 450 nm and 650 nm light are far from its absorption bands. At 450 nm, \(k=1.40\times10^7\) m⁻¹. Substitution gives \(\sigma_{\rm sc}= (8\pi/3)k^4(10^{-30}\,{\rm m^3})^2\simeq3.18\times10^{-31}\) m². At 650 nm the cross section is smaller by \((650/450)^4=4.35\), about \(7.32\times10^{-32}\) m². Equal incident irradiances therefore give that same ratio of total scattered powers.

The angular pattern remains dipolar, so a small detector also needs a known collection angle and polarization geometry. At a direction parallel to the incident electric polarization the ideal driven dipole has a null, irrespective of wavelength. A distribution of particle sizes with some dimensions comparable to wavelength can fill such a null and alter the colour ratio. Comparing angular scans and wavelength scans separates these two experimental predictions rather than attributing every departure to a changed number of scatterers.

An ideal absorption-free dielectric particle still removes some power from the original forward beam by redirecting radiation. For a dilute ensemble one might infer scattering cross section from the weak reduction in collimated transmitted power, provided light scattered into the detector aperture is excluded. If the material also absorbs, the reduction measures extinction and is larger than scattering alone. Measuring total scattered power and forward-beam attenuation separately then distinguishes reradiated energy from internal dissipation. Comparing measurements at several concentrations checks whether neighbouring objects remain independent scatterers or instead interfere appreciably across the ensemble.

The polarizability here is in SI units. Tabulated polarizability volumes must first be multiplied by \(4\pi\epsilon_0\) before substitution in the SI cross-section expression.

Chapter 7 · Multipole fields

Predict the leading far field of a localized charge distribution and use symmetry to decide how rapidly it decays.

Reading a charge distribution from far away

The radiation of an oscillating dipole was described through its time-dependent moment. A static distribution also has a hierarchy of moments. Consider fixed charges \(q_a\) inside a sphere of radius \(a\) and an observation point \(\mathbf r=r\mathbf n\) in vacuum. Measuring the complete charge map from far away would require extraordinary precision. Two distributions sharing their first few moments produce nearly the same voltage there. We can quantify which differences survive.

Superposition gives the exact potential as a sum of \(q_a/(4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_a|)\). Factor out \(r\) and expand \((1+x)^{-1/2}\) with \(x=-2\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a/r+r_a^2/r^2\). Keep both the first-order contribution from \(r_a^2\) and the squared contribution from \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a\). They have the same order in source size.

\[\frac{1}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_a|}=\frac1r+\frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a}{r^2}+\frac{3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a)^2-r_a^2}{2r^3}+O(a^3/r^4).\]

The total charge multiplies the first term. The second depends on the dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\sum q_a\mathbf r_a\). Translation of the coordinate origin by \(\mathbf d\) changes the dipole to \(\mathbf p-Q\mathbf d\). A neutral system therefore has an origin-independent dipole. When charge and dipole vanish, the leading quadrupole is likewise independent of the chosen origin.

From potential to a vector measurement

For an axial dipole the leading potential is \(p\cos\theta/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). A probe measures a force per unit charge, requiring both radial and angular derivatives. The angular gradient contains \(1/r\) because the physical arc length is \(r\,d\theta\). This produces

\[E_r=\frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3},\qquad E_\theta=\frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3}.\]

A voltage reading of zero on the equatorial plane thus coexists with a nonzero transverse field. The vector result also supplies a laboratory diagnostic. Doubling distance reduces an ideal dipole field by eight, while a residual net charge produces a fourfold reduction. A small net charge can dominate sufficiently far from a nominally neutral device.

A quadrupole on the bench

Put \(+1.0\) nC at \(z=\pm1.0\) cm and \(-2.0\) nC at the origin. Charge, dipole and all odd moments vanish. On the positive axis the exact result simplifies before expansion,

\[V(z)=kq\left(\frac1{z-a}+\frac1{z+a}-\frac2z\right)=\frac{2kqa^2}{z(z^2-a^2)},\qquad z>a .\]

At \(z=0.100\) m the leading term \(2kqa^2/z^3\) gives \(1.798\) V. The exact value is \(1.816\) V, so the relative error referred to the exact value is \(a^2/z^2=1.0\%\). In the equatorial plane the same arrangement gives \(2kq[1/\sqrt{r^2+a^2}-1/r]\), with a negative leading voltage of half the axial magnitude. Along directions where the angular factor of a leading moment vanishes, a relative-error estimate based on that term alone loses meaning. Compare absolute errors there or retain the next surviving moment.

A simple way to detect an experimental origin error is to compare voltages on opposite sides at equal nominal distances. For a pure axial dipole these voltages have equal magnitude and opposite signs. Translating either probe along the axis changes the magnitude because of the radial gradient. An apparent even contribution can then be caused by mismatched distances as easily as by a physical quadrupole. Reverse the source orientation while leaving the two probes fixed to separate a geometric offset from a source component that retains its sign.

A field gradient exerts a net force on a neutral dipole

Far-field multipole moments also determine how a localized source responds to an imposed field that varies little across it. Consider two charges \(\pm q\) separated by a small vector \(\mathbf d\) pointing from negative to positive charge. Their dipole moment is \(\mathbf p=q\mathbf d\). Expand the external potential around their midpoint \(\mathbf R\). The constant contributions cancel, leaving interaction energy \(q[V(\mathbf R+\mathbf d/2)-V(\mathbf R-\mathbf d/2)]\simeq-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E(\mathbf R)\). The next terms are negligible when the external field varies on a scale much longer than the separation.

For a fixed dipole orientation the force follows by taking the gradient with respect to the midpoint. In components \(F_i=p_j\partial_i E_j\). The electrostatic field is curl free, so this equals \(p_j\partial_jE_i\), the difference of the forces on the two ends obtained by direct expansion. A uniform field consequently exerts torque \(\mathbf p\times\mathbf E\) but no net force on a fixed neutral dipole.

An induced dipole changes its magnitude as the field is raised. For a linear polarizable object with \(\mathbf p=\alpha\mathbf E\), the work integrated over field buildup gives energy \(U=-\alpha E^2/2\). The one-half also follows by adding the internal energy cost \(p^2/(2\alpha)\) to the coupling \(-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E\) and minimizing over \(\mathbf p\). The force is then \(\mathbf F=(\alpha/2)\nabla E^2\) for real constant \(\alpha\) in a static field. Simply substituting \(\alpha E\) into the permanent-dipole energy before differentiating would double the answer.

Predict a force on a dielectric bead

A neutral bead in vacuum has static polarizability \(\alpha=4.00\times10^{-21}\) C m²/V. Locally on a symmetry axis the field is \(E_z=2.00\times10^4\) V/m and its axial gradient is \(3.00\times10^6\) V/m². Assume the bead is much smaller than the variation length \(E/|\partial_zE|=6.67\) mm and other field components vanish on that axis. The induced moment is \(8.00\times10^{-17}\) C m. Its axial force is \(\alpha E_z\partial_z E_z=2.40\times10^{-10}\) N, directed toward increasing squared field.

If the field amplitude of the electrodes is doubled without changing their geometry, both field and gradient double, so the force rises fourfold. A fixed, mechanically aligned permanent dipole would show only a twofold force increase. This scaling and a reversal of electrode polarity offer practical discrimination between the two models. Near a field zero an induced dipole and its leading force both vanish; finite-size and higher-moment effects must be considered if a measurable force persists there.

The validity condition on field gradients can be phrased in terms of an observable length scale for a real bead. Moving it by a fraction of a millimetre along the axis and measuring its induced moment probes the local derivative of field. A moment tracking field linearly while force follows its squared-field gradient supports the same polarizability in both tests. If the bead size becomes comparable to the variation length, different parts of it experience different applied fields. Higher induced moments and local field distortion then enter the force balance, so one scalar dipole polarizability is no longer sufficient.

Chapter 8 · Polarization of a dielectric sphere

Match electrostatic fields at a curved interface and infer a material’s induced dipole from its exterior potential.

An inclusion in a uniform field

Place a homogeneous dielectric sphere of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon\) in vacuum. Distant electrodes produce a static uniform field \(E_0\mathbf e_z\) before insertion. Take the dielectric to be linear, isotropic and free of mobile charge. Bound charge can develop at its surface even though the object is electrically neutral. The monopole measurement from the previous lecture consequently misses the material response. The dipole moment provides a measurable alternative.

In each constant-permittivity region Gauss’s law for \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) give Laplace’s equation. The background potential is \(-E_0r\cos\theta\), an angular pattern of degree one. A spherical interface does not couple different angular degrees. The allowed radial functions for this degree are \(r\) and \(r^{-2}\). Finiteness at the centre prohibits a singular interior term. Matching the imposed field far away fixes the growing exterior term.

\[V_{\rm in}=-Ar\cos\theta,\qquad V_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2.\]

Solve at the surface

Continuity of voltage gives \(A=E_0-B/a^3\). It also guarantees continuity of tangential field, as a tangential displacement compares points along the same surface potential. Normal displacement is continuous in the absence of free surface charge. Differentiating radially changes the sign and supplies a factor of two for the decaying term, so \(\epsilon A=\epsilon_0(E_0+2B/a^3)\). Elimination gives

\[A=\frac{3\epsilon_0}{\epsilon+2\epsilon_0}E_0,\qquad B=a^3\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_0}{\epsilon+2\epsilon_0}E_0.\]

The uniform internal field polarizes the sphere along the field direction. Its bound surface charge is \(\sigma_b=(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)A\cos\theta\). Opposite hemispheres cancel in total charge. Their separation gives an induced dipole \(p=4\pi\epsilon_0B\). Equivalently integrate the uniform polarization over the volume. Both constructions give the same dipole, one from exterior voltage and one from microscopic charge displacement. When \(\epsilon=\epsilon_0\) the exterior perturbation vanishes. A very large positive permittivity suppresses the internal field and approaches the conductor result.

Predict a voltage difference

Take \(a=2.00\) mm, \(\epsilon=4\epsilon_0\) and \(E_0=30.0\) kV/m. The internal field is \(15.0\) kV/m, and \(B=1.20\times10^{-4}\) V m². Multiplication by \(4\pi\epsilon_0\) gives \(p=1.34\times10^{-14}\) C m. At an axial probe 10.0 mm from the centre the sphere increases voltage relative to the uniform-field baseline by \(B/r^2=1.20\) V. The opposite axial probe sees a shift of \(-1.20\) V. A differential measurement doubles the signal and removes a common voltage offset.

For the assumed uniform applied field the exterior dipole solution is exact at every point outside the sphere. A nearby field-producing electrode can spoil that simplification. A field that varies across the particle contains higher angular orders in its local expansion and their induced responses survive at the surface. In an experiment, repeating the measurement with a smaller sphere tests the predicted \(a^3\) law while reducing sensitivity to field curvature.

The assumption of real, constant permittivity is experimentally testable. A slowly varying applied field should give a dipole that follows the field with negligible lag. If the measured signal shifts in phase while frequency is raised, the response must be described by a frequency-dependent complex polarization. A static voltage estimate alone cannot supply that extra timescale.

Build an effective permittivity from many inclusions

A single dielectric sphere in a smooth applied field develops a dipole. A dilute suspension of such spheres can therefore alter the bulk capacitance of a sample. Suppose identical spheres of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon_i\) occupy a small fraction \(f\) of a matrix with permittivity \(\epsilon_m\). The matrix is homogeneous, both materials are isotropic, and no conducting path or interfacial free charge is present. Each sphere sees nearly the matrix field when it is far from all neighbours. The single-sphere boundary calculation gives an excess dipole \(p=4\pi\epsilon_m a^3 b E_m\), with \(b=(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_m)/(\epsilon_i+2\epsilon_m)\).

The number of inclusions per unit sample volume is \(n=f/(4\pi a^3/3)\). At first order the excess polarization density is \(np=3f\epsilon_m b E_m\). The difference between \(E_m\) and the sample-average field is itself first order in \(f\), so replacing one by the other in this correction changes only higher-order terms. Comparing average displacement with \(\epsilon_{\rm eff}E\) gives

\[\epsilon_{\rm eff}=\epsilon_m[1+3fb+O(f^2)] .\]

This is a dilute effective-medium prediction. The absolute radius cancels at fixed volume fraction because each dipole scales as \(a^3\) while number density scales as \(a^{-3}\). The cancellation requires particles small compared with the scale of the imposed field. Aggregation, closely spaced pairs and a large occupied fraction can change local fields and produce a nonlinear dependence on concentration.

Measure a composite using two capacitor readings

A parallel-plate measurement cell has electrode area \(25.0\) cm² and gap 1.00 mm. Filling it with matrix alone gives \(\epsilon_m=2.00\epsilon_0\), hence \(C_m=44.3\) pF after subtraction of stray capacitance. Disperse spheres with \(\epsilon_i=8.00\epsilon_0\) at \(f=0.0200\). The contrast factor is \((8-2)/(8+4)=0.500\). Consequently \(\epsilon_{\rm eff}/\epsilon_m\simeq1+3(0.0200)(0.500)=1.030\). The expected reading is \(45.6\) pF, an increase of approximately \(1.33\) pF.

A second sample with the same volume fraction but twice the sphere radius should give the same leading result. A systematic change might indicate settling, aggregation, finite-gap effects or frequency-dependent interface polarization. In the equal-permittivity limit the contrast factor vanishes and no capacitive signal should remain. In the high-permittivity limit \(b\) approaches one, so the fractional increase saturates at approximately \(3f\) to first order. This bounded prediction gives a useful check when a simple arithmetic mixing rule appears to predict an arbitrarily large capacitance increase from an arbitrarily dilute concentration.

The hypothesis that the suspension is dilute can be tested without a microscopic field probe. Make a concentration series by adding material with known volume at fixed particle size. The predicted initial slope of relative capacitance versus volume fraction for the numerical pair is \(3b=1.50\). A linear fit restricted to the lowest concentrations estimates the contrast parameter and hence the inclusion permittivity if the matrix is known. Strong curvature at higher concentration then measures the breakdown of the independent-inclusion approximation. Extrapolating that curvature back as a new single-particle dielectric constant would blend collective and material effects.

Chapter 9 · Charge relaxation and magnetic diffusion

Distinguish two conductor timescales and estimate how deeply an alternating magnetic field penetrates a solid.

What relaxes when a field is applied?

A metal plate inserted in a static electric field rapidly rearranges its free surface charge. A metal plate subjected to a changing magnetic field can carry eddy currents for an appreciable time. The two observations concern different field equations. Model a uniform material using \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\), treating the coefficients as constants over the frequencies used.

Free charge conservation gives \(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\). Within the uniform bulk, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon\) gives \(\partial_t\rho=-(\sigma/\epsilon)\rho\). A prepared bulk charge perturbation therefore decays exponentially with characteristic time \(\tau_q=\epsilon/\sigma\). The derivation says nothing about the disappearance of total charge from an isolated body. Charge can move to its boundary, where the bulk equation with constant coefficients across the interface no longer applies. Extremely short formal times in good metals also probe frequencies beyond the constant-conductivity model.

Curl fields obey a different evolution

Use Ampère’s law without displacement current when \(\omega\epsilon\ll\sigma\) and the frequency lies within the material model. Combining \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\sigma\mathbf E\) with Faraday’s law gives \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=-\mu\sigma\partial_t\mathbf B\). Because magnetic flux has zero divergence, the left-hand side is \(-\nabla^2\mathbf B\). Thus

\[\partial_t\mathbf B=D_m\nabla^2\mathbf B,\qquad D_m=\frac1{\mu\sigma}.\]

A spatial Fourier component with wavenumber \(k\) decays as \(\exp(-D_mk^2t)\). Long spatial scales persist longer because the electric field required to change a large magnetic flux drives stronger circulating currents. Dimensional analysis gives a diffusion time of order \(\mu\sigma L^2\), with a geometric factor determined by the actual boundaries.

An alternating field outside a copper block

Take a tangential field at the plane \(x=0\) bounding a conductor occupying \(x>0\). Substitute \(B(x,t)=\mathrm{Re}\{B_0e^{i\omega t-\kappa x}\}\) into diffusion. The admissible square root of \(\kappa^2=i\omega\mu\sigma\) has positive real part,

\[\kappa=(1+i)/\delta,\qquad \delta=\sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)} .\]

At depth \(\delta\) the amplitude has dropped to \(1/e\) and the phase lags by one radian. These are independent observables for testing the semi-infinite, constant-property solution. The surface excitation sets the boundary amplitude. It need not equal the field that an external coil would generate with the metal absent.

For copper take \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\) S/m, \(\mu=\mu_0\) and \(f=1.00\) kHz. Then \(\delta=2.09\) mm. At \(x=4.00\) mm the field amplitude is \(0.148\) of its surface value and the phase lag is \(1.91\) rad. Raising frequency by a factor of four halves the penetration depth. If a plate thickness is comparable to \(\delta\), fields from the opposite face also affect the solution and a second boundary condition must replace the one-sided decay condition. This geometric check matters as much as the conductivity value when using an amplitude ratio to infer a material property.

The oscillating solution also predicts current concentrated near the surface, since Ampère’s law makes the current density proportional to the local spatial derivative of magnetic field. As frequency rises, less of the metal cross-section transports the alternating current at a given field amplitude. This mechanism helps explain why a conductor’s measured AC resistance can exceed its DC value even when the local conductivity is unchanged.

Relate surface fields to the power dissipated in a metal

The skin depth gives the distance over which current flows, yet a circuit measurement usually returns an impedance or a heating rate. Use the conducting half-space \(x>0\) and time convention \(e^{i\omega t}\). Choose magnetic field along \(z\), with phasor \(H_z=H_0e^{-(1+i)x/\delta}\). From Ampère's law without displacement current, the only current component is \(J_y=-\partial_xH_z=(1+i)H_z/\delta\). Ohm’s law therefore fixes the tangential electric field immediately inside the conductor.

\[Z_s=\frac{E_y(0)}{H_z(0)}=\frac{1+i}{\sigma\delta},\qquad R_s=\mathrm{Re}\,Z_s=\sqrt{\frac{\pi f\mu}{\sigma}} .\]

The surface resistance \(R_s\) is expressed in ohms even though neither field in this ratio is a terminal voltage or current. Current integrated over depth is a sheet current with units A/m equal to the surface magnetic amplitude. The real part of the surface impedance relates mean heating per unit area to this current, while the imaginary part describes reversible magnetic energy storage near the surface.

For peak phasors, mean inward Poynting flux is \(\mathrm{Re}[E_yH_z^*]/2=R_s|H_0|^2/2\). The same result must follow from local heating. Insert \(|J_y|^2=2|H_0|^2e^{-2x/\delta}/\delta^2\) into \(\int_0^\infty |J_y|^2/(2\sigma)\,dx\). The integral supplies \(\delta/2\), reproducing the surface formula. This equality is a sensitive check on the distinction between amplitude decay over \(\delta\) and squared-amplitude decay over \(\delta/2\).

Estimate loss in a microwave wall

Take copper with constant \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\) S/m, \(\mu=\mu_0\) at \(f=10.0\) MHz. The skin depth is \(\delta=20.9\) μm and \(R_s=1/(\sigma\delta)=0.825\) mΩ. A nearly uniform tangential peak magnetic field of \(80.0\) A/m illuminates a wall patch of area \(0.0200\) m². Surface heating density is \(0.000825(80.0)^2/2=2.64\) W/m², corresponding to \(52.8\) mW for the patch. If the supplied field were an RMS amplitude the division by two would be inappropriate.

Increasing frequency fourfold at constant peak magnetic field doubles the loss and halves the skin depth. A coating 10 μm thick at the original frequency is less than one skin depth, so a calculation assuming all current decays within that coating is unreliable; substrate conductivity and the second interface matter. A very thick low-loss conductor can instead be represented by the surface condition without resolving the field throughout its interior. A numerical mode solver using that substitution should recover both the inferred loss and its square-root frequency dependence.

The complex sign in the impedance also makes a phase prediction. For the stated time convention the surface electric field leads the tangential magnetic field by 45 degrees. Squaring the electric amplitude alone and dividing by the bulk resistivity would give a volumetric rather than an areal heating rate. To obtain an areal quantity one must integrate over its penetration profile. A measured phase substantially different from 45 degrees can indicate a magnetic permeability with appreciable losses, a finite film thickness, or a displacement current no longer negligible compared with conduction.

A calibrated surface-field phase measurement tests this condition independently of absolute field amplitude.

Chapter 10 · Waves in a hollow guide

Derive a rectangular guide’s cutoff and use its dispersion relation to distinguish phase delay from signal transit time.

Fields constrained by metal walls

Can a hollow metal tube carry an arbitrarily slowly varying electromagnetic wave? Take perfectly conducting walls, a uniform vacuum interior, width \(a\) along \(x\), height \(b\) along \(y\) and translation symmetry along \(z\). Tangential electric field must vanish on each wall. In free space a plane wave can have uniform transverse fields. Inside a hollow simply connected guide a nonzero transverse electrostatic potential difference between separate conductors cannot be maintained. The lowest guided pattern consequently has transverse variation.

For the simplest transverse-electric mode set \(E_y=E_m\sin(\pi x/a)\exp[i(\beta z-\omega t)]\), taking real parts for physical fields. At the side walls \(E_y\) is tangential and the sine vanishes. At the top and bottom the electric field is normal, as allowed by surface charge. Its zero divergence and accompanying magnetic field follow from Maxwell’s equations. In particular the curl includes an \(x\) derivative, giving a longitudinal magnetic component and the conventional label TE\(_{10}\).

Separate transverse and longitudinal wavenumbers

Substitute the field into the vacuum wave equation. The two spatial derivatives contribute \(-(\pi/a)^2\) and \(-\beta^2\), while the time derivative contributes \(-\omega^2/c^2\). Hence

\[\beta^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^2,\qquad f_c=\frac{c}{2a}.\]

Below cutoff the longitudinal dependence is exponential. In a long uniform section the bounded solution decays along the guide. A finite closed-off section can also have a growing exponential determined by reflections at its far end. Above cutoff the phase velocity is \(\omega/\beta\), while differentiating the dispersion relation gives the narrowband envelope velocity \(d\omega/d\beta=c^2\beta/\omega\). Their product is \(c^2\). Phase fronts move faster than \(c\) because the fixed transverse field variation contributes to total wavenumber. A modulation envelope in this lossless guide propagates more slowly than \(c\).

Choose a usable frequency band

For a guide with \(a=22.86\) mm and \(b=10.16\) mm, the first cutoff is \(6.56\) GHz. Higher-order patterns have transverse wavenumber squared \((m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2\). The next candidate cutoffs are \(13.11\) GHz for TE\(_{20}\) and \(14.75\) GHz for TE\(_{01}\). Consequently a 10.0 GHz drive supports one propagating mode in this ideal guide.

At 10.0 GHz the group velocity is \(c\sqrt{1-(6.56/10)^2}=0.755c\). A 2.00 m section delays a narrow envelope by \(8.84\) ns. Its guided wavelength is \(c/[f\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}]=39.7\) mm, exceeding the 30.0 mm free-space wavelength. Near cutoff the group velocity varies rapidly across a finite bandwidth, so quoting one envelope delay can conceal pulse distortion. Separately, real metal walls introduce attenuation and bends can excite evanescent modes even in the quoted single-mode band.

A practical way to measure the guided wavelength is to create a partial reflection at the termination. The forward and backward fields interfere and their stationary intensity maxima are separated by half the guided wavelength. At the example frequency the spacing should be about 19.9 mm. That measurement tests the propagation constant at one frequency. To test the group velocity, launch a short modulated burst or measure how transmitted phase changes between two nearby frequencies.

Close a guide to make a cavity

Placing conducting end plates at \(z=0\) and \(z=L\) turns a propagating guide mode into a standing field with discrete frequencies. For the TE pattern of the previous derivation, the electric field has only \(E_y\) and is tangential to both end plates. The conductor therefore requires it to vanish there. A consistent mode has \(E_y\propto\sin(\pi x/a)\sin(p\pi z/L)\) with positive integer \(p\). The transverse electric field still points normal to the walls at fixed \(y\), satisfying the original conditions. Combining longitudinal and transverse wavenumbers in the wave equation gives

\[f_{10p}=\frac c2\sqrt{\frac1{a^2}+\frac{p^2}{L^2}} .\]

The lowest such cavity resonance lies above the guide's cutoff even though no net power travels through the end plates. In a standing mode electric and magnetic energies exchange, and cycle-average net axial transport vanishes. Increasing cavity length at fixed \(p\) brings the frequency progressively nearer the guide cutoff. Conversely, if several longitudinal nodes fit into a short cavity, the longitudinal contribution can dominate the resonance frequency.

Weak finite conductivity or an output coupler causes stored energy to leave the mode. Define \(Q=\omega U/P_{\rm loss}\) with \(U\) the cycle-averaged stored energy and \(P_{\rm loss}\) the average dissipated or extracted power. When \(Q\) is approximately constant after switching off the drive, \(dU/dt=-\omega U/Q\) gives an energy decay time \(Q/\omega\). A field-amplitude measurement follows the square root of energy and consequently has twice that time constant.

Identify a resonance and estimate a loaded quality factor

Choose \(a=30.0\) mm, \(L=40.0\) mm and cavity height 12.0 mm. The lowest member of this family has \(f_{101}=(c/2)\sqrt{(1/0.0300)^2+(1/0.0400)^2}=6.25\) GHz using \(c=3.00\times10^8\) m/s. The parent guide’s cutoff is 5.00 GHz. Raising longitudinal index to two gives \(f_{102}=9.01\) GHz. Measured field nodes along the cavity distinguish these patterns from other possible modes near the same frequency.

After terminating the drive at the first resonance, suppose a weak probe's voltage amplitude falls by a factor \(e\) in 2.00 μs. Then \(Q=\omega\tau_{\rm amplitude}/2=\pi f\tau_{\rm amplitude}=3.93\times10^4\). The corresponding full resonance width is approximately \(f/Q=159\) kHz for a single weakly damped mode. A fit to detected power would return a 1.00 μs time constant instead. Increasing the probe coupling shortens the measured lifetime, so this ring-down gives the loaded quality factor. Measurements over several coupling strengths help separate internal wall losses from energy deliberately extracted by the probe.

The energy decay also predicts how much source power is needed to sustain a measured field. For example, if the resonant cavity in this measurement stores \(2.00\) nJ, total time-averaged loss is \(U/\tau_U=2.00\) mW. Source power actually coupled into the mode must replenish that loss in steady state. The source's forward power can be larger because some incident energy reflects at the coupling port. An independently measured coupling efficiency is therefore needed before converting a power-meter reading at the source into a stored field or a wall heating rate.

Chapter 11 · Pulses on a transmission line

Use charge and flux conservation to predict the first voltages seen when a pulse meets a mismatched load.

A line resolves the travel time

A cable can store energy all along its length. When a change of source voltage travels an appreciable fraction of the cable during its rise time, a single lumped capacitor cannot encode both its arrival and its reflection. Model a uniform two-conductor line using inductance \(L'\) and capacitance \(C'\) per unit length. Omit conductor resistance and dielectric leakage initially. Let \(V(z,t)\) be voltage between conductors and \(I(z,t)\) current toward increasing \(z\).

A short section \(dz\) experiences an inductive voltage difference \(L'dz\,\partial_t I\). Its net incoming current charges \(C'dz\) at the rate \(\partial_t V\). Accounting for the positive current direction gives the pair

\[\partial_z V=-L'\partial_t I,\qquad \partial_z I=-C'\partial_t V.\]

Differentiating once more gives wave equations with \(v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}\). Substitution of a travelling waveform shows that a forward wave has \(I_+=V_+/Z_0\), while a backward wave has \(I_-=-V_-/Z_0\), where \(Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}\). The characteristic impedance thus describes the voltage-to-current ratio of one travelling component. A resistance meter at the end of a finite DC cable ordinarily measures something different.

How the load launches a return wave

At a resistive termination \(R_L\) the total voltage equals \(R_L\) times total current. Write \(V_-= \Gamma V_+\) and use the opposite current signs above. Rearrangement gives

\[\Gamma=\frac{R_L-Z_0}{R_L+Z_0},\qquad V_L=(1+\Gamma)V_+ .\]

An open end doubles voltage and cancels current. A shorted end cancels voltage and doubles current. With positive real \(Z_0\) the reflected fraction of incoming wave power is \(\Gamma^2\). A voltage reflection of either sign carries energy back toward the source. A matched resistor absorbs all the incident energy, a condition also useful for interpreting the first step in a time-domain voltage trace.

Locate and characterize a mismatch

A line with \(L'=250\) nH/m and \(C'=100\) pF/m has \(v=2.00\times10^8\) m/s and \(Z_0=50.0\) Ω. A 2.00 V source step with series resistance 50.0 Ω launches 1.00 V into a 30.0 m cable. Initially the source sees the characteristic impedance because no reflection has had time to return. At 150 ns the leading edge reaches a 150 Ω load, where \(\Gamma=0.500\). The load rises to 1.50 V and draws 10.0 mA.

The return step of 0.50 V reaches the source at 300 ns. The source resistance is matched, so it absorbs the returning wave with no further reflection and the source-end voltage becomes 1.50 V. The result agrees with the final DC divider. A measured return time locates the discontinuity through \(d=vt_{\rm return}/2\), while its signed amplitude identifies an impedance increase or decrease. Distributed losses must be estimated before using this amplitude ratio on a long real cable.

Changing the source resistance leaves the first arrival time unchanged while altering the incident amplitude. It also changes what happens when the first reflection returns. At a source whose internal resistance differs from the line impedance, part of that returning pulse is launched outward again. Repeated round trips then generate a staircase of voltages separated by twice the propagation time.

Match unequal impedances with a finite line segment

A resistive load can be matched at a selected frequency even when its resistance differs from the feeding line’s characteristic impedance. Insert a uniform lossless section of impedance \(Z_t\) and length \(\ell\) between the original line \(Z_0\) and the load \(R_L\). Inside the added section decompose voltage into forward and backward waves. With the load plane at \(z=0\), a convenient phasor convention gives \(V(z)=V_+e^{-i\beta z}+V_-e^{i\beta z}\) and \(I(z)=(V_+e^{-i\beta z}-V_-e^{i\beta z})/Z_t\). At \(z=0\) use \(V(0)/I(0)=R_L\) to remove the unknown reflection coefficient.

Evaluate the ratio at the input plane \(z=-\ell\). Collecting the exponentials into cosines and sines gives

\[Z_{\rm in}=Z_t\frac{R_L\cos(\beta\ell)+iZ_t\sin(\beta\ell)}{Z_t\cos(\beta\ell)+iR_L\sin(\beta\ell)}.\]

For an electrically short segment the input simply approaches the load. At a quarter wavelength \(\beta\ell=\pi/2\) and \(Z_{\rm in}=Z_t^2/R_L\). Setting this equal to \(Z_0\) gives \(Z_t=\sqrt{Z_0R_L}\). Reflections still occur inside the added section, but their summed contribution at its input cancels at the design frequency. This is a phase-dependent match, so its behaviour under a suddenly applied step differs from a resistor matched at every frequency.

Design a section and test its bandwidth

A 50.0 Ω source line drives a 100 Ω resistor at 100 MHz. Available cable has phase speed \(2.00\times10^8\) m/s. The required matching impedance is \(70.7\) Ω and the length is \(v/(4f)=0.500\) m. At design frequency the input impedance equals 50.0 Ω, whereas directly attaching the resistor would produce reflection amplitude \((100-50)/(100+50)=1/3\) and reflect 11.1 percent of the incident power.

To test the frequency limit without dividing by a nearly infinite tangent, evaluate the sine–cosine form at 50.0 MHz. The electrical length is \(\pi/4\), so the trigonometric factors cancel. Substituting the three real impedances gives \(Z_{\rm in}=66.7-23.6i\) Ω. The feeding-line reflection magnitude \(|(Z_{\rm in}-50)/(Z_{\rm in}+50)|\) is about \(0.243\), reflecting \(5.9\) percent of incident power. At 200 MHz the section is half a wavelength and the input again equals the bare load. A frequency sweep should therefore show a matched centre frequency and increasing reflected power away from it. Loss or an appreciably reactive load changes both the depth and position of that minimum.

A frequency-domain match can also be understood through a short series of time-domain reflections. The impedance first rises from 50.0 to 70.7 ohms and then rises again from 70.7 to 100 ohms. The two interface reflections have the same sign when considered locally. At a quarter wavelength, propagation through the matching section and back adds a half-cycle phase, making the returning contributions cancel after all internal reflections are summed. An abrupt step arrives at those interfaces at different times, so the earliest observed reflected step need not vanish. A narrowband sinusoid and a broadband step perform different tests of the same lossless device.

An actual connector of finite electrical length introduces another discontinuity that may be visible in either test.

Send a voltage step down a lossless line into a real load. Continuity gives \(\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)\). Use the original wave impedance for both incident and backward power, which makes reflected power scale as \(\Gamma^2\).

Chapter 12 · Force carried by a field

Compute electrical pressure from a local stress balance and reconcile two energy calculations for a moving capacitor plate.

Momentum at a boundary

A capacitor can pull its plates together across vacuum. The ordinary mechanical contact stress of a solid is absent in the gap, yet a surrounding surface still records a force. In vacuum, the electric field contributes the electrostatic stress tensor \(T_{ij}=\epsilon_0(E_iE_j-\delta_{ij}E^2/2)\). Integrating the traction \(\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n\) around a body gives the electric force on enclosed material in a static configuration. An enclosing surface wholly in vacuum avoids having to specify internal material stresses.

To see the origin of this expression, use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\). The derivative of \(E_i E_j\) contains a field-gradient term \(E_j\partial_jE_i\). Electrostatic symmetry of derivatives makes this \(\partial_i(E^2/2)\), which cancels the isotropic term in the divergence. The remaining term is \(\rho E_i\), the local electric force density. The divergence theorem converts the volume force integral into the stated surface integral.

Pressure on a conducting plate

For a planar metal surface the field immediately outside is normal. The magnitude of traction is then \(p=\epsilon_0E^2/2\). Another derivation splits the surface-sheet field symmetrically between its two sides. The charge feels the average of the limiting fields, excluding the sheet’s own force. With zero interior field the result is \(\sigma E/2\), identical since \(\sigma=\epsilon_0 E\). Using the full outside field as the field acting on every surface charge would double the force.

For plate area \(A\) and separation \(d\), neglect fringing and put \(C=\epsilon_0A/d\). If the plates are isolated with charge magnitude \(Q\), stored energy \(U=Q^2/(2C)\) increases linearly with separation. The signed force in the direction of increasing \(d\) is \(-\partial U/\partial d=-Q^2/(2\epsilon_0A)\). The force tends to close the gap, consistent with the traction calculation.

Include the supply in a fixed-voltage experiment

When a voltage source remains connected, \(Q\) varies as \(d\) changes. The source supplies work \(V\,dQ\). The relevant mechanical energy at fixed \(V\) is \(U-VQ=-CV^2/2\). Differentiating this expression gives \(F_d=(V^2/2)dC/dd=-\epsilon_0AV^2/(2d^2)\). The electrical stress formula gives the same instantaneous force for either boundary condition. What changes during motion is the relationship between field and separation.

Take \(A=0.0100\) m², \(d=1.00\) mm and \(V=500\) V. The field magnitude is \(5.00\times10^5\) V/m, the pressure is \(1.11\) Pa and attraction is \(0.0111\) N. Doubling the gap at fixed voltage quarters the force. Disconnecting first holds \(Q\) fixed and leaves force unchanged in the ideal wide-plate limit. A direct classroom test is to measure reaction force while controlling either voltage or charge. Departures as separation approaches plate width reveal fringing fields, which redistribute both stress and capacitance.

The planar calculation can be checked by dimensions before using any geometry. Electric-field energy density has units of joules per cubic metre, identical to newtons per square metre. It therefore has the units of pressure. This equality of dimensions alone cannot fix the direction of a force in a nonuniform field. Around the edge of a plate, for example, field lines bend and the tensor traction must be projected onto the direction being measured before integration.

Pressure from an incident and a reflected wave

For time-dependent fields the force on matter combines a flux of electromagnetic momentum through its boundary with the change of field momentum inside. A useful steady special case is a normally incident plane wave on a macroscopic stationary surface in vacuum. Its cycle-averaged energy flux is \(I\), and transport of energy \(dU\) carries momentum \(dU/c\) in the propagation direction. Let the surface reflect fraction \(\mathcal R\), transmit fraction \(\mathcal T\) into vacuum on the far side, and absorb fraction \(\mathcal A=1-\mathcal R-\mathcal T\). Assume all beams are collinear and neglect thermal reradiation in the mechanical measurement.

For an area \(A\) over time \(dt\), the incoming axial momentum is \(IA\,dt/c\). The reflected beam leaves with negative momentum and the transmitted beam with positive momentum. The difference delivered to the surface gives

\[p_{\rm rad}=\frac Ic(1+\mathcal R-\mathcal T)=\frac Ic(\mathcal A+2\mathcal R).\]

A fully absorbing sheet receives \(I/c\) even though there is no exiting optical wave. A perfect mirror receives \(2I/c\) because it reverses photon momentum. A perfectly transmitting transparent sheet with no reflection takes no average axial momentum in this stationary, vacuum-to-vacuum accounting. Interfaces bounding different media require a joint treatment of material and electromagnetic momentum; the simple outgoing-vacuum relation has intentionally fixed that complication.

The same mirror result follows from the stress at a conducting boundary. At normal incidence the tangential electric field cancels while magnetic field amplitudes add. Using the cycle-averaged magnetic pressure at the boundary then gives \(2I/c\). This provides an independent check on the momentum-flux interpretation without assigning a mechanical collision trajectory to a wave.

Predict a torsion-balance signal

Direct a continuous 2.00 W beam entirely onto a small mirror at normal incidence. Measured fractions are \(\mathcal R=0.960\), \(\mathcal T=0.0200\), leaving \(\mathcal A=0.0200\). Integrated axial force is \((2.00/c)(0.0200+1.920)=1.29\times10^{-8}\) N. Mount the mirror so its force acts 40.0 mm from a torsion fibre axis with a perpendicular lever arm. Torque is \(5.18\times10^{-10}\) N m. A torsion constant \(2.00\times10^{-7}\) N m/rad gives a static twist of \(0.00259\) rad or \(0.148^\circ\).

The absorbed power is \(0.0400\) W. Heating can drive air currents or slow distortions that also deflect the balance, particularly outside vacuum. Reversing the mechanical lever arm while leaving absorbed power constant reverses the optical torque. Modulating the beam and measuring both delay and amplitude provides an additional way to distinguish the rapid radiation force from a slower thermal response.

For a plane mirror at oblique incidence the reflected momentum reverses only its component normal to the surface. A beam of known total power fully intercepted by the mirror imparts normal force \(2P\cos\alpha/c\) when it is perfectly reflected at angle \(\alpha\) to the normal. If the controlled quantity is irradiance per area perpendicular to the beam and the physical mirror area is fixed, intercepted power adds another projection factor. The pressure per unit mirror area is then \(2I\cos^2\alpha/c\). Specifying whether power or surface footprint is held fixed is essential before comparing an angular torque scan with either formula.

Applied study

Derive the fields of a coaxial cable and follow how electromagnetic energy travels along it.

Compare the experimental runs

Where does energy travel in a cable?

A coaxial cable contains two conductors separated by an insulator. Current travels along the metal surfaces. To locate the energy delivered to a load, find the electric and magnetic fields in the space between them. Consider concentric, perfectly conducting cylinders of radii \(a\) and \(b\), separated by a uniform lossless dielectric of permittivity \(\epsilon\) and permeability \(\mu\). Treat a straight, uniform section far from connectors.

Place charge per unit length \(+q'\) on the inner conductor and \(-q'\) on the facing shield. A cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\), with \(a<r<b\), gives \(2\pi rD_r=q'\) per unit length. Integrating \(E_r=D_r/\epsilon\) from inner to outer conductor gives \(V=q'\ln(b/a)/(2\pi\epsilon)\). Thus \(C'=q'/V=2\pi\epsilon/\ln(b/a)\) is capacitance per metre. More charge fits at the same voltage when the dielectric permittivity rises. Fields and voltage at a dielectric interface.

Find the second energy store

Let a current \(I\) flow along the inner conductor and return on the shield. Ampère’s law gives a circumferential magnetic field \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi r)\). Integrate its energy density \(\mu H_\phi^2/2\) over the annular cross-section. The resulting magnetic energy per metre is \(\mu I^2\ln(b/a)/(4\pi)\). Equating this to \(L'I^2/2\) gives \(L'=\mu\ln(b/a)/(2\pi)\). The product \(L'C'=\mu\epsilon\) is independent of the two radii for this homogeneous line. Magnetic fields from steady currents.

Couple the fields into a travelling wave

A slice of cable of length \(dz\) gains charge when more current enters than leaves. Its charge is \(C'V\,dz\), giving \(\partial_z I=-C'\partial_t V\). The changing magnetic flux gives the complementary voltage drop \(\partial_z V=-L'\partial_t I\) by Faraday’s law. Differentiate once more to obtain \(\partial_{zz}V=L'C'\partial_{tt}V\). For a waveform travelling in the positive \(z\) direction, these equations require

\[v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}},\qquad \frac{V}{I}=Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}.\]

Here \(Z_0\) is the ratio for a single travelling wave. The radial electric field crossed with the circumferential magnetic field gives axial Poynting flux. Indeed \(\int_a^b E_r H_\phi\,2\pi r\,dr=VI\). The surrounding dielectric carries this electromagnetic power in the ideal model.

What crosses the gap in a charging capacitor?

A changing electric field can also generate a magnetic field where there are no moving free charges. Charge a parallel-plate capacitor through a wire at steady current \(I\). A loop encircling the wire bounds a surface cut by the wire, but also a deformed surface passing between the plates. A consistent magnetic circulation for both surfaces requires the Maxwell term in Ampère’s law, \(\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{\rm free}+d\int\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf A/dt\). Between wide circular plates the total electric displacement flux is the plate charge, so its derivative is exactly \(I\).

Take plate radius \(R=5.0\) cm and \(I=1.0\) A. Inside the gap, neglect fringing and assume slow charging, so that \(D\) is uniform over the plate area. A centred loop of radius \(r=2.5\) cm encloses the fraction \(r^2/R^2=1/4\) of the changing flux. The magnetic flux density there is \(B=\mu_0 I r/(2\pi R^2)=2.0\) µT. A loop just outside the plate radius encloses the full displacement current. Charge conservation, the electric field and the magnetic circulation thus give the same current whether the chosen surface cuts a wire or a dielectric.

A short pulse in polyethylene

Use \(a=0.450\) mm, \(b=1.475\) mm, \(\epsilon=2.25\epsilon_0\) and \(\mu=\mu_0\). The logarithm of the radius ratio is 1.187. The line constants are 105.44 pF/m and 237.43 nH/m. They give \(Z_0=47.45\) Ω and a propagation speed of \(2.00\times10^8\) m/s. A 1 V forward pulse has current \(1/47.45=21.1\) mA and instantaneous power 21.1 mW while its flat top passes a cross-section. It reaches the end of a 10 m line about 50 ns after launch.

Exercises on transmission and reflection

1. Change voltage without changing geometry

Double the amplitude of the forward pulse. Compare electric energy per metre, magnetic energy per metre and transmitted power with their original values.

Work out the energy factors

\(I=V/Z_0\) doubles. Thus the two energies \(C'V^2/2\) and \(L'I^2/2\) both quadruple, and \(VI\) also quadruples. Their ratio is unity for a travelling wave because \(L'/Z_0^2=C'\). The pulse speed stays fixed since neither line constant changed.

2. Match the load

Terminate the cable in \(R_L=2Z_0\). At the load the forward and backward voltages add, while their currents have opposite propagation signs. Find the reflected fraction of incident power.

Apply the boundary condition

Write \(V_++V_-=R_L(V_+-V_-)/Z_0\). This gives \(V_-/V_+=(R_L-Z_0)/(R_L+Z_0)=1/3\). Each wave’s power magnitude is proportional to its voltage squared, so \(1/9\) returns and \(8/9\) is absorbed. For \(R_L=Z_0\), the boundary condition accepts the incident wave without a reflected wave.
