Radiating multipoles and what a detector actually integrates

A small oscillating source looks point-like from a distant detector, yet its angular pattern reveals its structure. Derive electric and magnetic dipole radiation from a conserved current, identify what survives when electric dipoles cancel, and turn a detector aperture into a calibrated estimate of total power.

Name the small parameters before discarding a part of the field

Let d be the characteristic source size, r the source-to-detector distance, and \(k=\omega/c\). The condition \(d/r\ll1\) permits geometric expansions of the radial amplitude. The condition \(kr\gg1\) selects the radiation field over induction and quasistatic fields. The condition \(kd\ll1\) permits a multipole expansion of the source's directional phase. These inequalities concern different pairs of lengths. A source can be electrically small while a probe sits in its near field. A large optical aperture can be many wavelengths wide while its far field is measurable at a sufficiently large r, though it then requires many multipoles or a full Fourier integral.

For harmonic localized sources in vacuum the outgoing far-zone vector potential is proportional to \(\mathbf I(\mathbf n)=\int\widetilde{\mathbf J}(\mathbf r')e^{-ik\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r'}d^3r'\). Differentiating the rapidly varying \(e^{ikr}/r\) factor gives the magnetic field; using the radiation plane-wave relationship gives the electric field. This removes the component of \(\mathbf I\) parallel to the observation direction:

\[\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{\rm rad}={i\omega\mu_0e^{ikr}\over4\pi r}\,[\mathbf I-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf I)],\]
\[\widetilde{\mathbf B}_{\rm rad}={1\over c}\mathbf n\times\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{\rm rad}.\]

The transverse projection is essential. It forces zero electric-dipole radiation along the dipole axis. Treating the current integral as an already transverse scalar misses this geometrical zero and produces the wrong total power.

Eliminate an integrated current with charge conservation

The electric dipole moment is

\[\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho\,d^3r'.\]

Differentiate this definition and use \(\dot\rho=-\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J\). For component j, integration by parts changes \(-\int x'_j\partial'_lJ_l\) into \(\int J_j\), since the surface term vanishes outside a localized current. Thus \(\int\widetilde{\mathbf J}=-i\omega\widetilde{\mathbf p}\). For \(kd\ll1\) the leading term of the phase expansion is one. Inserting it gives

\[\widetilde{\mathbf E}_p={k^2e^{ikr}\over4\pi\epsilon_0r}[\mathbf p-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p)],\]
\[{d\overline P\over d\Omega}={\omega^4|\mathbf n\times\mathbf p|^2\over32\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}.\]

The phasor p is a peak moment. For a linearly oscillating real-axis dipole along z the intensity follows \(\sin^2\theta\), independent of azimuth. Integrating \(\sin^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta d\phi\) gives \(8\pi/3\). Hence the cycle-averaged total is \(\overline P=\omega^4|\mathbf p|^2/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)\). A complex vector p describing an elliptical source is allowed in the vector formula. With equal orthogonal components in temporal quadrature, radiation along the normal to their plane is circularly polarized and there is no direction parallel to both components at once.

Consider an effective moment \(p_0=1.0\times10^{-12}\) C m at 100 MHz. A vacuum far-field receiver at 10 m on the equator sees peak \(E_0=k^2p_0/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)=3.95\) mV/m. Mean flux is \(E_0^2/(2Z_0)=20.7\) nW/m². Total power is \((8\pi/3)r^2 S_{\rm eq}=17.4\) μW. At the same distance but a 30° polar angle the field peak halves and flux falls to a quarter. Increasing frequency while holding moment fixed raises power as the fourth power. Maintaining that same moment with a specified driven current introduces its own frequency dependence, so a frequency sweep must state the held source variable.

Recover the magnetic dipole from the antisymmetric current moment

Expand the phase as \(1-ik n_j x'_j+\cdots\). The first directional correction involves \(M_{ij}=\int x'_j J_i\,d^3r'\). Its symmetric and antisymmetric parts have distinct origins. Define \(\mathbf m=\tfrac12\int\mathbf r'\times\mathbf J\,d^3r'\). Then the antisymmetric contraction with n gives a current contribution \(ik\mathbf n\times\mathbf m\) for the index convention above. Substituting into the far-field electric expression yields

\[\widetilde{\mathbf E}_m=-{Z_0 k^2 e^{ikr}\over4\pi r}\mathbf n\times\mathbf m,\]
\[\overline P_m={\mu_0\omega^4|\mathbf m|^2\over12\pi c^3}.\]

A small planar current loop has \(\mathbf m=I\,\mathbf A\) with oriented area vector. For a loop in the xy plane its magnetic-dipole intensity has the same \(\sin^2\theta\) dependence as a z electric dipole, while its electric polarization is azimuthal instead of meridional. An intensity detector without polarization analysis can therefore confuse two fundamentally different sources. A rotating linear analyser placed at the equator can distinguish them even if each delivers the same incident power.

For a loop of radius 10 mm carrying a 10 mA peak sinusoidal current at 100 MHz, \(m_0=3.14\times10^{-6}\) A m² and the equivalent effective electric-moment magnitude for equal power is \(m_0/c=1.05\times10^{-14}\) C m. Its power is about \(17.4\,\mu{\rm W}(0.0105)^2=1.91\) nW. The current and geometry are assumed uniform around the loop. Larger loops require retaining retardation along the current path and a feed model before scaling this result.

Keep the symmetric part when the electric dipole vanishes

Define \(D_{ij}=\int \rho x'_ix'_j\,d^3r'\). One integration by parts gives

\[\dot D_{ij}=\int(x'_iJ_j+x'_jJ_i)d^3r'.\]

Therefore \(M_{(ij)}=-i\omega D_{ij}/2\) for harmonic sources. Split D into trace and traceless parts, defining \(Q_{ij}=3D_{ij}-\delta_{ij}\operatorname{tr}D\). The trace contribution after contraction is parallel to n and vanishes under transverse projection. Relative to the electric dipole field the effective transverse source is consequently

\[\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{p,Q}={k^2e^{ikr}\over4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\,\mathsf P_\perp\!\left[\mathbf p-{ik\over6}Q\mathbf n\right],\]
\[\mathsf P_\perp\mathbf v=\mathbf v-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v).\]

This coefficient belongs to the stated quadrupole convention. Changing the factor three in Q changes the prefactor in the radiation field. Comparing numerical tensors from different sources therefore requires checking their definitions. Electric quadrupole and magnetic dipole fields both arise at the same first correction to a general localized current. One cannot always describe a source whose electric dipole vanishes as purely quadrupolar without examining the current's antisymmetric moment.

A transparent demonstration uses two opposite, equal z-directed electric dipoles separated along z by distance d. Their individual small-source patterns are proportional to \(\sin\theta\). The pair's far-field phase sum is \(-2i\sin(kd\cos\theta/2)\) up to an overall phase. When \(kd\ll1\) the field pattern is proportional to \(\sin\theta\cos\theta\), with zeros on the axis and equator and maxima at 45° and 135°. Expansion of the exact array factor gives a controlled test of the approximation. At \(kd=0.4\), \(\theta=45^\circ\), the argument of the sine is 0.1414. Replacing the sine by its argument overestimates amplitude there by about \(0.1414^2/6=0.33\%\). A small amplitude imbalance in the opposite dipoles can reintroduce an electric-dipole contribution even where the ideal quadrupole vanishes.

Integrate over the detector aperture

A detector collecting directions within a cone normally measures an integral of \(dP/d\Omega\) times throughput and polarization acceptance. For a z electric dipole and a complete azimuthal cone of half-angle \(\theta_a\) centred on +z, the fraction is

\[f_{\rm pole}={\int_0^{\theta_a}\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\over\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta}={2-3\cos\theta_a+\cos^3\theta_a\over4}.\]

For small \(\theta_a\) expand the integrand rather than subtracting nearly equal cosines in floating point: \(f_{\rm pole}\simeq3\theta_a^4/16\). Although a true axial ray carries no power, a finite cone collects a small positive flux varying as the fourth power of its opening angle. An isotropic-emission approximation would instead scale as \(\theta_a^2/4\), badly overestimating the near-null signal. An equatorial belt between \(\theta=\pi/2-\delta\) and \(\pi/2+\delta\), spanning all azimuths, captures fraction \((3\sin\delta-\sin^3\delta)/2\). For \(\delta=10^\circ\) this is 0.258, even though the belt covers only 0.174 of the full solid angle.

To convert a small planar detector into solid angle, include its projected area. For a detector of area \(A_d=1.0\) cm² normal to the line of sight at 2.00 m, \(\Delta\Omega\simeq A_d/r^2=2.5\times10^{-5}\) sr. At an equatorial maximum the normalized dipole density is \(3/(8\pi)\) per steradian, giving a collected fraction \(2.98\times10^{-6}\). Rotating its face 60° to the line of sight halves this fraction before considering polarization-sensitive throughput. An extrapolation from a small aperture to total emission is therefore a model-based estimate, whose validity can be assessed with readings at additional angles.

Separate geometrical zeros from coherence and background

Suppose equal geometry and detectors at polar angles 90°, 45° and 0° return powers 100, 50 and 5 in arbitrary units. An ideal point dipole with no offset would suggest 100, 50 and zero. Fitting a constant isotropic additive background using the axial reading leaves 95, 45 and zero. A pure dipole would predict 47.5 at 45° after subtraction. The discrepancy is small but informative if calibration uncertainty is smaller. Check finite aperture before interpreting the axial excess as a second emitter; make the aperture twice as narrow and see whether its captured fraction near the null falls by roughly sixteen, as for an ideal axial dipole cone.

If two excited source moments are coherent, add their complex fields before squaring. Their cross term can create a fore-aft asymmetry that disappears upon incoherent averaging. If their relative phase changes randomly over the integration window, add the angle-dependent intensities instead. A single angular sweep measures some combinations of magnitudes and relative phases and leaves others degenerate. Repeating with an analyser or a known phase reference helps distinguish a weak quadrupole contribution from an independent weak background. A total-power reading alone discards even more directional information.

Directionality from coherent electric and magnetic moments

A compact source can have two comparable leading moments of different type. This can happen if a resonant dielectric particle supports electric and magnetic responses at the same optical frequency. The long-wavelength multipole expansion still applies to the exterior observation, but symmetry or resonance can change the relative magnitudes of the moments. Add the electric fields before calculating power whenever the moments are coherently driven. Adding two separately calculated angular powers would erase a measurable directional effect.

Take an electric moment \(p\mathbf e_x\) and a magnetic moment \(m\mathbf e_y\), both complex peak amplitudes with the same time convention. Their leading radiation fields share the common factor \(k^2e^{ikr}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)\). The electric vector is \(\mathbf p-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p)\). The magnetic contribution in these units is \(-\mathbf n\times\mathbf m/c\). Thus in the \(+z\) and \(-z\) directions their electric polarisations coincide with \(x\), but one magnetic contribution changes sign. After removal of the common amplitude their intensities are proportional to

\[I_+=|p+m/c|^2,\]
\[I_-=|p-m/c|^2,\]
\[\frac{I_+-I_-}{I_++I_-}=\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(p^*m/c)}{|p|^2+|m/c|^2}.\]

If \(m/c=p\), the intensity vanishes exactly in the backward direction. A quadrature phase between equal magnitudes removes the forward–backward difference, even though both moments radiate. A pair of intensity measurements therefore constrains a real cross term but does not determine a unique pair of complex moments. Rotating analysers and adding another angular direction can supply additional independent constraints.

To understand the full angular pattern, sum the squared magnitudes of the two vector contributions for an arbitrary direction. The separate terms are \(|p|^2(1-n_x^2)\) and \(|m/c|^2(1-n_y^2)\); their cross term is \(2 n_z\operatorname{Re}(p^*m/c)\). For the equal in-phase case the sum reduces to \(|p|^2(1+\cos\theta)^2\). Its integral over the sphere is \(16\pi|p|^2/3\), twice the integral for one electric dipole alone at this amplitude. The cross term integrates to zero because it reverses sign between opposite hemispheres. Directional suppression redistributes existing radiation while leaving the sphere-integrated powers of these two orthogonal multipole types additive.

The mean angular intensity is proportional to \(4|p|^2/3\), and the forward value to \(4|p|^2\). The corresponding directivity is three. The backward hemisphere still contains radiation even when its axial intensity is exactly zero. Integrating \((1+u)^2\) from \(u=-1\) to 0 and dividing by the integral from \(-1\) to 1 gives a fraction \(1/8\). A detector measuring integrated backward hemispherical power would report a clear finite signal. Two teams reporting an axial null and one eighth backward power are describing consistent observations if their angular acceptances differ.

The extinction of a null is exceptionally sensitive to field errors

Set \(m/(cp)=(1+\epsilon)e^{i\varphi}\), with real small amplitude imbalance \(\epsilon\) and phase error \(\varphi\) in radians. Expanding the backward intensity to the first nonzero order gives

\[I_-/|p|^2\simeq\epsilon^2+\varphi^2;\]

the forward intensity is about \(4|p|^2\). Misalignment, background scattering and angular acceptance can also fill the nominal null. The ratio alone cannot identify an electric–magnetic amplitude mismatch unless phase and background are independently known.

A 5.0 percent amplitude error together with a five-degree phase error gives a leading estimate \(I_-/I_+\simeq0.00253\). Using the exact formula gives about 0.00250. With 100,000 forward counts in matched exposures a perfectly calibrated backward detector would register about 250 signal counts. A background of 200 counts is comparable to this signal, and subtracting it adds uncertainty from the background exposure. Doubling the integration time does not remove an incorrectly modelled constant fraction of forward stray light reaching the backward detector.

Finite acceptance near an exact null has a different scaling. In the ideal balanced case write \(\theta=\pi-\delta\). The intensity \((1-\cos\delta)^2\) grows as \(\delta^4/4\). Integrated over a cone of half-angle \(\alpha\) it scales as \(\alpha^6\), because \(d\Omega\) contributes a further factor of \(\delta\). A small constant background integrated over the same cone instead scales as \(\alpha^2\). Measuring with several aperture stops can distinguish these trends only if the instrument's point-spread function and angular centring are known. For a simple electric dipole aligned with the viewing direction, the zero is quadratic in angle, giving yet another scaling.

A set of checks can distinguish a physical interference null from an insensitive receiver. Rotate the source by half a turn about the \(y\) axis so that its electric moment changes sign but its magnetic moment does not. The predicted dark direction should reverse. Reverse one measurement cable's reference phase in a coherent receiver and verify that the reported phase changes while intensity does not. Use an independent isotropic calibration source or a known dipole orientation to calibrate gain in both directions. Finally scan frequency through the resonances that create the two moments. The null should follow the relative amplitude and phase inferred from independently measured spectral responses, not just one accidentally quiet detector channel.

Worked angular problem · fit an axis tilt from two detectors

A linearly oscillating electric dipole intended to lie along \(z\) is tilted by a small unknown angle \(\epsilon\) toward \(+x\). Matched detectors in the \(xz\) plane are placed at \(+45^\circ\) and \(-45^\circ\) relative to \(z\). Their background-subtracted counts are 4200 and 5800 in equal exposures. Infer the tilt and its approximate counting uncertainty. Would reversing the electric dipole's oscillation phase change the answer?

Keep the two detector directions separate

With positive angles toward \(+x\), the expected intensities vary as \(\sin^2(\pi/4-\epsilon)\) and \(\sin^2(\pi/4+\epsilon)\). Their sum is independent of tilt, and the signed difference divided by their sum is \(\sin(2\epsilon)\) when the difference is defined as \(I_- - I_+\). Its measured value is 0.160. The small solution is

\[\epsilon=\arcsin(0.160)/2=4.60^\circ.\]

Additional mathematical solutions are excluded by knowing the source is close to the desired initial axis.

Treat the counts as independent Poisson measurements with background uncertainties negligible for this calculation. Condition on their total of ten thousand. The normalised difference has estimated standard error \(\sqrt{(1-0.160^2)/10000}=0.00987\). Propagating through the inverse sine gives a tilt error of about 0.0050 radians or 0.29 degrees. A relative detector-gain error can imitate part of this difference. Swap the detectors physically while preserving source orientation or interchange the source tilt to separate a fixed gain bias.

Reversing the dipole phase multiplies the entire electric field by minus one, leaving both intensities unchanged. A phase-sensitive receiver with a common reference could detect the reversal. A finite aperture averages a range of angles, reducing the sensitivity if the apertures are wide. Integrate the angular predictions across the measured fields of view before applying the point-detector formula to a precision tilt.

A translated expansion can mix multipole labels

The exact far field is independent of where the expansion origin is drawn. Individual moments need not be. A translation of the source origin by a vector changes the electric dipole by the total source charge times that vector, with sign depending on the translation convention. For an electrically neutral compact source the dipole is therefore origin independent. A corresponding expansion of the quadrupole contains products of the translation with the dipole as well as the monopole. If both lower moments vanish, the first surviving electric quadrupole is likewise robust under a shift.

In a directional Fourier integral, changing the origin also changes the external phase that multiplies the expanded moments. At a fixed observation direction this common phase has no effect on intensity, but at a given truncation order its series must be kept consistently when comparing complex amplitudes. An origin-dependent angular intensity at the nominal retained order is a warning that a translated lower-order contribution or a same-order magnetic term may have been dropped.

A numerical check translates the integration grid by a known small vector while preserving the physical source and detector positions. Recompute both the unexpanded far-field integral and its truncated multipole prediction. The exact physical comparison should agree after phase referencing. The discrepancy between a leading-order expansion and the exact result should scale with the neglected power of source size over wavelength. This test probes moments, phase conventions and coordinate bookkeeping with one calculation.

Tutorial · infer a dipole moment and estimate detector uncertainty

A calibrated equatorial probe at 5.0 m observes a 300 MHz linearly oscillating dipole with RMS electric amplitude 2.00 mV/m. Determine peak moment and total emitted power. The receiver angle is uncertain by ±5° around the equator. Estimate the peak possible relative bias in a power estimate that assumes exact equatorial placement.

Keep the RMS calibration through the flux calculation

Peak E is \(\sqrt2(0.00200)=0.002828\) V/m. With \(k=2\pi f/c\simeq6.29\) m⁻¹, \(p_0=4\pi\epsilon_0r E_{\rm peak}/k^2\simeq3.98\times10^{-14}\) C m. Alternatively use RMS E directly to find

\[S=E_{\rm rms}^2/Z_0=1.06\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W/m^2}.\]

The inferred total is \((8\pi/3)(25)S=2.22\) μW. At a 5° displacement from the maximum the measured flux is multiplied by \(\cos^2 5^\circ=0.9924\), so the inferred total can be low by 0.76%. A comparable angular error near an axial null produces a much larger relative uncertainty.

Tutorial · use a symmetry-breaking parameter as a diagnostic

Two opposing parallel dipoles have moment magnitudes \(p(1+\delta)\) and \(p(1-\delta)\), with \(kd=0.20\). Derive the exact direction-dependent pair factor, estimate the equatorial-to-45° intensity ratio for \(\delta=0.010\), and explain why this experiment cannot determine the sign of a small real imbalance from total intensities alone in this geometry.

Retain the phase of each current element

With \(q=kd\cos\theta/2\), choose pair factor \((1+\delta)e^{-iq}-(1-\delta)e^{iq}=2\delta\cos q-2i\sin q\). The intensity pattern is proportional to \(4\sin^2\theta(\delta^2\cos^2q+\sin^2q)\). At the equator q=0, so the intensity factor is

\[4\delta^2=0.0004.\]

At 45° q=0.07071 and the factor is about 0.01018. The ratio is 0.0393. The residual electric-dipole and leading quadrupole terms here occur in quadrature, eliminating their cross term for a real imbalance. Reversing its sign leaves each intensity unchanged. A phase-sensitive reference at the equator could recover that sign.
