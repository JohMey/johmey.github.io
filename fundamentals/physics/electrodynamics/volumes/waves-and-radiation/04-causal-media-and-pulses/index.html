Causal material response and the propagation of a finite pulse

An optical pulse entering a transparent slab is delayed, broadened and sometimes reshaped. Derive these effects from the motion of bound charge, and learn what a measured group delay actually says about a pulse carrying information.

Turn the idea of delayed response into an equation

At a point in a stationary isotropic dielectric, a short electric impulse displaces bound charge. The displacement can continue after the applied field disappears. In a linear time-translation-invariant material write the polarization as a weighted sum over earlier values of the local electric field:

\[P(t)=\epsilon_0\int_0^\infty h(\tau)E(t-\tau)\,d\tau,\]
\[\chi(\omega)=\int_0^\infty h(\tau)e^{i\omega\tau}\,d\tau .\]

The lower limit encodes causality. Switching to steady harmonic fields yields \(\widetilde P=\epsilon_0\chi(\omega)\widetilde E\) under the \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention. An instantaneous contribution could also be represented by a delta function at zero delay. The response kernel h has dimensions of inverse time when susceptibility is dimensionless. A short memory compared with the period can sometimes be approximated by a constant susceptibility over a limited band. Extending a constant complex response to all frequencies would generally violate either causality or the requirement of a real time-domain polarization.

For real h, \(\chi(-\omega)=\chi(\omega)^*\), so its real part is even and its imaginary part odd. For a decaying kernel, extending \(\omega\) into the upper half of the complex plane introduces \(e^{-\operatorname{Im}\omega\,\tau}\), ensuring convergence. Analyticity there links dispersion and absorption. Conversely, a fitted pole in the upper half-plane in this convention would imply growth, an incompatible time convention, or an unstable model. Report the phasor convention beside an empirical complex permittivity to assign the sign of loss correctly.

Obtain a susceptibility with a recognizable resonance

Represent a bound charge of magnitude q and mass m by a displacement x, restored with frequency \(\omega_0\) and damped at rate \(\gamma\). Choose x along the direction of the force on a positive effective charge; the resulting susceptibility depends on \(q^2\). For a density N of independent oscillators the polarization is \(Nqx\). The equation of motion and its phasor solution give

\[m(\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x)=qE,\]
\[\chi={\omega_p^2\over\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\]
\[\omega_p^2={Nq^2\over m\epsilon_0}.\]

Writing \(D=(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2\) gives

\[\chi'=\omega_p^2(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)/D,\]
\[\chi''=\omega_p^2\gamma\omega/D.\]

A positive damping rate makes \(\chi''>0\) at positive frequency. At low frequency the displacement follows the drive almost in phase, while far above resonance inertia produces the opposite displacement. At resonance the displacement lags the drive by a quarter cycle, making the velocity align with force and maximizing the ability of damping to absorb average work for a given displacement amplitude.

The energy balance follows directly by multiplying the equation by \(N\dot x\). The field supplies \(E\dot P\), equal to the rate of change of oscillator kinetic plus restoring energy and the dissipated power \(Nm\gamma\dot x^2\). Averaging a harmonic cycle eliminates the change of stored mechanical energy. With peak phasors,

\[\langle E\dot P\rangle=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\chi''|\widetilde E|^2=\tfrac12 Nm\gamma\omega^2|\widetilde x|^2 .\]

This supplies a practical sign check on both the material fit and the assumed time dependence. The imaginary part that attenuates a passive forward wave must also give nonnegative cycle-averaged heating.

Reconstruct the dispersive shift from its loss spectrum

For a response that vanishes sufficiently fast at high frequency, analyticity in the upper half-plane permits a contour integral of \(\chi(z)/(z-\omega)\). Close the contour above and indent around the pole on the real axis. The large arc vanishes under the decay assumption. Taking real and imaginary parts of the resulting principal-value relation, then using the symmetry between positive and negative frequencies, gives

\[\chi'(\omega)={2\over\pi}\,\mathcal P\int_0^\infty{\Omega\chi''(\Omega)\over\Omega^2-\omega^2}\,d\Omega .\]

The principal value means combine contributions on both sides of the singular frequency before taking the limit. At zero frequency there is no interior singularity and \(\chi'(0)=(2/\pi)\int_0^\infty\chi''(\Omega)/\Omega\,d\Omega\). A passive absorption band produces a finite static polarization contribution even when it lies far above the optical frequencies being measured. If instantaneous high-frequency polarization is included in a model, subtract that constant background before using this vanishing-at-infinity form.

As a simple check, let a measured narrow absorption band have area \(A=\int\chi''\,d\Omega=3.0\times10^{14}\) s⁻¹ centered at \(\Omega_0=2.0\times10^{15}\) s⁻¹. Across the band \(1/\Omega\) varies little, giving an estimated contribution \(2A/(\pi\Omega_0)=0.0955\) to \(\chi'(0)\). The same numerical area translated to twice the frequency contributes only half as much. A spectrometer limited to one window cannot recover an absolute static susceptibility without assumptions about absorption outside its window. Tail models and independent low-frequency measurements constrain this extrapolation.

Distinguish the carrier phase from the envelope delay

In a nonmagnetic bulk medium define \(k(\omega)=\omega n(\omega)/c\), choosing the branch of n that attenuates a wave travelling in positive z. A component accumulates \(\exp[ik(\omega)L]\), so its field amplitude decays as \(\exp[-\operatorname{Im}k\,L]\) and its intensity twice as rapidly in the exponent. In a transparent band take k effectively real and expand around carrier \(\omega_c\):

\[k(\omega_c+\Omega)=k_c+k_1\Omega+\tfrac12 k_2\Omega^2+\cdots,\]
\[k_1={1\over v_g}={n+\omega_c\,dn/d\omega\over c}.\]

The constant \(k_cL\) rotates the carrier phase. The term \(k_1L\,\Omega\) multiplies the envelope spectrum by a linear phase, corresponding to translation in time by \(k_1L\). The second derivative produces different delays for different spectral components and generally changes the pulse shape. These three operations can be measured separately with an interferometer, a time-of-flight experiment and a pulse-duration measurement. A continuous-wave phase measurement at one frequency does not independently determine \(k_1\) or \(k_2\).

At 800 nm suppose an index measurement gives \(n=1.50\) and \(dn/d\lambda_0=-0.050\) per μm, where \(\lambda_0\) is vacuum wavelength. By differentiating \(\omega=2\pi c/\lambda_0\),

\[n_g=n-\lambda_0\,dn/d\lambda_0=1.54.\]

A 100 mm slab has carrier phase travel time \(nL/c=0.5003\) ns and narrowband envelope time \(n_gL/c=0.5137\) ns. Compared with a vacuum path of equal length the envelope delay is \(0.1801\) ns. The 13.3 ps difference between phase and group times is small relative to a nanosecond but enormous compared with a 50 fs pulse width.

Derive the width of a dispersed Gaussian by completing a square

Use an input envelope \(A(0,t)=A_0\exp[-t^2/(2T_0^2)]\), so its intensity is proportional to \(\exp[-t^2/T_0^2]\). The amplitude spectrum is proportional to \(\exp[-T_0^2\Omega^2/2]\). In a frame at retarded time \(u=t-k_1L\), multiplication by the quadratic propagation phase gives a Gaussian integral with complex coefficient \(T_0^2-i k_2L\). Evaluate that integral with its physical square-root branch:

\[{A(L,u)\over A_0}={1\over\sqrt{1-iD}}\exp\!\left[-{u^2\over2T_0^2(1-iD)}\right],\]
\[D={k_2L\over T_0^2}.\]

Hence \(I(L,u)=I_0(1+D^2)^{-1/2}\exp[-u^2/(T_0^2(1+D^2))]\). The intensity width broadens by \(\sqrt{1+D^2}\) and the peak falls by the reciprocal factor. Integrating over u recovers the original pulse energy per area, as expected for lossless propagation. The phase inside the exponential is quadratic in u, so the instantaneous frequency varies across the envelope. Measuring only output intensity cannot tell the sign of \(k_2\), since the width depends on its square. A phase-sensitive measurement or a known initial chirp can reveal that sign.

Take \(T_0=20\) fs and integrated dispersion \(k_2L=400\) fs². Then D=1. The incident intensity FWHM is \(2\sqrt{\ln2}T_0=33.3\) fs, and the emerging FWHM is 47.1 fs. The peak intensity falls to 70.7% while the energy remains fixed. In a second slab of the same length with \(k_2L=-400\) fs², the quadratic phases cancel, provided loss and higher-order dispersion remain negligible and the beams couple without appreciable filtering. Propagating with group velocity alone would predict the arrival of the centre but miss these measurable width changes.

When a measured peak time no longer tracks an unaltered envelope

Near an absorption line both real and imaginary parts of k can vary strongly over a pulse spectrum. A simple propagation experiment then applies a complex filter \(H(\omega)=t_{\rm interfaces}(\omega)e^{ik(\omega)L}\), where interface reflections may also matter. A severely attenuated output maximum may move forward because the trailing part of the incoming waveform is preferentially removed. A negative slope of the transmission phase can therefore coexist with a causal impulse response. The arrival of a newly imposed nonanalytic wavefront requires the high-frequency behaviour of the full causal transfer function. A band-limited Gaussian already has tails at every finite time and has no sharp front.

There is also a distinction between phase delay and energy deposition in an opaque medium. If \(n=n'+i n''=1.5+0.02i\) at 600 nm, \(\operatorname{Im}k=2\pi(0.02)/\lambda_0=2.09\times10^5\) m⁻¹. Ignoring reflections, intensity falls to \(1/e\) in \(1/[2\operatorname{Im}k]=2.39\) μm. After 5.0 μm the intensity fraction is

\[e^{-2.094}=0.123.\]

A pulse delay inferred from the remaining photons is a conditional timing measurement on transmitted energy; it does not describe energy that was deposited at shallower depths.

Separate a resonance shift, a loss peak and a spectral weight

In the oscillator calculation, hold oscillator strength fixed and compare two damping rates. Predict the imaginary susceptibility at resonance from \(\omega_p^2/(\gamma\omega_0)\); halving damping should double this height. The static susceptibility \(\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2\) stays fixed. A narrower taller peak can preserve its weighted spectral contribution. Record real susceptibility on both sides of the resonance, where its sign changes. Then change the oscillator strength and explain why this change scales absorption and dispersion together. Use the separate half-space calculation to check that the absorbed power integrated over depth accounts for the part of incoming flux that passes the surface.

A causal relaxation whose spectrum can be integrated exactly

As a second material model, consider permanent molecular dipoles that reorient toward a weak applied field. When rotational inertia relaxes much faster than the times of interest, a single exponential approach to equilibrium is useful. Let the additional static susceptibility of these dipoles be \(\Delta\chi\), and let \(\tau\) be the relaxation time. For the extra polarisation write \(\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\chi E\). This is a different material approximation from a weakly damped oscillator. It still has a retarded response and provides an independent test of the dispersion–loss relation.

Solve the inhomogeneous differential equation by multiplying it by \(e^{t/\tau}\), integrating from an unpolarised remote past and dividing by the same factor. A unit step turns on exponentially. A harmonic drive gives a complex susceptibility with one pole on the lower imaginary frequency axis for the chosen \(e^{-i\omega t}\) sign convention.

\[P(t)=\frac{\epsilon_0\Delta\chi}{\tau}\int_{-\infty}^t e^{-(t-t')/\tau}E(t')\,dt',\]
\[\chi'=\frac{\Delta\chi}{1+\omega^2\tau^2},\]
\[\chi''=\frac{\Delta\chi\,\omega\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}.\]

At \(\omega\tau=1\) the in-phase and lagging components are equal to \(\Delta\chi/2\), and the loss component reaches its maximum. A material with \(\tau=8.0\) ns therefore peaks near 19.9 MHz. A step reaches 63.2 percent of its final additional polarisation after 8.0 ns and 95.0 percent after 24.0 ns. If the chosen observable is current rather than polarisation, the relaxation current is proportional to \(\dot P\) after the step and decays, while \(P\) itself rises. An instantaneous background polarisation can deliver a narrow current spike in addition to this resolved tail.

Substitute \(\chi''\) into the zero-frequency dispersion relation. With \(x=\omega\tau\), the integral becomes

\[(2\Delta\chi/\pi)\int_0^\infty dx/(1+x^2)=\Delta\chi.\]

The high-frequency background, if present, must be added separately to obtain the total static response. This exact integral is also a test for loss-spectrum simulations on a logarithmic grid. The integration measure is \(d\omega/\omega=d\log\omega\). Equal weights per ordinary frequency or a missing conversion between natural and decimal logarithms gives an incorrect static increment even if the plotted peak is perfect.

A spectrometer covering \(0.1\leq\omega\tau\leq10\) captures \(2[\arctan 10-\arctan(0.1)]/\pi=0.873\) of the static increment. Two full decades centred on the peak miss almost thirteen percent. The missing low and high frequency contributions are equal in this particular symmetric logarithmic loss model. A superposition of slower relaxations or a second unrelated absorption feature destroys this equality. Report a static measurement or bounds on those extra contributions before interpreting a small discrepancy as failure of causality.

Drive the relaxation twice and distinguish memory from propagation delay

Apply a rectangular field \(E_0\) from time zero to \(T\), then switch it off. Continuity of polarisation at both switching instants gives

\[P(T)=\epsilon_0\Delta\chi E_0(1-e^{-T/\tau}).\]

After switch-off the value decays as \(P(T)e^{-(t-T)/\tau}\). For \(T=\tau\) the maximum is 0.632 of the static value and at \(2\tau\) the remainder is 0.233. A pure delay with the same time of maximum would instead translate the rectangular waveform with unchanged amplitude and abrupt edges. One can test this distinction from the complete waveform even if a slow instrument makes identifying the first nonzero response difficult.

Follow the first pulse by an opposite pulse of equal height starting after a zero-field gap \(g\). At the second start the memory is \(P_s=\epsilon_0\Delta\chi E_0(1-e^{-T/\tau})e^{-g/\tau}\). The second pulse drives the polarisation toward the opposite equilibrium. It crosses zero during the pulse after a further time \(\tau\ln[1+P_s/(\epsilon_0\Delta\chi E_0)]\). For \(T=\tau\) and \(g=\tau\) the result is \(0.209\tau\), or 1.67 ns with the above time constant. This cancellation depends on the gap. A nonlinear saturation or a detector recovery effect can also produce interactions between pulses, so repeat with smaller field and with a reference cell.

Because the material equation is linear, scaling both pulses by the same factor leaves the zero-crossing time unchanged. The delay-based measurement rejects a constant gain error, but an additive baseline shifts the crossing. An experimentally estimated timing uncertainty is the noise level divided by the local slope magnitude there. At zero polarisation within the negative pulse the equation gives \(\dot P=-\epsilon_0\Delta\chi E_0/\tau\), making the conversion from amplitude noise especially simple. This also explains why decreasing the field too far increases the timing noise even though it improves the validity of a linear material response.

To separate propagation from memory, take data through two cell thicknesses using the same cables and electronics. A phase accumulating in the material scales with thickness, while a common cable or detector lag cancels in their phase difference. Reflections give additional echoes whose times scale with thickness too, but follow round-trip distances and can interfere with the direct signal. Gate a sufficiently isolated first arrival or include interface scattering in the transfer function. Taking a ratio of Fourier spectra near a zero of the reference pulse magnifies noise; discarding or regularising those frequencies changes the reconstructed temporal resolution and should be stated with the result.

Worked pulse problem · bound a group-delay measurement

A pulse traverses two samples cut from one transparent material, of lengths 10.0 and 30.0 mm. The measured difference between the carrier-envelope peak times is 103 ps. The extra material replaces air in a fixed total optical path. Estimate the group index. A student obtains a substantially different value from the phase velocity at the carrier. Specify two physical measurements and one numerical test that can resolve the disagreement.

Use the replaced air and examine the pulse spectrum

The incremental delay is \((n_g-n_{g,\rm air})\Delta L/c\), with \(n_{g,\rm air}\) near one in this idealised setting. Thus \(n_g\simeq1+c(103\,\mathrm{ps})/(20.0\,\mathrm{mm})=2.54\). Treating the extra material path as an added path in an otherwise unchanged optical train would give 1.54 instead. A genuinely moved detector would require that different time reference and geometry. A 2 ps independent uncertainty in this differential delay alone contributes about 0.030 to \(n_g\).

First measure phase at several closely spaced frequencies and unwrap its slope. The slope gives group delay, whereas phase at one frequency determines a phase index only after fixing an integer number of waves. Second, shorten the spectrum while keeping its central frequency fixed and compare peak delay with a cross-correlation delay and a centre-of-energy delay. A strongly distorted pulse can yield three different answers even with perfect electronics. Record a transmitted spectrum for each sample to establish whether selective attenuation is significant.

As a numerical test, propagate the measured incident spectrum using the fitted complex wavenumber and reconstruct the physical detector observable, including its impulse response. Compare with the analytic linear-phase translation after suppressing higher dispersion derivatives and frequency-dependent attenuation. Keep the computational time window large when narrowing the spectrum, since the pulse then extends further in time. A wrapped pulse in a periodic FFT window can acquire an apparently early peak. Looking at the first and last portions of the unwrapped time trace is an inexpensive way to rule that out.

A deliberately chirped input reveals the sign of dispersion

The temporal width of an initially transform-limited Gaussian after propagation depends on squared group-delay dispersion. Measuring one broadened width therefore loses the sign of the quadratic phase. Add a known adjustable quadratic spectral phase before the sample. For a Gaussian with fixed spectral intensity, the broadening now depends on the square of the sum of the imposed and sample phase curvatures. Plot measured pulse width versus the imposed curvature. The minimum identifies the setting that compensates the sample and hence gives the opposite of its signed dispersion.

For example, suppose independent calibration of the phase device assigns its minimum output width to an imposed minus four hundred femtoseconds squared after inserting a plate. If the optical train without the plate minimises at minus one hundred femtoseconds squared, the plate adds plus three hundred femtoseconds squared. The displacement of the minimum removes a common phase offset from the optical train. The widths at their respective minima need not be exactly equal if the plate changes the transmitted spectrum.

Measure that spectrum before interpreting a displaced or broadened minimum. A filter narrowing the spectrum increases even the best possible transform-limited pulse width. Cubic spectral phase and detector response may make the measured width curve asymmetric or leave a long tail near the nominal optimum. Comparing a reconstructed complete temporal profile with its spectrum gives information that a single width at one propagation distance cannot contain.

Tutorial · infer a material slope with a pair of delay measurements

Two samples of the same transparent glass have lengths 20.0 and 70.0 mm. With the receiver and external air path arranged so that the added glass replaces an equal vacuum length, the narrowband arrival time differs by 90.0 ps. At 1.00 μm the independently measured phase index is 1.500. Determine group index and the local index slope. Estimate how an unaccounted fixed electronic time offset affects a two-length measurement.

Use the difference to eliminate common delays

With \(\Delta L=0.0500\) m and \(\Delta t=(n_g-1)\Delta L/c\), the inferred index is

\[n_g=1+c\Delta t/\Delta L=1.540.\]

Therefore \(dn/d\lambda_0=(n-n_g)/\lambda_0=-0.040\) per μm. A fixed additive trigger latency appears in both raw arrival times and cancels in the difference. A sample-dependent connector or surface coating that changes the pulse transmission phase need not cancel. Comparing a third length tests the assumed affine dependence of delay on material length, while measuring transmitted spectra can reveal frequency-dependent filtering.

Tutorial · distinguish dispersion from simple attenuation

An initially transform-limited Gaussian pulse has intensity FWHM 50 fs. After a transparent segment its width is 80 fs and its peak intensity is 0.50 times the original. Estimate \(|k_2L|\) under the Gaussian model, then infer the additional frequency-independent intensity transmission needed to account for the peak. Could the measured peak alone determine dispersion?

Recover a width and then an energy ratio

The initial amplitude parameter is \(T_0=50/(2\sqrt{\ln2})=30.0\) fs. The width ratio 1.60 gives

\[|D|=\sqrt{1.60^2-1}=1.249.\]

Thus \(|k_2L|\simeq1.13\times10^3\) fs². At conserved energy the peak would be \(1/1.60=0.625\), so an additional intensity transmission of \(0.50/0.625=0.80\) reconciles both observations. Equivalently the energy ratio is peak ratio times width ratio, 0.80. A peak value alone admits many combinations of filtering, dispersion and attenuation. The inference additionally assumes that higher-order spectral phase and spectral amplitude filtering do not change the Gaussian shape.
