From a local field balance to travelling waves and standing modes

A voltage probe moving along a line finds regularly spaced zeros. A second probe at the same point measures a magnetic field even when the electric reading vanishes. Derive both readings from Maxwell’s equations, calculate the energy stored between the zeros, and decide what additional measurement would reveal the direction in which energy travels.

Specify a medium, a region and a sign convention

Take a homogeneous, isotropic, transparent medium at rest. In the frequency range of interest its permittivity \(\epsilon\) and permeability \(\mu\) are positive, real constants. Neglect free charge and current in the region where we will propagate a field. Sources can still exist outside the region and set its incident wave. The electric and magnetic fields are functions of position and time. Maxwell's equations impose two divergence conditions in addition to the two evolution equations. In particular, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0\) here because \(\epsilon\) is uniform. At a material interface the normal condition concerns \(\mathbf D\), so one must reconsider this simplification before using the same wave equation across a boundary.

Consider first a plane field depending only on z and t, with electric polarization along x and magnetic field along y. Using a right-handed coordinate system, the y component of the curl of the electric field is \(+\partial_z E_x\). The x component of the curl of the magnetic field is \(-\partial_z H_y\). Faraday's law and Ampère–Maxwell's law then give

\[\partial_z E_x=-\mu\,\partial_t H_y,\]
\[\partial_z H_y=-\epsilon\,\partial_t E_x.\]

Imagine a localized positive electric bump travelling to the right. At a point just ahead of its centre the field grows in time. The second equation therefore requires a negative magnetic-field slope there. This checks the relative signs before making a harmonic assumption. With this convention a positive \(E_xH_y\) describes energy flow towards increasing z.

Find what travels and what remains constrained

Differentiate the first curl equation with respect to z and substitute the second. Constant \(\epsilon,\mu\) allow derivatives to commute with the coefficients. The electric field obeys \(E_{zz}=\epsilon\mu E_{tt}\). The same procedure gives the magnetic wave equation. More generally, taking the curl of Faraday's law produces

\[\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\mu\epsilon\mathbf E_{tt}.\]

The divergence term vanishes under the stated assumptions. A formal solution of three unrelated scalar wave equations can still violate the divergence or original curl conditions. These conditions connect the measurable polarizations and reduce the independent initial data.

\[v=(\epsilon\mu)^{-1/2},\]
\[Z=\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon},\]
\[\partial_t(E_x+ZH_y)+v\partial_z(E_x+ZH_y)=0 .\]

The corresponding combination with a minus sign travels to the left. To see the first equation, expand it and replace \(E_t=-H_z/\epsilon\), \(H_t=-E_z/\mu\). The coefficients cancel because \(vZ=1/\epsilon\) and \(Z/\mu=v\). Hence there exist arbitrary waveform functions F and G such that

\[E_x(z,t)=F(z-vt)+G(z+vt),\]
\[H_y(z,t)=[F(z-vt)-G(z+vt)]/Z.\]

The medium determines two different quantities. Delay measurements determine the product \(\epsilon\mu\); measuring electric-to-magnetic amplitude in a single travelling wave determines their ratio. Impedance has units volts per ampere. It relates fields in volts per metre and amperes per metre at one location. A port voltage divided by current additionally depends on how a particular line geometry integrates those fields over a cross-section.

If only the initial electric field \(E_x(z,0)\) is known, its decomposition into left- and right-moving components is ambiguous. A simultaneous magnetic measurement supplies the missing combination. For example, suppose a local measurement gives \(E_x=3.0\) V/m and \(H_y=4.0\) mA/m in a medium with \(Z=250\) Ω. There \(ZH_y=1.0\) V/m, so the right-moving field equals 2.0 V/m and the left-moving field equals 1.0 V/m. These numbers describe local instantaneous amplitudes. Separating time-averaged directional powers requires squared waveform records or a harmonic representation.

Recover the energy current and average it correctly

Multiply the first curl equation by H, the second by E, and add. The spatial terms form \(\partial_z(EH)\), while the time terms form minus the derivative of \(\epsilon E^2/2+\mu H^2/2\). Thus in the absence of work on current the field obeys a local conservation law. The two travelling pieces give an especially useful representation of both sides:

\[u={\epsilon E^2+\mu H^2\over2}=\epsilon(F^2+G^2),\]
\[S_z=EH={F^2-G^2\over Z},\]
\[u_t+\partial_zS_z=0.\]

The energy densities add while the currents subtract. A field carrying equal left- and right-going powers can have substantial stored energy and zero mean transport. A cancellation in E alone says little about stored energy because it often accompanies a maximum in H. For the two local amplitudes of the preceding example the instantaneous net flux is

\[(4-1)/250=0.012\,\mathrm{W/m^2}.\]

Multiplying the original readings gives \(3.0\times0.004=0.012\) W/m², an independent check.

For harmonic calculations use real physical fields equal to \(\operatorname{Re}[\widetilde{\mathbf E} e^{-i\omega t}]\), so the phasors denote peak amplitudes. A product of real sinusoids averages to one half the real part of one phasor times the conjugate of the other. Therefore \(\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde{\mathbf E}\times\widetilde{\mathbf H}^{*})/2\). A single forward sinusoid with electric peak 6.0 V/m in vacuum carries \(36/(2Z_0)=0.0478\) W/m², using \(Z_0=376.73\) Ω. If an instrument already reports RMS electric amplitude, the factor of one half is absorbed in that reading. Mistaking an RMS calibration for a peak calibration changes an inferred power by a factor of two.

Enforce a conductor condition on the two waves

Place a perfect conducting plane at z=0 and illuminate it from negative z. The tangential electric field must vanish at the surface at all times. For peak incident phasor \(E_0 e^{ikz}\), with \(k=\omega/v\), the reflected field must be \(-E_0e^{-ikz}\); the accompanying magnetic fields add at the surface. Choosing real \(E_0\), the total phasors are

\[\widetilde E_x=2iE_0\sin(kz),\]
\[\widetilde H_y={2E_0\over Z}\cos(kz).\]

Electric nodes are separated by \(\pi/k=\lambda/2\). The magnetic antinodes sit at those same locations. At a fixed interior point the two fields are a quarter cycle out of phase. Their mean product is zero but their instantaneous product alternates sign. Examine a quarter wavelength of the standing wave: energy crossing an internal plane feeds local changes between electric and magnetic storage, while the conductor absorbs no net power in the idealization.

In a nonmagnetic dielectric with \(\epsilon_r=4\), the phase speed is \(c/2\) and \(Z=Z_0/2\). At 500 MHz the wavelength is 0.2998 m and successive electric zeros lie about 150 mm apart. A 4 V/m incident peak gives an 8 V/m electric antinode and magnetic amplitude \(8/(188.4)=0.0425\) A/m at the conductor. A tiny electric probe positioned at the conductor would read essentially zero. A loop sensing magnetic flux can respond strongly there. The response of a real loop also depends on its orientation, area and the frequency-dependent conversion of flux to voltage.

Build a complete family between two walls

Put conductors at \(z=0,L\). Tangential E now vanishes at both endpoints. Try a separated solution \(E=X(z)T(t)\). Dividing the wave equation by XT gives \(X''/X=T''/(v^2T)=-k^2\) for an oscillatory solution. The first condition eliminates the cosine term in X. The second enforces \(k_n=n\pi/L\), \(n=1,2,\ldots\). Frequencies are

\[\omega_n=vk_n.\]

There is no nonzero constant tangential electric mode consistent with two zero boundary values. A static magnetic bias can be independently specified through initial flux; it does not belong to this oscillatory electric-mode series.

\[E(z,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin(k_nz)[A_n\cos(\omega_nt)+B_n\sin(\omega_nt)] .\]

To find coefficients, multiply the initial field by \(\sin(k_mz)\) and integrate over the interval. Product-to-sum identities give zero for \(n\ne m\), and \(L/2\) for \(n=m\). Thus \(A_m=(2/L)\int_0^L E(z,0)\sin(k_mz)\,dz\). Applying the same projection to the initial time derivative gives

\[B_m=(2/(\omega_m L))\int_0^L E_t(z,0)\sin(k_mz)\,dz.\]

The curl equation supplies \(E_t\) from the spatial derivative of the initial H field. These formulas convert measured initial data into mode coordinates. A point reading alone supplies one linear combination of all coordinates.

One can also derive orthogonality without already knowing the sines. Multiply the eigen-equations for \(X_n\) and \(X_m\) by the opposite functions, subtract, and integrate. The boundary term \([X'_n X_m-X'_m X_n]_0^L\) is zero, so

\[(k_n^2-k_m^2)\int X_nX_m dz=0.\]

Other homogeneous boundary conditions can preserve this cancellation. Spatially varying material parameters generally change the weight in an orthogonality relation. Knowing the inner product matters when estimating an amplitude from measured fields in unlike dielectrics.

Predict which modes a localized preparation actually excites

Imagine an idealized initial tangential electric field with a symmetric top-hat amplitude \(E_a\) on \(L/3<z<2L/3\), zero elsewhere, and zero initial H. Then \(B_n=0\) and integration gives

\[A_n={2E_a\over n\pi}\,[\cos(n\pi/3)-\cos(2n\pi/3)] .\]

Symmetry around the middle eliminates even n because their sine eigenfunctions change sign about that point. For \(n=1,3,5\) the coefficients are \(2E_a/\pi,\ -4E_a/(3\pi),\ 2E_a/(5\pi)\), respectively. Negative \(A_3\) is a relative phase; its contribution to stored energy is positive. Orthogonality gives initial total electric energy per transverse area \(\epsilon L\sum A_n^2/4=\epsilon L E_a^2/6\). The first mode carries \(6/\pi^2\approx60.8\%\) of that total, while the third carries \(8/(3\pi^2)\approx27.0\%\). A three-mode truncation reproduces a large share of the energy and still rounds the sharp initial edges. Measured peak error and integrated energy error answer different questions about that approximation.

Take \(L=0.60\) m filled with the dielectric above. The first frequency is about 124.9 MHz. A receiver at midspan sees modes 1 and 3 with opposite spatial signs. A second receiver at \(L/3\) has a node of mode 3 and will miss it. Detecting only one large peak after changing receiver position is therefore insufficient evidence that the source was single mode. Predict the source overlap, propagation loss and receiving overlap separately before interpreting a spectrum.

Use an open wave measurement as an independent power check

The linked calculation treats a propagating field and derives an energy-flux observable. Read its amplitude convention and the material properties beside the inputs. Predict which quantity changes quadratically when you vary only field amplitude. If a lab measures a total power, convert the calculated flux using the illuminated area and the angle between the flux vector and the detector normal. For a plane wave in a uniform medium, changing area at fixed field changes total intercepted power without changing any local field. In a narrow cavity beam the local field itself varies across the aperture, so the area product must be replaced by a surface integral.

An investigation of wave speed on a space–time mesh

A pulse simulation offers a second way to test the continuum mode calculation. Instead of assuming a single-frequency source, initialise a packet in a uniform one-dimensional region and record the times when it crosses two stations. First predict how the numerical method can change this transit measurement. A code that respects the differential equations as its mesh tends to zero can have a noticeably incorrect propagation speed at a finite resolution.

Work with an electric component \(E_y\) on integer mesh points and a magnetic component \(H_z\) on the intervening half points. Store the magnetic component half a time step later than the electric component. Faraday's law compares the electric field on the two ends of one spatial interval. Ampère's law compares the two magnetic components bounding the electric location. With \(d=\Delta x\) and \(s=\Delta t\), their integral balances become

\[H_{j+1/2}^{n+1/2}=H_{j+1/2}^{n-1/2}-\frac{s}{\mu d}(E_{j+1}^n-E_j^n),\]
\[E_j^{n+1}=E_j^n-\frac{s}{\epsilon d}(H_{j+1/2}^{n+1/2}-H_{j-1/2}^{n+1/2}).\]

For a forward wave the indicated components have the same sign. A change in either sign convention requires changing both updates consistently. Placing the variables on staggered meshes also matters when sampling power. A product of an electric field and the nearest stored magnetic number is not automatically their product at a common event. Interpolate both in time and space for a quantitative flux comparison, or use a discrete energy identity appropriate to the scheme.

Eliminate the magnetic values by taking a second time difference of the electric update. The resulting equation equates the centred second time difference to \(C^2\) times the centred second space difference, where \(C=us/d\) and \(u=1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\). For the discrete mode \(E_j^n=E_0e^{i(kjd-\omega ns)}\), the difference of the forward and backward phases becomes a sine. Take the branch connected to the continuum wave at small positive \(k\).

\[\sin(\omega s/2)=C\sin(kd/2),\]
\[\frac{v_{\rm phase}}u=\frac{\arcsin[C\sin(kd/2)]}{Ckd/2},\]
\[\frac{v_{\rm group}}u=\frac{\cos(kd/2)}{\sqrt{1-C^2\sin^2(kd/2)}} .\]

The group speed follows by differentiating the frequency with respect to wavenumber before choosing a particular packet. The right-hand side of the sine equation must remain within the range of real sines for every mesh mode. This gives \(C\leq1\) in this one-dimensional uniform problem. If a short-wavelength mode violates the condition, a growing discrete solution accompanies the decaying one. A seemingly smooth start may seed very little of either, but roundoff or a sharp boundary can excite the growing solution. A stable choice still permits frequency-dependent speed.

For ten samples per wavelength and \(C=0.50\), the phase and group speed ratios are approximately 0.988 and 0.963. Over twenty wavelengths a phase crest is about a quarter of a cycle late, and the envelope peak has an even larger relative lag. With twenty samples per wavelength the errors shrink roughly fourfold. Reducing only the time step at fixed space resolution need not improve the result: the spatial difference retains its own dispersion error, and the temporal error can partially compensate it. At \(C=1\) in this idealised one-dimensional uniform case the phase relation on the resolved branch is exact. A slanted multidimensional wave, a discontinuous medium or a different mesh does not inherit that special cancellation.

A worked time-of-flight measurement with uncertainty

To construct an initial forward pulse, give the electric field a broad Gaussian envelope multiplying its carrier and assign the associated magnetic field \(H=E/Z\) at its proper staggered locations and times. Setting the magnetic field to zero instead launches a superposition of left- and right-moving packets of comparable amplitude. A reflecting wall can return one packet through the sensor after the other. Before fitting a speed, identify a time window that excludes every return from the domain boundaries. Check this by increasing the distance to the nearest wall without changing either station.

Suppose the expected continuum transit between stations is 100.0 ns, each threshold crossing is timed to 0.3 ns and the station spacing is known to 0.2 percent. Independent timing errors contribute \(\sqrt2(0.3)/100=0.00424\) relative uncertainty to the difference. Combined in quadrature with the length uncertainty this gives 0.47 percent. The roughly 3.7 percent group-speed bias at ten points per wavelength should be distinguishable, while the smaller phase error calls for careful identification of the same crest at both stations. A common clock offset cancels from the difference, whereas a common timebase scale error does not.

A threshold on the leading edge tracks a changing fraction of peak amplitude if the model disperses the pulse. Use a complex narrow-band signal and fit the slope of the unwrapped phase difference against frequency as a second estimate of group delay. For a single frequency only the phase modulo \(2\pi\) is observed. An apparent speed that jumps as the station separation changes by a small fraction of a wavelength can be an unwrapping error, even though the electric time series looks smooth. Begin with stations close enough to choose a branch, then follow the branch as their separation increases.

An additional stringent check uses two packets with different carrier wavelengths but equal initial envelope width in space. If the narrow-band condition holds for both, their group delays should match the two predictions of the mesh dispersion relation. Measured delays that approach the same continuum value with refinement while their difference obeys the predicted wavelength dependence support the diagnosis of numerical dispersion. A nonvanishing delay difference in the refined limit calls for revisiting the assumed physical material law, the timing observable or the boundaries. A single apparent agreement at one wavelength cannot distinguish these possibilities.

Worked inverse problem · estimate the energy of an invisible mode

A conducting one-dimensional cavity contains its first and third electric standing modes. At a common electric maximum in time let \(E(x)=a\sin(\pi x/L)+b\sin(3\pi x/L)\). A small signed probe at \(L/2\) reads 2.0 V/m, and a probe at \(L/6\) reads 4.0 V/m. Determine the two amplitudes and the fraction of energy in the third mode. Compare this result with what could be concluded from two intensity probes alone.

Solve the signed and unsigned versions

The centre gives \(a-b=2.0\). The second station gives \(a/2+b=4.0\). Summing the equations yields \(a=4.0\) V/m and hence \(b=2.0\) V/m. At this instant the compatible free modes have zero magnetic amplitude. Spatial orthogonality makes integrated electric energy proportional to \(a^2+b^2\), even though there are local interference terms. The third mode therefore carries 20 percent of total energy. This fraction stays fixed during free evolution in an ideal lossless cavity. The instantaneous electric share of each mode alone oscillates in time.

Two intensity probes would give only magnitudes of the two readings. Choose the centre reading positive by a global sign convention. The second could then be \(-4.0\) instead. Solving \(a-b=2.0\), \(a/2+b=-4.0\) gives \(a=-4/3\) and \(b=-10/3\) V/m, for a third-mode fraction \(100/116=0.862\). Both field preparations produce the same two intensities. A third carefully chosen position, a phase-sensitive receiver or a frequency-resolved trace distinguishes them. A probe at a location that simply duplicates either existing linear combination adds precision but cannot supply the missing sign.

An eigenmode decomposition is therefore both a prediction tool and an experimental design tool. Before choosing sensors, form the matrix of sampled mode shapes and check whether its columns remain distinguishable within noise. If two stations approach a shared node, the inferred coefficients can grow unstable while the predicted signal at those stations remains an excellent fit. A held-out measurement away from the common node exposes the ambiguity.

Why a sharp preparation resists uniform modal convergence

A rectangular initial displacement has a jump. Any finite sum of the smooth sine modes is continuous and therefore cannot converge uniformly to a function with different one-sided limits. Near the edge a partial sum oscillates. Increasing the cutoff makes the oscillatory region narrower, but its largest overshoot does not tend to zero relative to the jump. Pointwise comparisons a fixed positive distance from the jump and spatially integrated energy tell a different story from the maximum error.

This is physically relevant to preparing an electromagnetic cavity. No source creates a perfectly discontinuous field with unbounded bandwidth. Replace the top hat by its convolution with a smooth narrow preparation profile. The modal coefficients then acquire the Fourier transform of that profile as a high-frequency envelope. Doubling the number of retained modes beyond the preparation bandwidth should change both the integrated energy and the measured field little. A finite-width detector performs its own additional averaging, even when the preparation is sharp.

An informative validation uses two limits in a stated order. Fix the physical source and detector widths while increasing the number of modes until the detector prediction converges. Then vary the physical widths to investigate the sharp-edge limit. Shrinking the model widths each time the numerical resolution increases changes the problem being solved and may keep the apparent peak error from decreasing.

Tutorial · reconstruct a partial reflection from three observations

A coherent line scan in a nonmagnetic medium of known \(Z=200\) Ω finds successive electric maxima 80 mm apart at 1.0 GHz. Maximum and minimum peak electric amplitudes are 5.0 and 1.0 V/m. Determine phase speed, incident and reflected peak fields and mean net flux. Explain exactly what fixes the phase of the reflection coefficient and what remains ambiguous in these observations.

Derive the two directional amplitudes

For nonnegative amplitude magnitudes \(a,b\), the maxima and minima of the sum of two opposite travelling waves are \(a+b\) and \(|a-b|\). If the source is known to lie to the left and net transport is rightward, \(a>b\), so \(a=3.0,\ b=2.0\) V/m. Wavelength is twice the 80 mm maximum spacing and \(v=f\lambda=1.60\times10^8\) m/s. The reflection magnitude is \(2/3\), and the average net flux is

\[(9-4)/(2\cdot200)=0.0125\,\mathrm{W/m^2}.\]

A measurement of node spacing constrains the relative phase change over distance. Determining reflection phase at a named load additionally requires the position of a maximum or minimum relative to that reference plane. Without an independent source direction, swapping a and b produces the same electric envelope but reverses the net flux.

Tutorial · test whether a receiver can reconstruct two stored modes

At an instant when both cosine time factors are one, only the first and third electric modes contribute. At midspan the field is 3.0 V/m; at one third span it is 1.732 V/m. Determine \(A_1,A_3\) and the stored electric energy per unit area for \(L=0.60\) m,

\[\epsilon=4\epsilon_0.\]

Would a second sensor at two thirds span provide independent information if it replaced the midspan sensor?

Solve the observation system and identify its rank

At one third span \(\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt3/2\) and \(\sin\pi=0\), so \(A_1\simeq2.00\) V/m. At midspan the combination is \(A_1-A_3=3.0\), hence \(A_3=-1.0\) V/m. The energy per area is \(\epsilon L(4+1)/4=2.66\times10^{-11}\) J/m². At two thirds both sinusoids have the same values as at one third for these odd modes: the first is \(\sqrt3/2\) and the third is zero. Those two rows would be identical. More digits in either measurement cannot resolve \(A_3\) from that pair. An offset from a node helps, but near a node a small measured voltage also amplifies calibration noise in the reconstruction.
