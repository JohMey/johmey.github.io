Constructing the retarded Green function from an outgoing spherical shell

A short source pulse reaches two receivers at different times and amplitudes. Derive the signal from a localized spacetime impulse, see why the potential is evaluated at a past time, and identify which observables change when the source occupies an extended region.

Turn the curl constraints into wave equations with sources

Use vacuum SI units, a localized charge density \(\rho\) and current density \(\mathbf J\) satisfying \(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\). Introducing \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\) enforces the absence of magnetic charge. Setting \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\) then enforces Faraday's law. These definitions leave freedom to add \(\nabla\Lambda\) to \(\mathbf A\) while subtracting \(\partial_t\Lambda\) from \(\Phi\). An individual value of scalar or vector potential therefore depends on convention, whereas electric and magnetic fields constructed from them do not.

Adopt the Lorenz condition \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\Phi=0\). Insert the potentials into Gauss's and Ampère's laws and use \(\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf A=\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf A-\nabla^2\mathbf A\). The gradient and time-mixed terms cancel in this gauge:

\[(\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2)\Phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0,\qquad \left(\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\right)\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J.\]

The same scalar propagation operator acts on each component. Taking a divergence and time derivative of these equations shows that the Lorenz-condition combination itself obeys a source-free wave equation when current is conserved. If it vanishes initially with compatible time derivative and boundary conditions it remains zero. Independently prescribing an oscillating point charge and zero current would violate continuity and can undermine both this consistency and any computed radiation field.

Determine the radial form and its source strength

Define a causal distribution \(G(\mathbf R,\tau)\) solving \((\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_\tau^2)G=-\delta^3(\mathbf R)\delta(\tau)\) and vanishing for \(\tau<0\). In free space rotational symmetry makes the impulse response depend on \(R=|\mathbf R|\). For \(R>0\),

\[\nabla^2G=R^{-1}\partial_R^2(RG).\]

Thus \(RG\) satisfies the ordinary one-dimensional wave equation. The solution for an outward-only signal from the origin has the form \(g(\tau-R/c)\). An incoming term \(g_{\rm in}(\tau+R/c)\) would specify radiation arriving from infinity. It can occur with an incident field, but is excluded for the initially quiet source experiment.

To fix g, integrate the equation over a small sphere of radius a and pair its time dependence with a smooth test function. The flux of \(\nabla G\) through that sphere has leading term \(-4\pi g(\tau-a/c)\). Additional terms proportional to \(a\,g'\) tend to zero as distributions; the integral of the time derivative likewise vanishes as the sphere shrinks. The prescribed delta requires \(-4\pi g(\tau)\to-\delta(\tau)\). Consequently

\[G(\mathbf R,\tau)={\delta(\tau-R/c)\over4\pi R}.\]

This normalization depends on which operator was given unit source. If instead one uses \(\partial_t^2-c^2\nabla^2\), the corresponding unit impulse response carries an additional \(1/c^2\). The energy spreading as \(R^{-2}\) does not replace the field amplitude \(R^{-1}\); the two concern different observables. In three spatial dimensions the free-space impulse is concentrated on the light cone. This sharp shell property need not hold in a material with memory, a waveguide, or a two-dimensional scalar wave problem.

Perform the time integral before interpreting retardation

A distributed source is a superposition of shifted impulses. Convolve each charge or current component with G over \(\mathbf r'\) and \(t'\), keeping the observation point \(\mathbf r,t\) fixed. The delta sets \(t'=t-R/c\), with \(R=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\). Because \(t'\) occurs linearly for a fixed spatial source coordinate, this integration has unit Jacobian. The potentials are

\[\Phi(\mathbf r,t)={1\over4\pi\epsilon_0}\int {\rho(\mathbf r',t-R/c)\over R}\,d^3r',\]
\[\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)={\mu_0\over4\pi}\int {\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t-R/c)\over R}\,d^3r'.\]

Every source point is sampled at its own retarded time. Replacing the whole extended source by its history at \(t-r/c\) discards phase variation across it. This may be acceptable only when that variation is small or has been explicitly integrated. A charge distribution fixed in space and time recovers the ordinary Coulomb potential after the initial transient passes. The specified radiation condition selects a particular solution; a homogeneous incident pulse may be added if the actual experiment includes one.

For the \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention the frequency-domain kernel is \(e^{ikR}/(4\pi R)\) with \(k=\omega/c\). Substituting a harmonic source into the time-domain potential gives the positive outward radial phase directly. The incoming Helmholtz kernel \(e^{-ikR}/(4\pi R)\) solves the same differential equation away from the origin but has the opposite boundary condition at infinity. One physical check is that outgoing radiation transports energy through a large sphere away from the source.

Background · Why does an ideal line source leave a tail after the front?

The three-dimensional scalar retarded kernel for the wave operator \(c^{-2}\partial_t^2-\nabla^2\) has support on the light cone. Now switch on an impulse simultaneously along an ideal infinite straight line. At perpendicular distance \(\rho\), the nearest segment contributes first, at \(t=\rho/c\). More distant segments continue to arrive even though each point source emitted only once. Integrating the point kernel along the line gives, for \(t>\rho/c\),

\[G_{\rm line}(\rho,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta(t-\sqrt{\rho^2+z^2}/c)}{4\pi\sqrt{\rho^2+z^2}}\,dz=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{t^2-\rho^2/c^2}}.\]

Before the first arrival the integral vanishes. At later times its two contributing segments lie at \(z=\pm\sqrt{c^2t^2-\rho^2}\). This geometrical tail can be present even with a nondispersive propagation speed. A finite line length cuts it off when the contributions from the ends have arrived.

Jefimenko equations from differentiating the source clock

Let brackets denote source quantities at \(t_R=t-R/c\) and \(\widehat{\mathbf R}=(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')/R\). At fixed observation time,

\[\nabla R=\widehat{\mathbf R},\]
\[\nabla t_R=-\widehat{\mathbf R}/c.\]

Therefore differentiating \( [\rho]/R\) gives both a derivative of inverse distance and a derivative of charge history. Combining \(-\nabla\Phi\) with \(-\partial_t\mathbf A\) leads to

\[\mathbf E={1\over4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\left({[\rho]\widehat{\mathbf R}\over R^2}+{[\dot\rho]\widehat{\mathbf R}\over cR}-{[\dot{\mathbf J}]\over c^2R}\right)d^3r'.\]

Similarly take the curl of \([\mathbf J]/R\). The gradient of \(1/R\) crossed into J gives \([\mathbf J]\times\widehat{\mathbf R}/R^2\), while the retarded-time derivative gives \([\dot{\mathbf J}]\times\widehat{\mathbf R}/(cR)\). The terms individually contain charge and current derivatives, but exact continuity produces important cancellations. For example, a spherically symmetric source with conserved total charge cannot radiate a time-varying electric monopole in vacuum. Outside a symmetric redistribution Gauss's law fixes the radial field by the constant total charge. Predicting radiation from just one term of the retarded electric integral would obscure that cancellation.

Use delay and geometrical spreading as separate diagnostics

Take an ideal compact scalar emitter as a model of a single outgoing far-field polarization trace, with waveform \(s(t)=s_0 e^{-(t/T)^2}\). A receiver measuring that field with negligible bandwidth limitation reads \(C s(t-r/c)/r\). At 0.60 m the peak is delayed 2.00 ns and at 1.80 m it is delayed 6.00 ns after emission. The field peak falls by three, so power flux falls by nine. Multiplying each waveform by its distance and shifting its time origin back by \(r/c\) should collapse the two records. A mismatch in width after doing so suggests dispersion, source extent, detector bandwidth, multipath or near-field contributions. A single peak-height comparison does not distinguish these effects.

Suppose the emission has compact duration 4 ns and receivers are separated radially by 1.20 m. Their onset times differ by 4.00 ns. If identical receivers have a 1 ns electrical delay, their differential time of flight remains unchanged; if their amplifiers have different group delays, the difference directly biases the inferred propagation speed. A pair of distances can determine one offset and one speed if their absolute radii are known. Three or more distances also permit checking whether a straight-line timing model fits the data.

Translate and rescale two records of a compact retarded pulse

Keep the directional phase when the source is far away

Write \(\mathbf r=r\mathbf n\) with source extent d and \(r\gg d\). Expand the distance as \(R=r-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r'+[r'^2-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r')^2]/(2r)+\cdots\). Relative amplitude variation in \(1/R\) is order \(d/r\). The first directional phase \(k\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r'\) can remain large even when \(d/r\) is tiny. For a harmonic current,

\[\widetilde{\mathbf A}(\mathbf r)\simeq{\mu_0 e^{ikr}\over4\pi r}\int\widetilde{\mathbf J}(\mathbf r')e^{-ik\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r'}\,d^3r'.\]

Neglecting the next, quadratic, phase requires \(kd^2/r\) small up to a geometry-dependent coefficient. This is a separate far-zone condition from \(r\gg d\). The integral is a spatial Fourier component of the current, evaluated on the sphere of radius k. Measurements from one propagation direction reveal one projected spatial frequency and generally cannot reconstruct an arbitrary extended source.

For two equal emitters separated by d along z, observed at angle \(\theta\) to z, the two contributions differ by delay \(d\cos\theta/c\). In harmonic operation with equal drive phase the amplitude factor is \(2\cos(kd\cos\theta/2)\). At 1.00 GHz, \(d=0.150\) m is almost half a free-space wavelength. Along the source axis the two arrivals differ by 0.500 ns, nearly half a period, so the equal contributions cancel. Broadside they arrive together and add. For pulsed excitation with a duration much shorter than 0.500 ns, the end-on receiver sees two time-separated pulses instead of a permanent null across the whole spectrum. The time-domain and frequency-domain statements agree after applying the appropriate bandwidth.

Recover source time from a moving point instead of a fixed grid

The earlier time integral held the source coordinate fixed. A point following a trajectory contributes \(\rho(\mathbf r',t')=q\,\delta^3[\mathbf r'-\mathbf r_q(t')]\). If one instead integrates the spatial delta first, the remaining condition is

\[g(t')=t-t'-|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_q(t')|/c=0.\]

The source-to-observer distance now depends on the integration time. Its derivative is \(-1+\widehat{\mathbf R}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\), giving

\[\int f(t')\delta(g(t'))dt'={f(t_{\rm ret})\over1-\widehat{\mathbf R}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta_{\rm ret}} .\]

For subluminal motion this denominator is positive. It records how emission intervals map into observation intervals and must appear when evaluating retarded point-source potentials. Simply replacing position by retarded position in a static Coulomb formula misses this Jacobian. A charge moving towards a distant receiver compresses successive arrival intervals; transverse motion has no first-order time compression along that line of sight. Later units relate this observable to Doppler shifts and relativistic angular radiation patterns.

As a numerical example, two features emitted 10 ns apart by a compact laboratory source whose radial velocity component is \(0.20c\) arrive about 8.0 ns apart at a distant fixed receiver, provided the direction and velocity vary negligibly during the interval. The same proper-time interval of a fast moving clock would also require time dilation in converting to the laboratory emission interval. Keeping those two clocks separate avoids applying the Doppler factor twice.

A causal Green function in front of a grounded plane

The free-space Green function encodes an outgoing wave from a point impulse. A boundary changes the response and can be built into the Green function before integrating over sources. As an exactly soluble example choose the scalar wave equation in \(z>0\) with a homogeneous Dirichlet condition on \(z=0\). This is a scalar field problem, and can describe a selected tangential electric component in a geometry whose conducting boundary sets that component to zero. It is not a universal image rule for every vector potential component. The boundary conditions on electromagnetic scalar and vector potentials depend on the chosen gauge and physical surface.

Place a source at \(\mathbf s=(x_s,y_s,h)\) and its mirror at \(\mathbf s^*=(x_s,y_s,-h)\). Every point on the plane is equidistant from these points. Hence two opposite-sign delayed spherical responses cancel there at every time. The mirror source lies outside the physical domain and introduces no delta function inside it. Both contributions vanish before their respective light cones. Uniqueness of the retarded boundary-value problem identifies their difference as the required Green function.

\[G_D(\mathbf r,\mathbf s,t)=\frac{\delta(t-R/c)}{4\pi R}-\frac{\delta(t-R^*/c)}{4\pi R^*},\]
\[R=|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|,\]
\[R^*=|\mathbf r-\mathbf s^*|.\]

A receiver at height 0.40 m is displaced horizontally 1.20 m from an impulse source at height 0.30 m. The direct path is \(\sqrt{1.20^2+0.10^2}=1.204\) m and the reflected path is

\[\sqrt{1.20^2+0.70^2}=1.389\,\mathrm{m}.\]

The reflected pulse follows the direct one by 0.617 ns, with opposite sign and amplitude magnitude about 0.867 of the direct amplitude for this scalar geometrical model. A receiver that only rectifies the signal will lose this sign information. For two overlapping pulses their sign then still affects intensity, so rectification before adding the predicted pulses is incorrect.

For a scalar Neumann condition one adds the mirror instead. Although the two path lengths are identical at the boundary, their normal derivatives there are opposite, including the derivative of the retarded-time argument. Thus the sum has zero normal derivative. A rigid acoustic wall and a pressure-release surface furnish experimentally familiar examples of different scalar boundary rules, but the field variable must be named before assigning a reflection sign. Two surfaces can yield the same return time despite opposite returned amplitudes.

If a source is monochromatic, the two impulses become terms \(e^{ikR}/R\) and \(-e^{ikR^*}/R^*\). Where the amplitudes are nearly equal, a path difference equal to one wavelength gives nearly complete cancellation, while a half wavelength can give reinforcement because the boundary reversal supplies the other half cycle. The image solution therefore connects the time-domain echo test to a spatial fringe prediction. Moving the receiver a few centimetres may change interference dramatically without changing the material reflectance.

Can two receiver traces determine a short source waveform?

Let \(g(t)\) denote the waveform emitted by a small scalar source. After correcting a receiver's direct-path amplitude and arrival time, the ideal plane response above has the form \(y(t)=g(t)-b\,g(t-\Delta)\). Both \(b=R/R^*\) and \(\Delta=(R^*-R)/c\) are known from geometry. The transfer function for this normalised signal has modulus squared \(1+b^2-2b\cos\omega\Delta\). Frequencies with \(\omega\Delta=2\pi n\) have poor signal. Merely subtracting an independently smooth fitted baseline does not recover their phase.

For the preceding geometry \(b=0.867\), so response amplitude ranges from 0.133 to 1.867 times the corrected direct-path amplitude. Unregularised division at a minimum magnifies amplitude noise by about 7.5. The spacing between minima is roughly 1.62 GHz. A second receiver at a different height changes \(b\) and \(\Delta\). Choose a geometry for which its minima avoid the first receiver's vulnerable frequencies across the band of interest. Two receivers at symmetric points with equal path lengths give more counts but no new spectral constraint.

Given complex measurements \(Y_j(\omega)=H_j(\omega)G(\omega)+\eta_j\) with independent mean-square noise levels \(s_j^2\), minimise \(\sum_j|Y_j-H_jG|^2/s_j^2\) at each frequency. Differentiating in the real and imaginary parts of \(G\) gives

\[\widehat G=\frac{\sum_j H_j^*Y_j/s_j^2}{\sum_j|H_j|^2/s_j^2}.\]

The reciprocal denominator sets the estimated error variance under the model. If both instruments have a common phase calibration error it does not average down like independent noise. If both transfer functions are small, probabilistic regularisation or a finite source-support constraint may stabilise a fit, but the inferred high-frequency waveform then depends on the extra assumptions. Present the bandwidth over which the data genuinely constrain the result.

A clean time-domain cross-check is available when the pulse is shorter than the known path separation. Window the first arrival before any reflected contribution enters and predict both later echoes using the measured first pulse. If the first pulse is not fully separated, the retarded equation can still be solved forward in time by \(g(t)=y(t)+b\,g(t-\Delta)\), starting from a known zero prehistory. Its noise after many recurrences accumulates, particularly for \(b\) near one. A frequency fit and this delayed recursion use the same physics and have related conditioning. Agreement over the accurately measured band with disagreement confined to a spectral minimum identifies an inverse problem limitation before it is mistaken for a new source feature.

Worked propagation problem · a cell-centred retarded-time approximation

A compact source is represented by cubic cells of edge \(d=2.0\) mm at frequency 5.0 GHz. Each cell is replaced by its centre value in the far-field sum. Estimate the error for a cell with uniform complex current when observed along a grid axis. Distinguish this amplitude error from uncertainty in arrival time.

Integrate the phase across a cell before summing

Across one cell the leading far-zone phase is linear, \(e^{-ikx}\), for observation along the local \(x\) axis. Averaging exactly across its width multiplies the centre approximation by \(\sin(kd/2)/(kd/2)\). Here wavelength is approximately 60.0 mm and \(kd/2=0.1048\), giving a factor 0.99817 or about 0.18 percent amplitude reduction. The two transverse integrals have no phase variation in this particular direction. An oblique look multiplies three sinc factors evaluated at the separate direction-cosine components. Spatially varying current or strongly curved wavefronts require additional corrections.

The travel time changes by \(d/c=6.67\) ps across the cube along the axis. The centre convention therefore leaves a maximum individual delay discrepancy of 3.34 ps. Its effect on the amplitude of a symmetric uniform cell begins at second order because the first-order phase terms cancel. That cancellation can disappear for a strongly varying current or a clipped boundary cell. Adding the same delay bound over every cell in the source would grossly overestimate a coherent error; conversely, relative error in a near-null total field can be very large even when each absolute contribution is accurate.

Refine spatial resolution while separately refining interpolation of the source history at non-grid retarded times. The former controls geometric phase and boundary representation, the latter controls the recovered temporal spectrum. Compare a signed field at a generic angle, integrated radiated power and absolute field near an interference minimum. No single relative-error statistic is satisfactory for all three. Reporting a tiny percentage error for the total power can hide a phase error that materially changes the inverse source estimate at selected receivers.

How a finite measurement surface can test retardation

A closed surface of time-resolved field probes can test a proposed source history against local energy conservation. Integrate the measured outward Poynting vector over the surface at each receiver time. Between two concentric surfaces in free space with no charges, the difference between outward powers equals the rate at which field energy in the intervening shell changes, with the sign determined by whether the shell is filling or emptying. During a leading pulse edge the outward flux at the smaller sphere can exceed that at the larger one without any violation of conservation.

With a compact central source and far-zone probes, rescale radiation amplitudes by radius and shift their signals by the radial travel-time difference before imposing pointwise agreement. For a radial separation of thirty centimetres the shift is approximately one nanosecond. A modest timing mismatch can produce an apparent amplitude discrepancy on a steep edge even when both probes are correctly calibrated. Integrating through the complete pulse removes that sensitivity if the integration windows include its entire support.

Residual disagreement can identify omitted directional dependence, environmental reflections or nonradiative near-field terms. Repeat at increasing radii and compare their scaling. A coherent field proportional to inverse squared radius becomes relatively weaker compared with the outgoing radiation, while a reflected contribution may vary nonmonotonically as phase changes. A delayed free-space Green function makes distinct predictions for these different tests.

Tutorial · infer an unknown delay and test the amplitude law

A detector moved successively to 0.45, 0.90 and 1.35 m records field-peak times of 2.20, 3.70 and 5.20 ns and field amplitudes 6.0, 3.0 and 2.2 mV/m. Estimate speed and common timing offset. Which amplitude is least consistent with a single outgoing far field, and what additional observation could distinguish an additive background from a receiver gain change?

Fit the delay and audit the independent product

Each 0.45 m step adds 1.50 ns, giving \(v=3.00\times10^8\) m/s. Extrapolation to r=0 gives 0.70 ns offset. The products rE are 2.70, 2.70 and 2.97 mV, with the last 10% higher than the common value. A stationary additive background field could interfere coherently with the local signal; a gain error would scale the waveform. A source-off record tests an additive background under the same trigger. Moving the detector back to the first radius or swapping two calibrated receivers tests whether the excess follows the position or the instrument. Arrival-time agreement alone cannot verify absolute amplitude calibration.

Tutorial · decide when one common retarded time suffices

A 60 mm source stands 3.0 m from a receiver near broadside. It radiates near 20 GHz in vacuum. Bound the relative amplitude variation from replacing \(1/R\) by \(1/r\). For a source lying in a transverse disk of radius 30 mm, estimate the largest omitted quadratic phase when evaluating a far-field integral. Would neglecting the entire directional phase be justified for observation 20° away from broadside?

Compare three different expansion parameters

A conservative bound using the full size is order \(0.060/3=0.020\); the actual broadside amplitude variation across a transverse disk is much smaller. At the rim the omitted optical path is approximately \(0.030^2/(2\cdot3)=0.00015\) m. Wavelength is about 15.0 mm, so the omitted phase is 0.063 rad. Off broadside along a diameter the retained linear path difference over 60 mm is \(0.060\sin20^\circ=0.0205\) m, or 8.60 rad. Constant amplitude and linearized phase can therefore be excellent approximations while treating all parts of the source as simultaneous at the receiver is seriously wrong.
