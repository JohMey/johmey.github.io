A guided mode, a cavity line and the energy they carry

A microwave pulse sent through a rectangular guide arrives late even though the guide is evacuated. Terminating the guide converts the travelling pattern into a family of resonances. Use the same transverse eigenproblem to predict its cutoff, pulse delay, cavity frequency and measured linewidth.

State the longitudinal dependence before choosing a transverse basis

Let a hollow rectangular perfect-conductor guide occupy \(0<x<a,\ 0<y<b\) and extend in z. Fill it uniformly with a lossless dielectric. A single-frequency field varies as \(e^{i\beta z-i\omega t}\). In the interior each Cartesian component satisfies the Helmholtz equation. Separating the z dependence leaves the transverse equation \(\nabla_t^2F+k_c^2 F=0\), with \(k_c^2=k^2-\beta^2,\ k=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}\). Conducting walls impose zero tangential E at the perimeter. They do not impose zero H there, since surface current can flow and supply the required magnetic discontinuity.

A transverse-magnetic (TM) mode has \(H_z=0\) and uses \(E_z\) as its longitudinal scalar. Because z lies tangential to every side wall, \(E_z=0\) there. The allowed nonzero separable solutions are sine-sine products with positive integers m,n. A transverse-electric (TE) mode has \(E_z=0\) and uses \(H_z\) with vanishing outward normal derivative on the side walls. Its scalar modes are cosine-cosine products. One index may be zero but both cannot be zero in this hollow single-conductor guide. The apparent constant \(H_z\) TE00 solution fails to reconstruct a nonzero propagating transverse field from Maxwell's equations. A TEM mode can exist in a coaxial line with two conductors maintained at different potentials. A connected perfect conductor surrounding empty simply connected space admits only constant electrostatic boundary potential, precluding a nontrivial transverse potential difference for TEM.

\[k_{c,mn}^2=(m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2,\]
\[\beta^2=\omega^2\epsilon\mu-k_{c,mn}^2,\]
\[f_{c,mn}={1\over2\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}\sqrt{(m/a)^2+(n/b)^2}.\]

The cutoff emerges from the room a transverse standing field needs to fit between walls. It is a condition on frequency, geometry and filling material together. Below cutoff a decaying longitudinal solution can still store near fields and mediate coupling over finite lengths. A reading on the far side of a short below-cutoff section therefore need not be exactly zero.

Reconstruct one measurable field from the vector equations

Take \(a>b\) and the lowest TE10 mode. A useful choice is \(\widetilde E_y=E_0\sin(\pi x/a)e^{i\beta z}\), with \(E_x=E_z=0\). It vanishes at x walls as required. At the y walls \(E_y\) is normal and can end on surface charge. Use \(\nabla\times\widetilde{\mathbf E}=i\omega\mu\widetilde{\mathbf H}\) to reconstruct both magnetic components:

\[\widetilde H_x=-{\beta\over\omega\mu}\widetilde E_y,\]
\[\widetilde H_z=-{i\pi E_0\over\omega\mu a}\cos(\pi x/a)e^{i\beta z}.\]

Ampère's law then reproduces the dispersion relation. The negative x direction of H for positive E_y and positive \(\beta\) gives positive \(S_z\). The TE wave impedance is \(Z_{\rm TE}=\omega\mu/\beta\) using transverse magnitudes. Unlike a plane-wave impedance in a homogeneous bulk medium, this one changes with frequency even when \(\epsilon,\mu\) are constant. At cutoff transverse magnetic H_x vanishes for a fixed finite E_y, while H_z remains finite. This is why large field amplitude near cutoff need not imply substantial transmitted axial power.

Integrate power through the cross-section using \(\langle S_z\rangle=\beta|\widetilde E_y|^2/(2\omega\mu)\). The integral of \(\sin^2(\pi x/a)\) is \(a/2\). The mode therefore carries \(P=ab\,|E_0|^2/(4Z_{\rm TE})\). Using simply the area times the central peak flux would overestimate power by a factor of two. A small receiving probe samples a spatially weighted field. Its calibration can be checked by moving it from the centre to x=a/6, where field amplitude halves and local axial flux falls by a factor of four.

Compute two velocities and check them by energy transport

With bulk phase speed \(v=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}\), the dispersion relation is

\[\omega^2=v^2(\beta^2+k_c^2).\]

Taking the frequency-to-wavenumber ratio and its derivative gives

\[v_{\rm ph}={\omega\over\beta}={v\over\sqrt{1-(\omega_c/\omega)^2}},\]
\[v_g={d\omega\over d\beta}=v\sqrt{1-(\omega_c/\omega)^2},\]
\[v_{\rm ph}v_g=v^2 .\]

Phase fronts can move faster than the bulk wave speed along the guide. They are surfaces of equal phase and carry no independent message. A narrowband unattenuated envelope travels at the smaller group speed. An energy calculation provides a second route to the same quantity. Insert all three TE10 field components into \(\overline u=(\epsilon|\widetilde E|^2+\mu|\widetilde H|^2)/4\), integrate over the cross-section, and use \(\beta^2+(\pi/a)^2=\omega^2\epsilon\mu\). Electric and magnetic energies per length are each \(\epsilon ab|E_0|^2/8\). Dividing P by total energy per length yields

\[\beta/(\omega\epsilon\mu)=v_g.\]

Keeping only the transverse magnetic component would spoil the equality.

For an evacuated guide \(a=30.0\) mm, \(b=12.0\) mm, \(f_{c,10}=4.997\) GHz. At 8.00 GHz, \(\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}=0.781\), \(v_g=2.34\times10^8\) m/s and guide wavelength \(2\pi/\beta=48.0\) mm. A 2 m section gives 8.54 ns group delay, compared with 6.67 ns in bulk vacuum. TE20 turns on at 9.99 GHz and TE01 at 12.49 GHz, so the chosen frequency is inside the single-propagating-mode band. With central electric peak 100 V/m, \(Z_{\rm TE}\simeq482\) Ω and \(P\simeq1.87\) mW. Knowing frequency is essential to infer power from the same central probe in another band.

Close the guide and quantize its longitudinal phase

Add perfect end walls at z=0 and z=L. Tangential E_y must vanish at each end, so superpose equal counterpropagating modes into \(\sin(p\pi z/L)\) with \(p=1,2,\ldots\) for this TE10 family. The resonance frequencies become

\[f_{10p}={v\over2}\sqrt{a^{-2}+(p/L)^2}.\]

The common estimate \(c/(2L)\) is the free spectral range for a nondispersive one-dimensional TEM cavity. It omits the transverse wavenumber in this example. For the guide above closed at \(L=60\) mm, \(f_{101}=5.59\) GHz and \(f_{102}=7.07\) GHz. Their separation is about 1.48 GHz, considerably smaller than the nominal 2.50 GHz TEM spacing. At sufficiently high longitudinal order the separation approaches \(v/(2L)\), though other transverse families can then populate the spectrum.

The mode's field has nodes and antinodes in two coordinates. A tiny probe on an electric node may leave a resonance undetected, and moving a lossy probe can also shift its frequency and damping. Two nearby spectral peaks could be adjacent p indices, two different transverse modes or a degeneracy split by manufacturing errors. Compare measured spatial patterns and polarization selection rules before indexing solely by a frequency spacing.

Derive amplitude decay, stored-energy decay and linewidth

In practice wall conduction, dielectric loss and deliberate port coupling all remove stored energy. If a lightly damped mode retains the same shape as it decays, energy obeys \(dU/dt=-\kappa U\). Then \(U=U_0e^{-\kappa t}\), field amplitude falls as \(e^{-\kappa t/2}\), and loaded quality factor is

\[Q_L=\omega_0/\kappa.\]

Energy lost per cycle is approximately \(2\pi U/Q_L\) for small fractional loss. The exact integrated energy lost over a cycle is \(U[1-e^{-2\pi/Q_L}]\). These expressions distinguish a rate in radians per second from linewidth in ordinary hertz.

Write a complex amplitude in a frame rotating at \(\omega_0\). Under a weak frequency-swept harmonic drive its steady amplitude is proportional to \([\,\kappa/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)\,]^{-1}\). The power response is a Lorentzian. Its half-maximum points have \(|\omega-\omega_0|=\kappa/2\), so the full power width is \(\kappa\) in angular frequency, or \(f_0/Q_L\) in hertz. A field-amplitude reading falls to \(1/\sqrt2\) at those same frequencies. Half the field magnitude corresponds to a different bandwidth.

Consider \(f_0=6.00\) GHz and \(Q_L=6000\). The power-line FWHM is 1.00 MHz, the energy e-folding time \(1/\kappa=159\) ns, and the field e-folding time 318 ns. Observe a field e-folding time of 159 ns instead, and the inferred Q would be 3000, even though the number printed on the oscilloscope is unchanged. Always determine whether an instrument's output voltage is proportional to field, instantaneous power or a filtered logarithm of either.

Separate a feed port from dissipative losses

Normalize a mode amplitude a so \(|a|^2=U\), and an incident port amplitude \(s_+\) so \(|s_+|^2=P_{\rm in}\). In a simple one-port cavity the internal loss rate \(\kappa_i\) and external escape rate \(\kappa_e\) add. A consistent choice of port phase gives

\[\dot a=[i\Delta-(\kappa_i+\kappa_e)/2]a+\sqrt{\kappa_e}s_+,\]
\[s_-=s_+-\sqrt{\kappa_e}a.\]

At steady on-resonance drive the reflected amplitude fraction is \((\kappa_i-\kappa_e)/(\kappa_i+\kappa_e)\). Exact cancellation at critical coupling is compatible with continued reflection when driving off resonance, and with finite resonant energy stored in the cavity. Once the drive is removed that energy decays through both channels.

With rates \(\kappa_i/(2\pi)=0.30\) MHz and \(\kappa_e/(2\pi)=0.70\) MHz, the resonant reflected power fraction is 0.16. The remaining 84% of the steady incoming power is dissipated internally in this one-port model. Interchanging the two rates preserves both the linewidth and reflected power at resonance but reverses the reflected phase. A calibrated coherent reflection or a second measurement after deliberately changing coupling can resolve this ambiguity.

Use a small bead to map a cavity mode

A resonator offers a way to measure a field without placing a conventional field sensor at its maximum. Insert a small dielectric perturbation, record the change in resonance frequency, then scan its position. The derivation requires a closed, lossless cavity or a sufficiently high-Q nearly closed cavity for which one isolated mode is meaningful. Take fixed conducting walls, nondispersive permittivity and permeability, and a perturbation small enough to change the frequency and the shape smoothly. A nearby degeneracy requires a coupled-mode treatment.

In the source-free eigenproblem \(\nabla\times\mu^{-1}\nabla\times\mathbf E=\omega^2\epsilon\mathbf E\), multiply by the complex conjugate of the unperturbed mode and integrate over the volume. Subtract the conjugate unperturbed equation multiplied by the perturbed mode. Integration by parts transfers the double curl with no surface contribution at a fixed ideal conducting wall. Expand \(\omega^2\) and \(\epsilon+\delta\epsilon\) through first order. The unknown first-order change of mode shape cancels from the difference, leaving

\[\frac{\delta\omega}{\omega}=-\frac12\frac{\int\delta\epsilon(\mathbf r)|\mathbf E(\mathbf r)|^2\,dV}{\int\epsilon(\mathbf r)|\mathbf E(\mathbf r)|^2\,dV}.\]

A positive permittivity increment lowers the resonance frequency. The denominator is proportional to total electric energy for a nondispersive mode, and the fractional shift is the electric-energy overlap with the perturbation. The result describes a small perturbation of the permittivity distribution, not automatically a high-contrast conducting bead. A high-index sphere changes its own internal field even when it occupies a tiny volume; its electrostatic polarizability can then replace the simple weak-contrast volume approximation. The distinction matters if the shift is used for absolute calibration.

Suppose a weak dielectric insert raises local permittivity by 10.0 percent and covers a region containing 0.40 percent of the unperturbed electric energy. At 3.00 GHz the predicted fractional shift is \(-0.00020\), or \(-600\) kHz. Moving it to a position where the electric amplitude is half as large gives a 150 kHz shift for the same orientation, volume and local background. A map of resonance displacement measures squared field magnitude, so by itself it cannot determine the sign of an oscillating electric component. Two neighbouring antinodes separated by a nodal plane can produce nearly equal shifts with opposite electric phases.

A mode with loaded \(Q=3000\) at 3.00 GHz has a full linewidth of about 1.00 MHz. Its centre can still be fitted more accurately than the linewidth if signal-to-noise ratio and frequency stability are adequate. Record an unloaded baseline between bead positions and keep the probe coupling fixed. A systematic drift of 50 kHz is a small part of a linewidth but already one third of the expected shift at the lower-field position. If two modes draw close when the bead is moved, fitting one Lorentzian over their sum can imitate a position-dependent loss as well as a frequency shift.

A finite below-cutoff length is a boundary problem

A guide section whose infinite-guide propagation constant is imaginary does not simply have zero field. It supports decaying and growing spatial solutions. In a semi-infinite region an outgoing bounded solution picks the decaying branch. A finite narrowed section joined to a second propagating guide needs both branches to satisfy the two interfaces. This is the electromagnetic version of transmitting through a classically forbidden interval in a one-dimensional wave equation. No energy flows out of an isolated purely decaying transverse mode of the uniform lossless section, yet a properly driven combination can carry power through the interval.

Write a scalar model of one isolated mode as \(\psi''+k^2\psi=0\) in identical leads and \(\psi''-\kappa^2\psi=0\) in the central interval of length \(d\). Assume continuity of \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) at both seams. This mimics a single matched constitutive parameter and deliberately neglects higher transverse modes excited at a real step. Propagation of the pair \((\psi,\psi')\) across the barrier uses hyperbolic functions. Matching it to an incident plus reflected wave on the left and one transmitted wave on the right gives

\[T=\left[1+\frac{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2(\kappa d)\right]^{-1}.\]

At \(k=\kappa\) this simplifies to \(T=\mathrm{sech}^2(\kappa d)\). For \(\kappa=40\) per metre and lengths 25 and 50 mm, the transmitted powers are 0.420 and 0.0707 times incident. Doubling length does not square the transmission, because the reflected wave and the coupling at both interfaces matter. At large \(\kappa d\) an exponential remains, multiplied by a matching-dependent prefactor. Extrapolating a long-section straight line on a log-power plot to zero length should therefore not be interpreted as an additional physical loss in a zero-length guide.

For a real device use modal matching, or a numerical Maxwell solution retaining evanescent transverse modes at a discontinuity. Check convergence against the number of included modes at each step, not just the resolution along the uniform section. Measurements with several lengths can separate the uniform attenuation constant from entrance and exit effects. Measuring reflection as well as transmission discriminates a lossless cutoff barrier, where rejected energy returns to the source, from an absorber that dissipates it. Finally use a time-domain envelope narrow enough in frequency that the fraction above the cutoff is negligible. A small above-cutoff spectral tail can dominate a pulse recorded after a long barrier and give a misleading apparent decay length.

Worked resonance problem · a square guide with a slight width error

A nominally square air-filled guide of side 20.0 mm supports two orthogonal fundamental patterns with identical cutoff in the ideal square section. The fabricated horizontal dimension is 20.10 mm; the other remains 20.00 mm. Estimate the two cutoffs and the polarisation beating length at 10.0 GHz. How could a single scalar power measurement miss the effect?

Follow the phases of the two modes

For the rectangular TE modes with one half-cycle across either side, the cutoffs are \(c/(2a)\) and \(c/(2b)\). These are 7.458 and 7.495 GHz, separated by about 37 MHz. At 10.0 GHz the longitudinal propagation constants are \(k_{zj}=(2\pi/c)\sqrt{f^2-f_{cj}^2}\). Their difference is about 0.88 per metre, corresponding to a full relative phase cycle over approximately 7.1 m. It takes only one quarter of this length to build a quarter-cycle retardance between equally excited components.

Near cutoff the propagation-constant sensitivity to a small width change is enhanced because the longitudinal wavenumber appears in the denominator of its derivative. Approaching the higher cutoff too closely also makes the interpretation vulnerable to wall loss, poorly matched launchers and higher-order evanescent contributions at the ends. Above both cutoffs in a sufficiently long uniform section, project the exit field on a pair of known orthogonal receiving patterns and measure their coherent phase difference. Two power readings along the eigenaxes remain unchanged in the lossless model even while a diagonal analyser shows oscillating power.

Rotating the input launcher onto one eigenpattern should suppress the beating, since no second component then accumulates a relative phase. Repeating the experiment at several frequencies tests the predicted dispersion of \(k_{z1}-k_{z2}\), distinguishing a distributed width mismatch from a nearly constant phase offset in two receiver cables. A slow twist of the guide can additionally couple the patterns instead of merely advancing their phases. In that situation first measure the full two-by-two complex transfer matrix; treating every disappearing component as attenuation would misidentify coherent mode conversion.

A vanishing resonance can be a coupling zero

A cavity spectrum measures modes through a particular feed and pickup. For a short aligned electric probe, coupling to a mode is proportional at leading order to the projection of the modal electric field onto the probe's current distribution. A probe at a nodal plane or oriented perpendicular to that field can therefore fail to display a well-defined cavity mode. Moving the receiver away from the node or rotating it can restore the spectral line without changing the cavity eigenfrequency.

As a quantitative illustration consider a mode whose electric field varies as a sine across the width. Move a small probe from one quarter of that width to one twelfth. The ratio of its squared coupling magnitudes is approximately 0.134. With an unchanged weak drive and an effectively nonloading pickup, the transmitted power in that mode can shrink by almost an order of magnitude. If the same port also substantially loads the cavity, its movement changes the linewidth and the resonant intracavity field as well.

A mapping experiment should record peak frequency, linewidth and integrated spectral response alongside peak power. A frequency shift with little change in linewidth supports a predominantly reactive perturbation, whereas linewidth changes signal modified damping or port coupling. A changing peak alone cannot distinguish a weak field region, a rotated polarisation and an altered loaded resonance. Combining two feed orientations with a weak scanning pickup supplies a more informative field map.

Tutorial · infer guide width from a delay without assuming a vacuum transit

A pulse at 9.00 GHz traverses 1.50 m of evacuated rectangular guide in 6.00 ns after cable delays are removed. Assume a narrowband TE10 packet with negligible loss and \(b=10.0\) mm. Infer its cutoff and guide width, check the next two candidate cutoffs and estimate its modal impedance.

Invert the dispersion and verify the mode assignment

The group speed is \(2.50\times10^8\) m/s, or 0.834 of c. Hence \(f_c=f\sqrt{1-(v_g/c)^2}\simeq4.97\) GHz and \(a=c/(2f_c)\simeq30.2\) mm. TE20 cuts on at about 9.94 GHz and TE01 at 15.0 GHz, both above the test. The TE impedance is \(Z_0/(v_g/c)\simeq452\) Ω. A broad pulse overlapping 9.94 GHz would require a mode-launch and dispersion analysis before interpreting one transit time with this inversion. Measuring delay at a second centre frequency tests the inferred cutoff without another width parameter.

Tutorial · separate an internal Q from a port Q

A one-port cavity has \(f_0=5.00\) GHz and measured power linewidth 0.80 MHz. Halving the port coupling rate with negligible resonance shift reduces linewidth to 0.60 MHz. Infer both initial rates in hertz, both quality factors, the initial resonant reflected fraction and the energy lifetime after the coupling change.

Combine the rate sums from both tests

Let \(g_i=\kappa_i/(2\pi)\) and \(g_e=\kappa_e/(2\pi)\). Solve \(g_i+g_e=0.80,\ g_i+g_e/2=0.60\) MHz to get \(g_e=g_i=0.40\) MHz. Both initial individual quality factors are \(5.00{\rm\,GHz}/0.40{\rm\,MHz}=12500\), and initial loaded Q is 6250. Initial reflection vanishes by critical coupling. After the change the resonant reflected amplitude is 1/3 and reflected power 1/9. The energy lifetime is

\[1/(2\pi\,0.60{\rm\,MHz})=265\,\mathrm{ns}.\]

This result relies on a fixed internal loss. A coupler adjustment that introduces dielectric loss would invalidate the two-equation inference.
