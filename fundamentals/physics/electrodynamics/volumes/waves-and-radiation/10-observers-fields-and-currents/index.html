What different observers measure

A moving observer carries clocks, rulers and a calibrated test charge. A statement about the electric field must specify which observer held that test charge at rest. In this chapter we derive how different measurements can agree on the force law and yet assign different electric fields, magnetic fields and charge densities to the same physical apparatus. A current-carrying wire and a light pulse will provide independent checks.

Events, synchronisation and directed boosts

Use two inertial coordinate systems with aligned axes. The primed origin moves at speed \(v\) in the positive laboratory \(x\) direction. Choose coincident origins when both clocks read zero and synchronise distant clocks in each frame by light signals. Homogeneity makes the transformation linear. The worldline of the primed origin requires

\[x'=A(x-vt).\]

A light ray moving along either axial direction must still obey \(x'=\pm ct'\). Substituting its two possible worldlines into the linear map fixes the relative coefficients of \(x\) and \(t\) in \(t'\). Reversing the transformation by changing the sign of \(v\) fixes the remaining scale.

\[x'=\gamma(x-vt),\]
\[t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2),\]
\[y'=y,\]
\[z'=z,\]
\[\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.\]

The squared interval \(c^2\Delta t^2-|\Delta\mathbf r|^2\) is unchanged, a direct algebra check that detects a surprising number of sign choices. Two sparks separated by 3.0 m along a laboratory bench and simultaneous on laboratory clocks have \(\Delta t'=-7.51\) ns for \(v=0.60c\), if the second event is at larger \(x\). Their ordering in this frame is consistent with their spacelike separation. Events joined by a light signal are null-separated and retain their order under this boost.

Length measurements need two simultaneous endpoint readings in the chosen frame. A bar at rest in the lab and parallel to the axis has length \(L/\gamma\) in the primed frame. Substituting the simultaneous laboratory endpoint events into the boost instead gives \(\gamma L\), but their primed times differ. This distinction reappears when we count charge per unit length in a moving wire. The result of that count depends on the spacetime slice used to perform it.

A current is a rate of charge crossing a surface

Begin with a single population of particles, each carrying charge \(q\), whose proper number density is \(n_0\). Their counting volume is Lorentz-contracted only along their motion. The lab assigns density \(n=\gamma_u n_0\) and current density \(q n\mathbf u\). Both are components of \(j^\mu=q n_0 u^\mu\), where \(u^\mu=\gamma_u(c,\mathbf u)\) is the four-velocity. Independent species can be superposed. A total four-current can consequently be spacelike even though every material particle moves more slowly than light.

\[\rho'=\gamma(\rho-vJ_x/c^2),\]
\[J'_x=\gamma(J_x-v\rho),\]
\[J'_y=J_y,\]
\[J'_z=J_z .\]

The usual continuity law becomes \(\partial_\mu j^\mu=0\) with \(\partial_0=c^{-1}\partial_t\). Transformation of the derivative and of the current cancels exactly in this contraction. An isolated finite charge distribution has an observer-independent total charge if each observer integrates through a complete spacelike slice. One way to see this is to connect the two slices by a large timelike enclosing wall. Four-dimensional flux conservation sets the difference between the two charge integrals equal to the current crossing the wall. For a wall outside all the charge trajectories that last contribution vanishes.

For a charge-neutral straight wire carrying conventional current \(I=2.0\) A along \(+x\), a frame moving parallel to the wire at \(0.20c\) assigns line density \(\lambda'=-\gamma vI/c^2=-1.36\) nC/m. Transverse cross-section is unchanged. There is no requirement that electrons themselves travel at \(0.20c\). Both the mobile and immobile species contribute to the transformed density. The ideal wire is infinitely long, so one cannot integrate its transformed charge density out to infinity and apply the finite isolated system argument.

Obtaining field components from measured forces

Place a test charge at rest at an event in the primed frame. In the lab it has velocity \(v\mathbf e_x\) and force \(q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\). To transform the force, transform the corresponding momentum change. For a small transverse kick, \(dp'_y=dp_y\) while \(dt'=dt/\gamma\) on the test charge's path. Thus \(F'_y=\gamma F_y\) and similarly for \(z\). For the parallel component use \(dp'_x=\gamma(dp_x-v\,d\mathcal E/c^2)\) and \(d\mathcal E/dt=vF_x\). The result is

\[F'_x=F_x.\]

Dividing by \(q\) gives the electric field. Requiring the same force law for charges moving transversely, or transforming the magnetic flux measured by a moving loop, gives the magnetic field.

\[\mathbf E'_\parallel=\mathbf E_\parallel,\]
\[\mathbf B'_\parallel=\mathbf B_\parallel,\]
\[\mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)_\perp,\]
\[\mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf B-\mathbf v\times\mathbf E/c^2)_\perp .\]

Imagine a uniform \(+y\) electric field of 90.0 kV/m and a \(+z\) magnetic field with \(cB=150\) kV/m. For a boost along \(+x\), the \(y\) component of \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\) is negative. At \(\beta=0.60\) the electric field vanishes. The remaining magnetic field has \(cB'=1.25(150-54)=120\) kV/m or 0.400 mT. A particle comoving with that frame feels no force at that event. A different particle moving across the magnetic field can still curve.

Vary the observer speed and compare the electric and magnetic field invariants

Compare two force measurements near an ideal wire

Return to the 2.0 A wire and put the observation point 1.0 cm along \(+y\). Ampère's law gives \(B_z=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)=40.0\) microtesla. The lab radial electric field is zero in this idealised symmetric model. A positive test charge moving along \(+x\) at \(0.20c\) experiences an inward magnetic force per unit charge of 2.40 kV/m. In its momentary rest frame the wire's negative line charge produces an inward radial electric field. The field from Gauss's law is \(|\lambda'|/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)=2.45\) kV/m. The difference in the reported transverse forces is precisely the factor \(\gamma=1.0206\) derived from the momentum kick.

There is no contradiction between a field with zero laboratory divergence outside the wire and a different electric field in the primed frame. The charge density at the wire itself also transforms. If the laboratory wire carries a real driving surface charge and a return conductor, add their fields and charges before transforming. A claimed measurement of this ideal result without specifying the return path and electrostatic shielding needs a separate model for those additional fields.

When can a field be made purely electric or magnetic?

Direct substitution in the transverse transformation leaves the scalars \(B^2-E^2/c^2\) and \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) unchanged. The first is a difference, so retain its sign. If the second is nonzero, no observer sees either field vanish. If the fields are orthogonal and \(E<cB\), choose \(\mathbf v=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). The vector identity for \((\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B\) cancels \(\mathbf E\), and the required speed is below \(c\). With \(E>cB\), choosing \(c^2\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/E^2\) instead cancels \(\mathbf B\).

When both invariants vanish one must still distinguish a field identically zero from a nonzero null field. A vacuum travelling plane wave satisfies \(E=cB\) and orthogonality everywhere. The advertised boost would then require speed \(c\). An observer can lower its measured frequency and amplitude continuously by following the wave at increasing speed but can never transform away its nonzero field. A superposition of waves need not be null. A standing wave has positions and times where only one of the fields is present.

Phase matching across inertial frames

Every observer agrees on whether a particular spacetime event is a crest. The phase \(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t\) therefore labels the same event in each frame. Substituting the inverse coordinate transformation gives the transformation of frequency and wavevector. For vacuum propagation at laboratory angle \(\theta\) to the boost, divide the transformed longitudinal wavenumber by the transformed magnitude.

\[\omega'=\gamma\omega(1-\beta\cos\theta),\]
\[\cos\theta'=\frac{\cos\theta-\beta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}.\]

An observer chasing a collinear 600 nm light wave at \(0.60c\) sees half its frequency and a wavelength of 1200 nm. A beam entering at \(90^\circ\) to the boost appears at \(\cos\theta'=-0.60\) and frequency \(1.25\omega\). Asking for a transverse Doppler shift without saying in whose frame the angle is a right angle leaves the problem under-specified. A detector counts phase fronts crossing its own trajectory and its own clock sets the elapsed time.

A boosted light beam also transports momentum

Frequency alone does not specify the energy measured by a moving detector. Its collecting surface and clock must be assigned to an observer. For a monochromatic collimated beam in vacuum, one route is to transform both fields and calculate their new Poynting vector. Another route is to transform the stress-energy tensor, whose energy density, momentum density and momentum flux describe the same field together. This second route shows explicitly which measured quantity is being transformed and extends to superposed beams with zero mean momentum in one frame.

For a right-moving plane wave along \(x\), the energy density is \(u=S_x/c\), momentum density is \(u/c\) and normal momentum flux is \(u\). In coordinates with \(x^0=ct\), the relevant block has \(T^{00}=T^{01}=T^{10}=T^{11}=u\). Boost both indices using the same Lorentz matrix as for coordinates. The transformed energy density follows directly:

\[T'^{00}=\gamma^2(T^{00}-2\beta T^{01}+\beta^2T^{11})=\gamma^2(1-\beta)^2u .\]

The frequency Doppler factor enters twice. One factor corresponds to lower energy per photon and one to the changed number per unit volume. For a finite pulse, its length at a common primed time grows for an observer following it, so the total energy changes by only one Doppler factor. Volumes measured between equal-time spatial endpoints must be computed on the appropriate observer's simultaneity slice, just as for a moving rod.

Now superpose two equal-intensity mutually incoherent plane waves in opposite axial directions. In the laboratory \(T^{01}=0\), while \(T^{11}=T^{00}=u\) for the combined radiation. An observer moving at \(0.60c\) obtains \(u'=\gamma^2(1+\beta^2)u=2.125u\), with net flux \(S'_x=-2\gamma^2\beta\,cu=-1.875\,cu\). The counterpropagating beam that the observer meets head-on is blueshifted, while the other is redshifted. Energy density remains greater than the magnitude of momentum density times \(c\), as required for a superposition of distinct propagation directions. This check fails if one transforms a zero Poynting vector as though it were the spatial component of a four-vector with no stress information.

The direction-independent radiation field in an ideal cavity has \(T^{ii}=u/3\) for each spatial axis in its rest frame rather than \(T^{11}=u\). The one-third comes from averaging \(n_i^2\) over a sphere. The moving observer then sees \(u'=\gamma^2u(1+\beta^2/3)\) and a net longitudinal flux. At \(\beta=0.60\) the energy-density factor is 1.75. An isotropic thermal field becomes directionally distorted; assigning one Doppler factor to the whole sky would give neither the correct integrated energy density nor its directional spectrum.

A moving parallel-plate capacitor tests surface sources

Choose large parallel conducting plates at \(z=\pm d/2\) carrying opposite uniform surface densities \(\pm\sigma\). Ignore edge effects in the central region. In the lab the field between them has magnitude \(E_0=\sigma/\epsilon_0\) and there is no magnetic field. A frame boosted along \(+x\) sees both plates move at \(-v\). Their areas contract along \(x\), so their surface charge magnitudes become \(\gamma\sigma\); their equal and opposite surface currents point in opposite senses. Use Gauss's law and the magnetic jump condition on each sheet to reconstruct the transformed fields. The current sheets reinforce magnetically between the plates and cancel outside, just as electric sheets of opposite charge do electrostatically.

For laboratory electric field along \(+z\), the moving observer has \(E'_z=\gamma E_0\) and \(B'_y=+\gamma vE_0/c^2\). Cross the primed plate velocity \(-v\mathbf e_x\) with this magnetic field to find a force along \(-z\) on a positive surface charge, partly cancelling the enhanced electric attraction towards the opposite plate. When finding the force on a sheet, use the fields of the other sheet or the arithmetic mean of the two limiting fields. Substituting the total interior field without removing the self contribution would double the result.

At \(E_0=2.00\) MV/m and \(\beta=0.60\), the primed interior field is 2.50 MV/m and its magnetic field 5.00 mT. Per positive surface charge the transverse attraction from the opposite sheet has magnitude \(\gamma E_0(1-\beta^2)/2=E_0/(2\gamma)\). This is 0.800 MV/m, compared with 1.00 MV/m in the laboratory. The transformation factor is inverse \(\gamma\) because the charge in this example is at rest in the original laboratory; the earlier force derivation placed the test charge at rest in the primed frame. Interchanging those initial conditions without changing the time conversion reverses the factor.

Multiplying the primed force per charge by the larger surface density \(\gamma\sigma\) restores the same force per transverse plate area in this tangential boost. The cancellation supplies a local cross-check among length contraction, field transformation and mechanical force. A complete calculation of the total energy or momentum of an accelerated finite capacitor must also account for stresses in its supports and for the fields near its edges. Taking the uniform central field times a transformed volume is adequate for a local comparison, but does not by itself describe that whole closed mechanical system.

Worked observer problem · a null of one component does not imply a vanishing field

At one event a laboratory finds \(\mathbf E=(30,40,0)\) kV/m and \(c\mathbf B=(0,0,50)\) kV/m. Another laboratory reports that it can remove the magnetic field by boosting along the direction of \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\). Test the claim and predict the fields for a boost of speed \(0.80c\) along this direction. Does the local measurement establish that this is an infinite plane wave?

Evaluate the invariants and transform the transverse field

The electric and scaled magnetic fields both have magnitude 50 kV/m and are perpendicular. Both quadratic Lorentz invariants vanish, while the fields themselves are nonzero. With \(\mathbf v\) along \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\), both field vectors are perpendicular to the boost. The cross terms reduce their magnitudes by a common factor \(\gamma(1-\beta)=1/3\) for this speed, so \(E'=cB'=16.7\) kV/m. Their directions in this transverse configuration are unchanged. No subluminal boost removes either nonzero field, as follows also by applying the inverse transformation to an alleged zero field.

A pulse at one point can have these local field values, as can the field near a suitable superposition at a particular event. A local tensor and its invariants contain no information about all the field derivatives elsewhere. To establish a plane-wave model measure spatial phase across transverse probes, verify the propagation relation at separated stations and bound amplitude variation over the required observation region. Infinity is an idealisation established only to a scale specified by the experiment.

As an error check, compare the measured difference \(E^2-c^2B^2\) with the propagated uncertainties of both meters. Small nonzero estimated differences near a null may be statistically insignificant, while a systematic alignment error in \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) can obscure orthogonality. Classifying a field as electric-dominated or magnetic-dominated from a difference of nearly equal large squares requires an uncertainty budget. The exact mathematical classification and the experimental confidence in assigning it answer different questions.

Recover a source direction from two clocks and a known speed

Two perfectly synchronised receivers travel with equal speed in opposite directions along the laboratory axis. They sample the same monochromatic plane wave. Their measured frequencies are proportional to \(1-\beta\cos\theta\) and \(1+\beta\cos\theta\), with a common Lorentz factor. Taking their difference divided by their sum determines \(\beta\cos\theta\) without prior knowledge of emitted frequency. Their sum then fixes that frequency once the speed is calibrated. The construction combines two observer assignments rather than treating a transverse Doppler correction as an unknown empirical shift.

For speeds of half the speed of light and a measured frequency ratio of three to two, with the receiver travelling toward negative \(x\) recording the higher value, the inferred cosine is 0.40. If their average measured frequency is 600 THz, the emitted laboratory frequency is about 520 THz. A second direction on the opposite side of the boost axis has the same cosine, so the two receivers alone cannot determine the full incoming unit vector.

A third receiver with a nonparallel velocity supplies another directional projection. Detector clocks must be calibrated in their respective rest frames and each must see the same stable source frequency. If the source itself chirps between noncoincident measurements, that chirp can imitate an angular shift. Using overlapping signals and comparing phase histories addresses this limitation more directly than quoting only two peak frequencies.

Tutorial · Infer charge and current from two observers

A uniform channel carries an unknown lab line charge \(\lambda\) and current \(I\) along its axis. Observers moving at \(+0.30c\) and \(-0.30c\) measure line densities 1.0 and 3.0 nC/m respectively. Recover the laboratory quantities. Can a third observer travel alongside this current so that the measured current vanishes?

Recover the two independent quantities and classify the current

Adding the readings gives \(2\gamma\lambda=4.0\) nC/m, so \(\lambda=1.908\) nC/m. Subtraction gives \(2\gamma\beta I/c=2.0\) nC/m and hence \(I=0.953\) A. Reinsert both signs in the original equations as a check. The speed needed to cancel the current would be \(I/\lambda=5.00\times10^8\) m/s. It exceeds the speed of light. The current four-vector is spacelike since \(I^2>(c\lambda)^2\).

A frame with zero line charge can instead move at \(v=c^2\lambda/I=0.600c\). A spacelike total current is possible for superposed charge carriers of opposite sign. The calculation would rule out a model consisting solely of one sign of carriers all propagating with a common subluminal drift velocity. It does not rule out the measured pair of densities.

Tutorial · What does a moving pulse detector count?

A 10.0 ns rectangular-envelope light pulse moves in \(+x\) in vacuum. A detector moves along the same direction at \(0.60c\). Compute the interval between its encounters with the leading and trailing edges using laboratory kinematics, then its own clock. Predict its measured peak intensity and fluence relative to a stationary detector for a broad collimated beam.

Track the two crossing events and transform the flux

The pulse is \(c(10.0\,\mathrm{ns})\) long in the lab. Its back approaches the moving detector at coordinate closing speed \(c-v=0.40c\), so the two events occur 25.0 ns apart in laboratory time. Both events lie on the detector worldline, so its proper interval is 20.0 ns. This agrees with the inverse Doppler factor of two. A pulse of enough cycles to define a carrier now has half the frequency and twice its envelope duration.

For a transverse plane wave the field amplitude transforms as \(\gamma(1-\beta)=0.50\) times the laboratory value. The primed Poynting flux is therefore one quarter as large. Integrating over twice the proper duration gives one half the original energy per unit transverse area. The cross section perpendicular to the boost is unchanged. Photons are registered with half their laboratory energy and the same total number if the beam is entirely collected, an independent check on the fluence result.
