What an analyser measures about polarisation and coherence

Two red beams produce the same reading through every rotating linear polarizer. One might be circularly polarized and the other an incoherent mixture. Build the measurements that distinguish them, derive the visibility of their interference fringes, and decide which information is lost on a slow detector.

Begin with the measured quantity and the averaging time

Fix a beam travelling in positive z and choose perpendicular laboratory axes x and y in the detection plane. Represent a narrowband field by \(\operatorname{Re}[\mathbf e(t)e^{-i\omega_0 t}]\). The complex envelope \(\mathbf e=(e_x,e_y)\) varies slowly compared with an optical cycle. A photodiode collecting many cycles measures a time average of energy flux, possibly over many independent changes of the envelope. In a transparent isotropic medium a convenient intensity convention is \(I=\langle |e_x|^2+|e_y|^2\rangle/(2Z)\). All comparisons in this unit assume equal transmissive losses and detector responsivity unless a calibration factor is stated.

A deterministic monochromatic field has a Jones vector. Its overall complex phase disappears from all single-beam intensities, while a relative phase between components can change the polarization ellipse. For example, use the normalized vectors \((1,0),\ (1,1)/\sqrt2,\ (1,i)/\sqrt2\). The first is x-linear, the second linear at +45°, and the last circular. At a fixed point choose the time origin so the physical electric vector for the last case begins on the positive x axis and then moves towards positive y as t increases. This explicit convention avoids ambiguity in the words left and right circular, which depend on where an observer stands.

A fast field sampler would reconstruct both real components directly. Optical intensity measurements normally come with a response time much longer than a cycle. The data naturally contain squares and correlations of fields. A random global phase changing between shots destroys the inferred absolute phase but need not depolarize a single beam. If \(e_y=i e_x\) in every shot, their relative phase stays fixed however rapidly the common phase wanders. A detector can measure that relationship with suitable polarizing optics.

Derive a quadratic form for any analyser setting

An ideal analyser transmitting normalized Jones vector \(\mathbf a\) first projects the incident field to complex amplitude \(\mathbf a^\dagger\mathbf e\). Squaring and averaging gives a quadratic form in \(\mathbf a\). Define the coherency matrix in intensity units by \(J_{ij}=\langle e_i e_j^*\rangle/(2Z)\). It is Hermitian, its diagonal entries are nonnegative, and its trace is total intensity. For any vector b,

\[\mathbf b^\dagger J\mathbf b=\langle|\mathbf b^\dagger\mathbf e|^2\rangle/(2Z)\ge0.\]

This positivity will later provide an immediate check on measured data.

\[I_{\mathbf a}=\mathbf a^\dagger J\mathbf a,\]
\[S_0=J_{xx}+J_{yy},\]
\[S_1=J_{xx}-J_{yy},\]
\[S_2=2\Re J_{xy},\]
\[S_3=-2\Im J_{xy}.\]

With this sign convention the circular vector \((1,i)/\sqrt2\) has positive \(S_3\). Invert the four definitions to reconstruct \(J=\tfrac12\left(\begin{smallmatrix}S_0+S_1&S_2-iS_3\\S_2+iS_3&S_0-S_1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A linear polarizer with real \(\mathbf a=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\) gives

\[I(\alpha)=\tfrac12[S_0+S_1\cos(2\alpha)+S_2\sin(2\alpha)].\]

This follows by expanding the two squared direction cosines and the cross term. The imaginary part of the correlation drops out for every real analyser. Scanning a linear analyser with arbitrarily fine angular steps cannot supply that missing observable. Measuring the two circular analyser states supplies \(I_+-I_-=S_3\); their sum, like any pair of orthogonal ideal outputs, equals \(S_0\). In a dual-beam measurement it is useful to compare that sum with \(I_x+I_y\) as a consistency check on losses.

Build a circular analyser from a retarder

An ideal lossless plate with fast and slow axes x and y introduces a relative phase \(\delta\). Factoring out an unobservable common phase, its Jones matrix in those axes is \(D=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\delta})\) for the chosen sign of retardance. If the axes are rotated by an angle \(\alpha\), express the incoming vector in the rotated basis, apply D, then transform back. With \(R(\alpha)\) the ordinary real two-dimensional rotation, the laboratory matrix is \(R(\alpha)D R(-\alpha)\). Reversing the sign of \(\delta\) interchanges retardation conventions, so calibrate a handedness label using a known input.

For a quarter-wave plate with \(\delta=\pi/2\) followed by a +45° linear polarizer, the transmitted amplitude is \((e_x+i e_y)/\sqrt2\). The analyzer equivalent to this sequence is the vector \((1,-i)/\sqrt2\). Hence it reads \(I_-\) in the preceding convention. Interchange the downstream polarizer between +45° and −45° to realize the two orthogonal circular measurements. For a half-wave plate, a linear input at angle \(\psi\) relative to its fast axis emerges linearly polarized at angle \(-\psi\) relative to that axis, modulo \(\pi\). Rotating a half-wave plate by 1° rotates the linear output by 2° when the incident direction is held fixed.

Suppose a plate designed at 600 nm has physical birefringence \(\Delta n=0.010\). If the relative phase magnitude is \(2\pi\Delta n\,d/\lambda_0\), a first-order quarter wave needs \(d=\lambda_0/(4\Delta n)=15\) μm. At 650 nm, holding \(\Delta n\) fixed, the same plate delivers \(0.923(\pi/2)\), about 83.1°. With a +45° linear input, equal component amplitudes are preserved but a residual \(S_2/S_0=\cos\delta\approx0.121\) remains. A linear analyser scan that varies slightly with angle can therefore reveal retardance error even when the output looks nearly circular.

Reconstruct the degree and axes of polarization

Hermitian J has real nonnegative eigenvalues. Calculating the trace and determinant yields eigenvalues \(\lambda_\pm=(S_0\pm s)/2\), where \(s=(S_1^2+S_2^2+S_3^2)^{1/2}\). The degree of polarization is \(P=s/S_0\) for positive intensity. Positivity requires \(P\le1\). A single deterministic Jones vector gives a rank-one outer product and P=1. A completely unpolarized field gives \(J=(S_0/2)I\) and P=0. An isotropic unpolarized contribution adds equally to both eigenvalues. The excess of the larger eigenvalue over the smaller gives the uniquely polarized contribution in this decomposition.

\[2\psi=\operatorname{atan2}(S_2,S_1),\]
\[\sin(2\chi)=S_3/s .\]

Here \(\psi\) is the ellipse orientation modulo \(\pi\); \(\chi\) is an ellipticity angle between \(-\pi/4\) and \(+\pi/4\). The ratio of minor to major field axes is \(|\tan\chi|\). At circular polarization the orientation is undefined, and near this limit even small noise on \(S_1,S_2\) can rotate a fitted orientation substantially. At P=0 neither angle describes a measurable polarized component.

Consider readings of 7 and 3 mW for horizontal/vertical, 6 and 4 mW for ±45°, and 8 and 2 mW for the positive/negative circular analysers. Every pair sums to 10 mW. The Stokes vector is \((10,4,2,6)\) mW, \(s=\sqrt{56}=7.48\) mW, \(P=0.748\). The polarized-component angle is \(13.28^\circ\), and \(\chi=\tfrac12\arcsin(6/\sqrt{56})=26.6^\circ\). A linear analyser alone would have suggested a modulation depth \(\sqrt{20}/10=0.447\). The recovered eigenvalues, about 8.74 and 1.26 mW, are the largest and smallest transmitted powers over all ideal elliptical analysers. The largest intensity available to a linear analyser is only \((10+\sqrt{20})/2=7.24\) mW.

Derive the two-arm visibility and its ambiguities

Now split a quasimonochromatic scalar field between two interferometer arms with delay \(\tau\) and relative phase \(\varphi\). At one output combine complex fields \(e_1(t)\) and \(e_2(t-\tau)e^{i\varphi}\), absorbing beam-splitter factors into arm intensities \(I_1,I_2\). Expanding the squared sum gives

\[I(\varphi)=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\Re[\gamma_{12}(\tau)e^{i\varphi}],\]
\[\mathcal V={I_{\max}-I_{\min}\over I_{\max}+I_{\min}}={2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\over I_1+I_2}|\gamma_{12}(\tau)|.\]

Define \(\gamma_{12}=\langle e_1^*(t)e_2(t-\tau)\rangle/\sqrt{\langle|e_1|^2\rangle\langle|e_2|^2\rangle}\), provided both intensities are nonzero. Cauchy–Schwarz enforces \(|\gamma|\le1\). For vector beams with constant normalized polarizations \(\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2\), an additional overlap \(\mathbf a_1^\dagger\mathbf a_2\) appears. Orthogonal polarizations can erase fringes even when relative phase is perfectly stable. A common output analyser projects both beams into the same polarization and can restore modulation at a reduced transmitted flux.

An interferometer gives \(I_1=4.0\) mW, \(I_2=1.0\) mW and observed visibility 0.60 with matched polarization. The intensity imbalance alone limits visibility to \(2\sqrt4/5=0.80\). Thus \(|\gamma|=0.75\). If the polarizations are instead linear at 30° to each other, the polarization overlap is 0.866 and the inferred scalar degree of coherence is

\[0.60/(0.80\cdot0.866)=0.866.\]

Reporting visibility as a direct measure of source coherence requires both arm powers and polarization overlap. Detector backgrounds additionally increase the denominator without increasing fringe modulation.

Translate a spectrum into a coherence envelope

For a stationary scalar field, different frequency components of a long acquisition add in average intensity. A relative delay changes the phase of each by its own frequency times \(\tau\). Consequently the mutual coherence of two copies of the same field is proportional to the Fourier transform of the optical power spectrum. If the normalized spectrum is Gaussian in ordinary frequency, with centre \(\nu_0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_\nu\), completing the square in the Gaussian Fourier integral gives

\[|\gamma(\tau)|=\exp(-2\pi^2\sigma_\nu^2\tau^2),\]
\[\tau_{1/e}={1\over\sqrt2\pi\sigma_\nu}.\]

The rapid carrier phase varies at \(\nu_0\), while finite bandwidth sets the envelope. For \(\sigma_\nu=1.0\) THz the amplitude coherence drops to \(1/e\) at 0.225 ps. This is 67.5 μm of vacuum optical path difference. In a Michelson geometry the corresponding mirror travel is half that, since light traverses the additional gap twice. An instrument quoting a coherence length should specify which envelope level, which bandwidth convention and which path geometry it uses. Replacing a standard deviation by a full width at half maximum without conversion changes the answer substantially.

Predict three analyser readings before changing the source

Use the polarimetry calculation with a polarized fraction below one. Set the ellipse orientation to 0°, choose positive ellipticity, and compute the horizontal, diagonal and circular contrasts using the definitions above. A change in polarized fraction scales the three contrast components together. Changing orientation alone rotates the linear contrasts through twice the physical angle while leaving the circular contrast fixed. A separate crystal model follows energy flow when two eigenpolarizations propagate in a birefringent plate. For comparison, separate the output phase retardance of a plate from any spatial displacement that could reduce the overlap of two exiting beams on an interferometer detector.

An investigation with a photon-counting polarimeter

A polarimeter reports a count for each analyser, not an exact Stokes vector. A useful second reconstruction starts with a statistical model for those counts. Let all six exposures have the same duration and gain after calibration. For a pair of complementary analyser settings labelled \(+\) and \(-\), the mean counts at one exposure time are \(N(1+s)/2\) and \(N(1-s)/2\), with \(s=S_i/S_0\). Here \(N\) is the expected pair total after background removal. Independent Poisson counting models shot noise from a steady source. If the source changes between exposures this independence assumption no longer describes the common intensity drift.

Condition on the observed sum \(n=n_++n_-\). The number in the positive channel then has a binomial distribution with success probability \((1+s)/2\). The natural estimator and its conditional variance follow without assuming equal noise in bright and dark channels:

\[\widehat s=\frac{n_+-n_-}{n_++n_-},\]
\[\operatorname{var}(\widehat s\mid n)=\frac{1-s^2}{n}.\]

The uncertainty becomes small near a pure eigenpolarisation in the matching analyser, while a transverse component of the same beam has \(s=0\) and variance about \(1/n\). Exact intervals are preferable near the boundary, where a symmetric error bar would extend into unphysical values. Conditioning is appropriate for a single pair. If one uses the same intensity measurement to normalise several pairs, their reported components become correlated.

Consider the six counts \((800,200),(620,380),(550,450)\) in complementary linear-horizontal, diagonal and circular bases. Their normalised components are 0.60, 0.24 and 0.10. Raw degree of polarisation is

\[\sqrt{0.6^2+0.24^2+0.10^2}=0.654.\]

Individual shot-noise estimates are 0.025, 0.031 and 0.031. To first order, uncertainty in the degree is obtained by projecting the component variance along the measured unit vector. For independent pairs the result is approximately 0.026. Fitting an ellipse to this partially polarised beam recovers the polarised component's ellipse; the unpolarised intensity contributes to every positive and negative analyser reading.

For an unpolarised source, taking a square root of three squared noisy estimates always returns a nonnegative number. In fact the expected squared reported degree is approximately \(3/n\) when all pair totals equal \(n\). At \(n=1000\) the root-mean-square value is 0.055, even with a perfect instrument. A positive fitted degree around 0.04 in a single exposure therefore cannot establish weak polarisation. Repeat the acquisition at four times the counts. A statistical excess falls in scale by about two, whereas stable true components persist with their signs. A forced projection onto the physical Stokes sphere cannot remove calibration drift or substitute for this uncertainty calculation.

Which-path information encoded in a polarisation state

A balanced interferometer supplies a different quantitative probe. Suppose its path lengths are equal, the beam in arm one has unit horizontal polarisation \(\mathbf e_1\), and a rotator in arm two produces \(\mathbf e_2=\cos\alpha\,\mathbf e_1+\sin\alpha\,\mathbf e_y\). The detector receives equal-amplitude contributions with relative phase \(\delta\). Squaring the sum in the two output ports of an ideal lossless beamsplitter gives complementary fringes.

\[I_\pm=\frac{I_{\rm tot}}2(1\pm\cos\alpha\cos\delta).\]

For \(\alpha=60^\circ\), sweeping the phase from constructive to destructive moves one port between \(0.75I_{\rm tot}\) and \(0.25I_{\rm tot}\); the visibility is 0.50. Their sum should remain constant throughout the sweep. If both measured ports fade together, look for path loss, a changing source or detector gain. Polarisation distinguishability moves fringe energy between the two outputs in this ideal result. It does not remove energy from both.

At \(\alpha=90^\circ\), complete temporal coherence does not suffice for an unpolarised intensity detector to see fringes. The two field vectors are orthogonal and their scalar product vanishes. Insert an analyser at \(45^\circ\) in front of one detector. It projects both contributions onto the same unit vector with equal positive amplitude \(1/\sqrt2\), so the maximum visibility returns while the phase-averaged brightness of that port halves. An analyser at \(-45^\circ\) gives a fringe displaced by half a period. Summing those two orthogonal analyser intensities restores the original featureless signal. The recovered fringes do not require a change in either source clock or path length.

For unequal arm intensities and a time delay, the interference coefficient additionally contains \(2\sqrt{I_1I_2}/(I_1+I_2)\) and the magnitude of the normalised temporal coherence evaluated at that delay. This factorisation assumes the temporal waveform and polarisation can be separated in each arm. A broadband retarder with appreciable frequency-dependent axes couples the two. Then compute the spectral integral of the polarisation-resolved field products, preserving the complex phase at each frequency before integration. Multiplying three averaged magnitudes can give the wrong result even if every factor lies between zero and one.

A practical discrimination uses three sweeps at fixed geometry. First measure both single-arm intensities by blocking one arm at a time. Second sweep delay near zero with the analyser removed, and third repeat with the two orthogonal diagonal analysers. If the two diagonal traces regain equal but opposite fringes and sum to the original low-visibility result, distinguishability is a plausible explanation. If none shows variation after path intensities have been balanced, check whether the coherence envelope is narrower than the available delay step. Finite detector bandwidth during a fast phase sweep also suppresses every trace. Repeating the sweep more slowly and reversing its direction distinguishes that response delay from a static polarisation transformation.

Worked inverse problem · distinguish a retarder error from source ellipticity

A linear input is sent through a retarder whose fast axis defines the horizontal direction. The input makes \(45^\circ\) to that axis. A diagonal analyser records a normalised Stokes component \(s_2=0.100\); a circular analyser gives \(|s_3|=0.990\). Determine possible retardance values, then design a measurement separating imperfect retardance from a slightly elliptical input. Assume no diattenuation for the first inference.

Track the phase and design an independent preparation

The equal input components acquire a relative phase \(\delta\) while keeping equal intensities. The outgoing Stokes components are \(s_1=0\), \(s_2=\cos\delta\), with \(|s_3|=|\sin\delta|\). The first measurement gives \(\delta=84.26^\circ\) or \(275.74^\circ\) modulo one full turn. Both predict \(|s_3|=0.995\), close to the reported 0.990. A signed, calibrated circular analyser selects the sense of the quarter-wave action, provided the basis and time convention are stated. Measuring two intensities more precisely cannot tell a one-quarter from a five-quarter retardance at a single frequency.

If the input already possessed relative phase \(\delta_i\), the observation measures \(\delta+\delta_i\). Bypass the retarder and reconstruct the input. Then rotate the retarder by \(90^\circ\) to exchange its two eigenaxes while holding the input and detector fixed. The retarder-induced phase changes sign; the source phase does not. Two signed phase measurements can recover the sum and difference, modulo ambiguities eliminated by a nearby wavelength or a known approximate plate order.

Record the calibrated horizontal and vertical transmissions as well. Differential loss makes the two output amplitudes unequal and reduces the accessible circular Stokes component even at perfect quarter-wave phase. A measured degree less than one can result from averaging several slightly different retardances across a finite spectrum. Changing the spectral filter tests that hypothesis. These checks illustrate how independent preparations constrain a device and source separately, where one very precise polarisation measurement of their combination cannot.

A rotating source exposes the detector's averaging time

Imagine an otherwise perfectly linearly polarised beam whose axis rotates uniformly during an exposure. At every instant its degree of polarisation is one. A horizontal analyser transmits an intensity proportional to the squared cosine of the axis angle, and a diagonal one samples the same function displaced by forty-five degrees. Over a complete rotation their average intensities are equal. A circular analyser also receives half the total power. Time-integrated tomography would therefore assign zero net polarisation to the exposure.

For a shorter exposure covering an angular sweep \(\Delta\) about a central angle, average the complex combination \(s_1+i s_2=e^{2i\theta}\) uniformly over the sweep. Its magnitude becomes \(|\sin\Delta/\Delta|\). A ninety-degree sweep retains a degree of approximately 0.637, whereas a full half-turn yields zero because an unoriented linear polarisation axis repeats every half-turn. The reported degree can thus identify neither instantaneous purity nor temporal stability without an exposure model.

Repeat the measurement with shorter synchronised gates and accumulate many exposures starting at the same rotation phase. Stable rotation should recover the predicted phase-resolved Stokes trajectory. A genuinely random uncorrelated orientation cannot be recovered by simply synchronising to an unrelated clock. If the analyser states are acquired sequentially, synchronise their gates too; otherwise different components may sample different portions of the trajectory and combine into a physically impossible vector.

Tutorial · recover a missing analyser setting and audit positivity

A pair of equal-throughput linear analysers reports \(I_x=4,\ I_y=2\) and \(I_{45}=4.5\) in the same power units. Find the predicted −45° reading and bound a possible circular contrast. A circular analyser then reports \(I_+=6.0\). Can these measurements describe a single stationary ensemble?

Reconstruct only what the measured subspace determines

The orthogonal sum fixes \(S_0=6\), hence \(I_{-45}=1.5\), \(S_1=2\), and \(S_2=3\). Remaining positivity requires \(S_3^2\le6^2-2^2-3^2=23\), so \(|S_3|\le4.796\). A positive circular reading of 6 would imply \(I_-=0\) and \(S_3=6\). That would make the reconstructed coherency matrix have a negative smaller eigenvalue. Merely normalizing all six readings by their common sum would retain the inconsistency. Check gain, background, plate retardance and whether the source changed during sequential measurements. If data have known statistical error, fitting J subject to positive semidefiniteness can enforce physical consistency while exposing the residuals.

Tutorial · distinguish delay decorrelation from an unresolved doublet

Two equal narrow spectral lines are separated by 80 GHz, the arms have equal powers, and polarizations match. Obtain the fringe-visibility envelope. Find the first null and first revival in optical path difference. A single visibility reading at the null is compatible with several physical explanations. Propose a scan that tests the doublet hypothesis.

Add the complex correlations before taking the magnitude

Each narrow line contributes a phase at its optical frequency. Averaging the two phasors gives \(e^{i2\pi\nu_0\tau}\cos(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)\), so visibility is \(|\cos(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)|\). The first null occurs at \(1/(2\Delta\nu)=6.25\) ps, or 1.87 mm path difference. The first revival is at 12.5 ps or 3.75 mm. In a Michelson instrument those mirror travels are 0.937 and 1.87 mm. For real spectral lines of finite width the revival sits under their own coherence envelope. Scan beyond the first null, independently match arm powers and verify polarization. A clean revival distinguishes a narrow doublet from a single broad Gaussian spectrum reproducing the same initial contrast loss.
