Radiated power, mechanical work and energy limits

A charge can radiate while its speed is constant. A source can exchange energy with its nearby field before a pulse reaches a receiver. We will use the flux through a distant sphere, the work at the charge and the energy stored in a local field as three operationally distinct measurements. Agreement requires the relevant integration times and the range of validity of the particle model.

Integrate the far field of one slowly moving charge

For a source moving slowly compared with \(c\), the radiation field at a large distance is transverse to the viewing direction \(\mathbf n\). To leading order in \(v/c\), its electric amplitude is \(q\mathbf n\times(\mathbf n\times\mathbf a)/(4\pi\epsilon_0c^2r)\) evaluated at retarded time. The magnetic radiation field is \(\mathbf n\times\mathbf E/c\). Form the radial Poynting flux and integrate over the sphere. Choose the polar axis along the instantaneous acceleration.

\[\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^2a^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}\sin^2\theta,\]
\[\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=\frac{8\pi}{3},\]
\[P=\frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.\]

The angular integral contains a factor of \(\sin\theta\) from solid angle in addition to the square from transverse projection. The result is instantaneous emission power, not the average over a sinusoid. For acceleration \(a_0\cos\omega t\), the average is one half the power computed with peak acceleration \(a_0\). No acceleration component along the velocity is required. A charge in circular motion radiates even when the externally applied magnetic field does no work.

A trapped electron executing small prescribed oscillations at 100 MHz with amplitude 1.0 micrometre has peak speed 628 m/s and peak acceleration \(3.95\times10^{11}\) m/s squared. Its mean radiated power is \(4.45\times10^{-31}\) W. The energy scale of this illustrative classical orbit is only \(1.80\times10^{-25}\) J, much smaller than one oscillator quantum at 100 MHz. The field calculation is meaningful as a classical scaling or for an appropriately prepared mean current. Interpreting it as a literal well-defined single-electron mechanical orbit would require addressing the state preparation. Raising the amplitude to 1.0 mm raises energy and mean emitted power by a factor of a million, and keeps \(v/c\) below 0.003.

Transform instantaneous power using the rest frame

In the instantaneously comoving frame the nonrelativistic expression applies at that event, with the proper acceleration. The dipolar pattern there carries zero net three-momentum when integrated over all directions, by front–back symmetry. The radiation four-momentum emitted during \(d\tau\) is therefore \((P_0d\tau/c,\mathbf0)\). A boost multiplies its energy by \(\gamma\), but also multiplies the emission-event time interval by \(\gamma\). Thus the total laboratory power allocated per emitter coordinate time equals \(P_0\). The directional detector signal need not share this invariance.

At the instant of rest the proper acceleration is the Euclidean norm of the spatial part of \(du^\mu/d\tau\). Its square in any frame follows from the invariant \(-a^\mu a_\mu\). Differentiating \(u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)\), with \(d\gamma/dt=\gamma^3\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a/c^2\), and separating acceleration parallel and perpendicular to velocity gives

\[P=\frac{q^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}\left(\gamma^6 a_\parallel^2+\gamma^4a_\perp^2\right)=\frac{q^2\gamma^6}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}\left[a^2-|\boldsymbol\beta\times\mathbf a|^2\right].\]

For a fixed applied parallel electric force,

\[a_\parallel=qE/(\gamma^3m).\]

The \(\gamma^6\) cancels, so the ideal point-charge radiated power in a constant longitudinal field is independent of instantaneous speed. This conclusion keeps the field fixed. Holding coordinate acceleration fixed while increasing speed would require an increasing input force, producing a different power law. Always identify what is actually kept fixed before applying the exponents.

At fixed perpendicular magnetic field, \(a_\perp=|q|vB/(\gamma m)\), so power varies as \(\gamma^2v^2 B^2\). A gyrating electron gradually loses energy if there is no replenishing electric field. Over a short tracked interval one can treat its radius and frequency as constant only after comparing radiated energy with kinetic energy. The large-mass dependence makes the corresponding correction much smaller for heavy particles at comparable speeds and charges.

Arrival time and a detector's angular window

Let radiation from emission position \(\mathbf r_s(t_e)\) travel to a fixed distant receiver. Its arrival time is

\[t_o=t_e+|\mathbf R-\mathbf r_s(t_e)|/c.\]

Differentiating this relation gives

\[dt_o/dt_e=1-\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta.\]

A forward-moving charge can deliver the energy emitted over a long source time in a much shorter arrival interval. Angular radiation patterns specified per emission time and a directional Poynting flux specified per arrival time then differ by this Jacobian.

Explore how observation direction and motion change the received field

At \(\beta=0.90\) directed toward a distant receiver, radiation from two source events separated by 5.0 ns arrives about 0.50 ns apart if the direction remains nearly unchanged. The same events viewed transversely arrive 5.0 ns apart. A slow photodiode could register comparable integrated pulse energies after geometric corrections while reporting very different peak shapes from the true fields. If the acceleration also changes direction during the interval, account for the angular antenna pattern before attributing the whole difference to arrival-time compression.

A finite-time mechanical energy balance

Poynting's theorem balances electromagnetic energy, its outward flux and work on charges. In a small region surrounding an idealised particle one can separate a far-field flux associated with radiation from energy temporarily stored with the near field. In the nonrelativistic, weak-reaction expansion the leading self-force is \(m\tau_0\dot{\mathbf a}\), where \(\tau_0=q^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0mc^3)\). Dotting with velocity and applying the product rule gives

\[\mathbf F_{\rm self}\cdot\mathbf v=\frac{d}{dt}(m\tau_0\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v)-m\tau_0 a^2,\]
\[P_{\rm rad}=m\tau_0a^2 .\]

Adding the external mechanical work shows that \(W_{\rm ext}=\Delta K+E_{\rm rad}-[m\tau_0\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v]_{t_1}^{t_2}\). This last boundary term accounts for reversible energy exchange with the bound field in this separation. It vanishes if acceleration is zero at both endpoints, or over complete periods when the mechanical state repeats. During a limited interval positive radiated energy and instantaneous mechanical damping need not coincide.

A prescribed interval of constant acceleration illustrates the distinction. Within that interval \(\dot{\mathbf a}=0\) and the local leading self-force vanishes, yet the far-zone power is positive. The endpoint term changes because \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v\) grows with time. A physical pulse has switching intervals and any comparison of initial and final energies must include them. Treating an endlessly accelerated point charge as a mechanical system with ordinary finite reservoir energies introduces boundary questions absent in a compact laboratory pulse.

When a weak-reaction or classical model stops answering the question

The electron time \(\tau_0\) is approximately \(6.27\times10^{-24}\) s. For a driving angular frequency \(\omega\) the ratio \(\tau_0\omega\) estimates the size of the leading reaction force relative to inertial force in a harmonic nonrelativistic problem. At 100 MHz it is \(3.94\times10^{-15}\), tiny per cycle. Many cycles can sum to an appreciable energy loss when all other damping mechanisms are very weak. For a nonrelativistic harmonic oscillator the cycle-averaged Larmor loss equals \(\tau_0\omega^2\) times its stored mechanical energy, giving an energy lifetime \(1/(\tau_0\omega^2)\).

A perturbative approximation replaces acceleration inside the small self-force by the acceleration from the external force to leading order. For a prescribed, smooth, nonrelativistic \(F_{\rm ext}(t)\), this yields

\[m a\simeq F_{\rm ext}+\tau_0\dot F_{\rm ext}.\]

Errors scale with further time derivatives. Treating the unexpanded third-order point-particle equation as an arbitrary initial-value solver admits a homogeneous exponentially growing acceleration. That mode varies on a timescale the weak-reaction expansion did not resolve in the first place.

For a collective source also check dimensions against wavelength. A cloud much smaller than the wavelength can add fields coherently when all charges have the same driven phase. Then total power can scale as the squared number of radiators, while random phases usually leave only a contribution proportional to their number after averaging. At larger dimensions include the spatial Fourier transform of the source. Finally, photon recoil and quantisation can matter even when a classical average flux describes an ensemble. Compare \(\hbar\omega\) with the mechanical energy change that the measurement actually resolves.

Recover a scattering cross section from an energy balance

A free nonrelativistic electron in a weak nearly monochromatic incident wave is accelerated by the electric field. Let its peak incident field be \(E_0\), assume a quiver speed small compared with \(c\), and ignore the leading magnetic force in calculating the quiver. Then \(a_0=|q|E_0/m\). The time-averaged Larmor power divided by incident time-averaged intensity \(\epsilon_0cE_0^2/2\) gives a quantity with the dimensions of area. The amplitude cancels:

\[\sigma_T=\frac{q^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0^2m^2c^4}=\frac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2,\]
\[r_e=\frac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0mc^2}.\]

For an electron \(r_e=2.82\) femtometres and \(\sigma_T=6.65\times10^{-29}\) square metres. The radius here is a combination of charge, mass and speed of light, not an independently measured mechanical size of the electron. For an incident intensity of \(100\) watts per square metre the weak-drive prediction is \(6.65\times10^{-27}\) W per free electron. Calculating an expected photon count requires wavelength, collection solid angle and detector efficiency as well as that power.

There is a subtle question about work done by the incident wave. At lowest order the acceleration is exactly proportional to the applied field and the velocity lags it by a quarter cycle, giving zero average \(q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\). Yet the radiation formula predicts positive time-averaged outgoing energy. The resolution is an order count. Radiation power is already of the order of the small reaction correction, and an input-work calculation to that same order must include the correction to velocity that is in phase with the drive. The perturbatively reduced self-force introduces that component. Equivalently, far from the particle the incident–scattered interference contributes extinction equal to scattering in the absence of absorption.

For a charge bound by a spring, the quiver changes near and below its resonance. Well below resonance the displacement is nearly independent of driving frequency, so radiation power falls with the fourth power of frequency at fixed field. Far above resonance, but well within the classical low-recoil regime, the inertial free-electron response is recovered. This explains why applying the Thomson cross section indiscriminately to all the electrons in a transparent dielectric can give a misleading attenuation. Bound response, correlations, interfaces and coherent forward propagation can dominate a bulk optical measurement.

Count coherent radiators through a spatial form factor

Consider \(N\) nominally identical dipoles driven at positions \(\mathbf r_j\) by a plane wave with wavevector \(\mathbf k_i\). Their induced moments acquire incident phases \(e^{i\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r_j}\). Observation in direction \(\mathbf n\) supplies the outgoing source factor \(e^{-ik\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_j}\), so define the transfer wavevector \(\mathbf Q=\mathbf k_i-k\mathbf n\). With equal amplitudes and a fixed observed polarisation, the field relative to one dipole is \(\sum_j e^{i\mathbf Q\cdot\mathbf r_j}\). Squaring gives \(N\) self terms and ordered cross terms. If positions are independently drawn from a normalised spatial distribution \(w(\mathbf r)\),

\[\left\langle\left|\sum_j e^{i\mathbf Q\cdot\mathbf r_j}\right|^2\right\rangle=N+N(N-1)|\widehat w(\mathbf Q)|^2 .\]

At exact forward scattering \(\mathbf Q=0\) and all phases add regardless of source extent. Away from the forward direction a broad cloud can lose mutual coherence even though its drive remains perfectly monochromatic. The total power integrated over a sphere need not follow the on-axis scaling, because the enhanced forward cone narrows as the cloud grows. At sufficiently large density the incident field on one dipole includes scattered fields from others, invalidating the independent-dipole derivation. The formula alone cannot predict arbitrarily large emitted power from an optically thick weakly fed sample.

For a Gaussian cloud with one-coordinate standard deviation \(s\),

\[|\widehat w|^2=e^{-s^2Q^2},\]
\[Q=2k\sin(\theta/2).\]

Take wavelength 600 nm, \(s=5.0\) micrometres and \(N=100\). At one degree, \(sQ=0.914\) and the averaged intensity factor is about \(4.39\times10^3\) rather than either 100 or 10,000. At five degrees \(sQ=4.57\); the coherent excess becomes negligible and the factor approaches 100. An aperture spanning both these angles requires an angular integral, not the central value multiplied by its nominal solid angle.

One way to measure this distinction uses two nominally identical clouds with different sizes but equal independently counted particles. Record angular intensity through a series of apertures, keeping polarisation and illumination profile fixed. A growing forward peak with a narrowing angular width points toward a coherent form factor. If broad-angle intensity changes too, check whether the local density modifies each dipole's response or the sample heats. Compare a phase-sensitive measurement of forward extinction with a calibrated total scattered power rather than treating the forward peak as a direct measure of lost beam energy.

An energy audit of the complete apparatus must include the drive. If radiation from \(N\) synchronised elements is substantially larger than the sum for isolated elements, maintaining specified oscillations can demand additional work from the source through mutual fields. If the source instead supplies fixed power, the element amplitudes generally change. The external energy supply, field distribution and measurement solid angle are all part of the physical question. This accounting links a many-source optics measurement back to the finite-time mechanical work balance for an individual charge.

Worked detector problem · energy in a cone around a dipole axis

A nonrelativistic charge acceleration pulse has known total radiated energy 1.00 pJ. An ideal detector collects the entire cone of half-angle \(15^\circ\) about the direction of acceleration, at a distance large compared with the source and the radiation wavelength. Estimate the recorded energy. Compare it with a detector of the same small solid angle centred perpendicular to the acceleration.

Integrate the projected electric field before applying the photon count

For acceleration with fixed direction during the pulse, the radiated energy per unit solid angle is proportional to \(\sin^2\theta\). The axial fraction is

\[(3/4)\int_0^\alpha\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=(3/4)(2/3-\cos\alpha+\cos^3\alpha/3).\]

At fifteen degrees this gives about \(8.61\times10^{-4}\) and a collected energy of 0.861 fJ. The small-angle approximation \(3\alpha^4/16\) gives 0.881 fJ, consistent at this angle. A detector that assigns its on-axis zero intensity to the whole cone would incorrectly predict no signal.

At right angles, intensity varies slowly over a narrow aperture. Its leading fraction is \(3\Omega/(8\pi)\), where \(\Omega=2\pi(1-\cos15^\circ)\). This gives about 0.0256 or 25.6 fJ, with a small finite-angle correction. For an exact transverse cone average \(n_z^2\) in coordinates about the detector axis. The ring average is \(\sin^2\delta/2\) at polar offset \(\delta\), so the exact fraction becomes \((3/8)[(1-\cos\alpha)+(1-\cos^3\alpha)/3]\), or approximately 0.0251. Equal solid angle alone does not imply comparable collected energy.

These estimates assume unit efficiency across the entire pulse spectrum and a stable acceleration direction. A real detector with frequency-dependent response measures an integral over frequency and collection angle. Calibrate both before scaling its reading to total pulse energy. If the acceleration changes direction during the event, integrate the angular pattern appropriate to each emission time instead of assigning all the energy to a single measured peak direction.

A radiofrequency oscillator's finite lifetime creates a spectral width

An energy loss mechanism can be inferred from a time trace and independently tested in the frequency domain. Suppose an isolated weakly damped oscillator loses mechanical energy exponentially with time constant \(\tau_E\). Its position amplitude then falls as \(e^{-t/(2\tau_E)}\). Near a well-resolved oscillation frequency its Fourier amplitude has a simple pole shifted below the real axis by \(1/(2\tau_E)\). The squared Fourier amplitude is Lorentzian, with full width in ordinary frequency \(1/(2\pi\tau_E)\).

If the measured energy lifetime in the earlier trapped-electron example were limited by its predicted radiation alone, the resonance width would be roughly four tenths of a microhertz. A measurement lasting one second cannot directly resolve such a line through the uncomplicated Fourier width of that record. Truncating a long-lived signal after that interval multiplies it by the acquisition window and convolves its spectrum with a much broader instrumental response. A broad fitted peak from the short record is consequently insufficient evidence of a short physical lifetime.

Compare records of several durations and perform a phase-coherent fit where appropriate. Technical drive-frequency jitter broadens an ensemble spectrum while a single undriven decay can remain long. Additional true energy dissipation shortens the ringdown itself. Pairing a spectral-width measurement with a time-domain energy measurement thus distinguishes loss of phase coherence from loss of oscillator energy, two effects that simple line shapes alone often confound.

Tutorial · Does the field energy explain a measured ringdown?

A nonrelativistic electron is driven at 100 MHz in a harmonic potential. After the drive ceases, the measured energy decays with time constant 0.10 s. Calculate the radiative energy lifetime, the energy fraction radiated in 0.10 s under radiation alone and whether a measured exponential proves radiative damping.

Construct and compare the decay rates

Using \(\omega=2\pi10^8\) rad/s and the electron \(\tau_0\), the radiation-only energy decay rate is \(2.47\times10^{-6}\) per second and its time constant is \(4.04\times10^5\) s, about 4.68 days. The fraction radiated in 0.10 s is \(1-\exp(-2.47\times10^{-7})\simeq2.47\times10^{-7}\). The observed decay rate is about four million times larger. Under the assumptions of this single-particle free-space model it calls for further coupling or dissipation.

The amplitude decay time of an oscillator is twice its energy decay time. Confirm whether the measuring circuit reports amplitude, squared amplitude or extracted energy before fitting. A conducting environment can modify the density of radiation modes, and a measurement circuit can add a load. Compare separate runs with different coupling to that circuit and compare a calculated angular power with the collected signal. A single exponential identifies a rate, not a physical mechanism.

Tutorial · Extract a finite-pulse balance from an acceleration trace

During a short nonrelativistic interval the acceleration is \(a(t)=a_0t/T\), the velocity initially vanishes and motion is one-dimensional. Calculate the radiated energy and integrated self-force work over that interval. Then explain what additional information an experiment needs to compare final mechanical energy with radiation after the entire pulse.

Retain the endpoint before interpreting the sign

Integrating the acceleration gives

\[v(t)=a_0t^2/(2T).\]

The radiated energy is \(m\tau_0a_0^2T/3\). At the final endpoint \(m\tau_0av=m\tau_0a_0^2T/2\). Thus integrated self-force work is the boundary term minus radiated energy, \(+m\tau_0a_0^2T/6\). A direct integration of \((m\tau_0a_0/T)v(t)\) gives the same positive answer, despite an outward flux throughout the ramp.

At \(T\) the acceleration is still nonzero. A smooth continuation back to zero changes both the radiated energy and boundary contribution. To compare initial and final states without a bound-field endpoint term, measure through that continuation and wait for the pulse to reach the collecting surface. Specify angular coverage and collection efficiency if only a fraction of the radiation reaches the apparatus. An on-axis detector along the acceleration could see no leading dipole signal even though the sphere integral is positive.
