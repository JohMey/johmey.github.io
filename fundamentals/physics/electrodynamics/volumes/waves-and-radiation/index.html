Waves, radiation and relativity

Follow a disturbance from its source to a measurement. Work through polarisation, dispersion, scattering and changes of observer using measurable quantities.

    Contents

    1. From a local field balance to travelling waves and standing modes

      A voltage probe moving along a line finds regularly spaced zeros. A second probe at the same point measures a magnetic field even when the electric reading vanishes. Derive both readings from Maxwell’s equations, calculate the energy stored between the zeros, and decide what additional measurement would reveal the direction in which energy travels.

    2. What an analyser measures about polarisation and coherence

      Two red beams produce the same reading through every rotating linear polarizer. One might be circularly polarized and the other an incoherent mixture. Build the measurements that distinguish them, derive the visibility of their interference fringes, and decide which information is lost on a slow detector.

    3. Oblique interfaces and films whose reflections cancel

      A film only one quarter of a wavelength thick can suppress reflection from a transparent surface. Follow the boundary conditions all the way from an oblique incident wave to the input admittance of a stack, then calculate how much reflection returns when the wavelength, angle or layer order changes.

    4. Causal material response and the propagation of a finite pulse

      An optical pulse entering a transparent slab is delayed, broadened and sometimes reshaped. Derive these effects from the motion of bound charge, and learn what a measured group delay actually says about a pulse carrying information.

    5. A guided mode, a cavity line and the energy they carry

      A microwave pulse sent through a rectangular guide arrives late even though the guide is evacuated. Terminating the guide converts the travelling pattern into a family of resonances. Use the same transverse eigenproblem to predict its cutoff, pulse delay, cavity frequency and measured linewidth.

    6. Constructing the retarded Green function from an outgoing spherical shell

      A short source pulse reaches two receivers at different times and amplitudes. Derive the signal from a localized spacetime impulse, see why the potential is evaluated at a past time, and identify which observables change when the source occupies an extended region.

    7. Radiating multipoles and what a detector actually integrates

      A small oscillating source looks point-like from a distant detector, yet its angular pattern reveals its structure. Derive electric and magnetic dipole radiation from a conserved current, identify what survives when electric dipoles cancel, and turn a detector aperture into a calibrated estimate of total power.

    8. Scattering, extinction and the phase of the forward wave

      A suspension of transparent particles dims a narrow transmitted beam while absorbing almost none of its energy. Derive the scattered angular power, identify the interference term seen in transmission, and use energy conservation to put a bound on the response of a single small particle.

    9. Diffraction from the near field to the focal plane

      An illuminated aperture is a boundary condition for a wave. A screen a millimetre away, an intensity camera at a lens focus and a microscope measuring an evanescent field sample different consequences of the same condition. We will derive operators for propagating the field, estimate the approximations each instrument makes and use energy and sampling checks to tell numerical structure from a measurable fringe.

    10. What different observers measure

      A moving observer carries clocks, rulers and a calibrated test charge. A statement about the electric field must specify which observer held that test charge at rest. In this chapter we derive how different measurements can agree on the force law and yet assign different electric fields, magnetic fields and charge densities to the same physical apparatus. A current-carrying wire and a light pulse will provide independent checks.

    11. Relativistic particle dynamics and the field tensor

      A circular track measures momentum per charge. A clock following the particle registers proper time, and a detector records how much kinetic energy it has gained. We will put these observations in one dynamical equation, solve that equation in uniform fields and exhibit a numerical pusher that can get the energy right while its orbit phase slowly slips.

    12. Radiated power, mechanical work and energy limits

      A charge can radiate while its speed is constant. A source can exchange energy with its nearby field before a pulse reaches a receiver. We will use the flux through a distant sphere, the work at the charge and the energy stored in a local field as three operationally distinct measurements. Agreement requires the relevant integration times and the range of validity of the particle model.

    13. How can a field that decays in air transmit power to another prism?

      Place a thin air gap between identical prisms. Derive the TE transmission from the two interface conditions and test when an exponential attenuation length predicts the actual throughput.

    14. Resolve the self-force before taking a point-charge limit

      A finite spherical charge distribution introduces a propagation time. Follow its memory kernel to electromagnetic inertia, radiative recoil and the limits of a truncated equation.

    15. From light clocks to collision kinematics

      Construct spacetime measurements before transforming fields. Use the same invariant interval to compare clocks, add velocities and close a relativistic collision.
