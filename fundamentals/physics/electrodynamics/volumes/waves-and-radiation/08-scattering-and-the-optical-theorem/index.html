Scattering, extinction and the phase of the forward wave

A suspension of transparent particles dims a narrow transmitted beam while absorbing almost none of its energy. Derive the scattered angular power, identify the interference term seen in transmission, and use energy conservation to put a bound on the response of a single small particle.

Build the cross sections from a specified incident beam

Illuminate an isolated target in a uniform transparent host by a monochromatic plane wave of incident intensity \(I_0\). A far-away receiver collecting solid angle \(d\Omega\) measures scattered power \(dP_{\rm sca}\). Define \(d\sigma_{\rm sca}/d\Omega=(dP_{\rm sca}/d\Omega)/I_0\) and integrate over direction to obtain \(\sigma_{\rm sca}\). Dividing internally dissipated power by the same incident intensity defines \(\sigma_{\rm abs}\). Extinction is \(\sigma_{\rm ext}=\sigma_{\rm sca}+\sigma_{\rm abs}\) for a passive isolated object in a lossless surrounding medium. All three have dimensions of area, even for a particle that has no sharply defined physical footprint.

The geometrical area of an object and its cross section answer different questions. A transparent object much smaller than its wavelength may scatter only a small fraction of the power geometrically incident on its silhouette. A resonant dipole can draw energy from an effective area comparable to a wavelength squared, much larger than a nanoparticle's silhouette. The incident field and the disturbance it generates coexist in the forward direction. Removing a fixed area from a ray diagram cannot represent the interference between those fields.

Represent the incident polarization by a normalized complex vector \(\mathbf e_0\perp\widehat{\mathbf z}\). Define a vector scattering amplitude of dimensions length by \(\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{\rm sca}=E_0 e^{ikr}\mathbf f(\mathbf n)/r+O(r^{-2})\) for the same \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention. The scattered wave is transverse to n; for a polarization-insensitive receiver \(d\sigma/d\Omega=|\mathbf f|^2\). With a receiving analyser \(\mathbf a\) only \(|\mathbf a^\dagger\mathbf f|^2\) is detected. Thus an intensity pattern alone loses information about phase and possibly polarization. The forward extinction will depend on one particular quadrature of that phase.

Derive the small sphere's polarizability from two boundary equations

In vacuum take a dielectric sphere of radius a with scalar relative permittivity \(\epsilon_r\) at the driving frequency. For \(ka\ll1\) and negligible retardation inside the sphere, solve the instantaneous-looking boundary problem for a phasor incident field \(E_0\hat z\). Axial symmetry and the degree-one angular dependence admit \(\Phi_{\rm in}=-C r\cos\theta\) and \(\Phi_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+D\cos\theta/r^2\). At r=a continuity of tangential E is equivalently continuity of these potentials; continuity of normal D supplies the other equation. They give

\[C=E_0-D/a^3,\]
\[\epsilon_r C=E_0+2D/a^3,\]
\[D=a^3{\epsilon_r-1\over\epsilon_r+2}E_0.\]

Comparing the decaying term with a dipole potential gives

\[p=4\pi\epsilon_0D.\]

Define the volume polarizability by \(p=\epsilon_0\alpha E_0\), so \(\alpha_0=4\pi a^3(\epsilon_r-1)/(\epsilon_r+2)\) in m³. Some conventions absorb \(\epsilon_0\) in \(\alpha\). Naming the convention avoids spurious permittivity factors when scattering formulas are converted between sources. At \(\epsilon_r\to1\) the particle becomes indistinguishable from its environment and the dipole vanishes. In the formal electrostatic conducting limit the coefficient tends toward \(4\pi a^3\).

Radiation from this induced electric dipole gives

\[\mathbf f(\mathbf n)={k^2\alpha\over4\pi}\,[\mathbf e_0-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_0)],\]
\[\sigma_{\rm sca}={k^4\over6\pi}|\alpha|^2 .\]

The projection shows why scattered light from unpolarized illumination becomes partially polarized at an angle: average the power of two orthogonal transverse incident polarizations. For observation angle \(\theta\) to the incident wavevector, the normalized angular factor is \((1+\cos^2\theta)/2\). At 90° an analyser can suppress the surviving polarization. The total cross section is independent of the orientation of a single linear polarization because the isotropic sphere can be rotated with that polarization.

Predict an absolute scale as well as a wavelength slope

For a transparent sphere \(a=20\) nm, index 1.50 so \(\epsilon_r=2.25\), and \(\lambda_0=600\) nm, the contrast factor is 0.2941 and \(ka=0.2094\). The quasistatic volume polarizability is \(2.96\times10^{-23}\) m³. Substitution gives \(\sigma_{\rm sca}\simeq5.6\times10^{-19}\) m² compared with geometric area \(1.26\times10^{-15}\) m². At otherwise fixed properties, halving radius divides \(|p|\) by eight and \(\sigma_{\rm sca}\) by 64. Changing wavelength from 600 to 500 nm instead increases the leading cross section by \((600/500)^4=2.074\). This scaling assumes that material dispersion and finite-size corrections have stayed sufficiently small.

For a dilute suspension with \(N=10^{16}\) particles/m³ path loss from independent scattering gives

\[dI/dz=-N\sigma_{\rm ext}I.\]

Over 0.10 m at the 600 nm parameters, optical depth is about \(5.6\times10^{-4}\), or 0.056% collimated intensity loss. Collecting a wider transmitted cone can admit some of the scattered light and reduce that apparent extinction. At high density fields scattered by one particle drive others and positional correlations can alter angular scattering. The isolated-particle prediction and exponential independent-particle extrapolation should be tested separately.

Find an interference signal that survives at arbitrarily large distance

Write the total field outside the scatterer as incident plane wave plus outgoing spherical wave. The Poynting flux through a large sphere contains incident-only, scattered-only and cross terms. The net incident-only flux through a closed sphere vanishes. The scattered-only term tends to \(I_0\sigma_{\rm sca}\). The interference term accounts for the reduction of incident energy in the forward beam. To see its scaling, examine a narrow forward cap; in a scalar polarization-preserving calculation the relevant angular oscillation is \(e^{ikr(1-\cos\theta)}\). The interference field is order \(1/r\), but the integration area is order \(r^2\); oscillatory phases cancel over most directions and leave a forward contribution of finite order.

Near the cap \(\theta\ll1\), \(1-\cos\theta\simeq\theta^2/2\), \(d\Omega\simeq2\pi\theta d\theta\). Introduce a smooth angular acceptance equal to one near zero. The leading forward factor in the integral is

\[2\pi\int_0^\infty \theta e^{ikr\theta^2/2}d\theta=2\pi i/(kr),\]

with arbitrarily weak positive damping understood before taking the large-radius limit. Magnetic and electric interference terms have the same forward polarization and supply twice the real cross product in total flux. A backward stationary contribution is cancelled by the opposing propagation directions in the net flux. With the outgoing-wave convention this gives

\[\sigma_{\rm ext}={4\pi\over k}\Im[\mathbf e_0^*\cdot\mathbf f(\widehat{\mathbf z})].\]

This is the optical theorem. A forward detector sensitive only to \(|\mathbf f(0)|^2\) is insufficient; the unscattered incident field acts as a coherent reference that picks out the appropriate quadrature. Changing the scattering-amplitude convention changes corresponding signs. Inserting a known passive dipole is a quick way to verify the convention used in a computational implementation.

There is an equivalent small-particle derivation. The cycle-averaged work extracted from a prescribed incident electric field is

\[\langle\mathbf E_{\rm inc}\cdot\dot{\mathbf p}\rangle=(\omega/2)\operatorname{Im}(\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{\rm inc}^*\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf p}).\]

Dividing by \(I_0=|E_0|^2/(2Z_0)\) gives

\[\sigma_{\rm ext}=k\operatorname{Im}\alpha.\]

In the forward direction the dipole formula gives \(\mathbf e_0^*\cdot\mathbf f(0)=k^2\alpha/(4\pi)\), reproducing that result exactly. Comparing integrated scattered power with interference-derived extinction tests both amplitude normalization and global energy accounting.

Repair the apparent contradiction of a real static polarizability

A strictly real static \(\alpha_0\) gives positive dipole radiation in \(\sigma_{\rm sca}\), yet inserting that same real number into \(k\Im\alpha\) would give zero extinction. The two formulas then mix orders of approximation. Replacing the driving field by its electrostatic value determined the leading in-phase moment. The small imaginary part needed to supply radiated energy arises at the next electrodynamic order. For a lossless purely dipolar particle energy conservation requires \(\sigma_{\rm ext}=\sigma_{\rm sca}\), hence

\[\Im\alpha={k^3\over6\pi}|\alpha|^2,\]
\[\Im(1/\alpha)=-{k^3\over6\pi}.\]

Retaining the static inverse real part yields a simple radiation correction \(\alpha=[\alpha_0^{-1}-ik^3/(6\pi)]^{-1}\). For a real \(\alpha_0\) this expression obeys the lossless optical theorem. It captures the radiative imaginary correction while ignoring possible real dynamic depolarization and higher multipoles; it is not an exact all-size sphere solution. If the material itself absorbs, a passive \(\alpha_0\) contributes additional loss, and \(\sigma_{\rm abs}=k\Im\alpha-k^4|\alpha|^2/(6\pi)\) must remain nonnegative.

Define \(b=k^3\alpha/(6\pi)\). Passivity is \(\Im b\ge|b|^2\), which is the disk \((\Re b)^2+(\Im b-1/2)^2\le1/4\) in a complex-amplitude diagram. Points on the boundary are purely radiative dipoles; interior points have additional internal absorption. The largest possible \(|b|\) is one at \(b=i\), giving \(\sigma_{\rm sca}=6\pi/k^2=3\lambda^2/(2\pi)\). At that point the particle is lossless and extinction is all scattering. The largest absorption occurs instead at \(b=i/2\). There \(k\Im\alpha=3\pi/k^2\), scattering is \(1.5\pi/k^2\), and the difference \(1.5\pi/k^2\) is absorbed. The result applies to one ideal electric dipole channel with the stated normalization. Additional channels can contribute separately for a larger or more complex object.

Choose the detector geometry that answers the desired question

A narrow-aperture transmission experiment measures an extinction-like signal if it isolates the original mode. A large integrating sphere estimates total outgoing scattered flux over its acceptance and provides an independent \(\sigma_{\rm sca}\); a temperature-rise measurement estimates heat deposition. For the same incident beam, the combination should satisfy

\[P_{\rm ext}=P_{\rm sca}+P_{\rm abs}.\]

Measuring extinction and assuming it is all absorption can exaggerate heating by orders of magnitude for transparent dielectric particles. Conversely reflection plus a small-angle detector may miss side-scattered light and turn that deficit into an apparent unexplained source term.

Suppose a calibrated intensity is \(2.0\times10^4\) W/m², transmission implies 60 pW extracted per particle, and integrated scattering gives 45 pW. The inferred cross sections are \(3.0\times10^{-15}\) and \(2.25\times10^{-15}\) m², leaving \(0.75\times10^{-15}\) m² for absorption and 15 pW thermal load. If each of the two power readings has independent 5 pW standard uncertainty, the difference has about 7.1 pW uncertainty. The inferred absorption is then much less certain in relative terms than either directly measured quantity. A negative fitted difference within that uncertainty should prompt an examination of collection angles and background subtraction rather than an immediate interpretation as optical gain.

How energy conservation fixes a scattering phase

The forward optical theorem can appear surprising because it relates a single angular amplitude to a sphere-integrated power. For a spherical scatterer this relationship can be seen one angular momentum channel at a time. Begin with a scalar plane wave scattering elastically from a spherically symmetric object in a lossless exterior. This scalar example exposes the mathematics; electromagnetic spherical waves have separate electric and magnetic polarisation families and start at angular momentum one. A scalar zeroth channel should never be inserted directly into a vector light-scattering calculation.

Outside a sphere containing the object, the radial equation for each Legendre component has independent incoming and outgoing spherical solutions. Define \(S_\ell\) as the ratio of outgoing to incoming wave amplitudes relative to its value for free propagation. The radial energy flux in a channel is proportional to the squared magnitude of the outgoing amplitude minus that of the incoming amplitude. For an elastic scatterer flux balance demands \(|S_\ell|=1\). Set \(S_\ell=e^{2i\delta_\ell}\) with real phase shift \(\delta_\ell\). The scattered outgoing wave is the change from the unscattered outgoing part, proportional to \(S_\ell-1\).

\[f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(2\ell+1)e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta),\]
\[\sigma_{\rm sca}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)\sin^2\delta_\ell .\]

The second result follows by integrating \(|f|^2\) and using \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_\ell P_m\,du=2\delta_{\ell m}/(2\ell+1)\). At forward angle all Legendre functions equal one. The imaginary part of \(e^{i\delta}\sin\delta\) is \(\sin^2\delta\), exactly the quantity appearing in the integrated cross section. This equality in each uncoupled channel proves the elastic optical theorem in the scalar example. It does not require the forward intensity \(|f(0)|^2\) to have any particular value. The calibrated phase of the forward amplitude is essential.

For a hypothetical acoustic sphere at wavenumber \(k=20\) per metre, suppose only the scalar zeroth phase shift is appreciable and has \(\delta_0=\pi/6\). Its scattering amplitude has real part 0.0217 m and imaginary part 0.0125 m, independent of viewing angle. Integrating its differential cross section gives \(4\pi(0.025\,\mathrm m)^2=0.00785\) square metres. The forward identity gives \(4\pi(0.0125)/20=0.00785\) square metres as well. Shifting the phase through \(\pi/2\) at this wavenumber increases the cross section by four, to the single-channel elastic maximum.

What an incomplete outgoing channel tells the experimenter

For an absorbing or internally converting target write \(S_\ell=\eta_\ell e^{2i\delta_\ell}\), \(0\leq\eta_\ell\leq1\). The missing outgoing flux in that exterior channel contributes to absorption or to unobserved channels, depending on the physical system. Define \(a_\ell=(S_\ell-1)/(2i)\), so the scalar wave amplitude above uses \(a_\ell=e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\) in the purely elastic limit. Elementary algebra gives

\[\operatorname{Im}a_\ell-|a_\ell|^2=\frac{1-|S_\ell|^2}{4},\]
\[\sigma_{\rm abs}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)(1-\eta_\ell^2).\]

For a single channel with \(\eta=0\), the scattered amplitude is \(i/2\). The residual coherent outgoing component relative to its incident plane-wave background corresponds to a finite scattered power even though this channel has zero total outgoing radial amplitude. Its absorbed and scattered cross sections are equal, while extinction is their sum. This distinction is the angular-channel counterpart of interference between incident and scattered fields in the forward direction.

An experiment may infer \(\eta<1\) because the detector fails to collect another outgoing polarisation, a converted elastic mode or a large-angle part of the pattern. To assign true heat absorption one needs the full exterior channel accounting or a calorimetric measurement. For a homogeneous liquid suspension distinguish energy dissipated in the nominal particles from energy dissipated in the surrounding medium. The preceding cross-section definitions assume a nonabsorbing reference host, so a lossy host requires an explicit incident-reference subtraction and an agreed control surface.

Suppose a measurement models the target only as an isotropic scalar scatterer, yet detects a pronounced forward lobe. A one-channel fit can reproduce a forward complex amplitude by choosing an appropriate complex \(a_0\), but it also predicts the same intensity at every other angle. Add a possible \(\ell=1\) component and measure at \(\theta=0,\pi/2,\pi\). At right angles the term proportional to \(P_1\) vanishes, which helps isolate the \(\ell=0\) component. Sum and difference of complex forward and backward amplitudes separate even and odd parity. Intensity-only data lose signs and relative phase and may require additional incident conditions or interferometry.

To check a numerical partial-wave calculation, increase the highest retained order at fixed geometry and compare the integrated angular quadrature with the coefficient sum. These diagnostics test the angular grid and truncation separately. A narrow forward peak may contribute little at any grid point if no nodes resolve its centre, yet contribute substantially to extinction. Gauss–Legendre quadrature in \(u=\cos\theta\) automatically includes the solid-angle measure. Finally compare the coefficient-sum extinction against the imaginary forward value with the chosen time convention. A sign error in outgoing radial waves can produce a beautifully converged negative extinction for a passive sample.

Worked suspension problem · infer and check an optical depth

A dilute suspension has independently measured single-particle extinction cross section \(5.0\times10^{-15}\) square metres and particle density \(3.0\times10^{14}\) per cubic metre. A narrow stop rejects the scattered halo. Predict the direct transmission through 20 mm and estimate how many particles occupy a cylindrical illuminated volume of radius 0.10 mm. Which approximation is most vulnerable if the radius is increased?

Derive the transport law and identify the role of the aperture

In an infinitesimal length \(dz\), the direct-beam intensity loses a fraction \(n\sigma_{\rm ext}dz\) in the independent, uniform, dilute limit. Integrating gives

\[I/I_0=e^{-n\sigma_{\rm ext}L}.\]

The extinction coefficient is 1.50 per metre and optical depth 0.0300, implying direct transmission 0.9704. A first-order calculation gives 0.9700. The small difference is consistent with neglect of higher powers in optical depth, but the exponential by itself does not describe multiple scattering into the receiver.

The illuminated volume is \(\pi(10^{-4})^2(0.020)=6.28\times10^{-10}\) cubic metres and contains about 188,000 particles on average. Increasing beam radius at fixed density and path length raises this count but leaves ideal optical depth unchanged. Counting more independent scatterers reduces relative sample fluctuations. It can, however, change beam divergence, spatial illumination and what scattered angles reach a finite detector. There is no general reason for local diluteness to fail merely because the beam area is larger.

Interchange cells of several lengths and measure the slope of the logarithm of transmitted intensity. An intercept different from zero suggests interface loss or a calibration offset if both incident and transmitted powers are referenced outside the cell. Repeat the length scan with a wider angular stop. A reduced slope with the wide stop signals that the detector collects part of the scattering halo; a genuine absorption contribution persists. At higher densities, evaluate interactions between particles or coherent structure before extrapolating the dilute-solution cross section from the same attenuation law.

A scattering measurement weights the large end of a distribution

The small-particle scattering law grows as the sixth power of radius for fixed material contrast and wavelength. A suspension with a modest spread of radii can therefore scatter substantially more than a calculation using the mean radius for every particle. If radii are independently distributed with mean \(a_0\) and small fractional standard deviation \(s\), expand \(a^6=a_0^6(1+\delta)^6\). The first correction to the ensemble mean is \(15s^2\) times the monodisperse value when the centred deviations average to zero. At ten percent relative spread, the leading increase is fifteen percent.

Separately measuring total particle material changes the comparison. Material volume scales as the third power, whose corresponding correction is \(3s^2\). At fixed material volume, the scattering per unit volume therefore increases by approximately \(12s^2\) relative to spheres of mean radius, within the same narrow-distribution approximation. Reporting the quantity held fixed changes the numerical prediction meaningfully.

A few larger outliers can invalidate this expansion and even take the sample beyond the Rayleigh regime. Measure a size distribution independently and average the single-particle cross section over that distribution, including detector acceptance if it varies with size. A fit to a single apparent radius may match total intensity at one wavelength while failing its spectral slope or angular pattern. Comparing two wavelengths and a separate volume estimate gives a practical check on that hidden weighting.

Tutorial · examine two proposed dipole fits for passivity

At a fixed wavelength two fits to an angular intensity pattern return \(b_1=0.30+0.10i\) and \(b_2=0.30-0.10i\), with \(b=k^3\alpha/(6\pi)\). Can angular intensity distinguish them? Find normalized scattering, extinction and absorption by dividing each cross section by \(6\pi/k^2\). Which fit can describe a passive particle?

Compare interference with scattered intensity

Both have \(|b|^2=0.10\), giving the same angular scattering and normalized scattered cross section 0.10. Extinction in these units is \(\Im b\), hence +0.10 or −0.10. Absorption is the difference, zero or −0.20. The first sits on the lossless passivity circle. The second requires an active energy supply if described by these conventions. Even an angular pattern measured over the whole sphere would retain that ambiguity because it contains squared magnitudes. The forward interference phase or an independent extinction reading discriminates.

Tutorial · recover a particle radius with a stated systematic uncertainty

At fixed wavelength and index a dilute sample scatters 1.50 times the reference signal per unit volume. Assume initially the same number density and a Rayleigh regime. Infer the radius ratio. Then allow number density to be 20% higher than assumed. Estimate how much of the signal difference could instead be a concentration effect, and propose a phase-sensitive check of the dipole interpretation.

Track the powers of radius and concentration separately

Scattered power is proportional to \(Na^6\) under these assumptions, so the radius ratio is

\[1.50^{1/6}=1.070.\]

If number density is actually 1.20 times the reference, the ratio becomes

\[(1.50/1.20)^{1/6}=1.038.\]

An apparently precise intensity can therefore constrain a combined quantity quite well without determining either factor separately. Measure an independent particle count or concentration. A forward extinction measurement should satisfy the optical theorem, while the real part of the dilute coherent refractive response scales mainly with \(N\Re\alpha\sim Na^3\). Combining these observables is better conditioned than repeating a single intensity measurement.
