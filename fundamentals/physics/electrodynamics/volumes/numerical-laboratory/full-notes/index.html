Numerical field laboratory · 1. A converged field solver can still give the wrong voltage

Verify a two-dimensional electrostatic calculation with two independent charge patterns. Use symmetry to distinguish a transposed output, a coarse mesh and a poorly converged linear solve.

Choose a voltage before choosing a solver

A very long grounded tube has a square cross section \(0\le x,y\le L\), with \(L=1\) m. The contents are uniform along the axis. A model of its transverse charge density should predict what a voltage probe placed inside would measure. Three numerical statements that can sound similar are actually quite different: the discrete equations have a small residual, the grid solution approximates the continuous solution, and the physical source describes the tube. We can test the first two in a laboratory problem where the last is specified exactly.

Start by prescribing a voltage that satisfies all four walls. Introduce \(s_m(x)=\sin(m\pi x/L)\) and choose \(V_*(x,y)=[s_1(x)+g s_2(x)]s_1(y)\) volts. The parameter \(g\) is the signed amplitude of the second spatial pattern relative to the first. A probe at the centre sees exactly 1 V for every \(g\), since \(s_2(L/2)=0\). A calculation checked at that point alone could entirely lose the second pattern and still pass. At \(x=L/4\) on the centreline, \(V_*=1/\sqrt2+g\); at \(3L/4\) it is \(1/\sqrt2-g\). Their difference is \(2g\) volts. First write down all three expected readings for \(g=0.5\).

Manufacture the source by differentiating the field

The physical problem uses Gauss's law and the electrostatic relation \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\). For uniform vacuum permittivity they yield

\[-\nabla^2V=\rho/\epsilon_0.\]

A product \(s_m(x)s_1(y)\) returns the factor \((m^2+1)\pi^2/L^2\) when acted on by the negative Laplacian. Applying this separately to the two patterns gives

\[\rho/\epsilon_0=(\pi/L)^2[2s_1(x)+5g s_2(x)]s_1(y)\ {\rm V}.\]

The right side has dimensions V/m² after including the factor \(L^{-2}\); multiplication by permittivity gives C/m³. When \(g\) becomes large enough, part of the imposed density changes sign. That is permissible for a manufactured source. The calculation represents a solution of the stated Poisson problem. Judging whether a particular apparatus could maintain that charge requires its own charge-transport model.

Uniqueness gives the reference solution special status. If there were a different voltage for the same walls and charge, their difference \(w\) would satisfy Laplace's equation with zero boundary value. Integrate \(w(-\nabla^2 w)\) over the square. Integration by parts leaves \(\int|\nabla w|^2\), because \(w\) is zero on the boundary. The integral must vanish; \(w\) is constant and its boundary makes that constant zero. This is why agreement with the manufactured voltage is a test of the solver and its input, rather than a choice between two physical solutions.

Background · How can the same source test a second boundary condition?

A smooth manufactured solution that vanishes on every edge tests a homogeneous Dirichlet solve. A code that incorrectly sets every requested boundary voltage to zero may still pass. Add a harmonic lift to the known field on a square of width \(L\). For a constant permittivity the charge depends on the Laplacian, so the exact source is unchanged.

\[\widetilde V(x,y)=V(x,y)+V_0\!\left(1+\frac{x}{L}\right),\]
\[-\epsilon\nabla^2\widetilde V=-\epsilon\nabla^2 V.\]

Prescribe the lifted values on all four sides and solve again using the same charge samples. The difference of the computed solutions should be the linear lift, which a centred second difference represents exactly on a uniform grid. A discrepancy points first to boundary-value handling or to the way known values enter the right-hand side. This comparison isolates that error from the truncation error on the original sine modes.

Solve the discrete problem on paper as a second reference

Let \(h=L/n\). The standard negative Laplacian has centre coefficient \(4/h^2\) and four neighbouring coefficients \(-1/h^2\). Substitute the samples of \(s_m(x)s_1(y)\). In the \(x\) direction the sum of forward and backward neighbours is twice the centre value times \(\cos(m\pi/n)\). Collect the corresponding \(y\) term. The resulting positive grid eigenvalue is

\[\lambda_{h,m}=\frac{4}{h^2}\left[\sin^2\!\frac{m\pi}{2n}+\sin^2\!\frac{\pi}{2n}\right].\]

The continuum eigenvalue is \(\lambda_m=(m^2+1)(\pi/L)^2\). If the source is the continuous source sampled at the nodes, the discrete voltage coefficient is multiplied by \(\lambda_m/\lambda_{h,m}\). Since \(\sin z<z\) for positive \(z\), each factor exceeds one on a resolving grid. The faster spatial oscillation has the larger relative bias. The resulting total pointwise error can still change sign because the spatial modes themselves have signs.

This provides three independent calculations: a continuous closed-form field, an exact solution of the chosen difference equations and a numerical linear solve. Agreement of the latter two isolates assembly, indexing and algebraic errors. Comparing either with the continuous solution measures spatial discretisation error. Tightening an iterative tolerance indefinitely cannot remove a difference between \(\lambda_{h,m}\) and \(\lambda_m\).

Extend the verification to a genuinely three-dimensional field

Ground all six faces of a cube with side length L=1 m. A potential that varies in every coordinate is

\[V_*(x,y,z)=A\sin(\pi x/L)\sin(\pi y/L)\sin(\pi z/L),\]
\[\rho=3\epsilon(\pi/L)^2 V_*.\]

Each second derivative contributes −(π/L)² times the potential. Adding the three derivatives gives the source above; every boundary vanishes because one sine is zero. This is a manufactured source for checking a numerical method. In the supplied calculation ε=1 F/m and A=1 V; scaling both charge and permittivity together leaves the voltage unchanged.

On a uniform nodal grid, the seven-point negative Laplacian has centre coefficient 6/h² and six neighbour coefficients −1/h². The same sine addition identity used in two dimensions gives

\[\lambda_h={12\over h^2}\sin^2\!\left({\pi h\over2L}\right),\]
\[V_h={3(\pi/L)^2\over\lambda_h}V_* .\]

The browser evaluates this discrete eigenmode directly. The spatial view places a coloured slice inside the cube; its height is the physical z coordinate. Moving that slice reveals the interior field while the companion line plot compares the grid values with the exact three-dimensional solution. The numerical potential is slightly too large because λh is smaller than its continuum limit.

"""Solve a grounded three-dimensional cube with OneSimulation and verify the field."""
import argparse
from dataclasses import dataclass
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from onesim.cad.d3 import Box
from onesim.meshing import StructuredHexahedralMesher
from onesim.numerics import Numerics
from onesim.numerics.linear import SparseDirect
from onesim.numerics.fdm import FiniteDifference, CentralDifference, CentralLaplacian
from onesim.physics.electrostatics import FixedPotential, PoissonEquation
from onesim.simulation import Problem, Simulation

parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
parser.add_argument('--cells', type=int, default=16)
parser.add_argument('--amplitude', type=float, default=1.0, help='Reference potential amplitude in V')
parser.add_argument('--slice', type=float, default=50, help='Depth as a percentage of the cube')
args = parser.parse_args()
if not 8 <= args.cells <= 24 or not np.isfinite(args.amplitude) or args.amplitude <= 0 or not 0 <= args.slice <= 100:
    parser.error('Use 8–24 cells, a finite positive amplitude, and a slice between 0 and 100 percent')

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Permittivity:
    unit = 'F/m'
    def evaluate(self, **state):
        return 1.0

cube = Box(length=1, width=1, height=1)
exact = lambda x, y, z: args.amplitude * np.sin(np.pi*x) * np.sin(np.pi*y) * np.sin(np.pi*z)
field = PoissonEquation(domain=cube.volume, permittivity=Permittivity(),
                        charge_density=lambda x, y, z: 3*np.pi**2*exact(x,y,z))
problem = Problem(name='Three-dimensional manufactured potential', geometry=cube, physics=(field,),
                  boundary_conditions=(FixedPotential(physics=field, boundary=cube.boundaries.all, value=0.0),))
mesh = StructuredHexahedralMesher(cells=(args.cells,)*3).generate(cube)
simulation = Simulation(problem=problem, mesh=mesh, numerics=Numerics(
    spatial=FiniteDifference(gradient=CentralDifference(), diffusion=CentralLaplacian()),
    linear_solver=SparseDirect()))
simulation.validate().require_valid()
result = simulation.solve_stationary()
potential = result.field(field.potential)
centres = mesh.coordinates[mesh.cells].mean(axis=1)
computed = potential.evaluate(centres)
reference = exact(*centres.T)
error = np.sqrt(np.mean((computed-reference)**2))
z = round(args.cells*args.slice/100)/args.cells
print(f'{args.cells}³ cells; source amplitude {args.amplitude:g} V; slice z={z:.3f} m')
print(f'Cell-centre RMS error against the exact 3D solution: {error:.6e} V')

x = np.linspace(0,1,41)
X,Y = np.meshgrid(x,x)
points = np.column_stack([X.ravel(),Y.ravel(),np.full(X.size,z)])
V = potential.evaluate(points).reshape(X.shape)
line = np.column_stack([x,np.full_like(x,.5),np.full_like(x,z)])
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(10,4),layout='constrained')
ax = fig.add_subplot(121,projection='3d')
surface = ax.plot_surface(X,Y,V,cmap='viridis',linewidth=0)
ax.set(xlabel='x (m)',ylabel='y (m)',zlabel='Potential (V)',title=f'Computed slice at z={z:g} m')
ax = fig.add_subplot(122)
ax.plot(x,potential.evaluate(line),'o',markevery=2,label='OneSimulation FDM')
ax.plot(x,exact(x,.5,z),'--',label='Exact 3D solution')
ax.set(xlabel='x at y=0.5 m (m)',ylabel='Potential (V)')
ax.grid(alpha=.2); ax.legend()
fig.savefig('poisson-cube-3d.png',dpi=170)
assert np.isfinite(error) and error/args.amplitude < .02

Numerical field laboratory · 2. Measure the capacitance of a continuously graded dielectric

Find the exact solution of a variable-coefficient field equation, then estimate the same capacitance from charge, voltage drop and electrostatic energy.

Build the problem from what is continuous

Two very large parallel plates are separated by \(L=1\) m. Set their potentials to 0 and 1 V. Edge effects are negligible in the observation region. The relative permittivity of the material varies as \(\epsilon_r(x)=1+g x/L\), where \(g\ge0\). There is no free volume charge. A common first guess is a linear potential between the plates, because the boundary voltages impose an average field. Test that guess using the local field law.

For a thin pillbox whose faces are perpendicular to \(x\), zero enclosed free charge means equal normal displacement at both faces. Let \(D_*>0\) denote the magnitude of the constant displacement. Since potential increases with \(x\), the electric field points towards negative \(x\) and \(D_x=-D_*\). Hence \(V'=D_*/[\epsilon_0(1+g x/L)]\). Regions of low permittivity must sustain a larger electric field and account for a larger voltage drop per metre. We have a local constraint before solving a single matrix equation.

Obtain the voltage and both expressions for capacitance

Integrate \(V'\) from left to right and impose \(V(L)-V(0)=\Delta V\). For \(g>0\) this gives

\[\frac{C}{A}=\frac{D_*}{\Delta V}=\frac{\epsilon_0}{L}\frac{g}{\log(1+g)},\]
\[\frac{V(x)}{\Delta V}=\frac{\log(1+gx/L)}{\log(1+g)}.\]

As \(g\) approaches zero, both expressions have removable singularities. Since \(\log(1+g)=g-g^2/2+O(g^3)\), the effective relative permittivity approaches \(1+g/2+O(g^2)\). An arithmetic average can therefore look convincing at very small gradients despite having the wrong general derivation. For computation near zero, use a logarithm specifically evaluated as \(\log(1+g)\) with a small-argument routine such as log1p.

Check the result through stored energy. Each volume element contributes \(\epsilon E^2/2\), so

\[U/A=(D_*^2/2)\int_0^L dx/\epsilon(x).\]

Substituting the voltage relation gives

\[U/A=D_*\Delta V/2.\]

The same capacitance follows from \(C/A=2U/(A\Delta V^2)\). This equality requires a linear, lossless dielectric and a reversible charging path. A material whose permittivity changes with field needs the corresponding integral over the constitutive curve.

Work one case numerically before assembling the grid

At \(g=3\), the material goes from relative permittivity 1 to 4. The effective value is \(3/\log4\simeq2.1640\), smaller than the arithmetic mean 2.5. The midplane voltage is \(\log2.5/\log4\simeq0.6610\) V, not 0.5 V. The electric field at the left is four times as large as at the right. A measurement of the midpoint voltage alone is enough to reject a uniform-field model if the plates and material are well characterised.

As a dimensional check, \(C/A\simeq19.16\) pF/m² for \(L=1\) m. For a geometrically similar layer one millimetre thick the same effective permittivity gives \(19.16\) nF/m². The voltage profile versus \(x/L\) stays fixed when \(g\) and the plate voltages stay fixed, while the field and free surface charge increase in inverse proportion to separation.

Assemble by balancing fluxes before dividing by a coefficient

Divide the gap into \(n\) cells. Across cell \(i\) approximate the displacement magnitude by \(D_i=\epsilon_{i+1/2}(V_{i+1}-V_i)/h\). Enforcing \(D_i=D_{i-1}\) at each internal node gives a tridiagonal linear system. An independent route views each cell as a capacitor with inverse capacitance per area \(h/\epsilon_{i+1/2}\). These series contributions add, giving \(D_*^{(h)}=\Delta V/\sum h/\epsilon_{i+1/2}\). Cumulative sums of the same drops then recover the entire grid potential. Both routes implement identical physics and the same discrete constitutive assumption.

The browser uses this cumulative series construction. The OneSimulation input instead supplies the spatial coefficient \(1+gx/L\), zero source and the two exterior potential values to the coefficient-form PDE solver. Their agreement checks assembly against a distinct algorithm. In this example the integral of inverse permittivity also tells us what the midpoint discretisation approximates. Since \(1/(1+gx/L)\) is convex, midpoint quadrature underestimates its integral and therefore overestimates capacitance. This predicts the bias before looking at a convergence table.

Numerical field laboratory · 3. Is a computed screening layer resolved?

Use a measured decay length to select a mesh, derive the exact discrete penetration constant and decide whether a discrepancy belongs to the physical screening law or the numerical grid.

Derive the screened field and state its range

A dilute sample adjacent to an electrode can often be approximated, for small applied potentials, by a relation \(\rho(x)=-\epsilon V(x)/\lambda^2\). It says that an excess positive potential attracts net negative charge. The parameter \(\lambda\) is independently calibrated for the ambient material. Combining this relation with Poisson's equation yields

\[V''=V/\lambda^2.\]

A plus sign is essential: the physical disturbance decays into the sample. Changing the sign would turn a decaying layer into an oscillatory boundary-value problem.

Set \(V(0)=V_s\) and \(V(L)=0\). Scale the voltage by \(V_s\), position by \(L\), and write \(g=L/\lambda\). The dimensionless problem is

\[-u''+g^2u=0,\ u(0)=1,\ u(1)=0.\]

The program solves for unit boundary voltage as a normalisation. For a dilute monovalent electrolyte at room temperature the linearised microscopic model requires the physical \(|V_s|\) to be small compared with \(k_BT/e\), roughly 26 mV. A computed unit-amplitude solution can represent a small physical voltage by multiplication. Applying 1 physical volt to the linearised electrolyte would generally exceed this assumption.

Solve the finite slab before taking the half-space limit

The general solution contains \(e^{gx}\) and \(e^{-gx}\). Impose the right voltage first, using \(\sinh[g(1-x)]\), then divide by \(\sinh g\) to set the left voltage. Thus \(u=\sinh[g(1-x)]/\sinh g\). For \(g=0\) take the limit to get \(u=1-x\). For a thick slab and fixed physical distance from the left, use \(e^{-gx}(1-e^{-2g(1-x)})/(1-e^{-2g})\). Close to the right wall the subtracted exponential is needed to satisfy grounding. In the interior many screening lengths away, even a big relative error can correspond to a negligible physical voltage.

At \(g=4\), the midpoint voltage fraction is \(1/(2\cosh 2)\simeq0.1329\). The half-space estimate is

\[e^{-2}=0.1353.\]

A sample only four screening lengths across is already close to the half-space answer at its middle, though its right edge still has exactly zero voltage rather than \(e^{-4}\). Determine where your instrument can distinguish the profiles before treating one as a better physical model.

Obtain the grid behaviour without running a solver

With grid spacing \(h=1/n\), the interior stencil is

\[-u_{i-1}+(2+g^2h^2)u_i-u_{i+1}=0.\]

Try a geometric progression \(u_i=e^{-\gamma_h i h}\). Divide through by the trial value. The sum of its neighbours gives

\[2\cosh(\gamma_h h)=2+(gh)^2.\]

Since \(\cosh(2z)=1+2\sinh^2z\), the effective decay constant is

\[\gamma_h=\frac{2}{h}\operatorname{asinh}(gh/2)=g-\frac{g^3h^2}{24}+O(g^5h^4).\]

Thus the numerical layer is slightly too broad for a coarse mesh. The relevant resolution is \(gh=h/\lambda\) in consistent units, not the cell count alone. Keeping \(n\) fixed while increasing \(g\) makes a visually unchanged mesh physically coarser. The finite-slab grid reference follows by replacing \(g\) with \(\gamma_h\) in the hyperbolic-sine ratio at the nodes.

Numerical field laboratory · 4. What can a mesh measure about a point charge?

Distinguish conserved charge and measurable exterior fields from a numerical peak that diverges as the source becomes more localised.

Ask three detectors different questions

Imagine three instruments looking at a small charged body in vacuum. One measures the voltage on a spherical probe outside it, one measures the integrated electric flux through a closed surface, and one estimates the largest electric field somewhere near the source. If the body is idealised as a point, which readings have finite limits? The distinction matters when a calculation displays a dramatic change in maximum field after a perfectly reasonable mesh refinement.

For a positive point charge \(q\), the exterior potential \(V=kq/r\) tends to infinity as the observation point approaches the source. Its radial field magnitude \(kq/r^2\) also diverges. Gauss's law gives a different answer for the flux through any sphere of positive radius:

\[4\pi r^2E=q/\epsilon_0.\]

A finite integral and an unbounded maximum are compatible. Knowing what the instrument measures determines which numerical output should be expected to converge.

Use a smooth source with a known answer

Replace the potential by \(V_a=kq/\sqrt{r^2+a^2}\), with \(a>0\) a distance. This is a particular physical regularisation. Differentiating before sampling gives

\[E_a=kq r/(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}.\]

Divide the flux by \(q/\epsilon_0\). The putative enclosed-charge fraction becomes

\[F_a(r)=\frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}.\]

To recover the corresponding density, differentiate \(qF_a(r)\) with respect to radius and divide by shell area. The terms involving \(r^4\) cancel, leaving \(\rho_a(r)=3qa^2/[4\pi(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}]\). This density is smooth, nonnegative and integrates over all space to \(q\), since \(F_a(0)=0\) and \(F_a(\infty)=1\). It has an extended tail. The parameter \(a\) is a smoothing scale rather than an outer radius where charge abruptly stops.

At \(r=a\) only \(1/(2\sqrt2)\), about 35.36%, of the charge is enclosed. At \(r=2a\) the fraction is about 71.55%. A common intuition that two smoothing distances already contain essentially everything fails for this distribution. Measure the probe radius in units of the source size before blaming the flux calculation.

The exterior-field ratio relative to the true point charge is \([1+(a/r)^2]^{-3/2}\). At fixed physical \(r\), expanding the bracket gives \(1-\tfrac32(a/r)^2+O((a/r)^4)\). Thus a probe far enough away sees a second-order regularisation error. Locating the maximum of \(E_a\), however, requires setting \(a^2-2r^2=0\), giving \(r=a/\sqrt2\) and a peak proportional to \(1/a^2\). Use continuity as a numerical diagnostic

Suppose a code deposits a prescribed charge into nearby cells and then solves for a nodal potential. Summing deposited cell charges tests normalisation of the source. Numerical field laboratory · 5. Measure the phase speed of a numerical electromagnetic wave

Start with a lossless Maxwell wave, build a second-order time update and compare its actual travel time with what the continuum wave equation predicts.

A pulse can arrive late without losing energy

In a uniform lossless one-dimensional material take electric field along \(y\), magnetic field along \(z\) and propagation along \(x\). Maxwell's curl laws reduce to

\[\epsilon E_t=-H_x,\]
\[\mu H_t=-E_x.\]

Differentiate the first in time and substitute the second to obtain

\[E_{tt}=c^2 E_{xx},\]
\[c=1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}.\]

A real laboratory signal is a combination of spatial frequencies. Different errors in travel time for different components can distort its shape even when the scheme does not attenuate any resolved Fourier mode.

Use periodic boundaries so that reflection from an end cannot be confused with this effect. Scale distance by one wavelength and time by one exact period. The periodic field \(E(x,t)=\sin[2\pi(x-t)]\) has unit amplitude and returns to its initial shape after every integer period. A probe comparing just its envelope would miss a discrepancy in phase.

Derive the discrete dispersion relation

Take points separated by \(h\) and times separated by \(\Delta t\). Centred second differences give

\[u_j^{n+1}=2u_j^n-u_j^{n-1}+C^2(u_{j+1}^n-2u_j^n+u_{j-1}^n),\]

with \(C=c\Delta t/h\). Insert the complex trial field \(e^{i(j\theta-n\Omega)}\). The time pair contributes \(2\cos\Omega-2\), the spatial pair \(2\cos\theta-2\). The physical positive-frequency branch obeys

\[\sin(\Omega/2)=C\sin(\theta/2),\]
\[\frac{c_\mathrm{phase,grid}}{c}=\frac{\Omega}{C\theta}.\]

If \(0<C\le1\) a real \(\Omega\) exists for every grid phase, and each Fourier mode has constant discrete amplitude in exact arithmetic when started on one travelling branch. For \(C>1\) sufficiently rapid spatial oscillations have no real frequency. One time factor then grows. Testing stability on a single very smooth wave can miss this unstable part of the supported spectrum.

For long waves expand \(\sin\) and \(\arcsin\):

\[\Omega=C\theta+C(C^2-1)\theta^3/24+O(\theta^5).\]

The relative phase-speed error is \((C^2-1)\theta^2/24\) at leading order. Here increasing \(C\) towards one improves wave propagation. This special cancellation depends on a uniform one-dimensional grid, constant wave speed and the selected difference operators.

Initialise a clean numerical experiment

A second-order recurrence needs two time levels. Taking \(u_j^0=\sin(j\theta)\) and \(u_j^{-1}=\sin(j\theta+\Omega)\) excites a single right-going numerical mode. Using the exact continuum phase \(C\theta\) at the second level instead introduces a small mixture of right- and left-travelling grid modes. That is a valid test of initialisation, but it mixes errors if the quantity of interest is solely the numerical dispersion relation. State in your notes how the second level was produced.

Open the live experiment

The time is chosen as an integer number of numerical steps close to five exact periods and is stated with the result. At twelve points per wavelength and \(C=0.8\) there are fifteen steps per period. Both exact and discrete solutions have the same amplitude to roundoff. At the end of five periods examine how far their zero crossings have separated.

For a narrow packet, envelope motion is set by group velocity \(d\omega/dk\). Differentiate the discrete relation at fixed \(h,\Delta t\). The result is \(v_g/c=\cos(\theta/2)/\sqrt{1-C^2\sin^2(\theta/2)}\) on the positive branch for resolved nonendpoint modes. At \(C=0.8\) a mode close to the grid Nyquist limit has small group speed. A wave carrier and its envelope can show different speed errors. In a real dielectric wavelength dependence may also come from the material itself; a grid-refinement test with unchanged material law separates these contributions.

Numerical field laboratory · 6. Move the reference plane of a wave measurement

Recover a load reflection coefficient from a noncontact voltage measurement and explain why identical amplitude data can represent physically different terminations.

Specify which plane defines the load

A uniform lossless transmission line has real characteristic impedance \(Z_0=50\ \Omega\). Drive it sinusoidally at one frequency. Choose peak phasors with time factor \(e^{i\omega t}\) and measure distance \(d\) from the load towards the source. Incident and reflected waves move in opposite directions. Taking the load as phase origin, the ratio of the reflected to incident voltage at the load is

\[\Gamma_L=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0).\]

The ratio at a sensor plane a distance \(d\) away is \(\Gamma_\mathrm{in}=\Gamma_Le^{-2i\beta d}\). The factor two is the phase difference of two opposite travellers. Moving the sensor does not change a physical resistor.

For a real resistive load, \(\Gamma_L\) is real and ranges from −1 for a short to +1 for an open. A 75 Ω load gives +0.2 and a \(100/3\) Ω load gives −0.2. These loads have the same reflected power fraction of four per cent. Distinguishing them requires phase information or positioning the sensor at more than one known location.

Derive the envelope and the ambiguity

The local voltage is the sum of incident and reflected phasors. Factor out the incident phase and take the magnitude:

\[\frac{|V(d)|^2}{|V^+|^2}=1+|\Gamma_L|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\{\Gamma_Le^{-2i\beta d}\}.\]

Its maxima and minima have amplitude ratios \(1+|\Gamma|\) and \(1-|\Gamma|\) for a passive load, giving \(\mathrm{VSWR}=(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)\). The distance between successive maxima is \(\lambda/2\), because the measured intensity involves a relative wave number \(2\beta\). A measured ratio 1.5 yields \(|\Gamma|=0.2\) but leaves its sign and, for general impedances, its phase undetermined. Extremum positions contain information that a ratio discards.

At one eighth of a wavelength from a 75 Ω load the phase rotation is \(-\pi/2\), giving \(\Gamma_\mathrm{in}=-0.2i\). If a researcher plugs that complex coefficient directly into \(Z=Z_0(1+\Gamma)/(1-\Gamma)\), the inferred impedance is about \(46.15-19.23i\ \Omega\). That is the correct input impedance at the sensor plane. To infer the actual element multiply back by \(e^{2i\beta d}\) before the inversion. The recovered load is then 75 Ω with zero imaginary part.

Numerical field laboratory · 7. When a film is thinner than a skin depth

Solve a finite conducting layer in complex notation and independently account for its losses using a surface impedance and a volume integral.

Which field does the back face support?

A wide conducting film carries an alternating sheet current in the \(x\) direction. The field varies only with depth \(z\) across thickness \(t\). The surrounding configuration is arranged so the tangential magnetic field at the back face is negligible; at the front its peak phasor is \(H_s\). This back condition describes one particular excitation. A film illuminated from both sides, a nearby magnetic backing or a transmitted wave in a substrate need different conditions. A skin-depth formula by itself does not determine a film impedance.

Use the time convention \(e^{i\omega t}\), a real scalar conductivity \(\sigma\), real permeability \(\mu\), and the good-conductor approximation \(\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon\). Write \(H_y=H(z)\) and \(J_x=-H'(z)\). Faraday's law then gives

\[H''=i\omega\mu\sigma H.\]

Define \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)}\) and \(\gamma=(1+i)/\delta\), so \(\gamma^2=i\omega\mu\sigma\). \(H\) and its derivative can be complex even though the observable field is real.

Impose both surfaces before computing power

A solution satisfying the rear condition is

\[H(z)=H_s\sinh[\gamma(t-z)]/\sinh(\gamma t).\]

The electric field at the front is \(-H'(0)/\sigma\). Dividing it by the applied field gives the sheet impedance,

\[Z_s=\frac{\gamma}{\sigma}\coth(\gamma t).\]

The thick limit gives \(Z_s=(1+i)/(\sigma\delta)\), a resistive and reactive surface contribution of equal size. To find the thin limit expand \(\coth w=1/w+w/3+O(w^3)\). Substitution gives \(Z_s\simeq1/(\sigma t)+i\omega\mu t/3\). The first term is the ordinary DC resistance per square. The second is the internal inductive reactance for the chosen one-sided magnetic boundary condition. Its factor one third can be checked by integrating the magnetic energy of a linear field profile through the thickness.

Take \(\sigma=4.0\times10^7\) S/m and a frequency giving \(\delta=0.2\) mm. The thick-film resistance scale is \(1/(\sigma\delta)=0.125\) mΩ per square. A \(20\ \mu\mathrm m\) film has \(t/\delta=0.1\); its DC value is 1.25 mΩ per square, already ten times the thick-conductor scale. Treating a very thin film as a half-space can severely underestimate heat production for a specified sheet current.

Derive two independent loss measurements

The inward time-averaged Poynting flux per area equals \(\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E_s H_s^*)=|H_s|^2\operatorname{Re}Z_s/2\) for peak phasors in this geometry. Independently the local cycle-averaged Joule power density is \(|H'|^2/(2\sigma)\). Integrate it from front to back. A quick proof of equality avoids pages of hyperbolic algebra: integrate \((H'H^*)'=H''H^*+|H'|^2\) across the film. The term \(H''H^*=i\omega\mu\sigma|H|^2\) is purely imaginary. Take real parts, insert \(H(t)=0\), and the boundary term at zero reproduces the surface expression. An unexpected minus sign here often means the outward normal has been used as though it pointed into the sheet.

The imaginary part of the same identity measures reactive magnetic storage. This separation helps diagnose a phase-sign mistake: conjugating every phasor reverses the sign assigned to reactive quantities for the opposite time convention, while average Joule heating stays positive. An output that predicts negative dissipation for a passive film warrants checking definitions before interpreting a material resonance.

Recover the far-field angular spectrum of a smooth aperture, then isolate the errors from truncating and sampling the aperture field.

Begin with a transform that can be checked analytically

Consider a long slit system whose transmitted scalar field varies along one transverse coordinate \(x\). At a distant observation point in direction \(\theta\), a small aperture element accumulates an approximate relative phase \(-k x\sin\theta\). The Fraunhofer amplitude is proportional to \(\int A(x)e^{-2\pi i f x}\,dx\) with spatial frequency \(f=\sin\theta/\lambda\). The approximation neglects the quadratic phase across the aperture; either a suitable lens or sufficient propagation distance is needed. A numerical Fourier transform cannot cure violation of this optical assumption.

Take the ideal aperture field \(A(x)=e^{-x^2/w^2}\) and normalise far-field amplitude by its on-axis value. Integrating by parts or completing the square gives

\[F(f)/F(0)=e^{-(\pi wf)^2}.\]

One derivation avoids any complex contour: differentiate \(F\) with respect to \(f\) and replace \(xA=-(w^2/2)A'\). Integrating by parts gives

\[F'=-2\pi^2 w^2 f F.\]

Solve this first-order equation with the measured \(F(0)\). Thus a wider beam on the aperture narrows the far-field distribution. Squaring the amplitude gives the intensity measured by an ordinary detector.

At \(w=1\) mm, the \(1/e\) amplitude spatial frequency is \(1/(\pi w)\simeq0.3183\) cycles/mm. For 500 nm light this is a small angle of \(1.59\times10^{-4}\) radians. An instrument calibrated in radians per millimetre rather than cycles per millimetre would need a factor \(2\pi\); stating the Fourier convention prevents that frequent scale discrepancy.

What the instrument has really sampled

A scanner covers \(-W\le x\le W\) and gives one amplitude at each of \(N\) equally spaced cell midpoints. The numerical transform is the weighted sum \(\Delta x\sum_j A(x_j)e^{-2\pi ifx_j}\), where \(\Delta x=2W/N\). By symmetry its imaginary part vanishes for a perfectly centred real Gaussian; the program sums the cosine projection. It normalises by the numerical on-axis amplitude, allowing us to compare shapes on a common vertical scale.

Two separate approximations have entered. Truncating the aperture multiplies the field by a rectangle. In frequency space this convolves the Gaussian spectrum with a sinc and can produce signed sidelobes. Sampling at regular intervals makes the spectrum repeat up to a phase convention, with alias copies separated by \(1/\Delta x\). Increasing \(W\) at fixed \(N\) reduces truncation but worsens sampling. Widening the field of view and adding more detector pixels are not interchangeable changes.

Numerical field laboratory · 9. Estimate a cavity linewidth from a finite, biased record

Connect the time and frequency descriptions of one damped mode, then diagnose why a weak detector background systematically biases the inferred quality factor.

Know whether the detector reads field or energy

After a drive switches off, let a single cavity mode have complex analytic amplitude \(a(t)=a_0e^{-\kappa t/2}e^{-i\omega_0t}\) for \(t\ge0\). The energy is proportional to \(|a|^2\), giving \(U=U_0e^{-\kappa t}\). Its instantaneous decay rate \(-U'/U=\kappa\) implies

\[Q=\omega_0 U/P_\mathrm{loss}=\omega_0/\kappa.\]

A phase-sensitive receiver measuring field amplitude sees a time constant twice as long as the energy lifetime. Before fitting the data, ask how the measurement transducer converts the field into a reported number.

Fourier transforming the causal decaying amplitude gives an integral proportional to \(1/[\kappa/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)]\), up to the time convention. Its squared magnitude has half-power points at \(|\omega-\omega_0|=\kappa/2\). Therefore the full power linewidth in hertz is \(\Delta f=\kappa/(2\pi)\). A measurement of either the energy decay time \(\tau_U=1/\kappa\) or the spectral width can test the other without having to calibrate the absolute detector gain.

At \(f_0=2.0\) GHz and \(\tau_U=0.50\ \mu\mathrm s\), we obtain \(\kappa=2.0\times10^6\) s⁻¹, \(\Delta f\simeq318.3\) kHz and \(Q\simeq6283\). A field receiver would report an amplitude time constant of 1.0 μs. Substituting that time directly into \(Q=2\pi f_0\tau_U\) would double the apparent quality.

Derive the two-point fit with a detector offset

Let a power detector report \(S(t)=A e^{-\kappa t}+B\), with a constant background \(B>0\). In the absence of background two samples separated by \(\Delta t\) give

\[\widehat\kappa=\log[S(t)/S(t+\Delta t)]/\Delta t.\]

With background, the logarithmic derivative is

\[-\frac{d}{dt}\log S=\kappa\frac{Ae^{-\kappa t}}{Ae^{-\kappa t}+B}.\]

It starts below the true rate and tends to zero in the tail. A straight line fitted to late log data can therefore look impressively precise while overestimating the lifetime. Increasing the acquisition duration can worsen this bias if the entire record is forced into a straight-line model.

A parametric fit may extract a sub-bin frequency or decay rate by using its model assumptions; its uncertainty then depends on signal-to-noise ratio and on whether the one-mode theory is adequate.

Check the single-mode model before refining its fit

If two nearby modes reach the same detector, their fields add before squaring. The result contains an interference term oscillating at their frequency difference on top of a decaying envelope. A scalar positive background cannot explain that alternating residual. Test by narrowing the excitation band, moving the pickup to a symmetry node of one mode, or examining a complex receiver's quadratures. A model with two exponentials has more degrees of freedom and can fit noise as well as signal; require consistency across independent records.

An honest linewidth report specifies the measured quantity, acquisition window, background treatment, coupling state, reference frequency and uncertainty. A fit to stored energy measures total loss. Inferring internal absorption alone additionally requires a known port coupling or a measurement that changes it separately, as in the course's cavity project.

Numerical field laboratory · 10. A magnetic orbit that spirals outward in a perfect vacuum

Track a charged particle in a prescribed uniform field, derive a rotation update and separate numerical energy growth from a real magnetic force.

Use the work law before solving the trajectory

A nonrelativistic charged particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) moves perpendicular to a uniform, time-independent magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\). There is no electric field and the dilute test particles do not affect the applied field. The Lorentz force gives \(\dot v_x=\omega v_y,\ \dot v_y=-\omega v_x\) for \(\omega=qB/m\). Dotting with \(\mathbf v\) gives zero, so the speed should stay constant. This property holds for either sign of the charge; the direction of rotation changes. It is a better initial test of a trajectory solver than how circular a short segment looks on screen.

Define \(J(v_x,v_y)=(v_y,-v_x)\), so \(J^T=-J\) and \(J^2=-I\). The exact velocity over a time step \(h\) is \([\cos(\omega h)I+\sin(\omega h)J]\mathbf v\). Integration in time gives a circular orbit of radius \(v_\perp/|\omega|\) once the guiding centre is chosen. A magnetic field alone bends a trajectory without changing its kinetic energy; a slowly growing orbit radius at fixed \(B\) and fixed initial speed demands an explanation.

Why forward Euler adds energy at every step

Euler gives \(\mathbf v_+=(I+dJ)\mathbf v\) with \(d=\omega h\). Squaring the norm eliminates the cross term because \(v^TJv=0\), leaving \(\|\mathbf v_+\|^2=(1+d^2)\|\mathbf v\|^2\). No nonzero step has exact unit gain. A geometric picture makes the bias tangible: a tangent step leaves the initial velocity circle on its outside, whatever the point of departure.

After \(N\) steps the speed factor is \((1+d^2)^{N/2}\). With 24 steps per physical orbit, \(d=2\pi/24\simeq0.2618\), so a full orbit multiplies speed by roughly 2.2. At shorter times or smaller steps this can resemble weak physical acceleration. Reducing \(h\) at fixed physical duration reduces the growth. Testing one step at fixed count, rather than comparing over a fixed physical interval, underestimates the cumulative error.

Solve a centred magnetic step exactly

Use the time-centred velocity in the Lorentz force:

\[\mathbf v_+-\mathbf v=d J(\mathbf v_++\mathbf v)/2.\]

Premultiply by the transpose of \(\mathbf v_++\mathbf v\). The left side is the difference of squared speeds and the right side vanishes, so the update preserves speed for every \(d\) in exact arithmetic. Rearranging yields a two-dimensional linear solve; using \(J^2=-I\) turns it into a rotation,

\[\mathbf v_+=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\mathbf v+\frac{2t}{1+t^2}J\mathbf v,\]
\[t=d/2.\]

These coefficients are \(\cos\alpha\) and \(\sin\alpha\) for \(\alpha=2\arctan(d/2)\). This is the magnetic rotation used in the nonrelativistic Boris integrator. For simultaneous electric fields, the usual split step adds two half electric impulses around the magnetic rotation. The current experiment isolates the magnetic part; it makes no claim about a nonuniform electric field or the accuracy of interpolation from a field mesh to particles.

A phase error can be an important measurement error even when the speed is exact. To use the trajectory as a reference, refine the step until the crossing time changes by less than the experimental timing resolution. If the required integration spans many revolutions, a uniform field permits exact substeps. In realistic fields one can retain a structure-preserving rotation while resolving spatial variation independently.

A separate diagnostic is time reversal. Starting from the final velocity and applying the rotation with \(-d\) exactly inverts a centred step, up to rounding. Forward Euler with \(-d\) after \(+d\) gives

\[(I-dJ)(I+dJ)=(1+d^2)I.\]

A forward/backward test detects the lost reversibility without knowing a single experimental constant.

Numerical field laboratory · 11. When differentiating a measured dipole invents radiated power

Infer dipole radiation from a sampled displacement and show quantitatively why position noise can dominate a seemingly well-resolved power estimate.

From trajectory to a physically meaningful quantity

An electrically small source carries an oscillating electric dipole moment \(p(t)=p_0\cos\omega t\) in a fixed direction. In nonrelativistic electric dipole radiation, the total instantaneous far-zone power associated with retarded time is

\[P=|\ddot p|^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0c^3).\]

The average over a single sinusoidal cycle is \(p_0^2\omega^4/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)\). The fourth power of frequency expresses a physical sensitivity, but a numerical second derivative can introduce a separate sensitivity to high-frequency measurement errors.

A sensor supplies moment or displacement samples at times \(t_j=jh\). If the source is a known rigid displacement \(x(t)\) of charge \(q\), then \(p=qx\). The constant conversion from moment to displacement cancels when comparing a numerical power with a reference power. We therefore scale amplitude by \(p_0\) and time by \(1/\omega\). The true acceleration trace is \(-\cos t\). Comparing it with the estimated acceleration before squaring reveals signs and phases that a power curve conceals.

Derive the frequency response of the differentiation formula

Use \(D_2p_j=(p_{j-1}-2p_j+p_{j+1})/h^2\). On a complex exponential \(e^{i\nu jh}\) the symbol is \(-4\sin^2(\nu h/2)/h^2\). Dividing its magnitude by the exact \(\nu^2\) gives a squared sinc factor. At a fixed physical frequency the error is quadratic in \(h\). For a signal oscillating in sign from one sample to the next, \(\nu h=\pi\), and the numerical second derivative multiplies its amplitude by \(4/h^2\). A numerical derivative smooths the very highest frequency relative to an exact high-frequency derivative yet still strongly amplifies the raw high-frequency component relative to a low-frequency physical acceleration.

Add a deliberately reproducible alternating error \(\epsilon(-1)^j\) to the position readings. Its acceleration error is \(-4\epsilon(-1)^j/h^2\). Squared and averaged over a complete even grid, the alternating error and the fundamental are orthogonal. Therefore the normalised inferred mean power becomes

\[\frac{\langle P_\mathrm{est}\rangle}{\langle P_\mathrm{true}\rangle}\]

=\left[\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}\right]^4+2\left(\frac{4\epsilon}{h^2}\right)^2 .

The first term tends to one from below as sampling is refined. At a fixed per-sample alternating displacement amplitude the second grows as \(h^{-4}\). The experiment holds a particular *sampling-related* error fixed; it does not claim that all real noise changes frequency when the sampling rate changes. A fixed-frequency physical disturbance obeys the symbol at its own fixed \(\nu\).

A numerical example in which refinement hurts

For 32 samples per period, \(h=2\pi/32\simeq0.19635\), and a positional disturbance two parts per thousand of full amplitude gives a spurious acceleration peak about 0.2075 of the true peak. Position data plotted at ordinary scale can look nearly perfect. The normalised mean-square acceleration is already about 1.080. At 64 samples per period with the same alternating error the spurious acceleration amplitude grows fourfold, and its contribution to mean power grows sixteenfold.

Recover a physical model without claiming information you did not measure

If an independent measurement shows the source contains only one sinusoidal frequency, fit its sine and cosine coefficients to the positions rather than differentiating every neighbour. On a full uniformly sampled cycle these coefficients are orthogonal to the alternating offset. Multiplying the fitted phasor by \(-\omega^2\) gives the model-based acceleration. This can be a much more precise estimate, but it assumes other harmonics are absent or irrelevant. A weak physical second harmonic has an acceleration amplification of four and power weighting of sixteen relative to equal moment amplitude. Validate the assumed spectrum before suppressing it.

For broad-band signals one may fit a smooth basis with an explicit high-frequency cutoff or use a model of detector bandwidth. Record the chosen cutoff, its physical justification and the uncertainty propagated into power. Repeating the computation with nearby cutoffs is a test of robustness, not a derivation of the correct filter. Where possible, compare the inferred radiated energy with a distant calibrated flux measurement over the same retarded-time interval. These two measurements have different systematic errors.

Numerical field laboratory · 12. Choose a frequency that identifies a coil's stored and dissipated energy

Design a small-signal experiment to estimate resistance and inductance, propagate amplitude and phase uncertainty and decide when a distributed field model is needed.

A measured impedance is one complex number

A laboratory sensing coil is driven by a small sinusoidal current at angular frequency \(\omega\). In its simplest usable range, a series model predicts \(Z=R+i\omega L\). The voltage across a port defines the sign of current entering the positive terminal. With peak phasors, complex power is

\[S=VI^*/2=|I|^2(R+i\omega L)/2.\]

Thus the real part measures average irreversible loss. Magnetic energy at peak current is \(L|I|^2/2\); reactive power equals \(\omega\) times this peak energy. These equalities supply two routes to the same parameters provided parasitic capacitance and frequency-dependent losses remain negligible.

At 100 Hz, a coil with \(R=20\ \Omega\) and \(L=40\) mH has reactance \(2\pi(100)(0.040)=25.13\ \Omega\), magnitude 32.12 Ω and phase about 51.49 degrees. A 10 mA peak current produces about 0.321 V peak and 1 mW average resistive loss. The peak magnetic energy is only \(2\,\mu\mathrm J\). Check that the reactive power obtained from the phase and current is about 1.26 mvar. Confusion between RMS and peak phasors easily introduces a factor two while still leaving the impedance ratio correct.

Derive what an amplitude error and a timing error do

Suppose the readout gives magnitude \(M\) and phase \(\phi\). Then \(R=M\cos\phi\), \(L=M\sin\phi/\omega\). For small independent uncertainties and known \(\omega\), differentiate both expressions. The relative first-order perturbations are

\[\frac{\delta R}{R}=\frac{\delta M}{M}-\tan\phi\,\delta\phi,\qquad\]

\frac{\delta L}{L}=\frac{\delta M}{M}+\cot\phi\,\delta\phi .

The phase uncertainty must be expressed in radians. For independent random errors their variances add with the squared coefficients; the signed equations above describe individual perturbations. Near zero phase even a very accurate amplitude tells little about inductance, while near 90 degrees inferring resistance requires exceptional phase accuracy. A near-45-degree measurement balances these two sensitivities when the uncertainty budget treats them similarly.

If the phase uncertainty is 0.5° and relative magnitude uncertainty is 1%, the 45-degree standard deviation in either recovered parameter is approximately \(\sqrt{0.01^2+0.00873^2}=0.0133\), or 1.33%. At five degrees the relative inductance contribution from phase alone is \(\cot5^\circ \times0.00873\simeq10\%\). Taking data at more digits does not improve phase calibration.

The lumped magnetic energy \(LI^2/2\) should match the field integral \(\int \mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H\,dV/2\) for a linear reversible system that includes the relevant surrounding field. Agreement of the two methods is stronger evidence than repeating a single instrument setup at the same frequency.

Numerical field laboratory · 13. Where should a numerical electrostatic domain end?

A finite mesh needs an outer boundary even when the physical field extends to infinity. Solve the artificial-boundary error exactly for a dipole and test whether enlarging the numerical domain or matching the exterior field is more effective.

Match a harmonic in an empty outer shell

At the surface of a source-containing sphere of radius \(a\) prescribe \(V(a,\theta)=V_a\cos\theta\). Outside this sphere there is no charge. Insert \(V=f(r)\cos\theta\) in Laplace's equation. The angular Laplacian supplies the eigenvalue \(-2\), so \((r^2 f')'-2f=0\). A power-law trial gives

\[f=Ar+B/r^2.\]

Decay at infinity removes the growing term and the open-space reference is \(f_\infty=V_a(a/r)^2\).

Terminate the calculation at \(R\) and ground the artificial wall. The two boundary equations instead give

\[f_R(r)=V_a\left(\frac ar\right)^2\frac{1-(r/R)^3}{1-(a/R)^3}.\]

A well-converged interior solve can reproduce this answer perfectly while retaining a systematic exterior-domain bias. An alternative boundary condition is \(f'(R)+2f(R)/R=0\). It admits the decaying dipole and annihilates the unwanted growing coefficient. A single local coefficient matches one spherical-harmonic order exactly.

Estimate the error before refining the mesh

At \(r=2a\) with \(R=5a\), the grounded boundary gives

\[f_R/f_\infty=(1-8/125)/(1-1/125)=117/124.\]

The amplitude error is about minus 5.65 percent. At a fixed observation radius, the leading bias decreases with the inverse cube of \(R\). Refining the interior grid at fixed \(R\) cannot remove it. Compare a second-order radial difference solution with both analytic curves to measure the two errors independently.

To see what the linear solver actually controls, put a uniform grid on \(r/a\). Approximate the outgoing flux \(r^2 f'\) at the right face by \(r_{i+1/2}^2(f_{i+1}-f_i)/h\) and form the same quantity at the left face. Subtract the fluxes, divide by \(h\) and equate to \(2f_i\). The resulting tridiagonal system has positive diagonal \(2+(r_{i+1/2}^2+r_{i-1/2}^2)/h^2\). Move the known inner and outer boundary terms to the right-hand side. For the grounded wall the outer contribution is zero; the inner value is the amplitude unit.

At \(r/a=2\) the open-domain amplitude is 0.2500. The grounded reference gives 0.2359 when \(R/a=5\). Repeating at \(R/a=10\) raises that prediction to 0.24825. Keep the physical grid spacing fixed when making this domain comparison. If the cell count stays fixed, increasing the radius also coarsens the mesh, and the two effects can mask each other. The first figure supplies both analytical references needed to separate them.
