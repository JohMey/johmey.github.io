What can a mesh measure about a point charge?

Distinguish conserved charge and measurable exterior fields from a numerical peak that diverges as the source becomes more localised.

Ask three detectors different questions

Imagine three instruments looking at a small charged body in vacuum. One measures the voltage on a spherical probe outside it, one measures the integrated electric flux through a closed surface, and one estimates the largest electric field somewhere near the source. If the body is idealised as a point, which readings have finite limits? The distinction matters when a calculation displays a dramatic change in maximum field after a perfectly reasonable mesh refinement.

For a positive point charge \(q\), the exterior potential \(V=kq/r\) tends to infinity as the observation point approaches the source. Its radial field magnitude \(kq/r^2\) also diverges. Gauss's law gives a different answer for the flux through any sphere of positive radius:

\[4\pi r^2E=q/\epsilon_0.\]

A finite integral and an unbounded maximum are compatible. Knowing what the instrument measures determines which numerical output should be expected to converge.

Use a smooth source with a known answer

Replace the potential by \(V_a=kq/\sqrt{r^2+a^2}\), with \(a>0\) a distance. This is a particular physical regularisation. Differentiating before sampling gives

\[E_a=kq r/(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}.\]

Divide the flux by \(q/\epsilon_0\). The putative enclosed-charge fraction becomes

\[F_a(r)=\frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}.\]

To recover the corresponding density, differentiate \(qF_a(r)\) with respect to radius and divide by shell area. The terms involving \(r^4\) cancel, leaving \(\rho_a(r)=3qa^2/[4\pi(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}]\). This density is smooth, nonnegative and integrates over all space to \(q\), since \(F_a(0)=0\) and \(F_a(\infty)=1\). It has an extended tail. The parameter \(a\) is a smoothing scale rather than an outer radius where charge abruptly stops.

At \(r=a\) only \(1/(2\sqrt2)\), about 35.36%, of the charge is enclosed. At \(r=2a\) the fraction is about 71.55%. A common intuition that two smoothing distances already contain essentially everything fails for this distribution. Measure the probe radius in units of the source size before blaming the flux calculation.

Hold the observable fixed while refining

The exterior-field ratio relative to the true point charge is \([1+(a/r)^2]^{-3/2}\). At fixed physical \(r\), expanding the bracket gives \(1-\tfrac32(a/r)^2+O((a/r)^4)\). Thus a probe far enough away sees a second-order regularisation error. Locating the maximum of \(E_a\), however, requires setting \(a^2-2r^2=0\), giving \(r=a/\sqrt2\) and a peak proportional to \(1/a^2\). Refine \(a\) and move the observer with the maximum: the reported peak grows even as a fixed exterior probe reading converges.

Open the live experiment

Keep the probe at 6 mm and reduce \(a\) from 3 to 1.5 mm. The flux rises from about 71.55% to 91.31% of the point-charge value. Now instead halve both the probe radius and \(a\). The measured fraction stays the same. The two procedures have distinct limiting observations; writing “halve the mesh” without recording the source representation and probe definition would make the experiment irreproducible.

Use continuity as a numerical diagnostic

Suppose a code deposits a prescribed charge into nearby cells and then solves for a nodal potential. Summing deposited cell charges tests normalisation of the source. Summing outward face fluxes over a larger box tests a separate constraint on the computed field. A correct finite-volume implementation cancels interior face pairs. Interpolating a nodal field to a smooth plotting surface with an unrelated quadrature need not conserve the same discrete quantity. A box-flux test using the solver's own numerical face fluxes and a smooth-surface test using the reconstructed field answer complementary questions.

A second check concerns energy. For a true point charge the field-energy integral near the origin scales as \(\int_0 r^2(r^{-2})^2dr\), which diverges. For a body of radius \(a\) this short-distance contribution typically scales like \(1/a\). Comparing two source regularisations through their total self-energy without specifying a physical charge radius is therefore an ill-defined validation exercise. Interaction energy with a smooth distant field, in contrast, can have a well-defined point-particle limit.

Tutorial

How far from this smooth charge should a flux probe be placed to enclose 99%?

Work through the solution

Solve \([1+(a/r)^2]^{-3/2}=0.99\). This gives

\[r/a=[0.99^{-2/3}-1]^{-1/2}\simeq12.2.\]

The answer depends on the chosen source shape. A uniform finite ball would have a different exterior behaviour.

A code uses \(a=2h\) and reports the field one cell away, \(r=h\), on every refined mesh. What do you expect for its flux fraction and pointwise field?

Work through the solution

The fixed ratio \(a/r=2\) gives \(F=5^{-3/2}\simeq0.0894\) at every resolution. The field still scales as \(r^{-2}\) times that factor and grows like \(h^{-2}\). The observer is moving towards the singular source.

If total deposited charge differs from \(q\) by 2% independently of mesh size, can adjusting \(a\) cure the exterior result?

Work through the solution

At large radius the flux follows the total charge that was actually deposited. Changing width cannot repair an incorrect integral. Fix the source normalisation and cell-volume weights before interpreting a convergence slope.
