Electromechanics, optics and measurement

Follow the field into an instrument. Predict which mechanical displacement, temperature change or optical signal can be observed and determine what can be learned from it.

    Contents

    1. When a suspended capacitor loses its stable equilibrium

      An electrode suspended on a spring can remain motionless at a bias that makes the calculated electrostatic force grow as it moves. Static force balance alone misses the voltage at which that equilibrium disappears. Deriving the small-signal frequency gives an independent measurement of the same instability.

    2. Which part of a sample changes a four-electrode impedance measurement?

      Driving one electrode pair and measuring another yields a transfer impedance whose spatial sensitivity can be positive, negative or nearly zero. A second field solution supplies the sensitivity without perturbing every element of a numerical mesh.

    3. Measuring the force on a sliding dielectric

      A suspended strip slides between two electrodes without touching them. Predict the pull, infer the air clearance from a capacitance trace and find where an electrical measurement can detect a change in mechanical stiffness.

    4. Designing a displacement bridge at its noise limit

      A moving electrode changes two capacitances in opposite directions. Derive the displacement signal, reconstruct its noise in metres and select a carrier voltage consistent with measured mechanical stiffness and electrical losses.

    5. A moving-coil galvanometer as an electrical and mechanical system

      A scale division records an angle, yet the quantity marked on the dial is electric current. A spring, a magnetic field and the resistance of the external measuring circuit connect the two. Derive the calibration, predict how a pointer approaches its reading, and find which experiment measures the charge of a short pulse.

    6. Eddy-current braking, magnetic penetration and the heat left behind

      A conducting loop moves above a row of magnetic poles. The drag first increases with speed, but a rapid crossing need not generate proportionally greater braking. Trace the work done against the Lorentz force into circuit energy, heat and a measurable temperature change. Then decide which features of a solid plate the single-loop model can capture.

    7. Measuring magnetic core loss by a hysteresis loop and by calorimetry

      A closed loop on a graph of magnetic induction against field encloses an area with units of energy per unit volume. A thermometer measures kelvins. Bring these two observations onto the same scale, account for the winding heat, and determine when the two experiments actually test the same material loss.

    8. A diamagnetic specimen in a gravitational and magnetic trap

      A suspended specimen can have exactly the right vertical magnetic force and still slide sideways out of its apparent equilibrium. Derive the force on induced magnetisation, impose gravity, and use the curvature of a measured field to decide whether small disturbances return the specimen to its starting point.

    9. Optical trapping and Brownian calibration of a dielectric bead

      An image of a bead remaining near a focus does not establish the strength of its trap. Derive the force pulling a small dielectric sphere into a bright region, compare it with the forward force from scattering and learn what a motion record can tell you about stiffness, drag and temperature. The model here uses an inert dielectric sphere in a homogeneous Newtonian liquid.

    10. Coupled optical and mechanical resonances

      A vibrating mirror can imprint its position on an optical field, while the stored light pushes back on the mirror. An apparent pair of peaks can indicate two hybrid modes, interference at a readout port or two unrelated resonances. Starting from energy in a moving cavity, derive the coupled response and learn which measurements establish a coupling strength and its uncertainty.

    11. Thermal radiance, étendue and detector calibration

      A square millimetre of a heated object emits thermal radiation into an entire hemisphere. A detector sees only the rays accepted by its optics and only wavelengths to which it responds. Derive the equilibrium spectrum, follow it through an optical collection system and infer what temperature or emissivity a calibrated reading can actually determine.

    12. Moving-mirror Doppler metrology

      A moving mirror changes optical phase, shifts the reflected frequency and exchanges energy and momentum with the light. In many displacement measurements the classical phase rule is extraordinarily accurate. Derive its exact inertial-frame counterpart, identify which time a detector samples and find the practical uncertainties that usually matter well before a relativistic correction does.

    13. Weighing a small deposit with a piezoelectric resonator

      Two nearby frequencies appear in the electrical response of one mechanical mode. Derive the series and parallel resonances, then use their different sensitivities to recognise added mass and stray capacitance.
