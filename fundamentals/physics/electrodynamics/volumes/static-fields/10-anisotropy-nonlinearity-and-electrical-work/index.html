Anisotropic and nonlinear dielectrics from local measurements to global fields

A single dielectric constant cannot describe a crystal whose displacement tilts away from its electric field, or a reversible material whose incremental response changes with bias. Starting with the constitutive measurement clarifies which parts of ordinary electrostatics survive.

Measure a tensor rather than assuming collinearity

In a homogeneous linear anisotropic material,

\[\mathbf D=\boldsymbol\epsilon\mathbf E.\]

The entry \(\epsilon_{ij}\) measures displacement component \(i\) in response to electric-field component \(j\). A reversible reciprocal dielectric has a symmetric positive-definite tensor. Its principal axes are orthogonal and its positive eigenvalues are the principal permittivities. Field-energy density \(u=\mathbf E\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon\mathbf E/2\) is then positive for a nonzero field. Positive definiteness concerns all directions; having three positive diagonal entries in one basis alone is insufficient.

Choose principal relative permittivities \(2,4,6\) along \(x,y,z\). With a field \((1000,0,1000)\) V/m the displacement is \(\epsilon_0(2000,0,6000)\). The field makes \(45^\circ\) to \(z\), the displacement only \(18.4^\circ\); their directions differ. Energy density is \(3.54\times10^{-5}\) J/m³. A flat free-charge sensor normal to \(z\) responds to \(D_z\); an electrostatic force probe constrained along \(x\) measures \(E_x\). Their readings cannot be interpreted as two component measurements of one vector differing by a single scalar.

Derive the field of a point charge in an anisotropic bulk

For a constant symmetric tensor write \(\boldsymbol\epsilon=\epsilon_0 K\), with positive dimensionless matrix \(K\). Poisson's equation is

\[-\epsilon_0\nabla_{\mathbf x}\cdot K\nabla_{\mathbf x}V=q\delta^3(\mathbf x).\]

Change coordinates to \(\mathbf y=K^{-1/2}\mathbf x\). The gradient transforms by the same symmetric matrix and the operator becomes the ordinary isotropic Laplacian in \(\mathbf y\). The source transforms too: physical volume is \(d^3x=\sqrt{\det K}\,d^3y\), so

\[\delta^3(\mathbf x)=\delta^3(\mathbf y)/\sqrt{\det K}.\]

The decaying solution is

\[V(\mathbf x)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{\det K}}\frac1{\sqrt{\mathbf x^TK^{-1}\mathbf x}},\]
\[\mathbf E=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{\det K}}\frac{K^{-1}\mathbf x}{(\mathbf x^TK^{-1}\mathbf x)^{3/2}} .\]

A check is the scalar case \(K=kI\). The determinant contributes \(k^{3/2}\) and the distance contributes \(1/\sqrt{k}\), returning \(q/(4\pi\epsilon_0kr)\). The equipotentials in laboratory coordinates are ellipsoids, while \(\mathbf D=\epsilon_0K\mathbf E\) happens to point along \(\mathbf x\) for this infinite homogeneous point-source problem. Its magnitude varies with direction on a physical sphere. Gauss's law still gives total free flux \(q\); equating that flux to \(4\pi r^2\) times a constant magnitude would be invalid. Conducting or material boundaries also transform under this coordinate map and generally cease to be simple physical spheres.

Along any principal axis \(i\), at distance \(r\), the potential is proportional to \(\sqrt{K_i}/\sqrt{\det K}\). With principal values \(2,4,6\), two probes equally far away on the \(z,x\) axes measure a voltage ratio \(\sqrt{6/2}=\sqrt3\). One might have expected the largest permittivity to reduce the voltage most strongly along that axis. The observed result reflects how the full anisotropic medium distributes the displacement of a point source; it cannot be derived by inserting a direction-specific permittivity into the isotropic radial formula.

Which permittivity does a slab capacitance measure?

Place broad metal plates normal to a unit vector \(\mathbf n\) and neglect edge effects. Constant electrode potentials force a purely normal electric field \(V\mathbf n/d\), independent of the off-diagonal dielectric entries. The displacement generally has a tangential component, but the metal's free-charge reading is \(D_n\). Thus the measured normal capacitance per area is

\[C/A=(\mathbf n^T\boldsymbol\epsilon\mathbf n)/d.\]

For axes with relative values 2 and 6, cut the normal at \(45^\circ\) between them. The relative plate permittivity is 4.

A different preparation constrains displacement to be purely normal, for instance in an effectively uniform bulk state with known flux vector. Then \(\mathbf E=\boldsymbol\epsilon^{-1}D_n\mathbf n\), so

\[D_n/E_n=1/(\mathbf n^T\boldsymbol\epsilon^{-1}\mathbf n).\]

For the same cut the result is \(1/[(1/2+1/6)/2]=3\) in relative units. Both are correct for their own field constraints. Flat equipotential plates do not generally enforce tangential displacement equal to zero. A boundary-value calculation using that extra condition could report the wrong dielectric constant from an otherwise perfect experiment.

With an interface between two tensors, integrate Faraday's law around a small static rectangular loop to enforce continuous tangential \(E\) and Gauss's law over a pillbox to enforce the specified normal \(D\) jump. Write the first material's measured tangential field into the second's constitutive law and solve for its normal electric field from \(D_n\). No scalar Snell-like rule involving only a ratio of two relative permittivities can represent arbitrary crystal orientations. Some field components may vanish by symmetry in one experiment and reappear after rotating the sample.

Reversible response distinguishes stored energy and coenergy

For a slowly and reversibly assembled homogeneous dielectric, increment of stored electrical energy per volume is \(du=\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf D\). A complementary density \(w(\mathbf E)=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D-u\) has \(dw=\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf E\). In a scalar nonlinear law \(D(E)=\epsilon E+\alpha E^3\) with \(\epsilon,\alpha>0\), integration gives

\[w(E)=\tfrac12\epsilon E^2+\tfrac14\alpha E^4,\]
\[u(E)=E D(E)-w(E)=\tfrac12\epsilon E^2+\tfrac34\alpha E^4 .\]

The familiar \(ED/2\) holds only in the linear case. For a small perturbation superposed on bias \(E_b\), \(dD/dE=\epsilon+3\alpha E_b^2\) is the differential permittivity. A dc charge-to-voltage ratio measures the secant value \(\epsilon+\alpha E_b^2\). At \(\alpha E_b^2=0.20\epsilon\) these are \(1.60\epsilon\) and \(1.20\epsilon\), a substantial difference even though the constitutive curve remains smooth.

The field equation becomes \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D(-\nabla V)=\rho_f\) and superposition generally fails. For a differentiable energy-based vector law, \(\partial D_i/\partial E_j=\partial^2w/\partial E_i\partial E_j\) is symmetric. A positive-definite differential tensor gives local stability and helps preserve ellipticity of the electrostatic boundary problem. A hysteretic material can depend on its prior history and need extra state variables; an area in a closed constitutive cycle then measures dissipated work, not reversible recoverable energy.

A nonlinear field calculation that needs only a scalar root

Place a sphere with charge \(q\) in an infinite isotropic nonlinear medium of the preceding type. Spherical symmetry and Gauss's law still determine \(D_r=q/(4\pi r^2)\) outside. Invert \(\epsilon E_r+\alpha E_r^3=D_r\) point by point. For \(\alpha>0\) the derivative is positive everywhere, ensuring one physical real root for each \(D_r\). A bisection bracket for \(q>0\) is \(0\le E_r\le D_r/\epsilon\); evaluating the residual at the upper endpoint gives positive cubic surplus. Compute voltage relative to infinity by integrating this field, rather than replacing \(D/E\) by an arbitrary constant taken at the surface.

For weak nonlinearity set \(E=D/\epsilon+\delta\) and keep the leading cubic correction. One obtains

\[E\simeq D/\epsilon-\alpha D^3/\epsilon^4.\]

At large radii a \(1/r^2\) displacement therefore acquires a negative \(1/r^6\) correction in the electric field, giving a \(1/r^5\) potential correction. A probe can misinterpret that extra radial scale as a change of source shape if material nonlinearity is not independently controlled. Double the source charge and compare the field after division by \(q\). In a linear medium those curves coincide; the perturbative deviation here scales as \(q^2\).

Resolve an oblique crystal interface component by component

A lateral detail or an oblique electrode can produce a field with both normal and tangential components at an interface. Consider a locally planar interface with tangent \(\hat{\mathbf x}\) and normal \(\hat{\mathbf z}\) from isotropic medium 1 into a crystal. In the \(xz\) plane let the crystal's relative tensor be \(\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\), with entries in the order \(x,z\). Medium 1 has relative permittivity 2 and measured field \(E_{1x}=120\) V/m, \(E_{1z}=240\) V/m. There is no free sheet at the interface. Neither input field is assumed normal to the boundary.

From a very short rectangular loop crossing the interface, the tangential electrostatic field is continuous, so \(E_{2x}=120\) V/m. The normal displacement on side 1 is \(D_{1z}/\epsilon_0=2(240)=480\) V/m. Equating it to \(E_{2x}+3E_{2z}\) yields \(E_{2z}=120\) V/m. The tangential displacement can jump. In the crystal it is \(D_{2x}/\epsilon_0=4(120)+120=600\) V/m, compared with \(D_{1x}/\epsilon_0=240\) V/m. Neither tangent \(D\) nor normal \(E\) is required to be continuous. A field solver that enforces continuity of all components can silently erase the very anisotropy the experiment was intended to measure.

Find the bound surface charge using two routes. The true charge jump is \(\epsilon_0(E_{2z}-E_{1z})=-120\epsilon_0=-1.06\) nC/m². With no free sheet this must be bound. Directly, \(P_{1z}/\epsilon_0=D_{1z}/\epsilon_0-E_{1z}=240\) V/m and \(P_{2z}/\epsilon_0=480-120=360\) V/m. The net bound charge from the two facing polarisation surfaces is \(P_{1z}-P_{2z}=-120\epsilon_0\), giving the same result. At a rotated interface it can be safer to perform these component checks than to infer charge from one visual change in field-line angle.

A controlled reversal can expose the off-diagonal tensor entry. Keep \(E_{1z}\) fixed and reverse \(E_{1x}\) using independent side electrodes. Then \(E_{2x}=-120\) V/m and \(E_{2z}=(480+120)/3=200\) V/m. The normal response therefore changes by 80 V/m even though the imposed normal displacement is unchanged. A scalar model, or a crystal whose tensor had zero \(xz\) entry in this coordinate system, would predict no such change. In practice the side electrodes must be far enough from the chosen interface patch for their intended approximately uniform fields to be realised locally.

Derive pressure for a reversible nonlinear capacitor under both electrical controls

Take a parallel-plate gap \(d\), area \(A\), completely filled by a reversible material with specified constitutive law \(D(E)\). For this idealised calculation assume that changing \(d\) changes only the volume occupied by otherwise identical material at fixed thermodynamic properties. Exclude any additional mechanical elastic stress, electrostriction or chemical work needed to preserve that preparation. Keep the field homogeneous and neglect fringing. Under charge control the displacement \(D=Q/A\) is fixed, so the stored electric energy is \(U=Ad\,u(D)\) with \(u=\int_0^D E(D')\,dD'\). The force tending to increase the gap is

\[F=-\partial_d U|_Q=-A u(D).\]

Thus the attractive field contribution has magnitude \(A u\).

Now hold voltage fixed. The electric field is \(E=V/d\); decreasing the gap also changes the charge the source must supply. Legendre transforming the energy by subtracting \(VQ\) gives the appropriate potential \(\mathcal G=U-VQ=-Ad\,w(E)\). Its derivative at fixed \(V\) is \(-A w(E)+AE\,dw/dE=-A w+AED=A u\). Hence \(F=-\partial_d\mathcal G|_V=-A u\), agreeing for the same instantaneous state. Differentiating \(U\) at fixed voltage without the source term would produce a different answer. The calculation identifies the source work excluded by that incorrect derivative.

For \(D=\epsilon E+\alpha E^3\) with \(\alpha E_b^2=0.20\epsilon\),

\[u(E_b)=0.65\epsilon E_b^2.\]

The often-used linear formula \(E_bD_b/2\) would give \(0.60\epsilon E_b^2\), underestimating the stated ideal field contribution. With \(\epsilon=4\epsilon_0,\ E_b=1.0\times10^5\) V/m, the predicted magnitude per area is \(0.230\) Pa. This number follows from a particular reversible constitutive model and mechanical variation. A real force between electrodes compressing a solid sample also contains material mechanical stresses, and the permittivity itself can change with density. The correct experiment must specify which matter moves when the gap changes.

An independent numerical test computes \(U(d\pm\delta d,Q)\) at fixed charge or \(\mathcal G(d\pm\delta d,V)\) at fixed voltage, forms centred derivatives and chooses the charges or voltages that describe the same midpoint state. If the two forces converge to opposite signs, check the mechanical displacement convention and the source-work term. Agreement of the pressures at a single bias does not prove the constitutive approximation is valid at other fields. A separate differential capacitance scan can verify the slope required by the energy model across the displacement range.

Solve a layered nonlinear capacitor by displacement continuation

A useful intermediate problem between a uniform nonlinear slab and a full finite-element solve is a pair of different nonlinear layers stacked normal to large plates. With no free interface charge, the same normal displacement \(D\) passes through both. Each has a reversible monotone law \(D_i(E_i)=\epsilon_iE_i+\alpha_iE_i^3\), \(\epsilon_i>0,\alpha_i\ge0\). At fixed terminal charge \(D=Q/A\) is known. Invert each material separately and add the voltages,

\[V(D)=d_1E_1(D)+d_2E_2(D).\]

At fixed voltage solve this last scalar equation for \(D\). Its derivative is positive:

\[\frac{dV}{dD}=\frac{d_1}{\epsilon_1+3\alpha_1E_1^2}+\frac{d_2}{\epsilon_2+3\alpha_2E_2^2},\]
\[\frac{C_{\rm diff}}A=\left(\frac{dV}{dD}\right)^{-1}.\]

Thus it is the differential permittivities, evaluated at the two actual and generally different local fields, that combine as a series small-signal capacitance. Using a differential response measured at the overall average field can give an inconsistent result even when the series formula itself is correct. The secant ratio \(D/V\) has another interpretation and should be compared only with a dc charge reading.

Choose a field scale \(E_*=1000\) V/m and \(D_*=\epsilon_0E_*\). In the first material let \(D/D_*=2e_1+e_1^3\), \(e_1=E_1/E_*\), while the second is linear \(D/D_*=6e_2\). Give both layers thickness 1 mm. At \(D=3D_*\), the first equation is solved by \(e_1=1\); the second gives \(e_2=0.50\). The total voltage is 1.50 V, and \(D=26.56\) nC/m². The dc capacitance per area is 17.71 nF/m². At this same operating point the differential relative permittivities are 5 and 6, giving \(C_{\rm diff}/A=\epsilon_0/[0.001/5+0.001/6]=24.15\) nF/m². A small voltage ripple measures a capacitance about 36% larger than the secant value.

Check the bias state from energy without using terminal current. The stored energy per volume in layer 1 is \(\epsilon_0E_*^2[e_1^2+3e_1^4/4]\) and in layer 2 is \(3\epsilon_0E_*^2e_2^2\). Multiplying by the respective thicknesses at the chosen state gives total energy per area \(0.00250\epsilon_0E_*^2=22.14\) nJ/m². Integrating the voltage as a function of displacement from zero to \(3D_*\) must give the same result, since \(d(U/A)=V\,dD\). The shortcut \(VD/2\) would instead give 19.92 nJ/m². It lies between neither two guaranteed bounds nor two voltage controls; it is simply unjustified for this nonlinear path.

For voltage-controlled computation use a continuation in applied voltage. Start at low bias with the known linear two-layer solution and reuse its displacement as a first guess for the next monotone scalar solve. A bracketing method with increasing positive endpoints always finds the root for positive applied voltage in these specific laws. Once converged, evaluate both layers' displacement residuals, their voltage sum and the independent energy integral. A lower iteration error alone cannot correct a model that accidentally multiplied the cubic coefficient by field rather than field cubed, or used a common \(E\) across both materials. Direct field checks at a selected bias make the numerical experiment physically interpretable.

Recover dielectric alignment torque from electrode work

Will positive or negative rotation increase capacitance?

A slab completely filling an ideal parallel-plate cell can rotate its uniaxial permittivity tensor reversibly without changing plate geometry. The angle \(\theta\) between plate normal and the larger principal-permittivity axis is adjustable. Derive the torque on this ideal material coordinate, compare voltage and charge control, and calculate the maximum for a stated geometry.

Derive and check the result

With area \(A\), separation \(d\), principal absolute permittivities \(\epsilon_\parallel>\epsilon_\perp\), the normal capacitance is \(C(\theta)=(A/d)(\epsilon_\parallel\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_\perp\sin^2\theta)\). For fixed voltage electrical coenergy is \(C V^2/2\). The torque doing work in the direction of increasing \(\theta\) is its derivative,

\[T=(A/d)(\epsilon_\perp-\epsilon_\parallel)V^2\sin(2\theta)/2.\]

For \(0<\theta<\pi/2\) it drives the larger-permittivity principal axis towards the plate normal, increasing capacitance. The configurations at 0° and 90° both have zero torque but only the former minimises the fixed-voltage mechanical potential \(-CV^2/2\).

Under fixed charge the stored energy is \(Q^2/(2C)\), so the torque is \(-\partial_\theta U=Q^2C'/(2C^2)\). At the same instantaneous voltage \(Q/C\) it agrees with the voltage-controlled result. Following two entire rotation curves with different preparations generally yields different magnitudes because the isolated cell voltage varies with angle. This equality at the same state is an independent sign check and distinguishes the field torque from the external holding torque of opposite sign.

Take \(A=1.0\) cm², \(d=1.0\) mm, principal relative permittivities 6 and 2, and \(V=20\) V. At \(45^\circ\), the magnitude is \(0.5(A/d)4\epsilon_0 V^2=7.08\times10^{-10}\) N m. A small-angle torque measurement close to the aligned state would also reveal a positive angular restoring stiffness. In a real sample the crystal lattice, liquid-crystal elasticity or mount supplies additional energy and boundary conditions for rotation. The electrostatic contribution just calculated can be isolated by varying \(V^2\) and checking the \(\sin2\theta\) angular dependence at sufficiently slow drive.

Tutorial · diagnose a tensor and a bias dependence

Recover a rotated tensor from three plate measurements

In a material plane, cuts with normals \(x,y,(x+y)/\sqrt2\) yield relative plate permittivities \(3,5,5\), respectively. Reconstruct the \(2\times2\) tensor, check positivity and predict the result for the other bisector.

Work through the solution

The first two values are

\[K_{xx}=3,K_{yy}=5.\]

The third is \((3+5)/2+K_{xy}=5\), so \(K_{xy}=1\). The symmetric matrix has determinant 14 and positive first diagonal entry, hence is positive definite. Its principal values are \(4\pm\sqrt2\). The other bisector gives \(4-1=3\). This extra reading can test the assumed orientation and symmetry independently. All four inferences assume the plate experiment imposes electric field normal to each cut and fringing is negligible.

Distinguish charge measurements before integrating electrical work

At a bias \(E_b\), a sample following the cubic constitutive law gives secant permittivity \(1.30\epsilon_*\) and differential permittivity \(1.70\epsilon_*\), where \(\epsilon_*\) is a calibration permittivity. Infer its linear coefficient, cubic contribution at bias and true stored energy density.

Work through the solution

Subtract the two measurements. The difference is \(2\alpha E_b^2=0.40\epsilon_*\), so \(\alpha E_b^2=0.20\epsilon_*\),

\[\epsilon=1.10\epsilon_*.\]

The energy at bias is

\[[0.55+0.15]\epsilon_* E_b^2=0.70\epsilon_*E_b^2.\]

Using \(ED/2\) would give \(0.65\epsilon_*E_b^2\). To test the law rather than just fit two parameters, obtain a second set of secant and differential readings at a different bias and check the predicted quadratic change with bias field.
