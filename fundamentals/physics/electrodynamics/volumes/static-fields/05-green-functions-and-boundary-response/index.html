Green functions, the Poisson kernel and what a small electrode can influence

A Green function is the response of an entire boundary-value problem to a unit source. Once that response is known, a continuous charge distribution and a patterned boundary voltage can both be treated by integration. The two integrations require different kernels.

Define a point response with its boundary conditions

Let \(\Omega\) be a vacuum domain with boundary on which the voltage is given. Use the convention \(-\nabla_{\mathbf y}^2G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\delta^3(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\) and \(G=0\) on the boundary as a function of \(\mathbf y\). In free space \(G=1/(4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|)\) if decay is required at infinity. This definition places no permittivity in \(G\); physical charge enters as \(\rho/\epsilon_0\). A different frequent convention puts \(4\pi\) in the defining equation, changing all subsequent prefactors. Comparing the near-source flux to unity is a straightforward way to fix which convention is being used.

Begin with two differentiable functions \(u,v\). The difference \(\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v-v\nabla u)=u\nabla^2v-v\nabla^2u\) has no mixed-gradient term. Integrate this identity with \(u=V(\mathbf y)\),

\[v=G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y).\]

The source equation for \(G\) samples \(V\) at \(\mathbf x\), while Poisson's equation for \(V\) supplies a volume density. With the outward normal taken from \(\Omega\),

\[V(\mathbf x)=\int_\Omega G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\frac{\rho(\mathbf y)}{\epsilon_0}\,d^3y-\oint_{\partial\Omega}V(\mathbf y)\partial_{n_{\mathbf y}}G(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,dA_{\mathbf y}.\]

The \(G\partial_n V\) boundary term vanishes because \(G=0\) there. If the singularity causes concern, exclude a small sphere about \(\mathbf x\), apply the ordinary identity, and shrink the sphere. The inward normal on the excised sphere produces the same \(V(\mathbf x)\) term. The representation separates charges in the volume from voltages maintained by electrode sources; it does not combine them into one fictitious material distribution.

Solve the half-space and differentiate with respect to source position

Consider \(\mathbf x=(\mathbf X,h)\) with \(h>0\) above the plane \(z=0\). For an interior source \(\mathbf y=(\mathbf Y,z)\), denote its reflection by \(\mathbf y^*=(\mathbf Y,-z)\). The grounded half-space Green function is

\[G=\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{|\mathbf X-\mathbf Y|^2+(h-z)^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{|\mathbf X-\mathbf Y|^2+(h+z)^2}}\right].\]

It vanishes as the source coordinate approaches the plane. The outward domain normal there is \(-\hat{\mathbf z}\); therefore the coefficient of a prescribed boundary voltage is

\[P_h=-\partial_nG=\partial_zG|_{z=0}.\]

Differentiating the two terms gives equal positive contributions:

\[P_h(\mathbf X-\mathbf Y)=\frac{h}{2\pi[|\mathbf X-\mathbf Y|^2+h^2]^{3/2}} .\]

Its units are inverse area, while \(G\) has inverse length in this three-dimensional convention. For no interior charge \(V(\mathbf X,h)=\int P_h(\mathbf X-\mathbf Y)f(\mathbf Y)\,d^2Y\). The kernel is positive. Integrate over the whole plane using \(2\pi s\,ds\); the result is one. Hence a constant boundary voltage is reproduced, and an arbitrary voltage bounded between two numbers produces values between the same bounds inside. The integral solves the bounded half-space problem with the far-field condition appropriate to the data. Demanding decay for a nonzero constant boundary value would be inconsistent.

A detector at height \(h\) takes a weighted average over a neighbourhood of width comparable with \(h\). The kernel has algebraic tails, so its support is not a sharp circular footprint. The contribution from radii smaller than \(b\) is \(1-h/\sqrt{b^2+h^2}\). Half the total weight lies within \(b=\sqrt3h\). Ninety per cent requires

\[b=\sqrt{99}h\simeq9.95h.\]

These quite different radii show why a full-width-at-half-maximum measurement of the kernel cannot by itself define the region that contains most of its influence.

A finite driven electrode and a reciprocity experiment

Fix a circular disk of radius \(b\) in the plane at voltage \(V_d\) while grounding the rest of the plane, with ideal thin insulating seams. Directly over its centre,

\[V(0,h)=V_d\left(1-\frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2+b^2}}\right),\]
\[E_z(0,h)=V_d\frac{b^2}{(h^2+b^2)^{3/2}} .\]

At \(h\to0^+\) the electrode voltage is recovered at its centre. For \(h\gg b\) the voltage scales as \(V_d b^2/(2h^2)\). A small electrode surrounded by ground does not behave at long distance as an isolated charged electrode with a \(1/h\) potential. Its surrounding return charge makes a combined disturbance whose far field has a different order. At the sharp ideal edge the boundary voltage jumps, and local fields there require an actual seam geometry if one wants a bounded peak or a breakdown prediction.

For \(b=5.0\) mm, \(V_d=4.0\) V and \(h=5.0\) mm the axial voltage is 1.17 V and field is \(283\) V/m. A separate experiment grounds every electrode and places a point charge \(q\) at that observation point. Green reciprocity relates the net charge on the disk to the previous weighting potential \(w=V/V_d\): \(Q_d=-q\,w\). With \(q=20\) pC the disk collects \(-5.86\) pC. Direct integration of the image-plane surface charge \(\sigma(s)=-qh/[2\pi(s^2+h^2)^{3/2}]\) gives the same result. It is precisely \(-qP_h\). Thus the boundary kernel has both a voltage-producing interpretation and a charge-collection interpretation measurable with different instrumentation.

Recover the kernel of an electrode strip

A boundary pattern invariant in \(y\) should not require a two-dimensional source integral. Integrate \(P_h\) over the unused coordinate. With lateral separation \(s=X-X'\), the result is

\[p_h(s)=h/[\pi(s^2+h^2)].\]

It integrates to one over the real line. A strip between \(-b,b\) at \(V_d\) gives on its central normal \(V(0,h)=2V_d\arctan(b/h)/\pi\). At \(h=b\) the value is half the applied voltage, distinct from the circular electrode's \(1-1/\sqrt2\). The strip extends in one more direction and occupies a larger angular range of boundary influence.

For sinusoidal boundary data \(f(X)=\cos kX\) there is an independent way to determine the result without evaluating a difficult oscillatory integral. Translational symmetry and linearity preserve the sinusoid. Solving Laplace's equation with vertical factor \(Z(h)\) gives

\[Z''=k^2Z.\]

The bounded decaying solution with unit boundary amplitude is \(e^{-|k|h}\). Thus the Fourier transform of the strip kernel is \(e^{-|k|h}\). Convolving twice with heights \(h_1,h_2\) is equivalent to convolution once with height \(h_1+h_2\). This composition property checks a numerical propagation code without requiring separate analytic solutions for many different boundary patterns.

Which symmetries does an influence table possess?

Exchange source and detector in the defining equations for \(G(\mathbf x,\cdot)\) and \(G(\mathbf z,\cdot)\). Their difference identity gives \(G(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)-G(\mathbf z,\mathbf x)=0\), since both vanish on the same boundary. Symmetry extends to scalar or symmetric-tensor positive permittivity when the operator is defined as \(-\nabla\cdot\epsilon\nabla\). It refers to exactly the same material, boundary preparation and domain in both experiments. Replacing one experiment's grounded conductor by a floating conductor changes the Green function.

On a nonuniform mesh it matters whether influence entries correspond to equal point charges or equal charge densities in unequal cells. If a finite-volume solver stores sources as densities, a column's total injected charge scales with its cell volume. An unweighted table of sampled voltages can look nonsymmetric even when the discrete physics is reciprocal. Form the matrix with unit total source and consistent detector averaging, or retain the correct volume-weighted inner product. Test positivity for positive sources as a second check. Symmetric influence entries by themselves cannot expose a common incorrect normalisation or a misplaced outer boundary.

A kernel that reproduces every harmonic around a circle

In a two-dimensional circular domain of radius \(a\), prescribe a voltage \(f(\phi')\) around the wall. For a detector at \((r,\phi)\) let \(t=r/a<1\). The angular Fourier component with order \(m\) is multiplied by \(t^{|m|}\) on continuation into the disk. This follows directly from the regular radial power solution of Laplace's equation. Substitute the Fourier coefficient integral into the series and interchange the integral and the absolutely convergent series at fixed \(t<1\). The boundary response per unit angle is then

\[P_t(\delta)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}t^{|m|}e^{im\delta}=\frac{1-t^2}{2\pi(1-2t\cos\delta+t^2)},\]
\[\delta=\phi-\phi' .\]

To obtain the rational form, separate the \(m=0\) term and sum the two conjugate geometric series \(te^{\pm i\delta}/(1-te^{\pm i\delta})\). The result is positive for every interior point. Integrating it over angle leaves only the constant Fourier mode, so it is normalised to unity. At the centre the kernel is simply \(1/(2\pi)\), recovering the exact mean-value theorem for harmonic functions. Near the wall it becomes very sharply concentrated around the detector's azimuth while retaining a small nonlocal tail. Using prescribed boundary data as a flat average at every interior point would reproduce only the centre reading.

For a concrete experiment put a short arc of half-angle \(\alpha\) centred on \(\phi=0\) at 1 V and ground the remaining circular boundary. Along \(\phi=0\) the potential equals the integral of \(P_t\) between \(-\alpha,\alpha\). With \(u=\tan(\phi'/2)\) one has \(\cos\phi'=(1-u^2)/(1+u^2)\), \(d\phi'=2du/(1+u^2)\). Substitution gives the weight

\[w(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\!\left[\frac{1+t}{1-t}\tan(\alpha/2)\right] .\]

At \(t=0\) it gives \(\alpha/\pi\), exactly the fraction of the circumference occupied by the arc. For a 60°-wide arc \(\alpha=\pi/6\), hence the centre reads \(1/6\) V. At \(t=0.6\) on the centreline it reads about 0.522 V. Placing a small positive line charge there while grounding the whole cylindrical wall induces \(-0.522\) times the line charge on that arc by the same reciprocity argument used on the flat disk. The angular share depends on position inside the circle even though every point on the conductor has the same actual grounded voltage.

An independent check takes the limit of a very large circle with the probe only a distance \(h=a-r\) from the nearest boundary point. For small angular displacement \(\delta=s/a\), \(1-2t\cos\delta+t^2\simeq(h/a)^2+(s/a)^2\) and \(1-t^2\simeq2h/a\). The angular probability element \(P_t(\delta)d\delta\) therefore tends to \(h\,ds/[\pi(h^2+s^2)]\), precisely the translation-invariant strip kernel obtained by integrating the half-space formula over one coordinate. Curvature corrections can be estimated by increasing \(a/h\) at fixed physical arc width and comparing both predictions.

Why an insulated Green function needs a zero-mode subtraction

Consider the simpler interval \(0<x<L\) with prescribed zero flux at both ends. We would like a point response satisfying \(-G''=\delta(x-s)\), \(G'(0)=G'(L)=0\). Integrate this proposed equation over the interval: the left-hand side is \(-G'(L)+G'(0)=0\), whereas the right-hand side is 1. The attempted kernel violates compatibility. Physically a sealed insulating region cannot take a net free test charge without changing its prescribed outer flux. Adding an arbitrary constant to \(G\) cannot fix this contradiction.

A useful alternative removes the constant source mode and fixes the remaining voltage reference. Define \(-G_N''=\delta(x-s)-1/L\), \(G_N'(0)=G_N'(L)=0\), and \(\int_0^L G_N(x,s)\,dx=0\). Away from the point the curvature is \(+1/L\). The left derivative must be \(x/L\) to vanish at zero, the right derivative \(x/L-1\) to vanish at \(L\). They jump by \(-1\) at \(x=s\). Integrating, imposing continuity and subtracting the mean gives the compact symmetric expression

\[G_N(x,s)=\frac{x^2+s^2}{2L}-\max(x,s)+\frac L3 .\]

Differentiating verifies both end conditions and the jump. Integrating the quadratic part and the piecewise maximum over \(x\) verifies the mean condition. Its sign can be negative because the response includes an opposite uniform return source and uses a zero-mean reference. Unlike the positive grounded Dirichlet kernel, it is not a positive voltage weight to use for a maximum-principle test.

Apply a neutral pair of sheets \(+\sigma\) at \(s_1=0.2L\) and \(-\sigma\) at \(s_2=0.7L\). Their voltage is \(\sigma[G_N(x,s_1)-G_N(x,s_2)]/\epsilon\). The two artificial uniform return sources cancel exactly. To the left of both sheets the field vanishes, between them it equals \(+\sigma/\epsilon\), and to the right it vanishes again. The potential drops by \(0.5L\sigma/\epsilon\) across the two sheets. This matches direct Gauss-law jumps, and the zero-mean potential fixes the constant that Gauss's law could not determine. For more complex closed domains numerical solvers implement the same idea by projecting out the constant eigenvector and checking the compatible total charge before inverting. An arbitrary tiny diagonal regularisation of a singular matrix can hide a physically impossible net-source input.

Construct a point response when the medium itself screens charge

A linear ionic or phenomenological screening model between two grounded parallel electrodes leads in one dimension to \([-\partial_x^2+\kappa^2]V=\rho_{\rm imposed}/\epsilon\), \(0<x<L\). Here \(\rho_{\rm imposed}\) excludes the mobile linear response already represented by \(\kappa^2V\). We seek \(G_\kappa(x,s)\) with zero voltage at both ends. Away from its unit source the equation has hyperbolic solutions. Use \(A\sinh(\kappa x)\) on the left to vanish at zero and \(B\sinh[\kappa(L-x)]\) on the right to vanish at \(L\). Continuity at \(x=s\) removes one constant. Integrating across the source gives \(G'_\kappa(s^+)-G'_\kappa(s^-)=-1\); the finite mass term integrates to zero in a shrinking source interval.

To solve transparently use the functions \(u(x)=\sinh(\kappa x)\),

\[v(x)=\sinh[\kappa(L-x)].\]

Their Wronskian \(u'v-uv'\) is \(\kappa\sinh(\kappa L)\), independent of \(x\). Then continuity, the derivative jump, and symmetry under \(s\leftrightarrow x\) are manifest in

\[G_\kappa(x,s)=\frac{\sinh(\kappa x_<)\sinh[\kappa(L-x_>)]}{\kappa\sinh(\kappa L)},\]
\[x_<=\min(x,s),\]
\[x_>=\max(x,s).\]

For \(\kappa\to0\), expand each hyperbolic sine to first order. The result is \(x_<(L-x_>)/L\), the grounded unscreened response. When both observation and source lie many screening lengths from every boundary, the kernel tends to \(e^{-\kappa|x-s|}/(2\kappa)\), the infinite-line screened solution. Thus one formula contains both geometric screening by grounded conductors and material screening by mobile charges.

At the midpoint source and detector coincide. The finite kernel reduces to \(\tanh(\kappa L/2)/(2\kappa)\). Compared with the unscreened midpoint value \(L/4\), its ratio is \(2\tanh(\kappa L/2)/(\kappa L)\). At \(\kappa L=2\) a fixed inserted sheet therefore creates only 76.2% of its unscreened midpoint voltage. A naïve replacement of plate separation by the screening length would give a constant ratio and miss the smooth small-\(\kappa\) crossover. For long gaps the measured voltage saturates at fixed sheet charge, because the countercharge response near the sheet now sets the local scale.

Check the convolution using a uniform positive imposed charge density \(\rho_0\). Without integrating the kernel directly, seek a constant particular solution plus a symmetric homogeneous correction. The result is

\[V(x)=[\rho_0/(\epsilon\kappa^2)]\{1-\cosh[\kappa(x-L/2)]/\cosh(\kappa L/2)\}.\]

Twice differentiation reconstructs the source and zero endpoints. In the small-\(\kappa L\) limit it tends to \(\rho_0x(L-x)/(2\epsilon)\). A numerical integration of the point response against a uniform source should match this independent closed solution throughout the interval, including the near-electrode regions. It is a stronger test than checking the point kernel at the centre alone.

Integrate the original equation across the gap. The sum of outward displacement fluxes equals imposed charge minus \(\epsilon\kappa^2\int V\,dx\), the magnitude of the compensating mobile charge. For the uniform source the fraction of imposed charge balanced by electrode rather than mobile charge is \(2\tanh(\kappa L/2)/(\kappa L)\). For a long gap this fraction falls like twice the screening length divided by gap width, because only the two boundary regions communicate appreciably with the electrodes. A current meter attached to the electrodes and a chemical measurement of accumulated mobile ions can therefore close the same source budget independently.

Design a differential collecting electrode from its weighting functions

Two concentric electrodes supply one signed position signal

A grounded plane is divided into a central disk of radius \(a\), a surrounding annulus from \(a\) to \(2a\), and the remainder. A small charge moves along the central normal. Two charge amplifiers measure the disk and annulus. Construct an output that vanishes at \(h=a\), then find its local height sensitivity. Why can that output alone not distinguish uncertain charge from height away from the null?

Derive and check the result

Let \(w_a=1-h/\sqrt{h^2+a^2}\) and \(w_{2a}=1-h/\sqrt{h^2+4a^2}\). The disk reads \(Q_d=-qw_a\); the annulus reads \(Q_r=-q(w_{2a}-w_a)\). An output \(S=Q_d-\gamma Q_r\) vanishes at \(h=a\) if \(\gamma=(1-1/\sqrt2)/(1/\sqrt2-1/\sqrt5)=1.127\) approximately. Both readings are negative for positive \(q\), so it is their difference, not their sum, that rejects the reference position. The ideal zero does not depend on the magnitude of any nonzero \(q\).

Differentiate with respect to height using \(w_b'=-b^2/(h^2+b^2)^{3/2}\). At the null \(a\,w_a'=-1/(2\sqrt2)\) and \(a\,w_{2a}'=-4/(5\sqrt5)\). Hence

\[a\,d(S/q)/dh=-(1+\gamma)a w_a'+\gamma a w_{2a}'\simeq0.349.\]

For \(q=10\) pC, \(a=2.0\) mm, a small upward displacement of 0.10 mm produces approximately \(+0.175\) pC. Finite differences of the full two-disk formula at heights on both sides of \(a\) check the linear slope. For displacements that are no longer small compared with \(a\), use the full expression rather than extrapolating that single sensitivity.

An additional sum channel \(Q_d+Q_r=-q w_{2a}\) permits a dimensionless output \(S/(Q_d+Q_r)\) that cancels the magnitude and sign of \(q\) while retaining height dependence. This ratio becomes noise sensitive far from the electrodes, where collected charge is small. A null by itself can also mean that there is no charge at all, so a usable position detector checks the sum channel at the same time. The exercise combines electrostatic reciprocity, electronic weighting and a finite signal-to-noise constraint while requiring only two precomputed boundary responses.

How much charge belongs to an annular collector?

An annular electrode with inner radius \(h\) and outer radius \(3h\) lies in a grounded plane. A charge \(q\) sits at height \(h\) over its centre. Derive its induced charge. If a numerical integration omits all radii beyond \(3h\), where does the missing charge go?

Work through the solution

Subtract two circular weights. The annulus receives \(Q/q=-[1/\sqrt2-1/\sqrt{10}]=-0.3909\). The central disk receives \(-(1-1/\sqrt2)q=-0.2929q\). The whole plane should sum to \(-q\), so the part beyond the outer edge supplies

\[-q/\sqrt{10}=-0.3162q.\]

Nothing has been lost by the physical system; a finite integration patch excludes a considerable piece of the long-tailed kernel. Radial extrapolation with the analytic far tail or an expanding integration domain distinguishes missing area from poor quadrature within the patch.

Use resistance to a modelling mistake

A student proposes \(G\) itself as the weight for setting electrode voltages on the plane. Another replaces it by \(2P_h\), arguing that an image doubles the response. Predict two different tests that diagnose these errors.

Work through the solution

The first proposal gives zero contribution since the Dirichlet Green function is zero at the boundary. It confuses volume source response with a boundary normal derivative. The second produces twice the prescribed constant voltage because the kernel would integrate to two. Independent electrostatic reciprocity would assign \(-2q\) to the whole grounded plane around a charge \(q\), violating the charge sum derived from the real and image fields. For a numerical test, set successively a constant, a sinusoid, and a bounded disk on the same plane. Those cases examine normalisation, spectral attenuation, and spatial tails separately.
