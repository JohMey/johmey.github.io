Perfect conductivity and magnetic expulsion are different statements

A normal conductor has current density \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E\). Combining Faraday's and low-frequency Ampère laws in a homogeneous medium gives \(\partial_t\mathbf B=(\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf B\). A step in field diffuses inwards over a length of order \(\sqrt{t/(\mu\sigma)}\). Under sustained sinusoidal drive the skin depth is \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\). As \(\omega\) tends to zero, this skin depth tends to infinity. An ordinary nonmagnetic conductor in its final static equilibrium generally admits a uniform applied magnetic field.

The London description of an isotropic superconductor in a Meissner state makes a distinct, equilibrium constitutive assumption: \(\nabla\times\mathbf J_s=-\mathbf B/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2)\), with penetration length \(\lambda_L\). In a homogeneous specimen carrying supercurrent and at frequencies where displacement current and normal losses may be neglected, \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J_s\). Curl once more and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). The resulting field satisfies \(\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2\). Unlike the diffusive skin depth, the equilibrium length remains finite for a static applied field.

Taking the formal limit \(\sigma\to\infty\) in a normal conductor suppresses the electric field for a finite current and can freeze a pre-existing flux during changes. That argument does not determine which static field a specimen adopts when cooled through its transition. Magnetic expulsion is extra thermodynamic physics. The comparison below assumes a weak enough field and a suitable state, omitting flux vortices, trapped flux and nonlinear or nonlocal effects.

Solve a slab with equal applied fields on both faces

Let a broad slab fill \(-d/2<x<d/2\), and impose identical tangential magnetic fields \(B_a\hat{\mathbf z}\) on its two faces. Symmetry requires \(B'(0)=0\). Applying both boundaries to the London equation gives

\[B(x)=B_a\frac{\cosh(x/\lambda_L)}{\cosh[d/(2\lambda_L)]},\qquad\]

\frac{\overline B}{B_a}=\frac{2\lambda_L}{d}\tanh\frac{d}{2\lambda_L}.

Differentiating gives the screening current \(J_y=-B'_z/\mu_0\). Currents flow in opposite directions at opposite faces; their net transport current across the thickness is zero. For a thick slab each face approaches a half-space with exponential decay. For \(d\ll\lambda_L\), the two surface layers overlap. Expanding the average gives \(1-d^2/(12\lambda_L^2)+\cdots\), so even a superconductor can have a substantial mean internal field in a sufficiently thin specimen under this parallel-field geometry.

Take \(d=100\) nm and \(\lambda_L=50\) nm. The centre field is \(B_a/\cosh 1\simeq0.6481 B_a\); the mean is \(\tanh1\simeq0.7616 B_a\). The distinction between centre value and volume average is already larger than many instrumental error bars. A measurement of magnetic moment in a finite non-slab geometry additionally requires its demagnetising field. Do not directly substitute the infinite-slab mean into an arbitrary sample-shape calibration.

Obtain the equilibrium length as an energy minimum

The same static profile follows from minimising \(\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}[B^2+\lambda_L^2(B')^2]\,dx/(2\mu_0)\) with boundary values imposed. The first term is magnetic field energy; the second is the energy associated with the screening current in this effective description. Vary \(B\) by a function \(\eta\) vanishing at the faces. Integrating \(B'\eta'\) by parts leaves an interior factor \(B-\lambda_L^2B''\). Its vanishing returns the London equation. This route makes the physical tradeoff clear: complete expulsion would require extremely sharp gradients and costly currents, while uniform penetration pays magnetic energy throughout the bulk.

The reduction assumes a fixed superconducting condensate amplitude. Near a critical temperature or critical field, allowing that amplitude to change introduces an additional coherence length and may alter both the field state and the validity of London theory. A numerical fit giving a stable value of \(\lambda_L\) at one temperature does not prove this approximation over an entire phase diagram.

Compare two experimental controls

Open the live experiment

Change the thickness and the penetration length independently. Find two different specimens with the same \(d/\lambda_L\); their normalised profiles against fractional thickness match, even though their local physical gradients and current densities can differ. If all you can measure is a single normalised profile, geometry calibration is part of the inference of \(\lambda_L\).

A frequency sweep supplies another distinction. In a normal sheet the complex field has both attenuation and phase lag with depth, and \(\delta\) changes as \(f^{-1/2}\). The static London profile above is real and determined by a material length. At higher frequencies superconductors can also exhibit complex response and dissipation, so measuring a single attenuation length in one frequency band cannot by itself establish static flux expulsion. Use the normal-film impedance experiment for a controlled comparison with its different rear boundary condition.
