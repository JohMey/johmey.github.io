Can a capacitance measurement reveal the fraction of embedded particles?

A sample contains well separated dielectric spheres in an insulating matrix. Its capacitance changes when the particle fraction changes. Derive a dilute estimate from one sphere and a self-consistent approximation from a local-field model. Use the inverse calculation to discover when the fraction becomes poorly determined.

What does a single particle actually contribute?

Let the host and sphere have positive, frequency-independent relative permittivities \(h\) and \(s\) and let each sphere have radius \(a\). The imposed field near an isolated sphere is approximately uniform. The exterior potential is the sum of a uniform-field term and a dipole term proportional to \(\cos\theta/r^2\); the regular interior solution is linear in \(r\cos\theta\). Impose continuity of potential and of normal electric displacement at \(r=a\). The internal field then equals \(3hE_{\rm loc}/(s+2h)\). The induced dipole is its integrated excess polarisation relative to the host:

\[p=\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\epsilon_0(s-h)E_{\rm in}=4\pi\epsilon_0h a^3\frac{s-h}{s+2h}E_{\rm loc}.\]

A low-permittivity inclusion can have a dipole opposite to its local field. It has removed more polarisable host material. This sign need not imply any negative permittivity. Define the contrast \(b=(s-h)/(s+2h)\). The denominator includes two components of the host contribution from the exterior field. Simply averaging permittivities as if every particle experienced the applied field would ignore this boundary calculation.

Leading density and a local-field closure

For number density \(n\), the inclusion fraction is \(f=4\pi a^3n/3\). To first order in \(f\), replace \(E_{\rm loc}\) by the macroscopic field and add the dipoles. The predicted effective relative permittivity is \(h(1+3fb)\), up to terms of second and higher order. To obtain the Maxwell–Garnett approximation, imagine a spherical exclusion region containing a representative particle. Approximate the far distribution of particles by its smooth excess polarisation \(P=np\). Removing the polarised sphere produces a local field increment \(P/(3\epsilon_0h)\) along \(P\). Substituting \(E_{\rm loc}=E+P/(3\epsilon_0h)\) into \(P=n\alpha E_{\rm loc}\) gives

\[\frac{\epsilon_{\rm eff}-h}{\epsilon_{\rm eff}+2h}=fb,\]
\[\epsilon_{\rm eff}=h\,\frac{1+2fb}{1-fb}.\]

The closure resums some higher-order terms. It does not solve the exact many-particle boundary-value problem at arbitrary density. Short-range correlations, connected clusters, nonspherical particles and anisotropy can matter. Identifying a distinguished host and separated inclusions is an experimental assumption. Interchanging host and particle labels while preserving their volume fractions generally gives another microstructure and another prediction.

Infer a fraction from a measured capacitor

Take \(h=2\), \(s=8\) so \(b=0.5\). At a fraction of 0.15, Maxwell–Garnett gives an effective relative permittivity \(2(1.15)/(0.925)=2.4865\). The first-order estimate gives 2.45. For electrodes of area \(100\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) separated by \(0.5\,\mathrm{mm}\), and negligible edge fields, these numbers give 4.40 pF and 4.34 pF. A stray capacitance of 0.1 pF is larger than their difference. A regression reporting many fractional digits cannot decide between these approximations until the empty fixture and lead capacitances are known.

Invert the ratio before inserting a measured value. If \(\epsilon_{\rm eff}=2.50\) the inferred fraction is

\[(2.50-2)/[(2.50+4)0.5]=0.154.\]

The derivative of inferred fraction with respect to the measured effective value is \(3h/[b(\epsilon_{\rm eff}+2h)^2]\). An absolute uncertainty of 0.03 on the effective permittivity corresponds to approximately 0.0085 on the fraction for this example. If \(s\) approaches \(h\), \(b\) approaches zero and the same measurement loses sensitivity. Increasing the number of data points only helps random scatter. It cannot create constituent contrast.

Compare geometry-dependent predictions at the same volume fraction

The graph uses a host permittivity of two and lets the particle permittivity and selected volume fraction vary independently. Compare the sphere estimate to the exact arithmetic and harmonic values for a laminate with fields parallel and perpendicular to its layers. All curves coincide for equal constituents and pure host. Separated spheres have no preferred direction under isotropic sampling, whereas a laminate does. Agreement of one measured scalar with a mixing formula cannot establish which geometry was present.

If the particle radius is halved at fixed dilute volume fraction, what happens to the predicted effective permittivity? What unmodelled effect could reveal the smaller radius?

Work through the solution

There are eight times more particles per volume and each ideal dipole has one eighth of its original polarizability. Their product is unchanged in quasistatic volume mixing. A finite-thickness interfacial layer occupies an increasingly large fraction as radius shrinks. Dynamic scattering at non-negligible size can also distinguish the samples.

What does the local-field formula predict for cavities of relative permittivity one embedded in a host of four at \(f=0.10\)?

Work through the solution

Here \(b=(1-4)/(1+8)=-1/3\). The effective result is

\[4(1-2/30)/(1+1/30)=3.613.\]

The sign and magnitude lie between the pure constituent values. A value larger than four from this lossless model would require rechecking the sign of the excess dipole.

A series of samples has the same measured capacitance in one direction. Propose a measurement that can separate isotropically dispersed spheres from a stack of layers.

Work through the solution

Reorient the electrodes or the sample and measure normal and tangential components at fixed geometry and frequency. A laminate generally exhibits different arithmetic and harmonic responses. An isotropic distribution of dilute spheres has a scalar effective response. Repeat with a second fixture orientation to exclude direction-dependent stray capacitance.
