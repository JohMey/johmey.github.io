From a delayed polarisation to a constitutive equation

A slab of insulating material is placed between parallel electrodes. At first the measured charge increases very quickly when a small voltage is switched on. It then continues to rise slowly even though the applied voltage is fixed. A constant real relative permittivity can describe the final charge but cannot predict this record. The experiment reveals at least two relevant time scales. For a measurement window that resolves only the slower process, treat the fast electronic response as instantaneous and describe the slower polarisation \(P\) by its approach to an equilibrium value.

At fixed weak field the equilibrium slow polarisation is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E\), with \(\Delta\epsilon>0\). Assume the rate of change is proportional to the distance from that equilibrium, with one positive relaxation time \(\tau\). Then

\[\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E(t).\]

This is a phenomenological material law. Its assumptions include linear response about the prepared state, a time-independent \(\tau\), and a sample whose constituents can be described by one dominant relaxation. Testing those assumptions is part of the experiment.

For \(E(t)=E_s\) switched on at zero from an unpolarised state, solve using the integrating factor \(e^{t/\tau}\). The result is

\[P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E_s(1-e^{-t/\tau}).\]

After one \(\tau\), 63.21% of the slow response has developed; after \(3\tau\), 95.02%. Measuring an absolute fraction requires subtracting the fast initial jump and knowing the eventual plateau. A long baseline drift can look like a second relaxation if those references are not controlled.

Derive the complex dielectric response

Now use peak phasors with time dependence \(e^{-i\omega t}\). Differentiation contributes \(-i\omega\), so

\[P/E=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon/(1-i\omega\tau).\]

Include the high-frequency relative permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\), where “high” means above the slow process but below additional electronic resonances. The real and imaginary parts of relative permittivity are

\[\epsilon'=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{\Delta\epsilon}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\]
\[\epsilon''=\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.\]

At fixed field amplitude the polarisation current is

\[\dot P=-i\omega P.\]

The mean electrical work per unit time and volume is

\[\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E^*\dot P)=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon''|E|^2.\]

Loss therefore has positive \(\epsilon''\) in the chosen time convention. Using \(e^{+i\omega t}\) reverses the sign of the imaginary part of permittivity but leaves mean absorbed energy positive when the current convention is changed consistently.

Set \(u=\omega\tau\). Differentiating \(u/(1+u^2)\) gives \((1-u^2)/(1+u^2)^2\). Hence \(\epsilon''\) peaks at \(u=1\), with height \(\Delta\epsilon/2\), and the real part lies halfway between its low- and high-frequency plateaux. The peak identified here is in the imaginary *permittivity*. A plot of dissipated power at fixed field includes an additional factor of frequency and need not have a peak at the same location.

Tie a frequency sweep to a step test

Suppose a material has \(\epsilon_\infty=2\), \(\Delta\epsilon=6\), and a measured loss peak at 1 kHz. Then \(\tau=1/(2\pi\,1000)\simeq159\ \mu\mathrm s\). At the peak the relative permittivity is \(5+3i\) in our convention. A time-domain voltage step should show a slow charge transient reaching 63.2% of its added charge in roughly 159 μs. This comparison connects two experiments that weight electrode offsets and parasitic impedances differently.

An independent shape check follows by eliminating \(u\). Let \(X=\epsilon'-\epsilon_\infty\) and \(Y=\epsilon''\). From the formulas \(X^2+Y^2=\Delta\epsilon X\). Thus \((X-\Delta\epsilon/2)^2+Y^2=(\Delta\epsilon/2)^2\): a single Debye relaxation traces a semicircle in the complex plane. Departures from a semicircle can reflect several relaxation times, spatially inhomogeneous samples, leakage or imperfect fixture correction. They do not uniquely identify the microscopic mechanism.

Resolve a second process only when the data require it

Open the live experiment

The synthetic sample has two groups contributing a fixed total \(\Delta\epsilon=6\). Change the fraction in the slower group and its relaxation time relative to the faster group. At equal relaxation times the groups give exactly the single-process response whatever their fractions. When the times differ, the imaginary curves broaden and may separate. Observe the real dispersion steps as well. A broad lossy peak alone can be fitted by more than one pair of times and strengths.

In a practical measurement the low-frequency tail can contain DC conductivity. In the \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention,

\[\sigma E-i\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon E=-i\omega\epsilon_0[\epsilon+i\sigma/(\epsilon_0\omega)]E.\]

This added imaginary part is proportional to \(1/\omega\), whereas the low-frequency side of an isolated Debye relaxation rises as \(\omega\). Change electrode separation and use a DC conduction measurement to challenge the interpretation. An electrode interface can add a further time scale, so an observed fit parameter deserves an uncertainty and a physical validation step.

Tutorial

The loss maximum is at 2 kHz and its height is 4 in relative-permittivity units. The high-frequency plateau is 3. Predict the low-frequency plateau and the slow response at \(t=100\,\mu\mathrm s\) after a step.

Work through the solution

The single-time model gives \(\Delta\epsilon=8\) and the low-frequency plateau 11. The relaxation time is \(1/[2\pi(2000)]\simeq79.6\,\mu\mathrm s\). The slow fraction after 100 μs is \(1-e^{-100/79.6}\simeq0.715\). Repeat at another step amplitude to check linearity.

At the loss peak, is the phase difference between the *total* displacement and the electric field always 45°?

Work through the solution

The slow polarisation alone has a 45° phasor angle at \(\omega\tau=1\). The total displacement includes \(\epsilon_\infty E\). Its angle is \(\arctan[(\Delta\epsilon/2)/(\epsilon_\infty+\Delta\epsilon/2)]\), generally less than 45°.

Why does observing only a high-frequency tail constrain a ratio of parameters more tightly than the individual parameters?

Work through the solution

For large \(\omega\tau\),

\[\epsilon''\simeq\Delta\epsilon/(\omega\tau).\]

Many values with the same ratio \(\Delta\epsilon/\tau\) have very similar tails. Measure the peak or both plateaux to distinguish the two parameters.
