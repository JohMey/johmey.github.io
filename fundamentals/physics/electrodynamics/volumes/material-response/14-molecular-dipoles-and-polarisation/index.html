Displace a bound charge before defining a material coefficient

Consider a neutral molecule whose negative charge cloud can move a small distance relative to its positive centre. Represent the restoring force along the applied field by a spring constant k and the displaced charge magnitude by q. Static balance gives kx=qE. The dipole moment is p=qx, so its proportionality coefficient is fixed by the restoring physics.

\[p=\alpha E,\]
\[\alpha=q^2/k.\]

The energy includes both deformation and the coupling to the external field. Minimising kx²/2−qxE gives the equilibrium displacement and the equilibrium energy −αE²/2. The factor one half follows from creating the moment while raising the field. A permanent, already established dipole instead has orientation energy −pE cosθ. The difference matters in a force gradient and in a thermodynamic partition sum.

Recall · Dipole moment, torque and field energy

Two charges ±q separated by vector d have p=qd directed toward positive charge. Expanding their potential energy in a slowly varying external potential gives U=p·∇V=−p·E. Vary the angle to obtain torque p×E. Vary position to obtain the gradient force. This expansion requires the charge separation to be small compared with the scale on which the external field changes.

For N independently responding molecules per unit volume, P=NαE if the local and macroscopic fields agree. Combining D=ε₀E+P with D=εE gives χₑ=Nα/ε₀. Each equality has an assumption. A dense solid can change the local field, and a large displacement can change the spring constant.

Count orientations with the correct angular measure

Now take a freely rotating permanent dipole of fixed magnitude p in thermal equilibrium at temperature T. Put u=cosθ and x=pE/(kBT). A thin ring on the orientation sphere has area 2π sinθ dθ. Consequently equal intervals in u have equal orientation measure. Boltzmann weighting gives the normalised density below.

\[Z_u(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}e^{xu}\,du={2\sinh x\over x},\]
\[f(u)={e^{xu}\over Z_u(x)}.\]

Differentiate the integral with respect to x. The derivative inserts u in the numerator, so the alignment is the logarithmic derivative of Z. Differentiating a second time gives a variance and therefore a nonnegative differential response.

\[\langle u\rangle={d\log Z_u\over dx}=\coth x-{1\over x}=L(x),\]
\[{d\langle u\rangle\over dx}=\langle u^2\rangle-\langle u\rangle^2.\]

Series expansion of sinh and cosh gives L(x)=x/3−x³/45+… . Thus the orientational susceptibility of a dilute gas is Np²/(3ε₀kBT). At large x the alignment approaches 1−1/x, saturating at P=Np. A linear law cannot remain valid in that limit.

\[P=N\left(\alpha+{p^2\over3k_BT}\right)E+O(E^3).\]

For p=3.0×10⁻³⁰ C m, T=300 K and E=10⁶ V/m, x=7.24×10⁻⁴. The mean orientation is only 2.41×10⁻⁴. A visibly nonzero macroscopic polarisation can therefore coexist with almost random individual molecular directions. Doubling E doubles the weak-field response, while doubling T halves its orientational part. The induced contribution in this elementary model stays fixed.

Try alignment and the angular measure

Change pE/(kBT) to concentrate the probability near the field direction, then lower the integration resolution. Equal intervals in u = cosθ represent equal solid-angle bands. The zero-field density is therefore 1/2 on this axis. The reported mean compares a trapezoidal integral with the exact Langevin law and its weak-field expansion.

The browser uses a simple composite trapezoidal rule so that resolution error is visible. The OneSimulation notebook below obtains Gaussian quadrature nodes from an eigenproblem and provides an independent, higher-order calculation of the same integral. Increase order there and compare convergence at fixed field energy. The model assumes freely rotating, noninteracting permanent dipoles in thermal equilibrium.

Reproduce with OneSimulation

The code obtains Gaussian integration nodes and weights from a symmetric eigenproblem using OneSimulation. It then integrates the probability density derived above. The analytic Langevin law supplies an independent check. Run this locally with OneSimulation, NumPy and Matplotlib installed.

"""Orient freely rotating permanent dipoles and compare quadrature with the exact law."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from onesim.numerics.linear.matrices.dense_linear_algebra import symmetric_eigensystem

# The eigenvectors give Gaussian weights for the integral over u = cos(theta).
order = 32
indices = np.arange(1, order, dtype=float)
off_diagonal = indices / np.sqrt(4 * indices**2 - 1)
jacobi = np.diag(off_diagonal, 1) + np.diag(off_diagonal, -1)
nodes, vectors = symmetric_eigensystem(jacobi)
weights = 2 * vectors[0, :]**2
field_energy = np.linspace(0, 12, 121)  # x = p E / (k_B T)
mean_alignment = []
for x in field_energy:
    probability = weights * np.exp(x * (nodes - 1))
    mean_alignment.append(np.dot(nodes, probability) / probability.sum())
mean_alignment = np.asarray(mean_alignment)
exact = np.zeros_like(field_energy)
exact[1:] = 1 / np.tanh(field_energy[1:]) - 1 / field_energy[1:]
print(f"Maximum quadrature error: {np.max(abs(mean_alignment - exact)):.2e}")
for x in (0.2, 1.0, 3.0, 10.0):
    index = np.argmin(abs(field_energy - x))
    print(f"pE/kT = {x:4.1f}, <cos(theta)> = {mean_alignment[index]:.6f}, linear = {x/3:.6f}")
assert np.max(abs(mean_alignment - exact)) < 1e-12
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.8), layout="constrained")
axes[0].plot(field_energy, mean_alignment, label="OneSimulation quadrature")
axes[0].plot(field_energy, field_energy / 3, "--", label="Weak field")
axes[0].set(xlabel="Dipole energy / thermal energy", ylabel="Mean alignment", ylim=(0, 1.05))
axes[0].legend()
for x in (0, 1, 5):
    u = np.linspace(-1, 1, 301)
    normalizer = 2 if x == 0 else 2*np.sinh(x)/x
    axes[1].plot(u, np.exp(x*u)/normalizer, label=f"pE/kT = {x}")
axes[1].set(xlabel="cos(theta)", ylabel="Probability density")
axes[1].legend()
for axis in axes:
    axis.grid(alpha=.2)
fig.savefig("electrodynamics-orientation.png", dpi=170)

Close the local-field problem

In an isotropic, approximately cubic environment imagine a spherical cavity around the chosen molecule. Let n point from the centre into the material. The material normal at the cavity wall is −n, giving bound charge σb=−P·n. A surface patch contributes dE(0)=−σb n dΩ/(4πε₀). Integrating with ∫nᵢnⱼdΩ=4πδᵢⱼ/3 gives the cavity contribution P/(3ε₀) at its centre. Under the Lorentz-cavity approximation, which neglects the remaining discrete near-neighbour correction, E_loc=E+P/(3ε₀). Write α_eff=α+p²/(3kBT) in the weak-field regime and solve P=Nα_eff E_loc for P.

\[{\epsilon_r-1\over\epsilon_r+2}={N\alpha_{\rm eff}\over3\epsilon_0}.\]

For Nα_eff/(3ε₀)=0.10 this gives εr=1.3333. The independent-molecule estimate gives 1.30. The discrepancy increases with density. The divergence predicted as that parameter approaches unity indicates the breakdown or instability of this simple constitutive closure. Molecular correlations, anisotropy, or nonlinear ordering must then be considered before assigning a physical permittivity.

Remember · A cavity field is a model of the surroundings

The factor one third belongs to a spherical cavity in the isotropic construction. The macroscopic depolarising factor of the entire specimen is a different geometric quantity. The local-field calculation addresses what one molecule feels. The boundary-value calculation addresses how the specimen and electrodes determine the macroscopic E.

Follow the ambiguity of the unit cell in a polar crystal

Take an infinite alternating chain of charges +q at na and −q at na+a/2, with one chain per cross-sectional area A. A neutral cell containing these two sites has moment −qa/2 and polarisation −q/(2A). Choose the equivalent negative ion at na−a/2 instead, keeping the same infinite crystal. That cell has moment +qa/2 and polarisation +q/(2A). The two assignments differ by q/A although every actual charge position is unchanged.

A finite sample supplies the missing information. Its end charges depend on how it is cut and on whether charges are supplied at the surfaces. Reassigning the cell changes the book-keeping of bound and free end charge together. The measurable total surface charge and external field remain determined by the physical termination. A bulk dipole-per-cell convention alone therefore does not determine the surface charge of an ionic crystal. In three dimensions the corresponding polarisation increment is qR/Ω for lattice vector R and cell volume Ω.

Exercises from response to inference

1. At the same number density, the measured dilute susceptibility is 0.010 at 300 K and 0.007 at 600 K. Separate a temperature-independent contribution from a contribution proportional to 1/T.

Work through the solution

Write χ=A+B/T. Subtraction gives 0.003=B/600, hence B=1.8 K and A=0.004. At 300 K the orientational contribution is 0.006. To infer p from B also requires N. If density changes with temperature, correct that effect before fitting this law.

2. A linear extrapolation predicts P=1.5Np. Determine what assumption has failed and estimate the large-field response for x=10.

Work through the solution

A mean cosine cannot exceed unity. The small-x expansion has been used outside its domain. L(10)=0.900000004…, giving P≈0.900Np for freely rotating permanent dipoles. The induced contribution is a separate term and can change the total moment magnitude.

3. Repeat the unit-cell construction after replacing the cross-sectional area by 2A. Explain which quantity changes and what measurement must specify the crystal termination.

Work through the solution

The polarisation branch spacing becomes q/(2A). Surface charge per area and the external field are intensive quantities, so the chain density matters. An end-charge measurement with specified electrodes and termination determines the physical surface preparation.
