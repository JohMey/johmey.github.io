Can a flat interface guide light without mirrors?

Send light along a plane boundary between a dielectric and a metal. Seek a wave that propagates parallel to the interface while decaying away on both sides. The signs in the magnetic and electric boundary conditions determine whether any such wave can exist.

Decide what confinement requires

Put a homogeneous dielectric with positive relative permittivity \(\epsilon_d\) above \(z=0\), a lossless dispersive metal of relative permittivity \(\epsilon_m(\omega)\) below it and take both permeabilities to be \(\mu_0\). Use phasors with \(e^{i\beta x-i\omega t}\). For the transverse magnetic polarisation write \(H_y=H_0e^{-\kappa_d z}\) in the dielectric and \(H_y=H_0e^{+\kappa_m z}\) in the metal. Both \(\kappa\) must be positive real numbers for this bound, lossless mode. Substitution into the homogeneous wave equation gives

\[\kappa_j^2=\beta^2-\epsilon_j k_0^2,\]
\[k_0=\omega/c.\]

Continuity of tangential magnetic field without an externally imposed surface current identifies the two amplitudes \(H_0\). The metal's mobile charges are already included in its bulk permittivity. Introducing the same current a second time as an arbitrary sheet would describe another boundary. Continuity of normal electric displacement follows here from the same magnetic amplitude and common tangential wavenumber.

The sign that selects a material pair

Use \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=-i\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon_j\mathbf E\). Its \(x\) component is \(-\partial_zH_y\). Hence \(E_x=-i\partial_z H_y/(\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon_j)\). Continuity of this tangential electric field gives

\[\kappa_d/\epsilon_d=-\kappa_m/\epsilon_m.\]

No two positive real permittivities can satisfy that equation with two positive decay constants. Squaring and eliminating \(\kappa_j\) gives a candidate propagation constant.

\[\beta^2=k_0^2\frac{\epsilon_m\epsilon_d}{\epsilon_m+\epsilon_d}.\]

Check it in the unsquared equation. With positive dielectric permittivity the physical branch requires \(\epsilon_m<-\epsilon_d\). On this branch \(\beta>\sqrt{\epsilon_d}\,k_0\), so the mode lies outside the propagation cone of a plane wave in the dielectric. For transverse electric polarisation the corresponding condition replaces permittivities by permeabilities. With two ordinary positive permeabilities the sign requirement fails. The wave exists because of a coupled electric field and charge response at a surface.

Work an example before using the dispersion diagram

Let \(\epsilon_d=1\), \(\epsilon_m=-4\) at the chosen frequency. Then \(\beta/k_0=\sqrt{4/3}=1.155\),

\[\kappa_d/k_0=1/\sqrt3,\]
\[\kappa_m/k_0=4/\sqrt3.\]

At vacuum wavelength 600 nm the electric and magnetic field amplitudes in the dielectric fall by a factor \(e\) after 165 nm. The corresponding distance in the metal is 41 nm. An intensity measurement falls by a factor \(e\) at half these distances. A wave sent directly through air has a parallel wavevector no larger than \(k_0\). An index-1.50 prism could match the parallel component at \(\theta=\arcsin(1.155/1.50)\), about 50.4 degrees. A practical evanescent coupler also needs finite overlap with the surface field.

Identify the surface resonance without assuming an infinite measured signal

Take the lossless Drude law \(\epsilon_m=1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2\). The guided branch ends at \(\omega_{\rm sp}=\omega_p/\sqrt{1+\epsilon_d}\), where \(\epsilon_m+\epsilon_d\) tends to zero. Approaching this frequency from below increases \(\beta\) at finite \(\omega\). In the electrostatic limit the restoring electric field and inertia of mobile charge select the limiting frequency. At small \(\omega\), by contrast, \(\beta\) approaches the dielectric light line and the wave spreads far into the dielectric. A measured resonance has a finite width. Collisions, surface roughness, microscopic nonlocal response and the optical coupling geometry matter before any divergence could be observed.

If the dielectric permittivity becomes 2.25 with the same ideal metal, what bulk-normalised frequency does the surface mode approach? Does an air beam now have enough tangential momentum near the resonance?

Work through the solution

The asymptote moves from \(1/\sqrt2=0.7071\) to \(1/\sqrt{3.25}=0.5547\). The parallel wavenumber exceeds the dielectric light line \(1.5 k_0\), which already exceeds the air limit. Direct incidence from air cannot provide the necessary parallel phase gradient.

Two near-field probes lie 120 nm apart normal to an interface at the 600 nm example. Their measured intensities have ratio 0.48. Is that consistent with sampling only the bound mode in the dielectric?

Work through the solution

Its predicted intensity ratio is

\[\exp[-2(120/165)]=0.234.\]

A ratio near 0.48 is close to the amplitude ratio. Check whether the detector is actually a linear heterodyne field measurement. Background light would also flatten an intensity decay.

Suppose a calculation squares the boundary equation and reports a propagating surface wave for two positive dielectrics. How can it be rejected without repeating the whole derivation?

Work through the solution

Compute both positive normal decay constants and substitute them into \(\kappa_d/\epsilon_d+\kappa_m/\epsilon_m=0\). The sum cannot vanish. Even a real positive \(\beta^2\) alone does not establish decay plus the correct boundary sign.
