Fields and the response of matter · 1. Measure how quickly a dielectric follows an electric field

Derive the time and frequency responses of a relaxing polarisation, locate the loss maximum and decide whether a measured sample contains more than one relaxation process.

From a delayed polarisation to a constitutive equation

A slab of insulating material is placed between parallel electrodes. At first the measured charge increases very quickly when a small voltage is switched on. It then continues to rise slowly even though the applied voltage is fixed. A constant real relative permittivity can describe the final charge but cannot predict this record. The experiment reveals at least two relevant time scales. For a measurement window that resolves only the slower process, treat the fast electronic response as instantaneous and describe the slower polarisation \(P\) by its approach to an equilibrium value.

At fixed weak field the equilibrium slow polarisation is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E\), with \(\Delta\epsilon>0\). Assume the rate of change is proportional to the distance from that equilibrium, with one positive relaxation time \(\tau\). Then

\[\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E(t).\]

This is a phenomenological material law. Its assumptions include linear response about the prepared state, a time-independent \(\tau\), and a sample whose constituents can be described by one dominant relaxation. Testing those assumptions is part of the experiment.

For \(E(t)=E_s\) switched on at zero from an unpolarised state, solve using the integrating factor \(e^{t/\tau}\). The result is

\[P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E_s(1-e^{-t/\tau}).\]

After one \(\tau\), 63.21% of the slow response has developed; after \(3\tau\), 95.02%. Measuring an absolute fraction requires subtracting the fast initial jump and knowing the eventual plateau. A long baseline drift can look like a second relaxation if those references are not controlled.

Derive the complex dielectric response

Now use peak phasors with time dependence \(e^{-i\omega t}\). Differentiation contributes \(-i\omega\), so

\[P/E=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon/(1-i\omega\tau).\]

Include the high-frequency relative permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\), where “high” means above the slow process but below additional electronic resonances. The real and imaginary parts of relative permittivity are

\[\epsilon'=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{\Delta\epsilon}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\]
\[\epsilon''=\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.\]

At fixed field amplitude the polarisation current is

\[\dot P=-i\omega P.\]

The mean electrical work per unit time and volume is

\[\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E^*\dot P)=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon''|E|^2.\]

Loss therefore has positive \(\epsilon''\) in the chosen time convention. Using \(e^{+i\omega t}\) reverses the sign of the imaginary part of permittivity but leaves mean absorbed energy positive when the current convention is changed consistently.

Set \(u=\omega\tau\). Differentiating \(u/(1+u^2)\) gives \((1-u^2)/(1+u^2)^2\). Hence \(\epsilon''\) peaks at \(u=1\), with height \(\Delta\epsilon/2\), and the real part lies halfway between its low- and high-frequency plateaux. The peak identified here is in the imaginary *permittivity*. A plot of dissipated power at fixed field includes an additional factor of frequency and need not have a peak at the same location.

Tie a frequency sweep to a step test

Suppose a material has \(\epsilon_\infty=2\), \(\Delta\epsilon=6\), and a measured loss peak at 1 kHz. Then \(\tau=1/(2\pi\,1000)\simeq159\ \mu\mathrm s\). At the peak the relative permittivity is \(5+3i\) in our convention. A time-domain voltage step should show a slow charge transient reaching 63.2% of its added charge in roughly 159 μs. This comparison connects two experiments that weight electrode offsets and parasitic impedances differently.

An independent shape check follows by eliminating \(u\). Let \(X=\epsilon'-\epsilon_\infty\) and \(Y=\epsilon''\). From the formulas \(X^2+Y^2=\Delta\epsilon X\). Thus \((X-\Delta\epsilon/2)^2+Y^2=(\Delta\epsilon/2)^2\): a single Debye relaxation traces a semicircle in the complex plane. Departures from a semicircle can reflect several relaxation times, spatially inhomogeneous samples, leakage or imperfect fixture correction. They do not uniquely identify the microscopic mechanism.

The synthetic sample has two groups contributing a fixed total \(\Delta\epsilon=6\). Change the fraction in the slower group and its relaxation time relative to the faster group. At equal relaxation times the groups give exactly the single-process response whatever their fractions. When the times differ, the imaginary curves broaden and may separate. Observe the real dispersion steps as well. A broad lossy peak alone can be fitted by more than one pair of times and strengths.

In a practical measurement the low-frequency tail can contain DC conductivity. In the \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention,

\[\sigma E-i\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon E=-i\omega\epsilon_0[\epsilon+i\sigma/(\epsilon_0\omega)]E.\]

This added imaginary part is proportional to \(1/\omega\), whereas the low-frequency side of an isolated Debye relaxation rises as \(\omega\). Change electrode separation and use a DC conduction measurement to challenge the interpretation. An electrode interface can add a further time scale, so an observed fit parameter deserves an uncertainty and a physical validation step.

Fields and the response of matter · 2. Identify mobile charge with a transverse field

Derive a Hall-voltage measurement from transverse force balance, reverse current and field to reject offsets and explore what fails when two carrier types conduct.

Choose signs from a drawing

A rectangular conducting sample has its long axis along \(x\), width \(w\) along \(y\), and thickness \(t\) along \(z\). A uniform magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\) is applied. An external circuit maintains conventional current \(I>0\) in \(+x\). High-impedance transverse probes draw negligible current. Define the measured signal as \(V_H=V(y=0)-V(y=w)\). For uniform transverse field this convention gives

\[V_H=E_yw.\]

Reversing the voltmeter leads reverses the reported sign; writing a carrier sign without a lead convention is incomplete.

With one charge carrier species of number density \(n\) and signed charge \(q\), the drift velocity satisfies

\[j_x=nqv_x=I/(wt).\]

If the transverse velocity vanishes, its magnetic force component is \(-qv_xB\), opposed by \(qE_y\). Thus \(E_y=v_x B=j_xB/(nq)\). The resulting port measurement is

\[V_H=\frac{IB}{nq t}.\]

The width cancels for a uniform bar; changing the contact geometry or using a current source with a nonuniform injection profile can spoil that simplification near the terminals. The cancellation also exposes the sign. For electrons \(q<0\), positive conventional current means that the mobile charge drifts in \(-x\). The inferred transverse field is negative for positive \(B\) in the chosen convention.

Reconstruct density and mobility from separate observations

Take \(t=0.50\) mm, \(I=5.0\) mA, \(B=0.20\) T and a transverse signal of \(-1.25\) mV. The inferred signed Hall coefficient is

\[R_H=tV_H/(IB)=-6.25\times10^{-4}\ {\rm m^3/C}.\]

Using singly charged electrons gives

\[n=1/(e|R_H|)\simeq1.00\times10^{22}\ {\rm m^{-3}}.\]

A longitudinal voltage measurement, after correcting for probe separation, can yield the material conductivity. If \(\sigma=80\) S/m, the single-carrier mobility is

\[\mu=\sigma |R_H|=0.050\ {\rm m^2/(V\,s)}.\]

These operations use different pieces of information. The longitudinal measurement alone gives only the product \(n|q|\mu\). The transverse measurement supplies a sign and, in the one-carrier model, the density independently of mobility. This is an inverse experiment whose two observables have different sensitivities, not a more precise measurement of the same scalar.

One can derive the same result from a stationary Drude equation \(0=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)-m\mathbf v/\tau\). The longitudinal drag gives

\[E_x=mv_x/(q\tau);\]

force balance in \(y\) gives the transverse field just obtained. In a homogeneous single-carrier sample, \(E_y/E_x=q\tau B/m\), the signed Hall angle tangent. At fixed sample and field, doubling \(I\) doubles both voltages in this linear regime and leaves their ratio unchanged. Significant current-dependent apparent density can indicate heating or nonlinear contacts.

Isolate the transverse voltage from a misaligned contact

Practical side contacts seldom sit at exactly the same \(x\). A fraction of the longitudinal voltage can leak into the transverse pair. Write the readout at a fixed current as \(V_\mathrm{m}(B)=V_0+\alpha V_{xx}+V_H(B)\). A background that is even in field cancels in \([V_\mathrm m(B)-V_\mathrm m(-B)]/2\). For example, readings of 3.05 mV and 5.55 mV at \(+B,-B\) return \(-1.25\) mV Hall signal and 4.30 mV of even background. Taking a single measured sign would have diagnosed the wrong carriers.

When a Hall density stops being a particle count

With electrons and holes, longitudinal conductivities add. In a weak magnetic field their transverse contributions also depend on squared mobilities. For positive mobilities \(\mu_e,\mu_h\) and electron and hole densities \(n,p\), the two-carrier weak-field coefficient in the sign convention above is \(R_H=(p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2)/[e(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2]\). To see where the square appears, note that one factor of mobility converts the longitudinal field into drift; a second converts the resulting transverse magnetic force into a drift contribution. Summing species currents and imposing zero total transverse current gives the denominator management through the summed longitudinal conductivity.

Equal electron and hole densities with equal mobilities give zero Hall signal while current still flows readily. A single-species inversion would suggest unbounded density. At equal densities but unequal mobilities, the faster species can set the sign even though neither species is more numerous. Additional field dependence, temperature variation and independent structural information are needed before treating a Hall number as a carrier census.

Fields and the response of matter · 3. Measure equilibrium field fluctuations with a capacitor

Derive an electrical noise measurement in two ways, determine what fraction reaches a finite-bandwidth detector and use agreement to calibrate an experiment.

A zero-average voltage can store measurable energy

Connect a capacitor \(C\) across a resistor \(R\) at a uniform absolute temperature \(T\), with no external drive. Ideal circuit equations predict that any deliberately deposited charge decays on time scale \(RC\). At thermal equilibrium, microscopic fluctuations continually exchange charge and energy with the capacitor. Symmetry gives zero mean voltage, but the stored energy involves \(V^2\). In the classical limit a quadratic degree of freedom has mean energy \(k_BT/2\), giving \(\langle V^2\rangle=k_BT/C\). The resistor sets the time scale of equilibration; equilibrium mean-square voltage does not depend on resistance in this ideal model.

Check the dimensions: \(k_BT\) is an energy in joules and capacitance has units coulombs per volt. Their ratio is volts squared. At \(T=300\) K and \(C=100\) pF the RMS voltage is \(\sqrt{k_BT/C}\simeq6.44\ \mu{\rm V}\). A voltmeter that averages the signed voltage for a long time reports a value near zero. Measuring the noise requires an RMS, variance or appropriately calibrated spectral measurement.

Derive the same variance from a spectral measurement

Use a one-sided voltage power spectral density per hertz at positive frequencies. The classical Johnson–Nyquist open-circuit result for a resistor is \(S_R(f)=4k_BTR\) V²/Hz. Its square root is an amplitude spectral density in V/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\), not a voltage variance. Use an equivalent series voltage noise source and the RC transfer function \(H(f)=1/[1+2\pi i fRC]\). A stationary linear system multiplies the voltage power spectrum by \(|H|^2\), so

\[S_C(f)=\frac{4k_BTR}{1+(2\pi fRC)^2},\qquad\]

\int_0^\infty S_C(f)\,df=\frac{k_BT}{C}.

The integral follows by \(u=2\pi RCf\) and \(\int_0^\infty du/(1+u^2)=\pi/2\). It furnishes a detailed check on the factor four, the use of one-sided density, the conversion from hertz to angular frequency and the amplitude-versus-power distinction. If your result disagrees by a constant factor of two or \(2\pi\), audit these conventions before assigning a new noise temperature.

For \(R=10\) kΩ, \(T=300\) K, the low-frequency amplitude spectral density is approximately 12.9 nV/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). The RC corner at \(C=100\) pF is \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\simeq159\) kHz. The open-circuit low-frequency density rises with \(R\) while the bandwidth shrinks in inverse proportion. Their integrated product gives the earlier resistance-independent variance.

Include a real observation bandwidth

Suppose a first measurement adds an ideal low-pass cutoff \(B\). The recorded fraction of equilibrium variance is \(F=(2/\pi)\arctan(B/f_c)\). Half of the noise variance lies below the RC corner. That may be surprising if the corner was introduced as the frequency at which the spectral density reaches half its zero-frequency value. These two properties coincide for this single-pole spectrum for different mathematical reasons.

At \(B=0.1f_c\), \(F\simeq0.06345\), so the recorded RMS is about 25.2% of the infinite-band RMS. At \(B=10f_c\), \(F\simeq0.93655\), giving about 96.8% of the RMS. In an experiment, taking a square root one step too early changes this comparison. A real detector transfer function replaces the sharp cutoff by an integral of \(S_C(f)|H_\mathrm{det}(f)|^2\).

In the classical regime spectral density and integrated variance are proportional to \(T\), while RMS voltage grows only as \(\sqrt T\). Background · How does a longer voltage exposure alter the measured noise?

An equilibrium resistor–capacitor circuit has voltage covariance \(C_v(s)=(k_BT/C)\exp(-|s|/\tau)\), where \(\tau=RC\). Suppose a digitiser returns the mean voltage during an exposure \(T_m\). Squaring a sample of this mean measures a different variance from averaging the instantaneous voltage squared. In the classical stationary model the two-time integral becomes

\[\mathrm{var}(\bar v)=\frac{2}{T_m^2}\int_0^{T_m}(T_m-s)C_v(s)\,ds=\frac{2k_BT}{C}\frac{u-1+e^{-u}}{u^2},\]
\[u=\frac{T_m}{\tau}.\]

Short exposures recover \(k_BT/C\). At ten correlation times only about 18% of that variance remains. For very long exposures the variance decreases as \(2\tau/T_m\). Taking more closely spaced samples of one exposure cannot undo that averaging.

A physical check and a calibration check

An RC time-domain experiment provides a second observable beyond integrated power. Under the same single-pole equilibrium model the voltage autocovariance is

\[\langle V(t)V(t+\tau)\rangle=(k_BT/C)e^{-|\tau|/(RC)}.\]

Its variance at zero lag agrees with equipartition, and its decay time is obtained from the homogeneous charge-relaxation equation. Repeating the measurement with a known extra parallel capacitance lowers the RMS and lengthens the correlation time. Seeing only one of those changes suggests that the observed noise may include substantial amplifier input voltage or an independent background.

The classical approximation assumes \(hf\ll k_BT\) over the relevant band and a resistor that can be treated as frequency independent. A MHz room-temperature experiment is far inside this regime. At sufficiently high frequencies or cryogenic temperatures, a quantum noise model, an explicit definition of the measured ordering of the spectrum and the detector's energy exchange are needed. Extending an ideal classical white source to infinite frequency is only permissible as an approximation inside the convergent low-frequency RC calculation; it is not a microscopic model at arbitrarily high frequency.

Fields and the response of matter · 4. When does a polar crystal change its preferred state?

Derive stable and unstable dielectric responses from a free-energy landscape. Separate a reversible small-signal capacitance from a switching experiment.

Why one voltage can support several measured polarisations

Some insulating crystals develop a spontaneous electric polarisation as temperature is lowered. The high-temperature phase may be symmetric under inversion so that states with polarisation \(+P\) and \(-P\) are equivalent in zero field. Near a continuous transition, treat one component of polarisation as an order parameter. A local free-energy density compatible with the symmetry is \(f(P)=a(T-T_c)P^2/2+bP^4/4\) with \(a,b>0\). The omitted terms limit how far in temperature or field this truncated expansion is reliable. The positive quartic term keeps the energy bounded when the quadratic coefficient is negative.

For a device whose applied electric field \(E\) is controlled, the appropriate potential in this simple model is \(g(P;E)=f(P)-EP\). The linear term records external work; changing what is held fixed changes the energy to minimise. Differentiation gives the constitutive equation \(E=a(T-T_c)P+bP^3\). An algebraic root is an equilibrium candidate. A stable local response also requires positive second derivative \(a(T-T_c)+3bP^2\). A very accurate rootfinder can return an unstable stationary point if this second test is omitted.

Find the equilibrium branches and their response

Introduce scaled polarisation \(p\), field \(e\) and temperature parameter \(s\) such that \(g=p^4/4+sp^2/2-ep\). Then \(e=p^3+sp\). For \(s>0\), the slope \(3p^2+s\) stays positive. Each field has one stable solution. At \(e=0\), \(p=0\) and the small-signal susceptibility \(dp/de=1/s\) increases when approaching the ideal critical point from above.

For \(s=-1,\ e=0\), stationary values are \(-1,0,+1\). The middle root has curvature −1 and is a local maximum. Each outer root has curvature 2 and is a minimum. The susceptibility about a selected outer state is \(1/2\) in the scaled units. The fact that the two domains can carry opposite zero-field polarisation does not make the harmonic response negative. The mere existence of a negative-slope segment in the cubic constitutive curve is insufficient to infer a stable isolated small-signal device with that differential response.

Derive the limit of a metastable homogeneous state

Imagine preparing the negative-polarisation state at \(s=-1\), then increasing the applied field slowly. A positive field lowers the energy of the positive state. The negative state can persist locally behind a barrier even after it ceases to be the global minimum. A model of perfectly homogeneous evolution without early nucleation follows this metastable branch until the curvature vanishes. Solve \(3p^2-1=0\), choose \(p=-1/\sqrt3\), and insert it into \(e=p^3-p\). The positive limiting field is

\[e=2/(3\sqrt3)\simeq0.3849.\]

At the opposite spinodal the signs reverse.

This is a limit of the particular energy model. Real switching can occur earlier through domain walls, nucleation, defects or thermally activated barrier crossing. Observing a smaller coercive field in a specimen is not evidence that the cubic equation was solved inaccurately. To compare an actual hysteresis loop with a calculation, state the sweep rate, boundary circuit, sample preparation and a dynamical or nucleation model in addition to equilibrium free energy.

Read a landscape rather than tracing an unlabelled loop

A below-transition sample can also form spatially varying domains. Adding a positive gradient term \(K(\nabla P)^2/2\) penalises interfaces, while depolarising fields can favour a mixture with reduced net surface charge. This competition sets new length scales absent from the homogeneous model. An electrode measuring total charge can return near-zero mean polarisation from oppositely oriented domains even though each domain is strongly polarised. A local probe and a bulk capacitance measurement therefore constrain different parts of the model.

Turn a capacitance measurement into a consistency test

For a small harmonic voltage imposed about a prepared local minimum, the differential response is \(\delta P/\delta E=1/[a(T-T_c)+3bP^2]\), provided the driving frequency is slow enough for this local variable to respond and the amplitude is small enough to stay in the same well. Add the vacuum and fast polarisation terms when converting to total displacement \(D\). In a parallel-plate geometry capacitance per area then follows from \(\delta D/\delta E\) divided by thickness.

A large-signal charge–voltage measurement crosses different branches and may dissipate work around a loop. The energy per volume over a closed polarisation cycle is \(\oint E\,dP\) with the sign convention for electrical work. A single equilibrium graph of \(f(P)\) returns to its initial state with no prescribed loop area. Measured hysteresis thus carries dynamical information that a polynomial fit to one reversible branch cannot uniquely reconstruct.

Fields and the response of matter · 5. Distinguish magnetic screening from electrical diffusion

Compare the frequency dependence, phase and thickness dependence of field penetration in a normal metal and a superconducting slab.

Perfect conductivity and magnetic expulsion are different statements

A normal conductor has current density \(\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E\). Combining Faraday's and low-frequency Ampère laws in a homogeneous medium gives

\[\partial_t\mathbf B=(\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf B.\]

A step in field diffuses inwards over a length of order \(\sqrt{t/(\mu\sigma)}\). Under sustained sinusoidal drive the skin depth is \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}\). As \(\omega\) tends to zero, this skin depth tends to infinity. An ordinary nonmagnetic conductor in its final static equilibrium generally admits a uniform applied magnetic field.

The London description of an isotropic superconductor in a Meissner state makes a distinct, equilibrium constitutive assumption:

\[\nabla\times\mathbf J_s=-\mathbf B/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2),\]

with penetration length \(\lambda_L\). In a homogeneous specimen carrying supercurrent and at frequencies where displacement current and normal losses may be neglected,

\[\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J_s.\]

Curl once more and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). The resulting field satisfies

\[\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2.\]

Unlike the diffusive skin depth, the equilibrium length remains finite for a static applied field.

Taking the formal limit \(\sigma\to\infty\) in a normal conductor suppresses the electric field for a finite current and can freeze a pre-existing flux during changes. That argument does not determine which static field a specimen adopts when cooled through its transition. Magnetic expulsion is extra thermodynamic physics. The comparison below assumes a weak enough field and a suitable state, omitting flux vortices, trapped flux and nonlinear or nonlocal effects.

Solve a slab with equal applied fields on both faces

Let a broad slab fill \(-d/2<x<d/2\), and impose identical tangential magnetic fields \(B_a\hat{\mathbf z}\) on its two faces. Symmetry requires \(B'(0)=0\). Applying both boundaries to the London equation gives

\[B(x)=B_a\frac{\cosh(x/\lambda_L)}{\cosh[d/(2\lambda_L)]},\qquad\]

\frac{\overline B}{B_a}=\frac{2\lambda_L}{d}\tanh\frac{d}{2\lambda_L}.

Differentiating gives the screening current \(J_y=-B'_z/\mu_0\). Currents flow in opposite directions at opposite faces; their net transport current across the thickness is zero. For a thick slab each face approaches a half-space with exponential decay. For \(d\ll\lambda_L\), the two surface layers overlap. Expanding the average gives \(1-d^2/(12\lambda_L^2)+\cdots\), so even a superconductor can have a substantial mean internal field in a sufficiently thin specimen under this parallel-field geometry.

Take \(d=100\) nm and \(\lambda_L=50\) nm. The centre field is \(B_a/\cosh 1\simeq0.6481 B_a\); the mean is \(\tanh1\simeq0.7616 B_a\). The distinction between centre value and volume average is already larger than many instrumental error bars. A measurement of magnetic moment in a finite non-slab geometry additionally requires its demagnetising field. Do not directly substitute the infinite-slab mean into an arbitrary sample-shape calibration.

Obtain the equilibrium length as an energy minimum

The same static profile follows from minimising \(\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}[B^2+\lambda_L^2(B')^2]\,dx/(2\mu_0)\) with boundary values imposed. The first term is magnetic field energy; the second is the energy associated with the screening current in this effective description. Vary \(B\) by a function \(\eta\) vanishing at the faces. Integrating \(B'\eta'\) by parts leaves an interior factor \(B-\lambda_L^2B''\). Its vanishing returns the London equation. This route makes the physical tradeoff clear: complete expulsion would require extremely sharp gradients and costly currents, while uniform penetration pays magnetic energy throughout the bulk.

The reduction assumes a fixed superconducting condensate amplitude. Near a critical temperature or critical field, allowing that amplitude to change introduces an additional coherence length and may alter both the field state and the validity of London theory. A numerical fit giving a stable value of \(\lambda_L\) at one temperature does not prove this approximation over an entire phase diagram.

Derive a band gap using two lossless transparent materials and distinguish evanescent Bloch propagation from absorption of optical power.

Construct a unit cell whose waves interfere

Stack two transparent, lossless, nonmagnetic dielectrics periodically in one dimension. Their refractive indices are \(n_1,n_2\), their physical thicknesses \(d_1,d_2\), and the period \(a=d_1+d_2\). Work at normal incidence, so polarisation directions are equivalent for isotropic layers. Each separate material admits a propagating wave at the chosen optical frequency, yet the periodic stack may not. The effect comes from coherent interface reflections, not from a negative refractive index or an absorptive constituent.

Describe the local field by its tangential electric and magnetic components. Propagation through one homogeneous layer connects them by a \(2\times2\) transfer matrix with diagonal \(\cos\phi_j\), off-diagonal terms proportional to \(iZ_j\sin\phi_j\) and \(i\sin\phi_j/Z_j\), where \(\phi_j=\omega n_jd_j/c\) and \(Z_j=Z_0/n_j\). Changing the chosen propagation direction or time convention may reverse the two imaginary signs together. The real trace and unit determinant of the cell matrix below are unaffected.

Read the dispersion relation from the trace

At an interface both tangential components are continuous in the absence of imposed sheet currents. Multiply the two propagation matrices to form one cell. Its determinant is one because each layer's determinant is

\[\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi=1.\]

Thus the two eigenvalues are reciprocal. For a Bloch mode they are \(e^{\pm i k_Ba}\) on a pass band. Taking half the trace gives

\[\cos(k_Ba)=\cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2-\frac12\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}+\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)\sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2 .\]

When the right side lies between −1 and 1 a real Bloch phase exists. If its magnitude exceeds one, a decaying and a growing Bloch factor replace a pair of unit-modulus factors. For a bounded excitation of a semi-infinite stack choose the decaying direction. The attenuation per cell is \(\operatorname{arcosh}|\operatorname{trace}(M)/2|\). A lossless semi-infinite crystal in a stop band reflects incident power. Spatial attenuation of a Bloch amplitude in this region is compatible with zero volumetric heat production.

Obtain a quantitative stop-band width

Choose \(n_1d_1=n_2d_2=\lambda_0/4\). Writing \(s=\omega/\omega_0\), both layer phases are \(\phi=\pi s/2\). At \(s=1\) the half trace is \(-\tfrac12(n_2/n_1+n_1/n_2)\). Recognising \(\cosh(\log r)=(r+r^{-1})/2\) gives centre attenuation \(|\log(n_2/n_1)|\) per cell. For \(n_1=1.5,n_2=2.0\) this is 0.2877, so a decaying eigencomponent has amplitude factor 0.75 per cell. This statement about an eigencomponent should not be confused with the exact transmittance of a specific finite stack, which also depends on entrance and exit impedances.

The first gap edges obey half trace −1. With \(\cos^2\phi=1-\sin^2\phi\), solve for the edge sine or cosine. The two edge phases are symmetric about \(\pi/2\) and satisfy

\[|\cos\phi|=|n_2-n_1|/(n_1+n_2).\]

Hence the fractional angular-frequency width is \((4/\pi)\arcsin(|n_2-n_1|/(n_1+n_2))\), about 0.183 for the example. The lowest gap width tends continuously to zero as index contrast vanishes. A gap in a plotted numerical spectrum at \(n_1=n_2\) would indicate an implementation or plotting problem.

Fields and the response of matter · 7. Why rotating a layered dielectric changes a capacitance measurement

Derive the two principal permittivities of a fine laminate, rotate the constitutive tensor and distinguish the applied electrode condition from an assumed field direction.

Compare the same material in two orientations

Build a specimen by repeating layers with scalar permittivities \(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2>0\), occupying fractions \(f\) and \(1-f\). The period is much smaller than both the specimen scale and the spatial variation of the applied field. Suppose both materials are linear, free of mobile charge and effectively lossless in the measurement band. An observer using a large capacitor wants one law relating averaged displacement and electric field. A scalar average cannot describe both arrangements of the same layers relative to the plates.

Place the field tangential to all internal interfaces first. A narrow rectangular electrostatic loop straddling an interface gives continuity of tangential \(E\). Both layers see the same tangential field, but their displacements differ in proportion to permittivity. Averaging gives

\[\overline D_t=[f\epsilon_1+(1-f)\epsilon_2]\overline E_t.\]

The tangent-direction effective permittivity is an arithmetic mean. This is a parallel connection of capacitances at shared voltage in the special capacitor geometry.

Apply a purely normal displacement instead. No free charge lies at the internal interfaces, so their normal displacement is continuous. Normal fields differ as \(E_i=D_n/\epsilon_i\). Averaging the voltage drops gives

\[\overline E_n=[f/\epsilon_1+(1-f)/\epsilon_2]\overline D_n,\]

with a harmonic-mean effective permittivity. The local continuity condition changes which field is shared between constituents and thereby changes the relevant averaging operation.

Check the amount of anisotropy from field energy

For \(\epsilon_1=2\epsilon_0,\epsilon_2=10\epsilon_0\) at equal fractions,

\[\epsilon_t=6\epsilon_0,\]
\[\epsilon_n=(10/3)\epsilon_0.\]

The arithmetic-to-harmonic ratio is 1.8. A constituent does not need to be crystalline or anisotropic for the composite to show distinct principal responses. The geometry of the internal boundaries is enough.

Average the energy density for the tangential experiment,

\[u=f\epsilon_1E_t^2/2+(1-f)\epsilon_2E_t^2/2=\epsilon_t E_t^2/2.\]

For the normal experiment,

\[u=D_n^2[f/\epsilon_1+(1-f)/\epsilon_2]/2=D_n^2/(2\epsilon_n).\]

These expressions supply a check independent of the capacitor-circuit analogy and identify which energy should be used when field or free charge is controlled. Energy positivity follows directly from the positive constituent permittivities. It does not require guessing an effective law from the largest measured component.

Rotate the law, then specify what the electrodes control

Use principal axes \(\hat n,\hat t\) and suppose a macro-field makes angle \(\theta\) to the layer normal. If \(\overline{\mathbf E}=E(\cos\theta\,\hat n+\sin\theta\,\hat t)\), then \(\overline{\mathbf D}=E(\epsilon_n\cos\theta\,\hat n+\epsilon_t\sin\theta\,\hat t)\). Project along the imposed field and divide by \(E\):

\[\epsilon_E(\theta)=\epsilon_n\cos^2\theta+\epsilon_t\sin^2\theta .\]

The perpendicular displacement is \(E(\epsilon_t-\epsilon_n)\sin\theta\cos\theta\). It is generally nonzero. A device that instead fixes the direction of averaged displacement while allowing a transverse field measures \(\epsilon_D(\theta)=[\cos^2\theta/\epsilon_n+\sin^2\theta/\epsilon_t]^{-1}\). These two directional responses correspond to different constraints. They agree at principal axes and for isotropic material. A pair of unspecified “angled capacitor” measurements cannot be interpreted uniquely without describing the contacts and edge conditions.

At 45° in the example, the field-controlled projection gives about \(4.667\epsilon_0\), while the displacement-controlled projection gives \(4.286\epsilon_0\). These are both legitimate scalar ratios from one symmetric positive-definite tensor. Neither supplies a universal scalar permittivity for the rotated material. The complete vector response also predicts displacement tilt and field energy for any orientation.

Derive rotation from propagation rather than drawing an arrow

Let monochromatic light propagate along \(+z\) through a transparent magneto-optic sample, with an externally prepared axial magnetic field. Define fixed laboratory vectors \(\mathbf e_\pm=(\hat x\pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2\) and use phasors \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\). These definitions specify the handedness algebraically. Calling a vector simply “right circular” without stating the viewing and time conventions can reverse a reported rotation sign.

An input linearly polarised in \(x\) is \((\mathbf e_++\mathbf e_-)/\sqrt2\). Suppose the medium supports wave numbers \(k_\pm=2\pi n_\pm/\lambda_0\) for the two bases, with negligible absorption. After a distance \(L\), factor out the common phase \(e^{i(k_++k_-)L/2}\). The remaining Cartesian phasor is \(\hat x\cos\Delta-\hat y\sin\Delta\), where \(\Delta=(k_+-k_-)L/2\). Thus the polarisation angle in the fixed \(x,y\) plane is

\[\theta=\frac{\pi L}{\lambda_0}(n_- - n_+).\]

Equal transmissions of the two components preserve a linearly polarised output. Unequal absorption makes the circular amplitudes unequal and generally produces an ellipse. Rotating a linear analyser then finds a finite minimum, even for a perfectly coherent field. Residual intensity at the nominal extinction angle can also arise from stray light or imperfect polariser extinction, which must be measured independently.

Derive a magneto-optic eigenbasis

For axial magnetisation, a simple lossless transverse relative-permittivity block is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\epsilon&i g\\-i g&\epsilon\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) with real \(g\) at the frequency of observation. Acting on \((1,i)\) gives \((\epsilon-g)(1,i)\); acting on \((1,-i)\) gives \((\epsilon+g)(1,-i)\). For relative permeability near one, \(n_+^2=\epsilon-g\), \(n_-^2=\epsilon+g\). At small \(g\) their difference \(n_- - n_+\simeq g/\sqrt\epsilon\). Reversing magnetisation reverses the sign of the off-diagonal coefficient and interchanges the two eigenvalues. Both the existence of circular modes and the sign-reversal test now follow from a constitutive model.

If \(n_--n_+\) is proportional to magnetic field over a specified range, write \(\theta=VBL\), with Verdet constant \(V\) in rad/(T m). This is a measurable material and wavelength dependent coefficient. It is not determined by the mean refractive index alone. A strong nearby absorption line can give substantial frequency dependence and dichroism, invalidating a lossless narrow-band extrapolation.

Decide what confinement requires

Put a homogeneous dielectric with positive relative permittivity \(\epsilon_d\) above \(z=0\), a lossless dispersive metal of relative permittivity \(\epsilon_m(\omega)\) below it and take both permeabilities to be \(\mu_0\). Both \(\kappa\) must be positive real numbers for this bound, lossless mode. Substitution into the homogeneous wave equation gives

\[\kappa_j^2=\beta^2-\epsilon_j k_0^2,\]
\[k_0=\omega/c.\]

Continuity of tangential magnetic field without an externally imposed surface current identifies the two amplitudes \(H_0\). The metal's mobile charges are already included in its bulk permittivity. Introducing the same current a second time as an arbitrary sheet would describe another boundary. Continuity of normal electric displacement follows here from the same magnetic amplitude and common tangential wavenumber.

The sign that selects a material pair

Use \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=-i\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon_j\mathbf E\). Its \(x\) component is \(-\partial_zH_y\). Hence \(E_x=-i\partial_z H_y/(\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon_j)\). Continuity of this tangential electric field gives

\[\kappa_d/\epsilon_d=-\kappa_m/\epsilon_m.\]

No two positive real permittivities can satisfy that equation with two positive decay constants. Squaring and eliminating \(\kappa_j\) gives a candidate propagation constant.

\[\beta^2=k_0^2\frac{\epsilon_m\epsilon_d}{\epsilon_m+\epsilon_d}.\]

Check it in the unsquared equation. With positive dielectric permittivity the physical branch requires \(\epsilon_m<-\epsilon_d\). On this branch \(\beta>\sqrt{\epsilon_d}\,k_0\), so the mode lies outside the propagation cone of a plane wave in the dielectric. For transverse electric polarisation the corresponding condition replaces permittivities by permeabilities. With two ordinary positive permeabilities the sign requirement fails. Fields and the response of matter · 10. Infer charge discreteness from fluctuations in a steady photocurrent

A detector can deliver a perfectly steady mean current over a long interval while its charge per short exposure fluctuates. Derive both a counting prediction and its spectral version, then use a bandwidth-limited measurement to separate partition fluctuations from thermal and readout noise.

Start with what the counter actually reports

Let statistically independent detection events bring charge \(q\) at mean rate \(\nu\). Count events during a rectangular exposure of duration \(T\). Subdivide it into small bins of length \(\delta t\) in which a single event has probability \(\nu\delta t\) and two events have negligible probability compared with \(\delta t\). Independent Bernoulli bins have additive means and variances. Taking \(\delta t\) towards zero while \(T\) is fixed gives equal count mean and variance, both \(\nu T\). The integrated charge is \(Q=qN\), and the indicated average current is \(Q/T\).

\[I=q\nu,\]

Fields and the response of matter · 11. Why a nearby gate changes the speed law of a two-dimensional plasma

A current pulse excites a travelling density modulation in a thin conducting sheet. Move a metal gate towards the sheet and the shape of the dispersion curve changes. Rebuild the restoring electric field from the surface charge before using any oscillator analogy.

Solve the electrostatic problem for one Fourier mode

Place a two-dimensional electron sheet at \(z=0\) in a homogeneous dielectric of absolute permittivity \(\epsilon\). The equilibrium positive background is fixed. Let the electron number perturbation be \(\delta n\,e^{iqx-i\omega t}\) with \(q>0\), so the perturbation of charge per area is \(\sigma=-e\,\delta n\). Away from the sheet the potential solves \((\partial_z^2-q^2)\phi=0\). Decay on both sides selects \(A e^{-q|z|}\). Across zero, the normal field discontinuity gives

\[2\epsilon q A=\sigma.\]

Consequently the bare sheet has \(\phi(0)=\sigma/(2\epsilon q)\). The inverse power of \(q\) reflects the field extending a distance of order \(1/q\) away from the sheet.

Ground a parallel, perfectly conducting plane at \(z=d\). Place an image of opposite surface charge at \(2d\) to enforce zero potential at the conductor. The transverse exponential suppresses the contribution that travels from this image to the physical sheet. Linear superposition therefore gives

\[\phi(0)=\frac{-e\,\delta n}{2\epsilon q}(1-e^{-2qd}).\]

This result assumes that charge is free to flow into the gate from a ground reservoir and that the dielectric is homogeneous throughout the gap. A floating finite plate would require an additional global charge condition. The zero mode also needs its own electrostatic reference and is excluded from this derivation.

Combine continuity with the acceleration equation

Writing \(u\) for the sheet electron velocity along \(x\), number conservation gives

\[-i\omega\delta n+iqn_0u=0.\]

The electric force on a single electron is \(-eE_x=+ieq\phi(0)\), and the acceleration equation is

\[-i\omega m^*u=ieq\phi(0).\]

Solving for \(u\), substituting the potential and cancelling the common density perturbation gives

\[\omega^2(q)=\frac{n_0e^2q}{2\epsilon m^*}(1-e^{-2qd}).\]

Check dimensions using \(n_0\) as a number per square metre. A three-dimensional density in this formula would leave a spurious inverse length. In an ungated plane the frequency scales as \(\sqrt q\). Its group velocity \(d\omega/dq\) is half the phase velocity. When \(qd\) is small, the exponential has first-order difference \(2qd\) and the field is largely confined in the gate gap. Then \(\omega\simeq s q\) with \(s=\sqrt{n_0e^2d/(\epsilon m^*)}\). The field and inertia now behave like a distributed capacitance and kinetic inductance. Both waves are collective charge oscillations even though one has the same long-wavelength dispersion exponent as sound.

What does a single particle actually contribute?

Let the host and sphere have positive, frequency-independent relative permittivities \(h\) and \(s\) and let each sphere have radius \(a\). The imposed field near an isolated sphere is approximately uniform. The exterior potential is the sum of a uniform-field term and a dipole term proportional to \(\cos\theta/r^2\); the regular interior solution is linear in \(r\cos\theta\). Impose continuity of potential and of normal electric displacement at \(r=a\). The internal field then equals \(3hE_{\rm loc}/(s+2h)\). The induced dipole is its integrated excess polarisation relative to the host:

\[p=\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\epsilon_0(s-h)E_{\rm in}=4\pi\epsilon_0h a^3\frac{s-h}{s+2h}E_{\rm loc}.\]

A low-permittivity inclusion can have a dipole opposite to its local field. It has removed more polarisable host material. This sign need not imply any negative permittivity. Define the contrast \(b=(s-h)/(s+2h)\). The denominator includes two components of the host contribution from the exterior field. Simply averaging permittivities as if every particle experienced the applied field would ignore this boundary calculation.

Leading density and a local-field closure

For number density \(n\), the inclusion fraction is \(f=4\pi a^3n/3\). To first order in \(f\), replace \(E_{\rm loc}\) by the macroscopic field and add the dipoles. The predicted effective relative permittivity is \(h(1+3fb)\), up to terms of second and higher order. To obtain the Maxwell–Garnett approximation, imagine a spherical exclusion region containing a representative particle. Approximate the far distribution of particles by its smooth excess polarisation \(P=np\). Removing the polarised sphere produces a local field increment \(P/(3\epsilon_0h)\) along \(P\). Substituting \(E_{\rm loc}=E+P/(3\epsilon_0h)\) into \(P=n\alpha E_{\rm loc}\) gives

\[\frac{\epsilon_{\rm eff}-h}{\epsilon_{\rm eff}+2h}=fb,\]
\[\epsilon_{\rm eff}=h\,\frac{1+2fb}{1-fb}.\]

The closure resums some higher-order terms. It does not solve the exact many-particle boundary-value problem at arbitrary density. Short-range correlations, connected clusters, nonspherical particles and anisotropy can matter. Identifying a distinguished host and separated inclusions is an experimental assumption. Interchanging host and particle labels while preserving their volume fractions generally gives another microstructure and another prediction.

Match total current at a leaky interface

Take harmonic fields proportional to \(e^{i\omega t}\). In layer \(j\), Ohm's law and Maxwell's displacement current give \(J=Y_j E_j\) with \(Y_j=\sigma_j+i\omega\epsilon_j\). Use absolute permittivity in SI units. Total current is continuous across the interface. If the first layer occupies thickness fraction \(f\), the imposed mean field is

\[\bar E=fE_1+(1-f)E_2.\]

Eliminating the layer fields yields

\[Y_{\rm eff}=\frac{Y_1Y_2}{fY_2+(1-f)Y_1},\]
\[\tau=\frac{f\epsilon_2+(1-f)\epsilon_1}{f\sigma_2+(1-f)\sigma_1}.\]

The denominator is proportional to \(1+i\omega\tau\). A pole has appeared even though each material has frequency-independent conductivity and permittivity. The free interfacial charge per unit area is \(\epsilon_2 E_2-\epsilon_1 E_1\) with the normal from layer 1 to layer 2. At low frequency the less conducting layer takes a larger field. At high frequency the displacement currents determine the field sharing.

Separate dielectric storage from direct conduction

Fields and the response of matter · 14. From molecular dipoles to a dielectric susceptibility

Derive induced and orientational polarisation separately, then determine when temperature or local fields invalidate a constant dielectric coefficient.

Displace a bound charge before defining a material coefficient

Consider a neutral molecule whose negative charge cloud can move a small distance relative to its positive centre. Represent the restoring force along the applied field by a spring constant k and the displaced charge magnitude by q. Static balance gives kx=qE. The dipole moment is p=qx, so its proportionality coefficient is fixed by the restoring physics.

\[p=\alpha E,\]
\[\alpha=q^2/k.\]

The energy includes both deformation and the coupling to the external field. Minimising kx²/2−qxE gives the equilibrium displacement and the equilibrium energy −αE²/2. The factor one half follows from creating the moment while raising the field. A permanent, already established dipole instead has orientation energy −pE cosθ. The difference matters in a force gradient and in a thermodynamic partition sum.

Recall · Dipole moment, torque and field energy

Two charges ±q separated by vector d have p=qd directed toward positive charge. Expanding their potential energy in a slowly varying external potential gives U=p·∇V=−p·E. Vary the angle to obtain torque p×E. Vary position to obtain the gradient force. This expansion requires the charge separation to be small compared with the scale on which the external field changes.

For N independently responding molecules per unit volume, P=NαE if the local and macroscopic fields agree. Combining D=ε₀E+P with D=εE gives χₑ=Nα/ε₀. Each equality has an assumption. A dense solid can change the local field, and a large displacement can change the spring constant.

Count orientations with the correct angular measure

Now take a freely rotating permanent dipole of fixed magnitude p in thermal equilibrium at temperature T. Put u=cosθ and x=pE/(kBT). A thin ring on the orientation sphere has area 2π sinθ dθ. Consequently equal intervals in u have equal orientation measure. Boltzmann weighting gives the normalised density below.

\[Z_u(x)=\int_{-1}^{1}e^{xu}\,du={2\sinh x\over x},\]
\[f(u)={e^{xu}\over Z_u(x)}.\]

Differentiate the integral with respect to x. The derivative inserts u in the numerator, so the alignment is the logarithmic derivative of Z. Differentiating a second time gives a variance and therefore a nonnegative differential response.

\[\langle u\rangle={d\log Z_u\over dx}=\coth x-{1\over x}=L(x),\]
\[{d\langle u\rangle\over dx}=\langle u^2\rangle-\langle u\rangle^2.\]

Series expansion of sinh and cosh gives L(x)=x/3−x³/45+… . Thus the orientational susceptibility of a dilute gas is Np²/(3ε₀kBT). At large x the alignment approaches 1−1/x, saturating at P=Np. A linear law cannot remain valid in that limit.

\[P=N\left(\alpha+{p^2\over3k_BT}\right)E+O(E^3).\]

For p=3.0×10⁻³⁰ C m, T=300 K and E=10⁶ V/m, x=7.24×10⁻⁴. The mean orientation is only 2.41×10⁻⁴. A visibly nonzero macroscopic polarisation can therefore coexist with almost random individual molecular directions. Doubling E doubles the weak-field response, while doubling T halves its orientational part. The induced contribution in this elementary model stays fixed.

"""Orient freely rotating permanent dipoles and compare quadrature with the exact law."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from onesim.numerics.linear.matrices.dense_linear_algebra import symmetric_eigensystem

# The eigenvectors give Gaussian weights for the integral over u = cos(theta).
order = 32
indices = np.arange(1, order, dtype=float)
off_diagonal = indices / np.sqrt(4 * indices**2 - 1)
jacobi = np.diag(off_diagonal, 1) + np.diag(off_diagonal, -1)
nodes, vectors = symmetric_eigensystem(jacobi)
weights = 2 * vectors[0, :]**2
field_energy = np.linspace(0, 12, 121)  # x = p E / (k_B T)
mean_alignment = []
for x in field_energy:
    probability = weights * np.exp(x * (nodes - 1))
    mean_alignment.append(np.dot(nodes, probability) / probability.sum())
mean_alignment = np.asarray(mean_alignment)
exact = np.zeros_like(field_energy)
exact[1:] = 1 / np.tanh(field_energy[1:]) - 1 / field_energy[1:]
print(f"Maximum quadrature error: {np.max(abs(mean_alignment - exact)):.2e}")
for x in (0.2, 1.0, 3.0, 10.0):
    index = np.argmin(abs(field_energy - x))
    print(f"pE/kT = {x:4.1f}, <cos(theta)> = {mean_alignment[index]:.6f}, linear = {x/3:.6f}")
assert np.max(abs(mean_alignment - exact)) < 1e-12
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.8), layout="constrained")
axes[0].plot(field_energy, mean_alignment, label="OneSimulation quadrature")
axes[0].plot(field_energy, field_energy / 3, "--", label="Weak field")
axes[0].set(xlabel="Dipole energy / thermal energy", ylabel="Mean alignment", ylim=(0, 1.05))
axes[0].legend()
for x in (0, 1, 5):

axes[1].set(xlabel="cos(theta)", ylabel="Probability density")
axes[1].legend()
for axis in axes:
    axis.grid(alpha=.2)

In an isotropic, approximately cubic environment imagine a spherical cavity around the chosen molecule. Let n point from the centre into the material. The material normal at the cavity wall is −n, giving bound charge σb=−P·n. A surface patch contributes dE(0)=−σb n dΩ/(4πε₀). \[{\epsilon_r-1\over\epsilon_r+2}={N\alpha_{\rm eff}\over3\epsilon_0}.\]

Follow the ambiguity of the unit cell in a polar crystal

Take an infinite alternating chain of charges +q at na and −q at na+a/2, with one chain per cross-sectional area A. A neutral cell containing these two sites has moment −qa/2 and polarisation −q/(2A). Choose the equivalent negative ion at na−a/2 instead, keeping the same infinite crystal. A finite sample supplies the missing information. Its end charges depend on how it is cut and on whether charges are supplied at the surfaces. A bulk dipole-per-cell convention alone therefore does not determine the surface charge of an ionic crystal. In three dimensions the corresponding polarisation increment is qR/Ω for lattice vector R and cell volume Ω.
