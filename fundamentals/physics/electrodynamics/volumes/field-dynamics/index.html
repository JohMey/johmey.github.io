Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

Begin with a steady current and follow what changes when a source, a conductor or a field moves. Track energy and momentum with explicit system boundaries.

    Contents

    1. Building a magnetic field from a measured current

      A Hall probe beside a bent wire reports a direction and a magnitude. The current flowing past it is easy to measure, but a single current reading cannot specify the field unless the return path and geometry are also known. Starting with the local field equations, we will derive the integral law that makes those geometrical details calculable.

    2. The vector potential as an instrument for computing flux

      An experimenter can measure a flux change with a coil without measuring the magnetic field over every square millimetre of the coil area. The vector potential gives a corresponding computational tool. Its line integral determines flux, while the freedom to choose its gauge can turn a difficult boundary problem into a manageable one.

    3. Which features of a current distribution survive at a distance?

      A current loop just fits behind a probe mount. At a nearby point its size and placement matter. Far away the same winding behaves as though all that geometry were a single vector. Predicting when that replacement is accurate, and what remains when the vector cancels, requires an expansion of the source rather than an appeal to a field-line sketch.

    4. From microscopic moments to measured magnetisation

      A magnetised sample can make a compass turn without a power supply attached. The macroscopic theory assigns it bound currents in its bulk and on its surface. These are calculated sources for the magnetic field. We will reconstruct them from magnetic moments, predict signals from finite samples, and ask what an exterior field measurement can identify.

    5. Interfaces, demagnetising fields and the work of magnetisation

      Two samples made of the same material can respond very differently to an externally applied field if one is a long rod and the other is a thin disc. The induced moment depends on the internal field, which the magnetised sample helps create. The same distinction reappears when one tries to infer a force from stored magnetic energy.

    6. Motional emf and transformer emf in moving circuits

      Two laboratory demonstrations produce the same voltmeter trace. In one, a stationary loop sees a changing magnetic field. In the other, a conductor moves through a stationary field. The charges experience different local forces, yet under identifiable conditions both readings follow from the rate of change of magnetic flux.

    7. Flux linkage, coupled coils and magnetic coenergy

      A winding links flux through each turn. In two windings the current in either one can change the voltage in the other. A matrix of flux linkages describes this coupling, but predicting power or mechanical force requires specifying the current directions and the control conditions.

    8. How a magnetic field relaxes into a finite conductor

      A conducting sheet initially shields its interior from an applied magnetic step. Given time, a normal resistive metal admits a static tangential magnetic field. The same equations give a finite slab a discrete relaxation spectrum and a periodic drive a complex penetration depth. These are different initial and boundary conditions for one diffusion operator.

    9. Displacement current and the charging capacitor

      A wire feeds a capacitor whose dielectric blocks the transport of mobile charges between its plates. A magnetic probe near the gap still responds to charging. Resolving which surface a circulation law should use leads to displacement current, an electric field correction during a ramp, and an energy-flow test around the rim.

    10. Energy flow and mechanical force from vacuum fields

      An external support holds a charged capacitor plate or a current-carrying coil in place. One can infer the support force from fields just outside the body, without summing forces on every microscopic charge. A second surface integral tracks the energy feeding the same apparatus. Both methods start with Maxwell's equations, but they transport different conserved quantities.

    11. Field momentum, momentum exchange and the quasistatic limits

      A system can have static electric and magnetic fields with nonzero local electromagnetic momentum density. A circuit can also be electrically small while either induction or displacement current remains indispensable. Both puzzles become clearer when one follows the relevant conservation law and compares the scales of the discarded terms.

    12. Posing an electromagnetic evolution problem

      A solver cannot evolve 'a pulse in a box' from geometry and a time step alone. It needs material laws, compatible initial fields, a charge-conserving source, and enough boundary data to specify what enters the domain. The same bookkeeping is required when interpreting a real transient measurement.

    13. Why does a Helmholtz pair have a useful uniform region?

      A magnetometer averages the field over a finite sample. Derive the coil spacing that suppresses curvature and predict how rapidly the remaining calibration error grows away from the midpoint.

    14. From atomic motion to magnetic susceptibility

      Calculate the response of a bound orbit and of an ensemble of existing moments, then follow how interactions permit spontaneous magnetisation.

    15. The electric field of a rotating magnet

      Use charge balance and a surface matching calculation to predict a voltage around a rotating magnetic body, then close the mechanical and electrical power accounts.
