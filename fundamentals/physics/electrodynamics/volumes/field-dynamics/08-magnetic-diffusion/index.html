How a magnetic field relaxes into a finite conductor

A conducting sheet initially shields its interior from an applied magnetic step. Given time, a normal resistive metal admits a static tangential magnetic field. The same equations give a finite slab a discrete relaxation spectrum and a periodic drive a complex penetration depth. These are different initial and boundary conditions for one diffusion operator.

Identify the time derivative lost in the approximation

Let the conductor be stationary, Ohmic, uniform and isotropic with conductivity \(\sigma\), permeability \(\mu\) and permittivity \(\epsilon\). In the full Maxwell equations both \(\sigma\boldsymbol E\) and \(\epsilon\partial_t\boldsymbol E\) contribute to the curl of \(\boldsymbol H\). At a characteristic angular frequency \(\omega\), the ratio of the latter to the former is \(\omega\epsilon/\sigma\). For a good conductor at low frequencies this is very small. Neglect displacement current only in that constitutive region; a vacuum gap or a capacitor elsewhere can still carry significant displacement current. Ampère then gives

\[\boldsymbol E=(\nabla\times\boldsymbol B)/(\mu\sigma).\]

Substitution into Faraday and use of \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0\) yield

\[\partial_t\boldsymbol B=D\nabla^2\boldsymbol B,\]
\[D=1/(\mu\sigma).\]

The diffusion time for scale \(a\) is of order \(a^2/D\). Increasing conductivity reduces \(D\), because smaller electric fields can sustain stronger opposing currents. In an ideal mathematical conductor with \(\sigma\to\infty\), an initial flux can be frozen in this model. That is a different preparation from a superconductor expelling a field in equilibrium. Magnetic diffusion alone contains neither a thermodynamic superconducting transition nor London screening. At sufficiently short times the dropped displacement current also matters: an ideal step is an approximation to a finite-risetime drive, and the diffusion equation's nonzero Gaussian tails at arbitrarily large distances should not be used to infer a superluminal signal.

Obtain a transient similarity profile

Take a half-space \(x>0\) with field \(\boldsymbol B=B_z(x,t)\hat{\boldsymbol z}\); the surface is driven to \(B_s\) at \(t=0^+\), while initially the interior has zero field. Scaling \(x\) by \(\sqrt{Dt}\) suggests \(B_z/B_s=F(\eta)\), \(\eta=x/(2\sqrt{Dt})\). Compute \(\partial_tF=-\eta F'/(2t)\) and \(D\partial_x^2F=F''/(4t)\); hence

\[F''+2\eta F'=0.\]

Its first integral is

\[F'=Ce^{-\eta^2}.\]

Set \(F(0)=1,F(\infty)=0\) and use the Gaussian integral to obtain

\[F(\eta)=\operatorname{erfc}\eta.\]

The associated current \(J_y=-(1/\mu)\partial_xB_z\) is positive for this sign of surface field and has a Gaussian depth profile.

\[J_y(x,t)=\frac{B_s}{\mu\sqrt{\pi Dt}}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)},\]
\[\int_0^\infty J_y\,dx=\frac{B_s}{\mu}.\]

The current integral remains fixed as the profile spreads, even though the surface current density falls as \(t^{-1/2}\). A finite conductivity does not imply uniform current at every instant. For \(\sigma=5.8\times10^7\,\mathrm{S/m}\) and \(\mu=\mu_0\), \(D=0.01372\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). The point reaching half the surface field is

\[x=2\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(1/2)\sqrt{Dt}=0.9539\sqrt{Dt}.\]

At \(0.10\,\mathrm{ms}\) it is \(1.12\,\mathrm{mm}\) deep. At \(0.40\,\mathrm{ms}\), four times the time, it is twice as deep. Measuring several half-amplitude positions against time lets one fit \(D\) without calibrating the absolute magnetic field strength.

Satisfy both faces of a finite slab

Let the conductor occupy \(-a<x<a\). Impose the same sudden tangential field \(B_s\) at both faces. At long time the unique steady solution with these equal boundaries is uniform \(B_s\). Subtract this final value by defining the deficit \(u=1-B_z/B_s\). It satisfies homogeneous boundary values \(u(\pm a,t)=0\) and begins at one inside. Even symmetry permits cosine modes \(\cos(k_mx)\) with \(k_ma=(m+1/2)\pi\). Separating time gives the multiplier \(e^{-Dk_m^2t}\). Orthogonality on \((-a,a)\) gives squared norm \(a\) for each cosine and projection of the constant equal to \(2\sin(k_ma)/(ak_m)\). Thus

\[\frac{B_z(x,t)}{B_s}=1-\frac4\pi\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{2m+1}\cos\!\frac{(2m+1)\pi x}{2a}\,\exp\!\left[-(2m+1)^2t/\tau_0\right],\]
\[\tau_0=\frac{4a^2}{\pi^2D}.\]

The initial boundary jump is real in the idealisation; the series reproduces the interior initial value in the usual Fourier sense, while the imposed boundary value applies for \(t>0\). Later times damp the higher spatial harmonics most rapidly. At the centre and \(t=\tau_0\), the first term predicts

\[B/B_s=1-(4/\pi)e^{-1}=0.5316.\]

The next correction is \(4e^{-9}/(3\pi)=0.0000524\), so this is already accurate to within \(6\times10^{-5}\). At very early times many terms are needed because their cancellations encode a nearly field-free interior. A half-space similarity expression near each face can be more efficient there.

For a \(4.0\,\mathrm{mm}\)-thick copper slab \(a=2.0\,\mathrm{mm}\), giving \(\tau_0=0.118\,\mathrm{ms}\). At late times the centre deficit \(1-B/B_s\) approaches a single exponential with log slope \(-1/\tau_0\). Reaching 90 percent at the centre requires

\[t\simeq\tau_0\ln(4/(0.1\pi))=2.54\tau_0=0.300\,\mathrm{ms}.\]

The initial square-root penetration law cannot simply be extrapolated into this final regime, since the penetration fronts from both sides interact. Doubling full thickness at constant material quadruples the long relaxation time.

Compare a periodic solution and a surface impedance

Instead drive the half-space sinusoidally and adopt \(e^{i\omega t}\) peak phasors. Solving \(i\omega\widetilde B=D\,d^2\widetilde B/dx^2\) gives \(\widetilde B=B_se^{-(1+i)x/\delta}\) with the root \(\delta=\sqrt{2D/\omega}\) that decays towards infinity. The envelope falls as \(e^{-x/\delta}\), while phase lags the boundary by \(x/\delta\). The electrical surface field follows by differentiating, yielding \(\widetilde E_y=(1+i)\widetilde H_z/(\sigma\delta)\). The surface impedance in these orientations is \(Z_s=(1+i)/(\sigma\delta)\). Its positive real part measures dissipation, and the equal quadrature term records energy storage with this convention.

At \(f=1.0\,\mathrm{kHz}\) in the copper model, \(\delta=2.09\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(\operatorname{Re}Z_s=8.25\,\mu\Omega\). A tangential surface peak field \(H_s=1000\,\mathrm{A/m}\) gives mean incoming power density \(\operatorname{Re}Z_s|H_s|^2/2=4.13\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\). The same number follows from volume Joule heating. Integrate \(\sigma|\widetilde E_y|^2/2\) over depth; the squared envelope \(e^{-2x/\delta}\) integrates to \(\delta/2\). An experiment monitoring just the in-phase field can find a zero at \(x=\pi\delta/2\) while the full harmonic magnitude is still \(e^{-\pi/2}=0.208\) of the surface amplitude.

Use the solution to design a convergence test

A mesh across the finite slab can step \(B_j^{n+1}=B_j^n+r(B_{j+1}^n-2B_j^n+B_{j-1}^n)\), with \(r=D\Delta t/h^2\). A Fourier mode \(e^{ikjh}\) is multiplied by \(1-4r\sin^2(kh/2)\), so forward Euler is stable for \(0\le r\le1/2\). Above that bound a high-frequency grid mode can grow despite the physical diffusion being dissipative. For \(h=0.20\,\mathrm{mm}\) in the copper model, \(\Delta t\le1.46\,\mu\mathrm s\). Halving the mesh spacing demands quartering this explicit time step. An implicit scheme can overcome the same stability restriction but still needs small enough steps to resolve the measured transient.

Set both face values, initialise the interior once and record the centre at \(t=\tau_0\). Compare first against the series, then against a second mesh and time step. A mistaken use of full thickness in \(\tau_0\) would shift the inferred time by a factor four even if the code has converged perfectly. Independent checks include reflection symmetry about the slab centre, zero gradient at that centre, a bounded response between zero and \(B_s\) for the continuous problem, and ohmic heating that is nonnegative at every point. The latter can catch sign mistakes in an energy budget even though squaring \(J\) hides the sign of a local measured current.

Compare surface and centre in a periodically driven finite slab

At a fixed angular frequency a finite slab with equal tangential field on both faces has a different profile from a half-space. Write the phasor equation \(d^2\widetilde B/dx^2=k^2\widetilde B\), \(k=(1+i)/\delta\) for temporal dependence \(e^{i\omega t}\), and impose \(\widetilde B(\pm a)=B_s\). Even symmetry eliminates \(\sinh kx\), giving \(\widetilde B(x)=B_s\cosh(kx)/\cosh(ka)\). The centre amplitude is \(1/|\cosh[(1+i)a/\delta]|\) of the applied field, and its phase lag is the argument of that denominator. Both incoming penetration profiles must be combined as complex amplitudes satisfying boundary conditions; adding two decaying real envelopes is not a valid phasor solution.

The low-frequency limit has \(ka\ll1\), yielding \(B(0)/B_s\simeq1-(ka)^2/2=1-i(a/\delta)^2\). The leading correction is a phase lag, while the magnitude change starts at higher order. Thus a sensitive phase detector can pick up eddy-current effects before a large change of amplitude is visible. At \(a/\delta=1\), \(\cosh(1+i)=\cosh1\cos1+i\sinh1\sin1\simeq0.834+0.989i\), giving centre amplitude 0.773 and lag \(0.870\) radians. An isolated half-space viewed one skin depth in from a single face would have amplitude 0.368 and lag 1.00 radian. These are genuinely different field boundary conditions, not two definitions of skin depth.

The phasor of the current varies as \(\sinh(kx)\) and is odd. Current on one half of the slab runs opposite to that on the other; the net integrated current across both halves vanishes for equal exterior fields. That cancellation does not suppress losses, because local Joule heating is proportional to \(|J|^2\). In the thin-slab limit expand Faraday directly. If \(B_z(t)\) is nearly uniform,

\[\partial_x E_y=-\dot B_z.\]

Symmetry about the centre gives

\[E_y\simeq-x\dot B_z.\]

For a sinusoidal peak field \(B_s\), the mean volumetric loss averaged across thickness is \(\sigma\omega^2 B_s^2 a^2/6\). The \(a^2\) scaling at fixed total magnetic volume explains why dividing a core into electrically insulated thin laminations can reduce classical eddy losses when the applied field lies along the lamination faces. The thin-slab approximation, isolation between laminae and field orientation are essential parts of that conclusion.

For a material with \(\sigma=2.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{S/m}\), half-thickness \(0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\), sinusoidal peak field 0.10 T and frequency 50 Hz, the formula gives \(3.29\,\mathrm{W/m^3}\). If effective permeability were \(100\mu_0\), the skin depth at that frequency would be about \(5.03\,\mathrm{mm}\), so the thin-slab ordering is internally consistent for these model numbers. Hysteresis or anomalous dynamic losses can still dominate a measured sample. Halving full lamination thickness at fixed material volume should quarter only this classical contribution, not every loss mechanism in the test fixture.

A thin conducting cylindrical shell gives a single time constant

A different finite system exposes how geometry changes a diffusion transient. Consider a long cylindrical shell of radius \(a\) and wall thickness \(w\ll a\) surrounding vacuum. Assume the skin depth in the wall exceeds \(w\) on the important time scales, so current is approximately uniform across the wall, and the solenoid producing an approximately uniform external axial field is sufficiently long. An azimuthal sheet current \(K_\varphi\) produces an additional axial field inside and negligible external field in the long-cylinder approximation. The boundary condition is

\[B_{\rm in}=B_{\rm ext}+\mu_0K_\varphi.\]

Faraday's law around the shell gives

\[2\pi a E_\varphi=-\pi a^2\dot B_{\rm in}.\]

The sheet obeys \(K_\varphi=\sigma w E_\varphi\). Combining the three relations gives

\[B_{\rm in}+\tau_s\dot B_{\rm in}=B_{\rm ext},\]
\[\tau_s=\mu_0\sigma w a/2 .\]

For a step from zero to \(B_s\) the field inside grows as \(B_s(1-e^{-t/\tau_s})\). For a sinusoid the interior phasor transfer is \(1/(1+i\omega\tau_s)\). This model has a single time constant instead of a slab's infinite relaxation spectrum because the wall thickness has been collapsed into one azimuthal sheet-current degree of freedom; magnetic energy is stored mainly throughout the bore. Take copper with \(a=0.030\,\mathrm m,w=0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) to obtain \(\tau_s=0.109\,\mathrm{ms}\). At its half-power frequency \(f=1/(2\pi\tau_s)\simeq1.46\,\mathrm{kHz}\), the copper skin depth is about 1.73 mm, comfortably larger than the specified wall. At 100 times this frequency the uniform-wall assumption is much weaker.

This result also suggests a discriminating experiment. Compare shells made of the same material with fixed wall thickness and different bore radius. The leading screening time grows linearly with radius. Compare solid sheets of different normal penetration thickness in the earlier slab experiment and their time grows with thickness squared. Either system can display a late exponential, so an observed straight tail in a semilog trace does not alone reveal which diffusion path limits the response. The thickness and radius dependences, together with the phase response and the known current paths, provide independent evidence. A longitudinal insulating slit in the cylindrical shell interrupts its closed eddy-current path, substantially altering the ideal shielding despite leaving most of the metal present.

Account for every joule admitted by a magnetic step

A conducting half-space initially has zero internal field. At \(t=0\) a source clamps the surface induction to \(B_s\hat z\). Let \(x>0\) measure inward distance and \(D=1/(\mu\sigma)\). The previously derived field profile is \(B_z=B_s\,{\rm erfc}[x/(2\sqrt{Dt})]\). An observer sees an ever-deepening field front even though the surface induction subsequently remains constant. Someone must provide both the magnetic field energy and the Joule heat generated by the induced current. The appropriate bookkeeping is Poynting's theorem, with positive surface power defined into the conductor.

In this geometry Ampère's law gives

\[J_y=-(1/\mu)\partial_x B_z.\]

Differentiating the error function produces a Gaussian current density \(J_y=B_s[\mu\sqrt{\pi Dt}]^{-1}\exp[-x^2/(4Dt)]\). Ohm's law sets \(E_y=J_y/\sigma\). At the surface the Poynting vector \(E_yH_z\hat x\) points into the material and has magnitude

\[\frac{P_{\rm in}}{A}=\frac{B_s^2}{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{D}{\pi t}}.\]

The initial mathematical step creates an integrable square-root singularity in the surface power. Holding \(B_s\) fixed still requires the source to deliver power for \(t>0\). Faraday's law relates the surface electric field to the changing flux below the surface. Its value falls with time because the depth derivative of the induction spreads and weakens. A physical source with a finite rise time smooths the initial singularity and sets the earliest time for which the ideal step is a useful approximation.

To calculate heat production, square the full depth-dependent current and integrate \(J_y^2/\sigma\) from zero to infinity. The squared Gaussian has exponent \(-x^2/(2Dt)\), and its integral on the half-line is \(\sqrt{\pi Dt/2}\). The result is

\[P_{\rm heat}/A=(B_s^2/\mu)\sqrt{D/(2\pi t)}.\]

Consequently the heat fraction of instantaneous input is \(1/\sqrt{2}\), about \(70.7\%\). The rest, \(1-1/\sqrt{2}\), supplies an increasing store of magnetic field energy. Both powers decay as \(t^{-1/2}\); their integrals up to a finite time increase as \(\sqrt t\). These fractions belong to the step-driven semi-infinite problem. They do not describe steady sinusoidal loss or a finite specimen after the diffusion front traverses it.

With \(B_s=10\,{\rm mT}\), copper conductivity \(5.8\times10^7\,{\rm S/m}\), and permeability close to \(\mu_0\), the diffusion coefficient is \(0.0137\,{\rm m^2/s}\). At \(100\,\mu{\rm s}\), the incoming power is about \(526\,{\rm W/m^2}\). Joule heating accounts for \(372\,{\rm W/m^2}\), and field energy is increasing at about \(154\,{\rm W/m^2}\). Integration from the ideal step to this time gives \(2tP_{\rm in}(t)/A=0.105\,{\rm J/m^2}\), split into approximately \(0.0744\,{\rm J/m^2}\) of heat and \(0.0308\,{\rm J/m^2}\) of stored field energy. At that time \(2\sqrt{Dt}\) is about \(2.34\,{\rm mm}\). Applying these numbers to a foil much thinner than a millimetre would use the half-space solution beyond its range.

A finite slab of thickness \(d\) is driven from both faces by the same step. How much magnetic energy is eventually stored, and why must a decaying input replace the half-space power law?

Work through the solution

At long times, Ampère's law allows a uniform induction \(B_s\) with zero current and the prescribed values on both faces. Its stored magnetic energy is \(dB_s^2/(2\mu)\) per unit footprint, independent of conductivity. With \(d=5.0\,{\rm mm}\) and the parameters above this is about \(0.199\,{\rm J/m^2}\). At early times each face behaves approximately as an independent half-space. Once their diffusion profiles overlap, extended use of that approximation would predict unlimited energy accumulation in a finite volume with bounded field magnitude. The finite-slab modes replace the late algebraic law by exponential decay of the current and the surface electric field.

What is the total heat in a finite linear slab after full penetration if both surface inductions are clamped to the same ideal step?

Work through the solution

Integrate the inward surface Poynting power over all time. With symmetry about the midplane, Faraday's law on the upper half relates the magnitude of the surface electric field to \(d[\int_0^{d/2}B\,dx]/dt\). The surface magnetic field after the step is the constant \(B_s/\mu\). Including both faces gives total input energy per area \(B_s^2d/\mu\). Half remains stored, so half has been dissipated as heat, or about \(0.199\,{\rm J/m^2}\) in the example. The integrated fraction one half over the complete finite transient differs from the early-time half-space fraction. This energy identity provides a stringent check of a discretised slab calculation because it tests the surface flux, the volume heating and their time integration together.

Tutorial on extracting material parameters

The late centre deficit of a slab of full thickness 6.0 mm falls by a factor \(e\) in \(0.60\,\mathrm{ms}\). Find \(D\), and estimate \(\sigma\) if \(\mu=2\mu_0\).

Work through the solution

Using \(a=3.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) gives

\[D=4a^2/(\pi^2\tau_0)=0.00608\,\mathrm{m^2/s}.\]

Then

\[\sigma=1/(\mu D)=6.55\times10^7\,\mathrm{S/m}.\]

A transient alone determines \(\mu\sigma\); the independent permeability assumption is essential.

At fixed frequency the signal amplitude at one depth is 10 percent of the surface amplitude in a periodic half-space experiment. Find the phase lag there and predict the new amplitude when frequency drops to one quarter.

Work through the solution

The depth is \(x=\delta\ln10\), so the lag is 2.303 radians. Quartering frequency doubles the skin depth; the ratio becomes

\[e^{-(\ln10)/2}=0.3162.\]

Treating the original 10 percent as an intensity ratio instead of a field ratio would produce a different inferred depth.

A step field applied at \(t=0\) is removed again at \(t=T\). Obtain the field after removal and explain why it need not vanish immediately in the interior.

Work through the solution

By linearity superpose a positive step and a delayed negative step. For \(t>T\) the normalised half-space profile is \(\operatorname{erfc}[x/(2\sqrt{Dt})]-\operatorname{erfc}[x/(2\sqrt{D(t-T)})]\). It vanishes at the driven boundary but remains positive at interior points where the later disturbance has not substantially diffused. The induced currents subsequently relax and dissipate energy.
