Energy flow and mechanical force from vacuum fields

An external support holds a charged capacitor plate or a current-carrying coil in place. One can infer the support force from fields just outside the body, without summing forces on every microscopic charge. A second surface integral tracks the energy feeding the same apparatus. Both methods start with Maxwell's equations, but they transport different conserved quantities.

Derive a local energy balance before choosing the surface

In vacuum write \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol E=-\partial_t\boldsymbol B\) and \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol B/\mu_0=\boldsymbol J+\epsilon_0\partial_t\boldsymbol E\). Dot the second equation with \(\boldsymbol E\) and the first with \(\boldsymbol B/\mu_0\). The vector identity \(\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B)=\boldsymbol B\cdot\nabla\times\boldsymbol E-\boldsymbol E\cdot\nabla\times\boldsymbol B\) converts their difference into

\[\partial_t u+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol S=-\boldsymbol J\cdot\boldsymbol E,\]
\[u=\frac{\epsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0},\]
\[\boldsymbol S=\frac{\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B}{\mu_0}.\]

Integrate over a volume held fixed in the chosen frame. Outward power \(\oint\boldsymbol S\cdot d\boldsymbol a>0\) represents energy leaving; positive \(\int\boldsymbol J\cdot\boldsymbol E\,dV\) represents electromagnetic work on matter. They do not have to agree instantaneously: their sum plus the rate of increase of contained field energy is zero with these sign conventions. In matter, one can include the total microscopic currents inside the vacuum-form balance, or use an appropriate macroscopic constitutive energy description. A vacuum integration surface enclosing the whole body is particularly useful for a force calculation because it avoids assigning momentum to material polarisation separately.

Consider a long straight resistor with radius \(a\), length \(L\), uniform current along \(+z\) and voltage drop \(V\) in that direction. Immediately outside its curved face the tangential electric field is \(V/L\) under the ideal uniform model, and \(H_\varphi=I/(2\pi a)\). Their product makes a radial inward \(\boldsymbol S\). Integrating over \(2\pi aL\) gives \(VI\), equal to the internal \(I^2R\) in steady state. At \(V=4.0\,\mathrm V\), \(I=0.20\,\mathrm A\), \(a=1.0\,\mathrm{mm},L=0.10\,\mathrm m\), the ideal side flux density is \(1273\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\) and the total is \(0.80\,\mathrm W\). A directed charge drift down the resistor does not imply that its supply of energy travels entirely down the same path.

Convert the Lorentz force into field derivatives

The microscopic force density is \(\boldsymbol f=\rho\boldsymbol E+\boldsymbol J\times\boldsymbol B\). Eliminate charge with \(\rho=\epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol E\) and current with Ampère–Maxwell. The current term contains \(-\epsilon_0\partial_t\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B\). Add and subtract the missing piece to make \(-\partial_t(\epsilon_0\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B)\); replace the introduced \(\boldsymbol E\times\partial_t\boldsymbol B\) by \(-\boldsymbol E\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol E\) using Faraday. The spatial terms regroup with \((\boldsymbol F\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol F=\nabla(F^2/2)-\boldsymbol F\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol F)\). The result in Cartesian components is

\[f_i=\sum_j\partial_j T_{ij}-\partial_t g_i,\]
\[T_{ij}=\epsilon_0(E_iE_j-\tfrac12\delta_{ij}E^2)+\mu_0^{-1}(B_iB_j-\tfrac12\delta_{ij}B^2),\]
\[\boldsymbol g=\epsilon_0\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B=\boldsymbol S/c^2.\]

For a body enclosed by a fixed vacuum surface \(S\), \(F_i=\oint T_{ij}n_j\,dA-d(\int g_i dV)/dt\) if the enclosed Lorentz force is entirely on that body. The traction \(\boldsymbol T\cdot\boldsymbol n\) has units of pressure; the field momentum inside has units of \(\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\). The tensor convention used here makes \(-T_{ij}\) the outward flux density of \(i\)-momentum along \(j\) in the conservation equation. Texts that call this outward flux the stress can print the opposite sign. Test the convention on a simple physical force before interpreting a long component sum.

Obtain the attractive pressure on a conducting plate

Take a small vacuum enclosure straddling the surface of an ideal conductor in electrostatic equilibrium. Inside it \(\boldsymbol E=0\); just outside \(\boldsymbol E=E_n\boldsymbol n\). Tangential field vanishes. The traction from the exterior face is \(\epsilon_0E_n^2\boldsymbol n/2\); the other thin faces give no limiting contribution. For opposed charged plates this force draws each plate towards the facing gap. Equivalently, a surface charge \(\sigma=\epsilon_0E_n\) experiences the average of the one-sided fields, \(\boldsymbol E_{\rm acting}=E_n\boldsymbol n/2\). Using the outside field alone in \(\sigma\boldsymbol E\) would count the sheet's own field and give twice the measured force.

With area \(0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}\), air gap \(0.50\,\mathrm{mm}\) and voltage \(250\) V, \(E=5.0\times10^5\,\mathrm{V/m}\), pressure is \(1.11\,\mathrm{Pa}\) and force magnitude \(0.0111\,\mathrm N\). Holding voltage fixed while doubling the gap quarters both pressure and force. If the source is disconnected first, the initial free charge stays fixed and so does the pressure in the ideal large-plate limit. These two protocols agree at the starting configuration and immediately diverge when a plate moves. Neglecting the source when differentiating the fixed-voltage energy \(\tfrac12 C V^2\) makes a wrong force sign because \(C\) changes during the displacement.

Resolve magnetic tension and side pressure on a surface

For a pure magnetic field of magnitude \(B\) along \(z\), the vacuum stress tensor is diagonal with entries \((-B^2/2\mu_0,-B^2/2\mu_0,+B^2/2\mu_0)\). An element whose outward normal is parallel to the field feels positive normal traction in that direction in the tensor convention, while a normal perpendicular to the field gives negative normal traction. For a magnet pole face with a nearly normal gap field and negligible fringe fields elsewhere, integrating the appropriate vacuum surface gives an attraction across the gap of magnitude \(AB^2/(2\mu_0)\). For \(A=2.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and \(B=0.20\) T this is \(3.18\,\mathrm N\). The equal and opposite reaction belongs to the rest of the closed apparatus.

An oblique field produces shear. At a plane with \(\boldsymbol n=\hat{\boldsymbol z}\), \(\boldsymbol B=B_x\hat{\boldsymbol x}+B_z\hat{\boldsymbol z}\), one obtains \(t_x=B_x B_z/\mu_0\) and \(t_z=(B_z^2-B_x^2)/(2\mu_0)\). For \(B_x=0.020\) T, \(B_z=0.050\) T the respective values are \(796\) Pa and \(836\) Pa. The sign of the shear reverses when only one component reverses, unlike the pure normal pressure. A single scalar value \(B^2/(2\mu_0)\) cannot describe a general loaded face. The surface here is a calculation tool inside vacuum; a proposed net force on a body must still include all the other faces of the enclosure.

Test a radiation-pressure experiment using both integrals

A plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum has transverse electric and magnetic fields with \(E=cB\), so its instantaneous energy density is \(\epsilon_0E^2\). The Poynting magnitude is \(cu\), and the momentum density magnitude is \(u/c\). A normally incident pulse of energy \(U\) carries momentum \(U/c\) towards an absorbing surface. If fully reflected along the reverse direction it changes the field momentum by \(2U/c\), giving twice the impulse to the mirror. For a continuous beam of average intensity \(I\) on a perfectly absorbing target, pressure is \(I/c\); on a perfect reflector it is \(2I/c\). At \(I=600\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\) these are \(2.00\) and \(4.00\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}\). The lower pressure scale relative to the static capacitor example reflects an energy divided by a very large propagation speed.

A pulse striking a surface need not produce its final impulse from the stress term alone at every intermediate time. If the integration box includes part of an incoming or outgoing pulse, \(\int\boldsymbol g\,dV\) changes. Select a large time interval over which no pulse is present inside at its beginning or end. Then the integrated field-momentum derivative vanishes, and the mechanical impulse equals the time integral of surface traction. Alternatively use a smaller interval and calculate both terms explicitly. Independence of the final impulse from the box location and time window, when all terms are included, is a strong numerical test. It detects spurious forces from artificial absorbing boundaries or inconsistent subtraction of the incoming background.

A standing wave tests energy and momentum at the same surface

A mirror terminating a one-dimensional vacuum beam provides a case in which the cycle-averaged power entering an ideal mirror vanishes but the mechanical force remains finite. Let \(E_i\) be the incident peak amplitude polarised along \(y\), and \(B_i=E_i/c\) in the appropriate \(z\) direction. At an ideal conducting plane the reflected tangential electric field cancels the incident field. The corresponding reflected magnetic field adds there because reversing propagation reverses the sign between \(E\) and \(B\). The total magnetic peak is \(2E_i/c\), while the total tangential electric field on the surface is zero. Hence the normal instantaneous Poynting vector at the mirror vanishes. There is no energy deposition in a stationary lossless perfect conductor.

The vacuum magnetic stress on the mirror surface still has magnitude \(B^2/(2\mu_0)\) at each instant. Averaging its sinusoidal square gives \(\langle p\rangle=(2E_i/c)^2/(4\mu_0)=\epsilon_0 E_i^2=2I_i/c\), since the incident mean intensity is \(\epsilon_0cE_i^2/2\). A reflector can therefore experience twice the absorbing radiation pressure while dissipating no power. This does not imply a free mechanical power supply: holding a mirror fixed means its speed and the mechanical power \(\boldsymbol F\cdot\boldsymbol u\) are zero even when its support carries a continuous force. If the mirror moves significantly, the Doppler shift and work on it require an altered reflected field.

Away from the mirror the incident and reflected waves store electric and magnetic energy in spatially alternating regions. At a node of the electric field the magnetic field can be maximal. One quarter wavelength away there is an electric antinode and a magnetic node. The electric field there reaches its temporal maximum a quarter period after the magnetic maximum at the first position. The instantaneous normal Poynting vector generally oscillates and its cycle mean is zero in the lossless standing wave. A detector resolving only the cycle-averaged flux could incorrectly infer that the cavity is empty. A magnetic loop and an electric dipole probe at the same location would give complementary indications of stored energy. They have different coupling and must be calibrated before their raw voltages are compared.

Now consider partial reflection with intensity reflectance \(\mathcal R\), transmittance \(\mathcal T\) and absorptance \(\mathcal A=1-\mathcal R-\mathcal T\), with vacuum incident and exit regions and no additional scattering channels. At normal incidence the net incident-minus-outgoing momentum flow gives \(\langle p\rangle=(1+\mathcal R-\mathcal T)I_i/c=(2\mathcal R+\mathcal A)I_i/c\), while absorbed heating per area is \(\mathcal A I_i\). If \(I_i=300\,\mathrm{W/m^2},\mathcal R=0.60,\mathcal T=0.30\), the material absorbs \(30\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\) and the pressure is \(1.30\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}\). Measuring absorbed power alone would account for only \(0.10\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}\); the reversed momentum of the reflected fraction supplies the remainder. If transmitted light emerges at an angle or into a liquid, momentum components and material forces must be included with a surface appropriate to that experiment.

Compare stress and virtual work on a finite electrostatic source

A conductor held at zero potential occupies the lower half-space. Place a point charge \(q\) a height \(a\) above it. The image method gives the exterior field by adding an opposite charge at \(-a\); the conductor's interior field vanishes. Immediately above the conductor, the normal electric field has magnitude \(E_n(s)=|q|a/[2\pi\epsilon_0(s^2+a^2)^{3/2}]\) at radial distance \(s\). Its square gives a nonuniform surface attraction \(\epsilon_0 E_n^2/2\). Integrating it over rings on the full plane checks the force calculation without applying a singular self-field to the point charge.

Specifically, the total attractive force on the conductor has magnitude \(q^2a^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\int_0^\infty s(s^2+a^2)^{-3}ds\). Substituting \(v=s^2+a^2\) gives \(1/(4a^4)\) for the integral. The net force is \(q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a^2)\), exactly the Coulomb force between the real charge and the mathematical image. For \(q=1.0\,\mathrm{nC},a=20\,\mathrm{mm}\), this is \(5.62\,\mu\mathrm N\). The normal pressure is concentrated near the charge's projection even though the induced surface charge distribution extends indefinitely. Integrating only out to radius \(b\) captures a force fraction \(1-a^4/(a^2+b^2)^2\). Already at \(b=a\) one collects 75 percent of the force.

The electrostatic mechanical potential for the real charge relative to the grounded conductor is \(-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a)\), obtained by integrating the attractive force with respect to \(a\). A tempting use of the real-charge–image pair energy \(-q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0a)\) doubles that answer because the image is an induced representation and moves as the real charge moves. The correct assembly work is half \(q\phi_{\rm induced}\) for linear grounded boundary response: as the real charge is built up from zero, its induced potential grows linearly with it. This factor shares its origin with the half in electrostatic field energy, while the force on a test charge already present is the full \(q\boldsymbol E_{\rm induced}\). The surface pressure, image force and derivative of mechanical potential therefore agree when each calculation counts the experiment's degrees of freedom correctly.

Derive the compression of a straight current column

Consider a long nonmagnetic round wire or an ideal cylindrical conducting liquid column of radius \(a\), carrying a uniform axial current \(I\). End regions and any imposed external magnetic field are well removed from the section of interest. For a solid wire the stresses needed to maintain equilibrium depend on elastic properties. A simple fluid model allows us to calculate a scalar pressure difference directly from the magnetic body force. This example distinguishes the total force on a body from substantial internal stresses whose vector sum vanishes by symmetry.

In cylindrical coordinates the current density is \(J_z=I/(\pi a^2)\). Applying Ampère's law to an interior circle of radius \(s\) encloses the fraction \(s^2/a^2\) of the current and gives \(B_\phi(s)=\mu_0Is/(2\pi a^2)\). The radial Lorentz force density is \(f_s=-J_zB_\phi\) because \(\hat z\times\hat\phi=-\hat s\). Consequently the magnetic force on a small volume is inward everywhere except on the axis, where the local field vanishes. A static fluid satisfies \(0=-dp/ds+f_s\), so its radial pressure gradient must be negative. Integrating from the axis outwards gives

\[p(s)-p(a)=\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi^2a^4}(a^2-s^2),\]
\[p(0)-p(a)=\frac{B_\phi(a)^2}{\mu_0}.\]

Pressure at the centre must exceed pressure at the rim to balance the inward force. There is no factor one half in this centre-to-rim result. The expression \(B^2/(2\mu_0)\) often described as a magnetic pressure is a local Maxwell surface stress in vacuum. Here the field permeates the current-bearing material, and the interior magnetic force is distributed continuously through its volume. The stress on a single outer surface is insufficient to read off the interior fluid pressure profile. Likewise, integrating the radial vector force around a full circular cross section yields zero net translational force, while the scalar pressure difference remains finite.

With \(I=20\,{\rm A}\) and \(a=1.0\,{\rm mm}\), the edge induction is \(4.0\,{\rm mT}\), giving a centre-to-rim pressure difference of \(12.7\,{\rm Pa}\). Doubling the current quadruples the pressure difference; doubling the radius at fixed current reduces it to one quarter. Now consider a thin cylindrical current sheet of the same radius carrying the same total current. Its interior field vanishes, and the exterior field just outside the sheet equals the former edge field. The surface current density \(I/(2\pi a)\) experiences the average of the fields on its two sides. The resulting inward surface force has magnitude \(B_{\rm out}^2/(2\mu_0)=6.37\,{\rm Pa}\). The fields outside the two sources agree, yet their internal force distributions differ.

A practical test can exploit the square-law dependence of stress. If \(I(t)=I_{\rm pk}\cos\omega t\), and the magnetic profile follows the current quasistatically with negligible skin effect, the Lorentz force varies as \(\cos^2\omega t=[1+\cos(2\omega t)]/2\). A pressure or strain sensor can detect a steady signal and a component at twice the current frequency. The actual displacement follows the mechanical response and may resonate or shift in phase. An added external dc field perpendicular to the current gives a force at the current frequency, distinguishable by its angular pattern as well as its frequency.

If the conductor has the same externally measured field at its surface, how could one check whether the uniform-current pressure profile is applicable?

Work through the solution

Estimate the skin depth at the highest significant driving frequency and compare it with the radius. A skin depth comfortably larger than the radius favours a nearly uniform current in a homogeneous conductor far from nearby returns. A much smaller skin depth confines the current to the surface. A measurement of ac resistance or an interior-field measurement in a suitable analogue provides an independent check. A dc current can still be distributed unevenly in a conductor with nonuniform conductivity. Surface field alone measures total enclosed current and cannot distinguish these internal distributions.

A fluid column has an external mechanical pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\) and a uniform volume current all the way to the boundary. Must one add \(B(a)^2/(2\mu_0)\) as an extra fluid pressure jump at the rim?

Work through the solution

In the ideal nonmagnetic volume-current example no singular surface current is present. The tangential induction is continuous just across the rim, so the normal magnetic Maxwell stresses immediately outside and inside are equal there. Ignoring surface tension, other surface forces and fluid motion, the fluid pressure at the rim matches the external mechanical pressure. The inward volume force then builds the pressure toward the centre. In a model with a genuine surface current, the fields on the two sides differ and the magnetic stress jump is real. Checking which current distribution is assumed prevents counting an exterior magnetic traction twice.

Tutorial on mechanical checks

A lossless test mirror intercepts a pulse of energy \(3.0\,\mathrm{mJ}\) at normal incidence. It reflects 75 percent and transmits the remainder in the same direction in vacuum. Calculate its impulse and contrast the result if the remaining quarter is absorbed instead.

Work through the solution

Initial momentum is \(U/c\). The outgoing net momentum is \((0.25-0.75)U/c=-0.50U/c\), so impulse is \(1.50U/c=1.50\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,s}\). If the unreflected portion is absorbed, the outgoing momentum is \(-0.75U/c\) and impulse rises to \(1.75U/c=1.75\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,s}\). Absorption changes the energy budget and transfers its own share of incident momentum.

The fields at the opposite sides of an ideal current sheet are \(+B_0\hat{\boldsymbol x}\) and \(-B_0\hat{\boldsymbol x}\). Is its own field sufficient to exert a net transverse force on the sheet? What changes in a uniform additional field also along \(x\)?

Work through the solution

The two self-field magnitudes are equal, so their opposite vacuum-face tractions cancel. An external field changes the total magnitudes differently: the pressure difference is \([(B_{\rm ext}+B_0)^2-(B_{\rm ext}-B_0)^2]/(2\mu_0)=2B_{\rm ext}B_0/\mu_0\) in magnitude with orientation set by the actual sheet current. Since \(|K|=2B_0/\mu_0\), this equals \(|\boldsymbol K\times\boldsymbol B_{\rm ext}|\).

A simulation reports an outward energy flux of 1.2 W and positive work on matter of 0.30 W in a closed fixed region with no other sources there. What must happen to its contained field energy over a 2.0 ms interval if the rates stay constant?

Work through the solution

The rate is \(-1.2-0.30=-1.50\,\mathrm W\), so stored energy must fall by \(3.0\,\mathrm{mJ}\). A steady-state claim for the same numbers would violate Poynting's balance unless an additional input or change of sign were identified.
