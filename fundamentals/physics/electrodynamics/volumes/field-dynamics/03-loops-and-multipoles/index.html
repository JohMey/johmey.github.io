Which features of a current distribution survive at a distance?

A current loop just fits behind a probe mount. At a nearby point its size and placement matter. Far away the same winding behaves as though all that geometry were a single vector. Predicting when that replacement is accurate, and what remains when the vector cancels, requires an expansion of the source rather than an appeal to a field-line sketch.

Expand the kernel of a localised source

Take a bounded steady current distribution of size \(a\) near the origin, with observation distance \(r\gg a\). The Coulomb-gauge potential is \(\boldsymbol A=(\mu_0/4\pi)\int\boldsymbol J(\boldsymbol r')/|\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol r'|\,dV'\). Taylor expand the scalar kernel about the observation point while holding the source coordinates fixed. Since \(\partial_i(1/r)=-r_i/r^3\), the first two terms are \(1/r+\boldsymbol r'\cdot\boldsymbol r/r^3\). Terms one order farther involve quadratic products \(r'_ir'_j\) and second derivatives of \(1/r\). For a source entirely inside a sphere of radius \(a\) the expansion is systematically useful beyond that sphere; a probe near a wire can violate the required scale separation even at an apparently large distance from the centre of the apparatus.

The leading vector coefficient would be \(\int J_i\,dV'\). But \(J_i=\partial'_j(r'_i J_j)\) for a divergence-free steady source. Integrating converts it to an outer surface term that vanishes for localised currents. For a line loop the same fact reads \(\oint d\boldsymbol\ell'=0\). A finite source with a remote return omitted can give a spurious nonzero leading term. The disappearance of the \(1/r\) magnetostatic potential here follows from charge conservation and closed currents. A radiating time-dependent source is governed by a retarded integral with a different far-field expansion.

Identify the magnetic moment without assuming a circular loop

Define \(T_{ij}=\int r'_i J_j\,dV'\). The integral of \(\partial'_k(r'_ir'_jJ_k)\) is zero at large distance, giving \(T_{ij}+T_{ji}=0\). Thus only its antisymmetric part survives, and three independent components remain. Define \(\boldsymbol m=\frac12\int\boldsymbol r'\times\boldsymbol J\,dV'\). Contracting its components with the observation vector converts the first surviving potential into

\[\boldsymbol A_{\rm dip}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\boldsymbol m\times\boldsymbol r}{r^3},\]
\[\boldsymbol B_{\rm dip}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}[3(\boldsymbol m\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol r})\hat{\boldsymbol r}-\boldsymbol m]\quad(r>0).\]

To take this curl, apply the product rule to \(r^{-3}(\boldsymbol m\times\boldsymbol r)\). The curl of the second factor is \(2\boldsymbol m\), while \(\nabla r^{-3}=-3\boldsymbol r/r^5\). The vector triple product in the other term leaves the radial projection and subtracts three copies of the moment term, giving the expression above. It describes exterior points of the actual source; replacing the source by an ideal point dipole at its own location needs distributional terms. The moment is origin-independent for a closed current because shifting the origin adds a constant vector crossed with \(\int\boldsymbol J\,dV'=0\).

For a planar loop \(\boldsymbol m=(I/2)\oint\boldsymbol r'\times d\boldsymbol\ell'=I\boldsymbol a\), where the oriented area follows from projecting onto each Cartesian plane. For a polygon one may sum oriented triangles from any chosen origin; the edges to the origin cancel between adjacent triangles. A nonplanar closed wire still has an oriented projected area vector, although it has no unique scalar enclosed area. Stacking \(N\) identical turns multiplies the vector by \(N\). This definition makes a measured moment a current times a geometrical area, with units \(\mathrm{A\,m^2}=\mathrm{J/T}\).

Use distance and orientation to determine a moment

A 40-turn circular winding of radius \(15\,\mathrm{mm}\) carries \(0.20\,\mathrm A\). Its moment is

\[40(0.20)\pi(0.015)^2=5.65\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}.\]

At \(r=0.15\,\mathrm m\) the axial point-dipole field is

\[2(\mu_0/4\pi)m/r^3=0.335\,\mu\mathrm T;\]

at an equatorial point the same distance away it is \(0.168\,\mu\mathrm T\) directed opposite the moment. A scalar probe looking only at magnitudes would find a two-to-one change. A three-axis probe can use the sign reversal of the axial component to distinguish a dipole from a residual uniform background.

The exact axial field of the circular loop is \(B_z=(\mu_0NIa^2/2)(a^2+z^2)^{-3/2}\). Divide it by the dipole value to obtain \([1+(a/z)^2]^{-3/2}\). At ten radii it is 0.9852 of the dipole prediction, giving an error near 1.5 percent despite the large separation. Reducing that error below one percent relative to the exact value requires \([1+(a/z)^2]^{3/2}-1<0.01\), or \(z/a>12.3\). A far-field fit to the moment using several sensor distances can examine how the fitted \(z^3B_z\) approaches a constant. It also exposes uncertainty in the assumed source centre: a small offset in \(z\) produces a relative distance error amplified roughly threefold in the inferred moment.

Arrange two loops to cancel the leading term

Place two identical coaxial loops at \(z=\pm d/2\) with moments \(+m\hat{\boldsymbol z}\) on the positive side and \(-m\hat{\boldsymbol z}\) on the negative side. At a far positive point each small loop contributes its axial dipole field with its own signed moment. Define \(K=\mu_0/(2\pi)\). Their sum, \(Km[(z-d/2)^{-3}-(z+d/2)^{-3}]\), has no \(z^{-3}\) term. Taylor expansion to first order in \(d/z\) gives \(3Kmd/z^4\). This quadrupolar decay is steeper because probing from farther away makes the spatial separation of the opposed moments harder to resolve. The result applies when both \(d/z\) and individual loop radius divided by \(z\) are small.

Near the midpoint a different approximation is needed. The exact axial fields of the two finite loops cancel at \(z=0\) but their derivatives add. For single-turn loops of radius \(a\), differentiating the exact expression gives \(B'_z(0)=3\mu_0 I a^2d/[2(a^2+d^2/4)^{5/2}]\) in the stated orientation. With \(a=d=0.040\,\mathrm m\), \(I=1.0\,\mathrm A\), this is \(6.74\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T/m}\). A \(2.0\) mm displacement from the centre then produces approximately \(1.35\,\mu\mathrm T\). A gradiometer uses differences like these to suppress an approximately uniform background. A slight current imbalance gives a residual ordinary dipole, which eventually dominates at sufficiently large distance because it falls more slowly.

Measure torque and work as an independent calibration

A small loop in a uniform external field has forces \(I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\boldsymbol B\) on its sides. Their net force vanishes since \(\oint d\boldsymbol\ell=0\). The net torque survives: reducing \(\oint\boldsymbol r\times(I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\boldsymbol B)\) with the same antisymmetric geometrical tensor yields

\[\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol m\times\boldsymbol B.\]

With a fixed permanent moment or a maintained current in an imposed field, the mechanical potential for orientation is \(-\boldsymbol m\cdot\boldsymbol B\) up to a constant. If an external source maintains the current its electrical work matters in the total energy balance.

The winding above placed in \(B=0.030\,\mathrm T\) at \(30^\circ\) experiences \(8.48\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Turning it quasistatically from alignment to perpendicular increases this orientation energy by \(mB=1.70\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J\). A force measurement in a slowly varying field samples the gradient: along an axis for a fixed aligned moment \(F_z\simeq m\,dB_z/dz\). In a truly uniform field the dipole has torque but no translational force. A convenient test of an inferred field gradient is therefore to compare the force at the same point with moment parallel and antiparallel. Their difference \(2mB'_z\) rejects a constant support force. Magnetic susceptibility, moment reorientation or a large source spanning appreciable changes in gradient invalidate the rigid-point formula.

Calibrate a gradiometer without assuming the background vanishes

A paired pickup consists of two equal small sensing loops connected in opposition. Put their centres at \(z=z_0\pm l/2\), both normal to the same axial field component \(B(z)\), and use \(N_p\) turns of area \(A_p\) for each. In the small-loop limit their net linkage along the series path is \(N_pA_p[B(z_0+l/2)-B(z_0-l/2)]\). The spatially uniform part cancels algebraically. Expanding about the midpoint leaves \(N_p A_p l[B'(z_0)+l^2B'''(z_0)/24+\cdots]\). A finite separation therefore estimates the derivative averaged over the baseline. The cubic derivative, rather than the second derivative, sets its first truncation error because the measurement is centred.

Suppose a calibration winding produces a sinusoidal gradient of peak amplitude \(2.0\,\mathrm{mT/m}\) at 100 Hz across that baseline. With \(N_p=50,\ A_p=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}, l=0.030\,\mathrm m\), the peak linkage is \(6.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Wb}\). Its time derivative gives a peak emf \(0.377\,\mathrm{mV}\). Changing frequency to 200 Hz while keeping gradient amplitude unchanged should double this emf if pickup impedance and input instrumentation do not distort the measurement. A DC gradient alone produces no voltage in a stationary inductive pickup; a calibrated DC Hall pair or a deliberately moved sample implements a different measurement.

A small fractional area-turn imbalance \(\varepsilon\) couples a uniform field back into the differenced signal. For example take the positive pickup to have factor \(N_pA_p(1+\varepsilon/2)\) and the negative one \(N_pA_p(1-\varepsilon/2)\). Expansion now contains both \(lB'\) and \(\varepsilon B\). If a sinusoidal background of amplitude \(20\,\mu\mathrm T\) shares the drive frequency and \(\varepsilon=0.01\), its apparent gradient is

\[\varepsilon B/l=6.67\,\mu\mathrm{T/m}.\]

This is only a third of one percent of the calibration gradient but could dominate a much smaller sample signal. Reversing the sample while leaving the pickup fixed tests which contribution reverses. Rotating the whole assembly might also change its coupling to external wiring and would implement a less selective test.

For an axial dipole field \(B(z)=C/z^3\) sufficiently far from the loop, \(B'=-3C/z^4\) and \(B'''=-60C/z^6\). The relative leading baseline error is therefore \(5l^2/(6z_0^2)\). At \(l/z_0=0.30\), the differential reading overestimates the pointwise derivative magnitude by about 7.5 percent before accounting for finite sensing-loop area. A gradient device may be well calibrated in a nearly linear reference field and still give a biased point-gradient interpretation near a sample. Comparing measurements at two baselines tests this spatial averaging while retaining rejection of the common background.

Distinguish finite pickup area from multipole structure

Even a single coil usually records surface flux rather than the field at one mathematical point. For a dipole \(m\hat{\boldsymbol z}\) on the symmetry axis of a coaxial circular pickup of radius \(b\), one can calculate the flux exactly within the point-dipole approximation by integrating the vector potential along its boundary. The azimuthal potential is \(A_\varphi=\mu_0 m s/[4\pi(s^2+z^2)^{3/2}]\). Multiplication by \(2\pi b\) gives

\[\Phi(z)=\frac{\mu_0 m b^2}{2(b^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.\]

Compare with the point-field estimate \(\pi b^2 B_z(0,z)=\mu_0 m b^2/(2|z|^3)\). The exact flux is smaller by the factor \([1+(b/z)^2]^{-3/2}\). This has exactly the same mathematical form as the correction for a finite drive loop and point probe. There is a physical reason: the mutual inductance is reciprocal. A measured deviation from a dipole fit can result from the source size, the pickup size, or both. A measurement at one distance without knowledge of the pickup geometry cannot assign the correction uniquely.

For \(m=1.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\), \(b=10\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(z=30\,\mathrm{mm}\), the point-field estimate is \(2.33\,\mathrm{nWb}\) per turn. The actual dipole flux through that circle is \(1.99\,\mathrm{nWb}\), about 15 percent lower. If the pickup is doubled in radius, it does not simply collect four times as much flux at the same distance. In the formal limit \(b\to\infty\) the explicit expression falls as \(1/b\), tending to zero: the very distant return flux of the dipolar field then cancels the central flux in the whole plane. For fixed nonzero \(z\), differentiating the expression with respect to \(b\) gives an extremum at \(b=\sqrt2|z|\); collecting flux with an ever wider loop eventually reduces the signal.

One can turn that observation into a geometry choice for a weak-signal experiment. First choose a drive frequency slow enough for the moment to remain quasistatic and for the pickup circuit response to be calibrated. Select a pickup radius near the theoretical optimum for the fixed stand-off, then compare an intentionally smaller pickup whose field is more local. Agreement of the source moments inferred with each correct area formula provides evidence for the dipole model over the distances used. Disagreement that persists after correcting alignment and finite pickup radii motivates resolving higher moments or modelling the actual source current, rather than assigning a different magnetic moment to the same sample at each probe distance.

Build a current dipole from a rotating charged shell

Consider a thin spherical shell of radius \(a\) carrying charge \(Q\) uniformly painted onto its insulating surface. A motor rotates the shell with angular speed \(\Omega\) about \(z\). Every little patch moves, yet the charge density observed at a fixed point in space stays constant. The steady current density is a surface current \(\mathbf K=\sigma\Omega a\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\), where \(\sigma=Q/(4\pi a^2)\). This model assumes that charge remains attached to the shell and that the rim speed is small compared with the speed of light. A conducting shell allowed to rearrange its charges introduces another physical question.

Slice the sphere into narrow latitude bands. The distance across a band perpendicular to its current is \(a\,d\theta\), so the circulating current associated with the band is \(dI=K_\phi a\,d\theta\). Its loop area is \(\pi a^2\sin^2\theta\). All the band moments point along the rotation axis for positive charge. Summing them gives

\[m_z=\pi\sigma\Omega a^4\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=\frac{4\pi\sigma\Omega a^4}{3}=\frac{Q\Omega a^2}{3}.\]

The integral can be checked by substituting \(u=\cos\theta\), which changes \(\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\) into \(-(1-u^2)\,du\). The equatorial bands dominate much more strongly than their charge alone suggests. Each patch there travels faster and encloses a larger area. Using the full charge as though it were on an equatorial ring would give \(Q\Omega a^2/2\), an overestimate by a factor of three halves. Both models have the same radius and total charge, so the difference tests precisely what information a magnetic dipole moment contains.

There is a useful local check. For the latitude ring at angle \(\theta\), its radius squared is \(a^2\sin^2\theta\) and the distance squared from every source point to the sphere centre is \(a^2\). Insert these into the exact axial field of a loop. The result is

\[dB_z=\mu_0 dI\sin^2\theta/(2a).\]

Integrating gives

\[B_z(0)=2\mu_0\sigma\Omega a/3=\mu_0Q\Omega/(6\pi a).\]

This finite answer at the origin cannot be obtained by extrapolating a point-dipole field inwards. A uniformly magnetised sphere with \(\mathbf M=\sigma\Omega a\,\hat z\) has exactly this surface current. Its solution gives a uniform interior field and an exact dipolar exterior because its angular dependence is a single degree-one harmonic.

For \(a=50\,{\rm mm}\), \(Q=1.0\,\mu{\rm C}\) and \(\Omega=100\,{\rm rad/s}\), the dipole moment is \(8.33\times10^{-8}\,{\rm A\,m^2}\). The central field is about \(0.133\,{\rm nT}\). On axis at \(200\,{\rm mm}\), the field is only \(2.08\,{\rm pT}\). Separating a field that weak from an ambient steady field requires a mechanism such as reversing the rotation or the charge repeatedly and averaging synchronous differences. Rotor vibration in a spatially varying background can generate a signal correlated with rotation, too. An uncharged control run tests that contribution without changing the motor geometry.

Compare magnetic moment and angular momentum

Suppose the mass \(M\) is distributed over the same shell in the same proportion as its charge. A mass element \(dm\) contributes axial angular momentum \(dL_z=\Omega a^2\sin^2\theta\,dm\). A transported charge element \(dq\) returns after the period \(2\pi/\Omega\), so its contribution to the current moment is

\[dm_z=\Omega a^2\sin^2\theta\,dq/2.\]

If every element has \(dq/dm=Q/M\), these relations give \(m_z/L_z=Q/(2M)\) on any rigid axisymmetric body, independent of whether the matter fills a solid ball or only a shell. The simple proportionality is classical and assumes the same spatial distributions. It supplies no derivation of the intrinsic spin moment of an electron.

A solid insulating sphere of radius \(a\) carries a uniformly distributed volume charge instead. Recover its moment by adding thin spherical shells and compare the angular momentum ratio.

Work through the solution

The charge inside a radial shell is \(dQ=3Qr^2dr/a^3\). Each shell contributes \(\Omega r^2dQ/3\) from the latitude-band result. Integration from zero to \(a\) yields \(Q\Omega a^2/5\). More charge now occupies small cylindrical radii, which reduces the moment at fixed total charge. If mass is also uniform in volume, the moment of inertia is \(2Ma^2/5\). Their ratio is again \(Q/(2M)\). If instead essentially all the mass resides in a solid core while the charge remains on an insulating outer shell, inserting the shell charge result and solid inertia gives \(5Q/(6M)\), illustrating why the distribution hypothesis matters.

What mechanical measurement independently determines the moment of the shell without resolving its field in space?

Work through the solution

Place the rotor in a calibrated approximately uniform external magnetic field at right angles to its axis. The torque has magnitude \(mB\), obtained by summing the loop torques. With the numerical shell above and \(B=0.10\,{\rm T}\) it is \(8.33\times10^{-9}\,{\rm N\,m}\). Reverse the charge and repeat the torque measurement to isolate the contribution odd in charge. Maintaining the axis fixed requires a compensating support torque. A nonuniform imposed field would also exert a net force and could couple a spurious lever-arm torque into the measuring fixture.

Tutorial on testing a multipole approximation

Two nominally opposed loops have moment difference \(0.01m\) and separation \(d\). Estimate the positive axial distance at which their residual dipole equals their leading quadrupole in magnitude.

Work through the solution

The respective magnitudes are \(K(0.01m)/z^3\) and \(3Kmd/z^4\). Equating them gives \(z=300d\). An imbalance of one percent can therefore determine the most distant field even though a closer scan shows an excellent quadrupolar law.

A nonplanar wire follows three sides of the unit square in the \(xy\)-plane, then returns to its start via a raised corner. Can its dipole moment have an in-plane component? Give a construction that determines it without surface integration.

Work through the solution

Sum \(\frac I2\sum_j\boldsymbol r_j\times\boldsymbol r_{j+1}\) over its vertices in traversal order, including the closing edge. Its projections onto the vertical coordinate planes can span nonzero signed area, generating in-plane moment components. A rigid translation of every vertex adds a telescoping zero, providing a check.

Opposed windings produce fields that scale nearly as \(r^{-4}\). By what factor does a small transverse pickup's induced voltage amplitude change if source-to-pickup distance doubles while the whole source current is driven sinusoidally at the same sufficiently low frequency?

Work through the solution

If the pickup area and orientation remain fixed, its linked flux scales as its normal field. The time derivative supplies the same factor \(\omega\) in both runs. Its voltage amplitude therefore falls by about 16, provided the magnetostatic spatial approximation and quadrupolar regime remain valid. A dipole-dominated residual would instead give about eight.
