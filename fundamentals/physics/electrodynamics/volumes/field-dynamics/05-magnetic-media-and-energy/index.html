Interfaces, demagnetising fields and the work of magnetisation

Two samples made of the same material can respond very differently to an externally applied field if one is a long rod and the other is a thin disc. The induced moment depends on the internal field, which the magnetised sample helps create. The same distinction reappears when one tries to infer a force from stored magnetic energy.

Introduce the auxiliary field by removing a known current

With magnetisation present the total steady current is \(\boldsymbol J_f+\nabla\times\boldsymbol M\), where the curl includes any sheet contribution if \(\boldsymbol M\) is extended distributionally. Define \(\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol B/\mu_0-\boldsymbol M\), giving \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol J_f\). A small ribbon crossing an interface with normal \(\boldsymbol n\) directed from 1 to 2 gives \(\boldsymbol n\times(\boldsymbol H_2-\boldsymbol H_1)=\boldsymbol K_f\), and a thin pillbox gives

\[\boldsymbol n\cdot(\boldsymbol B_2-\boldsymbol B_1)=0.\]

The boundary conditions distinguish the field whose circulation counts free current from the field whose flux has no sources.

If both media are linear, isotropic and local,

\[\boldsymbol B_i=\mu_i\boldsymbol H_i.\]

In the absence of free surface current \(H_t\) and \(B_n\) are continuous. For angles measured from the normal, \(\tan\theta=B_t/B_n\) therefore gives \(\tan\theta_2/\tan\theta_1=\mu_2/\mu_1\) when the nonzero components permit the ratio. For instance, going from vacuum into a medium with \(\mu_r=5\), let \(B_{1n}=2\,\mathrm{mT}\) and \(B_{1t}=1\,\mathrm{mT}\). The new components are \(2\) and \(5\,\mathrm{mT}\), tilting the local field from \(26.6^\circ\) to \(68.2^\circ\). A mounted probe sensitive only to the normal component would register no jump. This local argument says which components can jump; it does not solve the complete field of a finite piece of material.

Solve a sphere to find its internal field

Put a uniform isotropic sphere of radius \(a\) and permeability \(\mu_r\mu_0\) in an originally uniform external \(\boldsymbol H_0=H_0\hat{\boldsymbol z}\). There is no free current in or near it, so locally \(\boldsymbol H=-\nabla\psi\). In each uniform domain \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0\) implies Laplace's equation for \(\psi\). Spherical symmetry of the geometry and the linear far-field potential select the angular harmonic \(\cos\theta\). The finite interior solution is \(-C r\cos\theta\); the exterior is \(-H_0r\cos\theta+D\cos\theta/r^2\). Tangential \(H\) agrees at the surface when \(\psi\) is continuous up to a chosen common constant. Normal \(B\) enforces the second equation:

\[C=H_0-D/a^3,\]
\[\mu_r C=H_0+2D/a^3,\]
\[C=\frac{3H_0}{\mu_r+2}.\]

Inside, \(H_{\rm int}=C\) is uniform. Multiplying by susceptibility \(\chi=\mu_r-1\) gives

\[M=\chi C.\]

Rearranging yields the memorable self-consistency relation \(H_{\rm int}=H_0-M/3\). The factor \(1/3\) came from solving both boundary conditions for a sphere and is not a universal material property. The dipolar exterior carries moment \(m=4\pi D=(4\pi a^3/3)M\), as expected from summing the uniform magnetisation. An ellipsoid with a principal axis parallel to the drive can likewise have uniform magnetisation and \(H_{\rm int}=H_0-N_dM\) with a geometric demagnetising factor; an arbitrary block usually has spatial variations near edges.

Take \(\mu_r=10\) and \(H_0=200\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The sphere has \(H_{\rm int}=50\,\mathrm{A/m}\), \(M=450\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and \(B_{\rm int}=0.628\,\mathrm{mT}\). A naive use of \(M=9H_0\) would give four times the actual moment. A long narrow specimen parallel to the drive, with very small \(N_d\), comes much closer to that latter value if the material remains linear. For the sphere \(\mu_r\to\infty\) gives \(B_{\rm int}\to3\mu_0H_0\) rather than infinity; meanwhile \(H_{\rm int}\to0\). These two trends are compatible because \(B/H\) is growing. Calling either simply a 'field enhancement' without specifying the measured field obscures the limiting result.

Derive the incremental energy for a reversible medium

Imagine increasing the current in a winding slowly while holding all material boundaries fixed. Faraday's law makes the terminal voltage, apart from resistance, \(d\lambda/dt\), where \(\lambda\) is the flux linkage. The source delivers incremental energy \(I\,d\lambda\). In a uniform toroidal core of mean path length \(\ell\), area \(A\), and \(N\) turns,

\[H=NI/\ell,\ \lambda=NBA.\]

Therefore \(I\,d\lambda=(A\ell)H\,dB\). In a lossless quasistatic medium the magnetic energy density accumulated along a reversible constitutive path is \(w(B)=\int_0^B H(B')\,dB'\). When \(B=\mu H\) with constant \(\mu\), this reduces to \(BH/2\). The corresponding coenergy \(w'(H)=\int_0^H B(H')\,dH'\) satisfies \(w+w'=BH\) for a single-valued curve through the origin.

As a nonlinear example, use \(H=\alpha B+\beta B^3\) with \(\alpha=10^3\,\mathrm{A/(m\,T)}\) and \(\beta=4.0\times10^3\,\mathrm{A/(m\,T^3)}\) over the measured range. At \(B=0.50\) T, \(H=1000\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The integrated energy is

\[w=\alpha B^2/2+\beta B^4/4=187.5\,\mathrm{J/m^3};\]

half the endpoint product gives \(250\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\) and misses the curvature. Coenergy is \(312.5\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\), compensating the difference. The differential permeability is \(dB/dH=(\alpha+3\beta B^2)^{-1}=2.50\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{H/m}\), whereas the ratio \(B/H=5.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{H/m}\). A small alternating signal about a DC operating point samples the differential value. These numbers are properties of the model curve and should not be mistaken for a universal ferromagnetic law.

Add an air gap and localise the energy

Give a toroidal magnetic circuit a thin gap of length \(g\), neglecting fringing, leakage and flux crowding. For equal cross sections the same normal \(B\) passes through core and gap. Ampère's law gives

\[NI=H_c\ell_c+B g/\mu_0.\]

For linear \(\mu_r\), this yields

\[B=\mu_0NI/(\ell_c/\mu_r+g).\]

With \(\ell_c=0.20\,\mathrm m,\ g=1.0\,\mathrm{mm},\ \mu_r=1000,\ NI=100\) A-turns the result is \(0.1047\) T. The gap consumes \(83.3\) A-turns; the much longer core consumes \(16.7\). Vary the two lengths separately to see why high permeability cannot remove the gap contribution.

Their energy ratio follows from \(B^2/(2\mu)\) times volume, giving \(U_g/U_c=g\mu_r/\ell_c=5.0\). For area \(A=1.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), the gap stores \(0.436\,\mathrm{mJ}\) and the core \(0.0873\,\mathrm{mJ}\). The simple force scale at one nearly uniform normal gap face is

\[B^2A/(2\mu_0)=0.436\,\mathrm N.\]

Decreasing \(g\) at fixed current changes the total flux, so differentiating only the instantaneous gap energy while holding everything else implicitly fixed need not reproduce the mechanical force. One may use a vacuum stress surface or a coenergy derivative at prescribed current. Both methods require a clear statement about what is held fixed during the imagined displacement.

Distinguish reversible storage from a measured loop

Real ferromagnetic samples can have history-dependent magnetisation. If \(B\) and \(H\) trace a closed hysteresis loop, the integral \(\oint H\,dB\) measures energy per volume delivered in one quasistatic cycle, with a positive sign for a dissipative cycle under passive conventions. A single-valued energy density of \(B\) alone cannot reproduce this nonzero path integral. At finite frequency conductive eddy losses and relaxation can broaden a measured loop, so a loop measured at one rate alone cannot isolate a purely quasistatic hysteresis loss.

For an approximately parallelogram-shaped cycle spanning switching fields \(\pm120\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and switching induction swing \(0.80\,\mathrm T\) from lower to upper branch, its approximate enclosed area is \(240\times0.80=192\,\mathrm{J/m^3}\) per cycle. At 50 Hz a \(12\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) sample dissipates approximately \(0.115\,\mathrm W\). The stated swing here is the full separation, eliminating a common factor-of-two ambiguity. A power meter on the terminals also sees the winding's \(I^2R\) and any energy leaving the apparatus; a separate resistance measurement and a frequency sweep help isolate the material contribution.

Reconstruct an anisotropic interface from boundary data

At an interface with a single crystal the relation between \(\boldsymbol H\) and \(\boldsymbol B\) may be tensorial. The formulas for angle refraction derived earlier assume scalar permeability; the underlying boundary conditions do not. Take a planar interface with normal \(+\hat{\boldsymbol z}\), vacuum below and a linear reciprocal crystal above. Choose units such that its relative permeability in the \(x,z\) plane is the symmetric matrix with entries \(\mu_{xx}=4,\mu_{xz}=\mu_{zx}=1,\mu_{zz}=2\). Its positive determinant and positive diagonal make the associated magnetic energy positive. Suppose the vacuum drive near the boundary has \(H_{1x}=100\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and \(B_{1z}/\mu_0=200\,\mathrm{A/m}\), with no \(y\) component and no free sheet current. These are local interface data, not a complete boundary condition for a finite crystal.

Continuity of tangential \(H\) gives \(H_{2x}=100\). Continuity of normal \(B\) gives \(100+2H_{2z}=200\), hence \(H_{2z}=50\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The crystal's tangential flux density then follows from \(B_{2x}/\mu_0=4(100)+50=450\,\mathrm{A/m}\); \(B_{2z}/\mu_0=200\) by construction. In the crystal \(\boldsymbol H\) and \(\boldsymbol B\) point at different angles to the normal, with tangent ratios 2 and 2.25 respectively. The local reversible magnetic energy density is

\[\boldsymbol H\cdot\boldsymbol B/2=\mu_0[100(450)+50(200)]/2=0.0346\,\mathrm{J/m^3}.\]

Replacing the tensor by a scalar fitted to the normal component would give the wrong tangent field and energy even though that fitted scalar reproduces one measurement by design.

For a general symmetric matrix, the cross energy in \(w(\boldsymbol H)=\boldsymbol H\cdot\boldsymbol\mu\boldsymbol H/2\) involves both off-diagonal elements. Its derivative with respect to one component yields the corresponding component of \(\boldsymbol B\). One can test reciprocity by preparing two small field increments about the same reversible operating point and interchanging their directions. The mixed incremental energy should agree. Large hysteresis loops, saturation along an easy axis or externally biased nonreciprocal media require more information before equating the two cross responses. The scalar material tables often used for a gapped iron core therefore encode a particular direction and operating regime.

Determine a demagnetising factor or an intrinsic susceptibility

For an ellipsoidal sample along a principal axis, combine \(H_{\rm int}=H_0-N_dM\) with \(M=\chi H_{\rm int}\). Define apparent susceptibility \(\chi_{\rm app}=M/H_0\). Rearrangement gives

\[1/\chi_{\rm app}=1/\chi+N_d.\]

This linear relation suggests a comparative measurement that can separate material and shape without having a probe inside either sample. Prepare geometrically distinct ellipsoids from one linear isotropic material, measure their moments along specified axes in the same calibrated uniform field, and divide by their respective volumes. If their demagnetising factors are independently known and the material history has not changed, the quantities \(1/\chi_{\rm app}-N_d\) should agree.

Take intrinsic \(\chi=20\). A sphere with \(N_d=1/3\) gives \(\chi_{\rm app}=2.609\); a slender ellipsoid with \(N_d=0.050\) gives \(\chi_{\rm app}=10.0\). One might attribute their nearly fourfold moment ratio to different compositions if the shapes were omitted. Suppose the measured slender-sample value is \(9.0\) instead. Its inferred intrinsic susceptibility would be \(1/(1/9-0.05)=16.36\). Comparing the sphere's inferred value of 20 tests whether the discrepancy exceeds shape, volume and magnetometer uncertainties. The inversion is especially ill-conditioned near the large-\(\chi\) apparent saturation \(1/N_d\); small errors in a heavily demagnetised sample then produce large errors in the recovered intrinsic response.

The sensitivity can be quantified without introducing a fitting convention. Differentiate \(\chi=\chi_{\rm app}/(1-N_d\chi_{\rm app})\), giving \(d\chi/d\chi_{\rm app}=1/(1-N_d\chi_{\rm app})^2\). At the preceding spherical point this derivative is nearly 59, so an absolute error 0.02 in the apparent value shifts the recovered intrinsic result by about 1.18. For the slender sample the derivative is only four at the stated point. The large spherical amplification follows from subtraction of nearly equal quantities, not poor electrical calibration. An elongated specimen may therefore yield a more informative intrinsic measurement even if a sphere is easier to machine and align.

Check a material explanation against mechanical and electrical work

Imagine measuring a single coil fitted with an adjustable magnetic insert. At a particular position the current ramp up from zero to 0.50 A produces linkage \(\lambda(I)=aI+bI^3\) with \(a=0.040\,\mathrm H\), \(b=-0.020\,\mathrm{H/A^2}\) over this restricted range. Differential inductance is \(a+3bI^2\) and stays positive here. Stored energy is \(\int I\,d\lambda=aI^2/2+3bI^4/4\), whereas coenergy is \(\int\lambda dI=aI^2/2+bI^4/4\). At the stated end current they are \(4.06\) and \(4.69\) mJ. Their sum \(8.75\) mJ equals \(I\lambda\). A measured terminal waveform can integrate the first expression directly by accumulating \(I(v-RI)dt\); independently plotting \(\lambda\) versus \(I\) allows the second by planimetric integration. These two areas have different shapes on a nonlinear constitutive curve.

If the insert moves, coenergy at each position can be obtained by repeating the reversible \(\lambda(I)\) scan. Its spatial difference divided by travel distance estimates the average force for fixed end current. Simply using half the product of the end current and linkage would predict neither energy correctly in this example. A hysteretic insert follows different curves on raising and lowering the current, so a measured mean force or cycle loss must state the prepared branch. A force predicted by one reversible branch can be checked with a load cell at the same position and current; an electrical scan at another bias is not enough to test the mechanical boundary condition.

Derive the directional permeability of a stack of thin layers

A material assembled from alternating sheets can respond differently to fields along and across the sheets even when each constituent is isotropic. Consider many parallel layers of two linear, reversible magnetic materials with absolute permeabilities \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\). Their volume fractions in a representative period are \(f\) and \(1-f\). Start far from the outer edges. Suppose the imposed field varies only on lengths much longer than a period, and take the frequency low enough that eddy currents do not change the field distribution. These conditions make a comparison with homogeneous bulk measurements meaningful.

First apply a field tangent to the layers. The tangential field \(H_t\) is continuous at each interface because no free sheet current flows there. Each material has a different magnetic induction \(B_{it}=\mu_i H_t\). Averaging the induction over one period and dividing by the shared field gives

\[\mu_\parallel=f\mu_1+(1-f)\mu_2.\]

This is an arithmetic mean. A heavily permeable layer can carry a large fraction of the tangential flux through only part of the period. No condition requires equal tangential induction on the two sides of the internal interface.

Next drive the magnetic flux through the stack in the normal direction. Normal induction is continuous because magnetic flux lines cannot end at a layer. The normal field in each layer is \(B_n/\mu_i\). Averaging the field at fixed common induction now gives a harmonic mean,

\[\frac{1}{\mu_\perp}=\frac{f}{\mu_1}+\frac{1-f}{\mu_2}.\]

A small fraction of a weakly permeable spacer can account for most of the magnetomotive drop across a stack. One can derive the same result by adding the magnetic reluctances of a column of common area. That circuit calculation works here because the interface conditions guarantee a common induction. Tilting the applied field creates both normal and tangential components and requires both averages at once. In axes attached to the layers,

\[\langle B_t\rangle=\mu_\parallel\langle H_t\rangle,\]
\[\langle B_n\rangle=\mu_\perp\langle H_n\rangle.\]

The averaged field vectors are generally not parallel.

For a stack containing \(90\%\) by volume of a material with relative permeability \(1000\) and \(10\%\) of a spacer with relative permeability one, the two effective relative permeabilities are \(900.1\) and approximately \(9.91\). Suppose a fixture produces equal average components \(H_t=H_n=10\,{\rm A/m}\). The induction components are then about \(11.31\,{\rm mT}\) and \(0.125\,{\rm mT}\). Their ratio corresponds to an induction angle only \(0.63^\circ\) above the layers while the average field points at \(45^\circ\). A probe sensitive to a single component could therefore return an apparently inconsistent permeability when the orientation of an anisotropic specimen is ignored.

Verify the energy before using the homogenised material law. For a purely normal induction the microscopic average energy density is

\[fB_n^2/(2\mu_1)+(1-f)B_n^2/(2\mu_2)=B_n^2/(2\mu_\perp).\]

For tangential field the microscopic average is

\[H_t^2[f\mu_1+(1-f)\mu_2]/2=\mu_\parallel H_t^2/2.\]

Adding both contributions gives \(\langle\mathbf B\rangle\cdot\langle\mathbf H\rangle/2\) for this linear model. Directly replacing each constituent field by the spatially averaged field before evaluating its energy would give an incorrect normal contribution. The field concentration in the spacer is a physical effect that survives passage to an effective continuum.

Estimate how precisely the spacer fraction must be known if a low-frequency normal-induction calibration is to be reproducible.

Work through the solution

Write \(s=1-f\) and \(\mu_{\perp,r}=[(1-s)/1000+s]^{-1}\). Differentiating its logarithm gives \(d\ln\mu_{\perp,r}/ds=-0.999/[0.001+0.999s]\), about \(-9.90\) at \(s=0.10\). Increasing the spacer volume fraction by one absolute percentage point therefore reduces normal permeability by roughly ten percent to first order. Direct substitution at \(s=0.11\) gives \(9.02\), or a reduction of about nine percent, showing the size of the linearisation error for that relatively large change. The same change reduces the tangential relative permeability from \(900.1\) to \(890.1\), only about one percent.

How does local saturation change the normal and tangential experiments?

Work through the solution

In the normal test the induction is shared. A high-permeability layer reaches a specified saturation induction at approximately the same common induction one measures outside a suitably shaped test column. In the tangential test the field is shared and the high-permeability phase can have a much larger local induction than the reported average. For an infinitesimal variation around a bias point, repeat the interface argument with the local differential permeabilities \(dB_i/dH_i\). The differential effective response obeys the same arithmetic or reciprocal averaging rules under the one-dimensional hypotheses. A finite path that traverses a hysteresis loop needs its history and dissipation as well; no single constant differential permeability specifies its loop area.

Tutorial on geometry and constitutive response

A spherical sample has apparent susceptibility \(M/H_0=2.4\). Infer its intrinsic linear susceptibility and the internal field fraction. Can an apparent value of 3.5 arise in this model with positive intrinsic susceptibility?

Work through the solution

For a sphere \(M/H_0=\chi/(1+\chi/3)\). Inversion gives

\[\chi=2.4/(1-0.8)=12,\]
\[H_{\rm int}/H_0=1/5.\]

Positive finite \(\chi\) gives apparent values less than three. A measured larger result would call for checking shape, units, background or the linear homogeneous assumption.

An unknown material is placed in a long gapped circuit. At a given operating point twice the current gives only 1.6 times the old induction. Can a fixed linear permeability with unchanged geometry explain it?

Work through the solution

A fixed linear reluctance at fixed geometry gives exactly proportional induction. One can infer \(H_c=(NI-Bg/\mu_0)/\ell_c\) at each point and compare the results with a measured constitutive curve. Field-dependent permeability, leakage, fringing or a changing physical gap would each need evaluation before calling the effect saturation.

An interface has no sheet current, the normal field happens to vanish on both sides, and the tangential magnetic flux density differs by a factor of eight. What material ratio follows if both media are scalar and linear? Does this require a discontinuity in \(H_t\)?

Work through the solution

Continuity of \(H_t\) gives \(B_{2t}/B_{1t}=\mu_2/\mu_1=8\) if the tangential drive is nonzero. No tangential \(H\) jump is needed. The magnetisation jump can supply an equivalent bound surface current producing the jump in \(B_t\).
