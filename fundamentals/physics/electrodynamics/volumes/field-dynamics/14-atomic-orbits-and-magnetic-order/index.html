Perturb an orbit at fixed restoring potential

Use a classical bound electron as a model for the induced orbital response. The field is weak enough that the binding potential remains the dominant influence. A circular orbit of radius r, angular speed ω₀ and electron mass m has an inward restoring force mrω₀². Switch on a uniform axial field B slowly. The magnetic force changes the radial balance, and the induced electric field during switching changes the angular momentum. At first order, with the unperturbed radius used in the magnetic correction, the frequency shift has magnitude eB/(2m). The sign of the induced magnetic moment opposes the applied field for either sense of the original orbit.

\[\Delta\omega={eB\over2m},\]
\[\Delta m_z=-{e^2r^2B\over4m}.\]

The factor one half in the frequency can be recovered by inserting ω=ω₀+δω in the radial balance and keeping 2ω₀δω. The magnetic term is proportional to ω₀B. The orbit's magnetic moment follows from current times area, with the negative charge determining its direction. For an isotropic shell, the squared radius transverse to the field averages to two thirds of the full squared radius.

\[\chi_{\rm dia}=-{\mu_0Ne^2\over6m}\sum_j\langle r_j^2\rangle.\]

Here N is the atom number density and the sum runs over the bound electrons in an atom. The susceptibility is negative. This elementary closed-shell result is largely independent of temperature if the atomic size stays fixed. It describes bound orbital response, and its microscopic model should be specified when comparing with conduction-electron magnetism or superconducting screening.

Recall · Current direction and the magnetic moment of an orbit

A charge q circulating with period 2π/ω produces current qω/(2π). Multiplication by the oriented orbit area yields m=qωr²/2. Its mechanical angular momentum is L=mr²ω. Therefore the orbital ratio is q/(2m). Intrinsic spin needs its own gyromagnetic factor and a quantum state description.

Align pre-existing moments statistically

A permanent magnetic moment μ has energy −μ·B. For freely rotating classical moments the orientation integral is identical to the permanent electric dipole calculation, with x=μB/(kBT). The density of moments N gives M=NμL(x). In weak fields B≈μ₀H for a dilute sample, and expansion yields Curie's law.

\[\chi_{\rm para}={\mu_0N\mu^2\over3k_BT}.\]

For quantum angular momentum magnitude J, label the magnetic-moment projections by m=−J,…,J. For an electron with positive g this m is the negative of its angular-momentum projection. The magnetic energy is −gμB mB and the partition function is a finite sum. Expanding the exponential at weak field uses Σm=0 and Σm²=(2J+1)J(J+1)/3.

\[Z=\sum_{m=-J}^{J}e^{g\mu_BmB/(k_BT)},\]
\[\chi={\mu_0N(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)\over3k_BT}.\]

For J=1/2 the exact moment is M=N(gμB/2)tanh[gμBB/(2kBT)]. It approaches saturation as temperature falls at fixed field. With μ=μB, T=10 K and B=1 T the argument is 0.06717. A spin-half ensemble is only about 6.71 percent aligned. The classical freely rotating model gives a different weak-field coefficient because its accessible orientations differ.

Allow moments to change each other's effective field

Represent interactions at mean-field level by H_eff=H+λM. Above the transition, insert this in M=(C/T)H_eff and solve the linear equation.

\[\chi={C\over T-C\lambda}={C\over T-T_c}.\]

The denominator identifies where the zero-magnetisation solution ceases to be stable. A spin-half model at zero external field gives m=tanh[(Tc/T)m] for reduced magnetisation m=M/Ms. Expand tanh to cubic order near Tc. Besides m=0, the nonzero branch satisfies m²≈3(Tc/T−1)/(Tc/T)³. This predicts a square-root onset within this approximation.

Remember · A mean-field transition and a measured hysteresis loop answer different questions

The equilibrium equation predicts possible uniform order. Hysteresis requires a history-dependent route through metastable configurations, often involving domain nucleation and motion. A multi-domain specimen can have large local magnetisation and small net moment. A fitted Curie–Weiss temperature alone does not determine coercivity.

A continuum model makes the domain scale explicit. For an easy-axis magnet use energy density A(θ′)²+K sin²θ in a one-dimensional wall. Translation invariance gives the first integral A(θ′)²=K sin²θ for a wall joining θ=0 and π. Integration yields tan(θ/2)=exp[(x−x₀)/δ], with δ=√(A/K). Substitution in the energy integral gives wall energy per area 4√(AK). Exchange penalises rapid variation, whereas anisotropy penalises spending distance away from the easy directions. Magnetostatic energy and sample shape determine whether forming walls lowers the total energy.

Use temperature and shape to discriminate models

1. A sample has χ=−2×10⁻⁵+0.012 K/T. Determine the temperature where the net weak-field response changes sign.

Work through the solution

Set both terms equal in magnitude. T=600 K. At 300 K χ=+2×10⁻⁵, while at 1200 K χ=−1×10⁻⁵. This inference assumes a fixed density and no transition over the interval.

2. For A=10⁻¹¹ J/m and K=10⁴ J/m³ find the wall length scale and energy. Predict the effect of quadrupling K.

Work through the solution

δ=31.6 nm and wall energy 1.265 mJ/m². Quadrupling K halves δ and doubles the wall energy. The full conventional wall width may include a factor π, so specify the definition when comparing measurements.

3. Derive the measured susceptibility of a uniformly magnetised ellipsoid with demagnetising factor Nd and intrinsic χ.

Work through the solution

Use H_int=H_applied−NdM and M=χH_int. Thus M/H_applied=χ/(1+Ndχ). A strong measured temperature dependence may be suppressed by specimen geometry. Inverting the expression requires the same axis and shape convention used to determine Nd.
