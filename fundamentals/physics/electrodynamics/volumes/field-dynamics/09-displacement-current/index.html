Displacement current and the charging capacitor

A wire feeds a capacitor whose dielectric blocks the transport of mobile charges between its plates. A magnetic probe near the gap still responds to charging. Resolving which surface a circulation law should use leads to displacement current, an electric field correction during a ramp, and an energy-flow test around the rim.

Start from a contradiction involving two surfaces

Draw a circle around the conductor leading to one plate. One spanning surface crosses the metal wire and records free current \(I\). Another bulges into the gap and misses the metal entirely. A single circulation \(\oint\boldsymbol H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) cannot equal two distinct free-current integrals for the same contour. This difficulty is already visible locally. If \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol J_f\) held at every time, its divergence would require \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol J_f=0\), but charge conservation near a plate gives

\[\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol J_f=-\partial_t\rho_f.\]

Now impose Gauss's law \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol D=\rho_f\). Differentiating gives

\[\nabla\cdot(\partial_t\boldsymbol D)=\partial_t\rho_f.\]

Therefore \(\boldsymbol J_f+\partial_t\boldsymbol D\) is divergence free. Choosing it in Maxwell's circulation law respects both the steady limit and surface independence. Conservation alone determines only the required divergence of the additional term; the full Maxwell law also specifies its transverse content and is tested by electromagnetic waves. A displacement current density has units \(\mathrm{A/m^2}\). Its flux can exist in a vacuum region in which no conduction charge crosses the integration surface.

\[\oint_C\boldsymbol H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\int_S(\boldsymbol J_f+\partial_t\boldsymbol D)\cdot d\boldsymbol a .\]

Subtract the fluxes through two surfaces with the same boundary. They form a closed surface when the second orientation is reversed. The difference vanishes by the divergence theorem applied to the sum. If one uses only the conduction term, the missing difference is the rate at which free charge accumulates in the closed volume. This explains why a DC magnetostatic approximation works in a settled resistor and fails for an insulating gap being charged through the same wire.

Resolve the magnetic field across the plate area

Model two circular conducting plates of radius \(a\) separated by \(d\ll a\), with a linear dielectric filling the gap. Ignore edge fringing for interior points, use symmetric feeding, and take the charging time long compared with the propagation time across the plates. Then \(D_z\simeq Q(t)/(\pi a^2)\). The total displacement current is \(I=\dot Q\), but a centred Amperian circle at \(s<a\) samples only fraction \(s^2/a^2\) of that area. Cylindrical symmetry gives

\[H_\varphi(s)\simeq\frac{I s}{2\pi a^2}\quad(s<a),\]
\[H_\varphi(s)\simeq\frac{I}{2\pi s}\quad(s>a)\]

for a symmetric idealisation with negligible other return paths within the chosen circle. The second expression is a useful intermediate description near the capacitor before the distant return or fringing geometry intervenes. At the rim the formulas match. At \(s=0\) the field vanishes, so assigning the full terminal current to every small circle would produce a fictitious on-axis singularity.

Take \(a=0.050\,\mathrm m,d=1.0\,\mathrm{mm},I=10\,\mathrm{mA}\), nonmagnetic dielectric. At \(s=a/2\) the field is \(H_\varphi=0.0159\,\mathrm{A/m}\) and \(B_\varphi=20.0\,\mathrm{nT}\). At the rim \(B_\varphi=40.0\,\mathrm{nT}\). The enclosed displacement current at half radius is \(2.50\,\mathrm{mA}\), a quarter of terminal current. Doubling current doubles both readings; doubling plate radius at the same fractional measurement radius halves them. For vacuum the voltage rises at \(\dot V=Id/(\epsilon_0\pi a^2)=1.44\times10^8\,\mathrm{V/s}\), consistent with the ordinary lumped \(I=C\dot V\).

Distinguish material and vacuum contributions

In matter \(\partial_t\boldsymbol D=\epsilon_0\partial_t\boldsymbol E+\partial_t\boldsymbol P\). In a transparent ideal linear dielectric with relative permittivity four, impose the same field ramp as in an empty capacitor. The required free current is four times as large, with three quarters of the total changing displacement supplied by polarisation current. If instead the external supply maintains the same current, \(\dot D\) is unchanged, the field ramp is four times smaller, and the interior magnetic field calculated above is unchanged. These statements compare different control conditions. Supplying an equivalent charge-free background with the same prescribed voltage waveform is a different experiment from supplying it with the same current.

Add weak leakage \(\boldsymbol J_f=\sigma\boldsymbol E\) in the dielectric. Under a harmonic spatially uniform drive the terminal current per area is \((\sigma+i\omega\epsilon)\widetilde E\) with \(e^{i\omega t}\). In phase with the voltage there is real conduction, and in quadrature there is changing polarisation and vacuum electric displacement. For area \(A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}\), thickness \(d=0.002\,\mathrm m\), \(\epsilon_r=5,\sigma=10^{-8}\,\mathrm{S/m}\), the static parallel model has \(C=221\,\mathrm{pF}\), conductance \(G=50.0\,\mathrm{nS}\), and crossover \(\omega=G/C=\sigma/\epsilon=226\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) or \(f=36.0\,\mathrm{Hz}\). At that frequency equal in-phase and quadrature currents do not imply equal dissipated powers; only the resistive component draws nonzero average power in the ideal nondispersive model.

Ask how energy gets into the gap

For \(\boldsymbol E=E_z\hat{\boldsymbol z}\) and \(\boldsymbol H=H_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}\), the Poynting vector is \(-E_zH_\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol s}\). During charging positive \(E_z\) and rising positive \(D_z\) give energy flow radially inward. Just inside the cylindrical edge the surface area through which this flow enters is \(2\pi ad\), and \(H_\varphi(a)=I/(2\pi a)\). Thus incoming electromagnetic power is

\[E_zH_\varphi(a)2\pi ad=VI,\]

which is \(d(CV^2/2)/dt\) at constant capacitance in this approximation. Energy enters the dielectric through field flow, although the mobile charges in the plates arrive through conductors. During discharge the sign reverses.

At \(V=100\) V and \(I=10\,\mathrm{mA}\) the instantaneous rate is 1.0 W. At half radius the local inward flux density is half its edge value, but the local cylindrical area is also half as large. Their product is one quarter, exactly the fraction of stored electric volume inside half radius. The radial Poynting profile therefore agrees with local energy conservation for every coaxial subvolume. When driving becomes fast enough that substantial magnetic energy also accumulates or radiative effects matter, the simple uniform-field model no longer exhausts this balance.

Check the first spatial correction to a uniform electric field

A strictly time-varying azimuthal magnetic field also enters Faraday's equation in the gap, forcing a small radial dependence of \(E_z\). For an axisymmetric \(z\)-directed field with negligible \(z\)-variation, \((\nabla\times\boldsymbol E)_\varphi=-\partial_sE_z=-\partial_t B_\varphi\), hence \(\partial_sE_z=\partial_tB_\varphi\). Insert the leading Ampère solution \(B_\varphi\simeq\mu\epsilon s\,\dot E_z(0,t)/2\) and integrate from zero to \(s\). The first correction is \(E_z(s,t)-E_z(0,t)\simeq\mu\epsilon s^2\ddot E_z(0,t)/4\). For a pure harmonic drive it scales as \(-(ks)^2E_z(0,t)/4\), with \(k=\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\). This expansion is valid when \(ka\ll1\); it also predicts no correction from a strictly constant-slope ramp after initial transients have settled.

In an idealised uniform-height cylindrical model the harmonic radial wave equation is

\[s^{-1}\partial_s(s\partial_s E_z)+k^2E_z=0.\]

Regularity selects \(E_z\propto J_0(ks)\), whose series starts with \(1-(ks)^2/4\). This independent result validates both sign and factor in the iterative argument. For a \(5\,\mathrm{cm}\) vacuum radius at \(10\,\mathrm{MHz}\), \(ka=0.0105\), giving an edge-to-centre relative difference only \(2.75\times10^{-5}\). At \(1\,\mathrm{GHz}\), \(ka\) is about 1.05 and the uniform premise itself requires a distributed treatment including leads and boundaries. Small geometrical thickness by itself does not ensure instantaneous behaviour across a broad plate.

Track where the conduction current flows on the plate

The uniform-gap approximation says how much displacement current passes a circle. Current in the metal plate is distributed differently. Put a feed contact at the centre of a thin circular top plate and inject free current \(I(t)\) there. Assume that the plate charge density \(\sigma_f=Q/(\pi a^2)\) is approximately uniform away from the contact and edge. On the metal surface let the radial sheet current be \(K_s(s)\), positive outward. For a disk of radius \(s\), the input current minus the outward sheet flux must equal the rate at which that disk acquires charge. This gives \(I-2\pi sK_s=I s^2/a^2\), hence

\[K_s(s)=\frac{I}{2\pi s}\left(1-\frac{s^2}{a^2}\right),\qquad 0<s<a .\]

At \(s=a/2\) the outward sheet current across the circle is \(0.75I\), whereas the displacement current through its interior is \(0.25I\). At the rim the radial sheet current goes to zero because there is no continuation beyond the ideal metal edge. Near \(s=0\) the point-contact idealisation gives \(K_s\propto1/s\), revealing its own range of validity. A finite feed contact rounds this behaviour; finite conductivity and inductance can change the assumed uniform charging during a rapid ramp. The estimated currents on the metal and in the insulator add consistently without a conduction electron having to traverse the dielectric.

With \(a=50\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(s=25\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(I=10\,\mathrm{mA}\), the radial sheet current at that point is \(0.0477\,\mathrm{A/m}\). A ring segment spanning \(30^\circ\) at that radius carries \(K_s s(\pi/6)=0.625\,\mathrm{mA}\) radially outwards across its arc. The corresponding sector has collected only one twelfth of the enclosed displacement current, \(0.208\,\mathrm{mA}\). Summing the two contributions over all twelve sectors restores the full terminal current. Measuring a single thin lead and assigning its whole current to a small patch of plate would give a wrong local Joule-loss density even though a terminal ammeter remained accurate.

For a plate with sheet resistance \(R_\square\), the resistive dissipation per area under this low-frequency distribution is \(R_\square K_s^2\). Integrate over \(b<s<a\) to cut off the finite contact at radius \(b\). The radial spreading contribution is

\[P_{\rm sheet}=\frac{R_\square I^2}{2\pi}\left[\ln\frac ab-\frac34+\frac{b^2}{a^2}-\frac{b^4}{4a^4}\right] .\]

The logarithm comes from the high current concentration near the feed. For \(a/b=10\), the bracket is \(1.563\) and the effective resistance \(P/I^2\) of this plate region is \(0.249R_\square\). This is a weak-loss estimate: if the voltage drop across the plate becomes comparable to the capacitor voltage, the current and charge distributions must instead be solved jointly. It is nevertheless an instructive correction to an apparently straightforward 'lossless capacitor' measurement, in which dissipation may actually be concentrated in the contacts or electrode sheets.

Recover an unknown dielectric response from a controlled ramp

Use a parallel-plate cell of area \(A\) and separation \(d\). Its measured small-signal admittance is \(Y=I/V\). In the low-frequency geometrical limit \(Y=(A/d)(\sigma+i\omega\epsilon)\) for simple frequency-independent real \(\epsilon,\sigma\) and phasor convention \(e^{i\omega t}\). Suppose \(A=0.0020\,\mathrm{m^2},d=0.50\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(f=10\,\mathrm{kHz}\), and after subtracting lead capacitance the measured admittance is \(0.80+i8.90\,\mu\mathrm S\). The capacitance is \(8.90\times10^{-6}/(2\pi10^4)=142\,\mathrm{pF}\), giving \(\epsilon_r=Cd/(A\epsilon_0)\simeq4.00\). Treating the real component as DC conduction gives

\[\sigma=Gd/A=2.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{S/m}.\]

A terminal measurement at a single frequency alone cannot confirm that interpretation.

A delayed polarisation can produce the same real terminal component without any mobile charge crossing the entire sample. For example take a single orientational relaxation \(\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\chi E\). Then \(\widetilde P=\epsilon_0\Delta\chi\widetilde E/(1+i\omega\tau)\), and the associated current is \(i\omega\widetilde P\). Its real coefficient is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\chi\omega^2\tau/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\), positive in this passive model. The quadrature coefficient is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\chi\omega/[1+(\omega\tau)^2]\). A signal analyser would assign the first part to an equivalent conductance. Its strong frequency dependence distinguishes this process from a constant leakage \(\sigma\) if a sufficient frequency range is measured.

A step up in voltage is another way to separate hypotheses. An instantaneous ideal capacitance responds during the step only, a Debye polarisation adds a decaying current over its characteristic \(\tau\), and a true Ohmic path continues to carry current at constant final voltage. In a cell whose polarisation term has \(\Delta C=50\,\mathrm{pF}\), step magnitude \(1.0\,\mathrm V\) and \(\tau=2.0\,\mathrm{ms}\), the delayed contribution starts at \(25\,\mathrm{nA}\) and integrates to \(50\,\mathrm{pC}\). The initial current in a measured trace will also contain the finite-risetime vacuum and fast-electronic capacitance currents. Integrating after a separately modelled step response is more robust than inferring charge from a single noisy peak. A steady \(0.80\,\mu\mathrm A\) residual under 1.0 V would support the constant-conductance interpretation for the above cell; decay toward zero would point to polarisation, instrument offsets or a more complex model.

Determine the charge that accumulates inside a layered capacitor

Stack two plane dielectric layers in series between extended parallel electrodes. Material 1 has thickness \(d_1\), permittivity \(\epsilon_1\) and nonzero conductivity \(\sigma_1\), with corresponding quantities for material 2. A source suddenly applies a fixed voltage \(V\), and the stack is initially uncharged. The Ohmic current density alone will generally differ between the two layers early in the transient. A conduction-current mismatch deposits free charge on their internal interface. The total current including displacement remains continuous because equal currents must pass through successive cross sections of this one-dimensional stack.

Choose the field positive from electrode 1 toward electrode 2. Voltage imposes \(d_1E_1+d_2E_2=V\). For positive times after the voltage step, current continuity imposes \(\sigma_1E_1+\epsilon_1\dot E_1=\sigma_2E_2+\epsilon_2\dot E_2\). Substituting \(E_2=(V-d_1E_1)/d_2\) and collecting terms gives

\[(\epsilon_1d_2+\epsilon_2d_1)\dot E_1+(\sigma_1d_2+\sigma_2d_1)E_1=\sigma_2V.\]

The coefficient of the derivative divided by the coefficient of \(E_1\) is a positive relaxation time \(\tau\). The initial split of voltage follows from the absence of pre-existing free interfacial charge, which requires

\[\epsilon_1E_1(0^+)=\epsilon_2E_2(0^+).\]

This is the capacitive voltage division of the instantaneous step. At long times, the two steady conduction currents must match, so \(\sigma_1E_1(\infty)=\sigma_2E_2(\infty)\). This is the resistive voltage division. At intermediate times each field relaxes exponentially from its initial value to its own final value with the same \(\tau\), while their thickness-weighted sum remains exactly fixed by the supply.

To see the signs in an example take \(d_1=d_2=1.0\,{\rm mm}\), \(V=10\,{\rm V}\), \(\epsilon_1=2\epsilon_0\), \(\epsilon_2=4\epsilon_0\),

\[\sigma_1=1.0\times10^{-8}\,{\rm S/m},\]
\[\sigma_2=4.0\times10^{-8}\,{\rm S/m}.\]

The fields just after charging are \(6.67\) and \(3.33\,{\rm kV/m}\), respectively. At long times they are \(8.00\) and \(2.00\,{\rm kV/m}\). The relaxation time is

\[6\epsilon_0/(5.0\times10^{-8}\,{\rm S/m})=1.06\,{\rm ms}.\]

Increasing a conductivity at fixed permittivity shortens this time because free charge can move faster to redistribute the internal electric field.

The normal directed from material 1 into material 2 gives a free interface charge \(q_s=\epsilon_2E_2-\epsilon_1E_1\) per unit area. Its final value is \(-7.08\times10^{-8}\,{\rm C/m^2}\). The initial conduction current into the interface from layer 1 is \(6.67\times10^{-5}\,{\rm A/m^2}\), while \(1.33\times10^{-4}\,{\rm A/m^2}\) leaves through layer 2. Thus our convention predicts decreasing, negative interface charge. Direct differentiation gives \(\dot q_s=J_{\rm cond,1}-J_{\rm cond,2}\), precisely the same sign. Measuring the total electrode current alone does not tell the experimenter that the current inside each layer is all conductive.

Find the total through-current just after the initial ideal charging impulse, and compare it with the long-time value.

Work through the solution

Immediately after the impulse, layer 1 has \(\dot E_1=(8000-6667)/(1.06\,{\rm ms})\), about \(1.25\times10^6\,{\rm V/(m\,s)}\). Its displacement contribution is therefore about \(2.22\times10^{-5}\,{\rm A/m^2}\). Adding layer 1 conduction gives \(8.89\times10^{-5}\,{\rm A/m^2}\). In layer 2 the derivative has the opposite sign because the layer thicknesses match. The negative displacement contribution subtracts about \(4.44\times10^{-5}\,{\rm A/m^2}\) from its larger conduction current and gives the same total. At long times only the matched conduction current \(8.0\times10^{-5}\,{\rm A/m^2}\) remains. A real pulse generator gives a finite initial charging burst whose duration should be separated from the material relaxation when fitting data.

Can this sample have nonzero conductivity and still produce no slowly accumulating free interface charge?

Work through the solution

It can if \(\sigma_1/\epsilon_1=\sigma_2/\epsilon_2\). In that case the initial capacitive field division also satisfies the steady conduction condition, and the two equilibrium values in the exponential coincide. The voltage step can still deliver an initial charging current and sustain later resistive heating. An absence of the interfacial relaxation feature does not by itself imply perfectly insulating layers. Conversely, a measured slow response from a stack need not indicate slow molecular reorientation in either homogeneous constituent. A change of layer thickness or replacement with a homogeneous control can separate that possibility from charge accumulation at an internal interface.

David Tong, Electrodynamics, on displacement current and field propagation.

Tutorial on two spanning surfaces

A gas-filled capacitor has \(C=35\,\mathrm{pF}\) and rms drive \(12\,\mathrm V\) at 20 kHz. Determine rms displacement current and the fraction surrounded by a centred circle at radius \(0.7a\).

Work through the solution
\[I_{\rm rms}=2\pi f C V_{\rm rms}=52.8\,\mu\mathrm A.\]

The fractional area is \(0.7^2=0.49\), so the smaller circle encloses \(25.9\,\mu\mathrm A\) in the uniform idealisation. Rms and peak conventions must be used consistently throughout the calculation.

An isolated capacitor initially carries \(Q_0>0\) on its positive plate. With weak dielectric conductivity and no external leads attached, derive its voltage decay time. What happens to the net current crossing an internal spanning surface?

Work through the solution

The parallel conductance \(G=\sigma A/d\) drains the free charge, so \(C\dot V+GV=0\) and \(V=V_0e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(\tau=C/G=\epsilon/\sigma\). Conduction current and displacement current through the gap have opposite signs and equal magnitudes in the uniform model. Their sum vanishes even though Joule heat is produced by the decreasing stored electric energy.

With the second plate connected through a distant return wire, a large probe loop encircles the feed and return together. Should the \(1/s\) exterior gap formula be extrapolated to this loop?

Work through the solution

The derivation assumed only the charging current was enclosed and the surrounding geometry retained the required symmetry. Including the return changes the enclosed free-current balance and often destroys cylindrical symmetry. Choose a new surface and include its complete total-current flux before predicting any circulation. The local field need not vanish just because the complete circulation is zero.
