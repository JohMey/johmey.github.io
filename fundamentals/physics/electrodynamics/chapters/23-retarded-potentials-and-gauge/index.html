Potentials, gauge freedom and the arrival of a signal

When a source changes at noon, which part of its history can affect a detector at twelve o’clock and one nanosecond? The answer emerges from Maxwell’s equations once we specify both the source and the boundary condition in time.

Why introduce two potentials?

A magnetic field with zero divergence can, on a contractible region, be written as \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\). Faraday's law then says that \(\mathbf E+\partial_t\mathbf A\) has zero curl. Define its scalar potential as \(-\nabla\phi\). These two definitions solve the homogeneous Maxwell equations identically. Two inhomogeneous equations still determine how charge and current generate the fields. The potentials contain more components than necessary. For any smooth function \(\chi\), put

\[\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\]
\[\phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi.\]

The curl of a gradient vanishes. The two extra terms in the electric field cancel because mixed derivatives commute. This freedom lets us choose which equations to solve while keeping every force measurement identical.

\[t-t_r=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_s(t_r)|/c\]

The source is evaluated at emission. For a moving source its location on the right also depends on the unknown emission time.

Uncoupling the field equations

Insert the potentials into Gauss's law. This gives

\[\nabla^2\phi+\partial_t\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=-\rho/\epsilon_0.\]

Now substitute them in Ampère–Maxwell and use the curl-of-curl identity. Keeping the gradient terms together gives

\[(\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2)\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi).\]

Choose the Lorenz condition \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0\). A gauge transformation can achieve it by solving the scalar wave equation for \(\chi\) with the negative of the original condition as source. Both potentials then obey wave equations with the same propagation speed. Under this choice, charge continuity is essential. Applying the wave operator to the Lorenz condition returns \(-\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho)\), which vanishes for a conserved source. Residual gauge functions obey the homogeneous wave equation.

Constructing the retarded kernel

In a source-free spherical shell a radial scalar field \(\psi\) obeys \(\partial_r^2(r\psi)=c^{-2}\partial_t^2(r\psi)\). The one-dimensional equation has an outgoing branch \(r\psi=f(t-r/c)\) and an incoming branch \(g(t+r/c)\). An isolated source with no incident wave selects the outgoing branch. The response to an instantaneous impulse is supported at \(t=r/c\). Its spatial normalisation follows by integrating the static kernel over a tiny sphere, where \(-\nabla^2(1/4\pi r)=\delta^3(\mathbf r)\). The causal kernel is therefore \(\delta(t-r/c)/(4\pi r)\). Integrating the source over time removes the delta function:

\[\phi(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\mathbf x',t-R/c)}{R}\,d^3x',\]
\[\mathbf A(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int \frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf x',t-R/c)}{R}\,d^3x',\]
\[R=|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|.\]

Every volume element contributes at its own source time. If an incident wave is already present, add its homogeneous solution. In Coulomb gauge the scalar potential instead satisfies an instantaneous Poisson equation. Its instantaneous contribution to \(-\nabla\phi\) combines with \(-\partial_t\mathbf A\) to give the same causal electric field.

Two calculations with potentials

A pulse recorded at two distances

Measure an isolated outgoing spherical wave component in a source-free exterior. At radius \(0.30\) m its field trace peaks at \(10.0\) ns. At \(1.80\) m its peak arrives \(1.50/c=5.00\) ns later. The peak amplitude is \(0.30/1.80=1/6\) of the first. Multiplying each trace by its radius removes spreading. Shifting the second trace back by \(5.00\) ns then superposes them. For an electromagnetic dipole these statements describe its outgoing transverse radiation term along the same ray. Reactive near-field terms have different radial dependences.

Two vector potentials for a uniform field

For a constant magnetic field \(B\hat{\mathbf z}\) one choice is \(\mathbf A=Bx\hat{\mathbf y}\), \(\phi=0\). Choose \(\chi=-Bxy/2\). Its gradient adds \(-By\hat{\mathbf x}/2-Bx\hat{\mathbf y}/2\), giving \(\mathbf A'=(-By\hat{\mathbf x}+Bx\hat{\mathbf y})/2\). Its curl has \(z\) component \(B/2-(-B/2)=B\). The line integrals around any closed loop match because the integral of the added gradient is zero.

Compare the arrival of a compact pulse at two radii

This source-free outgoing component has a finite start and finish. A 1.80 m detector records the front 5.00 ns after a 0.30 m detector and measures one sixth of its amplitude. Can changing the pulse duration change either observation?

Problem sheet · delays and a time-dependent gauge

A finite source

Two ends of a source are 12 cm apart along a detector's line of sight. Bound the difference in retarded source times. At what frequency does the corresponding maximum phase span reach one radian?

Work through the solution

The triangle inequality gives \(|R_1-R_2|\leq0.12\) m. Thus \(\Delta t\leq0.400\) ns. For a harmonic source the maximum phase difference is \(2\pi f\Delta t\). One radian requires \(f=398\) MHz approximately. Well below that frequency one common retarded time can be accurate for this part of the phase. A dipole approximation also requires a source small compared with the observation distance.

A gauge with a time derivative

Start with zero potentials. Set \(\chi=\alpha xt\), where \(\alpha\) is constant. Determine both transformed potentials and check the fields.

Work through the solution

The new potentials are \(\mathbf A'=\alpha t\hat{\mathbf x}\) and \(\phi'=-\alpha x\). Hence \(-\nabla\phi'=+\alpha\hat{\mathbf x}\) and \(-\partial_t\mathbf A'=-\alpha\hat{\mathbf x}\). Their sum and the curl of the spatially uniform vector potential are both zero. The example also satisfies the Lorenz condition, consistent with \(\chi\) solving the homogeneous wave equation.
