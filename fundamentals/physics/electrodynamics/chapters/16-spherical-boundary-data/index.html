Why a sphere selects angular patterns

Consider a spherical surface of radius \(a\) covered by closely spaced insulated electrodes. They can prescribe a voltage varying with position, whereas a single connected conductor in electrostatic equilibrium can have only one voltage. Suppose there are no other charges, the surrounding permittivity is \(\epsilon_0\), and the reference is zero at infinity. At first let the applied voltage be independent of azimuth. In spherical coordinates the charge-free equation becomes

\[\frac1{r^2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_r V)+\frac1{r^2\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta V)=0 .\]

Writing \(V=R(r)P(u)\) with \(u=\cos\theta\) and multiplying by \(r^2/(RP)\) separates the radial and angular parts. The angular operator becomes \(d[(1-u^2)dP/du]/du\); regularity at both poles selects eigenvalues \(\ell(\ell+1)\) for nonnegative integer \(\ell\). The regular eigenfunctions are the Legendre polynomials. The first three are \(1,u,(3u^2-1)/2\). Substitution of any of these into the angular equation verifies its eigenvalue.

For each angular order the radial equation reads \((r^2R')'=\ell(\ell+1)R\). Insert \(R=r^p\) to obtain

\[p(p+1)=\ell(\ell+1).\]

The two choices are \(p=\ell\) and \(p=-\ell-1\). The first stays regular at the centre; the second decays at infinity. These choices describe different physical domains for the same measured surface voltage.

Infer coefficients and the surface charge they require

On the spherical surface write \(f(u)=\sum_\ell b_\ell P_\ell(u)\). Legendre polynomials are orthogonal under the integral \(du\) from \(-1\) to 1; for equal indices the integral is \(2/(2\ell+1)\). The weight has a geometric origin: a narrow latitude strip subtends solid angle \(2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta=-2\pi\,du\). Uniform steps in polar angle must be weighted by \(\sin\theta\) before estimating the angular coefficients.

\[b_\ell=\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}f(u)P_\ell(u)\,du,\]
\[V_{\rm in}=\sum_\ell b_\ell(r/a)^\ell P_\ell(u),\]
\[V_{\rm out}=\sum_\ell b_\ell(a/r)^{\ell+1}P_\ell(u).\]

At the boundary both sides have the prescribed potential. Their normal derivatives generally differ. For each order the outward fields at \(a\) are \(+(\ell+1)b_\ell P_\ell/a\) outside and \(-\ell b_\ell P_\ell/a\) inside. Gauss’s pillbox therefore assigns the thin electrode array a net sheet charge density

\[\sigma(u)=\frac{\epsilon_0}{a}\sum_\ell (2\ell+1)b_\ell P_\ell(u).\]

Worked inverse problem with three voltmeter readings

Suppose independent measurements establish that orders above two are negligible. At the north pole, equator and south pole a scan gives 4, 1 and 2 V. The first and last readings differ only in the odd term, hence

\[b_1=(4-2)/2=1\,\mathrm{V}.\]

Their average gives \(b_0+b_2=3\) V and the equator gives

\[b_0-b_2/2=1\,\mathrm{V}.\]

Solving yields \(b_0=5/3\) V and \(b_2=4/3\) V. On the north axis at \(r=a/2\) the predicted voltage is

\[5/3+1/2+1/3=2.50\,\mathrm{V}.\]

The three readings alone would not exclude a higher-order angular pattern. Additional sample positions are needed to test that assumption.

Use radial decay to design a measurement

The exterior radial factor contains one extra power of \(a/r\) compared with the relative angular order. At \(r=2a\) a quadrupole potential has fallen to one eighth of its surface value. An order-six pattern has fallen to \(1/128\). A 20 mV order-six component at a distant detector would require 2.56 V at the surface. A 20 mV instrumental uncertainty has exactly the same amplification if one attempts a surface reconstruction from that single radius.

Worked cancellation and a charge measurement

For a pure quadrupole set \(a=50\) mm and \(b_2=3.0\) V. The north-pole surface density is \(5\epsilon_0 b_2/a=2.66\) nC/m²; at the equator it is half that magnitude with opposite sign. The net charge vanishes because \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_2\,du=0\). A charge meter that captures the entire array would read zero while a local probe measures finite voltage and field. Net charge measures precisely the \(\ell=0\) coefficient.

Recognise when a latitude scan is insufficient

A painted voltage pattern can vary with longitude as well. A periodic azimuthal factor has form \(e^{im\phi}\) with integer \(m\). The extra angular term in Laplace’s equation introduces \(-m^2/(1-u^2)\) into the Legendre equation. Regular solutions are associated Legendre functions with \(|m|\le\ell\). Combined with the azimuthal factor and normalisation they form \(Y_{\ell m}\). For an orthonormal convention the coefficient is the surface integral of \(Y_{\ell m}^*f\) over solid angle. The radial powers remain unchanged. In particular, \(f\propto\sin\theta\cos\phi\) is a dipolar pattern oriented along the \(x\) direction. Averaging every latitude ring would completely erase it.

The boundary has a constant 1 V term, a 1 V dipole coefficient and an adjustable quadrupole. At half the sphere radius with a 2 V quadrupole, the pole reads 2.00 V and the equator 0.75 V. Compare inside and outside measurements with the same angular resolution.

Distinguish angular resolution from total charge

Locate a field null inside a sphere

Take \(b_1=1.2\) V, \(b_2=-1.0\) V, \(a=60\) mm, with all other coefficients zero. Find a point on the positive axis at which the full electric field vanishes.

Work through the solution

On the axis, symmetry sets transverse field to zero. The potential is \(1.2z/a-(z/a)^2\) volts. Its derivative vanishes at \(z/a=0.6\), or 36 mm. The local axial extremum is compatible with Laplace’s equation because transverse curvature has the opposite sign. The point is a saddle of the three-dimensional potential.

Is an interior maximum consistent with the measured boundary?

A code sampling a smooth, charge-free sphere reports 6.0 V at its centre; the largest voltage applied anywhere on the boundary is 4.0 V. Explain the discrepancy using the harmonics.

Work through the solution

At the centre all terms with \(\ell>0\) vanish. The remaining \(b_0\) equals the angular average of the boundary voltage and cannot exceed 4 V. The check isolates a cause to investigate: interior sources, incorrect boundary data or a defective discretisation. It does not require knowing the other coefficients.
