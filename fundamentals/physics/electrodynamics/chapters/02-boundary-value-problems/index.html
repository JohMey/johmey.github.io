From first principles

Why the conductor voltage determines the exterior field

In electrostatic equilibrium a mobile charge cannot have a tangential electric force along a conductor. The surface is therefore at one potential. A grounded wire fixes that potential at zero. In the surrounding vacuum \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\). Apply Gauss’s law to a small volume and use the divergence theorem:

\[\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_\Omega \rho/\epsilon_0\,dV,\]
\[\nabla^2V=-\rho/\epsilon_0 .\]

Why is it enough to find one potential with the correct sources and boundary values? Let \(w=V_1-V_2\) for any two candidates with the same charges. The source cancels, leaving \(\nabla^2 w=0\). Their common prescribed voltage gives \(w=0\) on the boundary. Integrate the product rule for \(\nabla\cdot(w\nabla w)\):

\[\int_\Omega |\nabla w|^2\,dV=\oint_{\partial\Omega}w\,\partial_n w\,dA-\int_\Omega w\nabla^2w\,dV=0.\]

The integrand is nonnegative, so the two gradients coincide throughout the domain. For an exterior problem we also prescribe decay at infinity. An image charge placed outside the physical solution domain can therefore generate the exact field inside it, provided the potential satisfies all of these conditions. The induced surface charge then follows from the outward normal field just outside the metal.

Derivation and applications

Find the image charge of a grounded sphere

Curvature changes both the location and the size of an electrostatic image. Place a point charge \(q\) a distance \(a\) from the centre of a grounded conducting sphere of radius \(R<a\). Take vacuum outside, zero potential at infinity and no other sources. Axial symmetry suggests placing a trial image \(q'\) on the same axis at distance \(b<R\). At a surface point making angle \(\theta\) with the source axis, let \(d\) and \(d'\) denote the distances to charge and image respectively.

\[d^2=R^2+a^2-2Ra\cos\theta,\]
\[d'^2=R^2+b^2-2Rb\cos\theta.\]

To cancel the potentials at every angle, the two distances must be proportional by one constant. Choose \(b=R^2/a\). Both the constant and cosine terms in \(d'^2\) then equal those in \(d^2\) times \(R^2/a^2\). This fixes the required image strength through the grounded boundary condition.

\[\frac{d'}d=\frac Ra,\]
\[\frac qd+\frac{q'}{d'}=0,\]
\[q'=-\frac Ra q.\]

This construction has the physical charge as its only exterior singularity. It decays at infinity and satisfies the prescribed surface voltage, so uniqueness fixes the exterior potential. A Gaussian surface enclosing the metal but excluding the source encloses exactly the image in the mathematical field. Its flux therefore identifies the net induced charge with \(q'\). The force on the source points towards the centre. Using \(k=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\) gives its radial component

\[F_a=\frac{kqq'}{(a-b)^2}=-\frac{kq^2Ra}{(a^2-R^2)^2}.\]

Measure the voltage that a grounded enclosure can hold inside

Imagine a long grounded square tube, one metre on each side. Uniformity along the tube reduces the electrostatic problem to its square cross section. A prescribed volume charge \(\rho_0\sin(\pi x/L)\sin(\pi y/L)\), with \(L=1\ \mathrm m\), vanishes at all four walls. Ground fixes the boundary voltage. Does that also fix the interior voltage to zero?

Gauss's law with \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) gives

\[-\nabla^2 V=\rho/\epsilon_0.\]

Each second derivative of the sine product contributes \(-(\pi/L)^2\) times that product. Thus \(V_\mathrm{centre}=\rho_0 L^2/(2\pi^2\epsilon_0)\). The numerical experiment replaces \(-\nabla^2V\) at each interior node by \((4V_C-V_N-V_S-V_E-V_W)/h^2\). The charge is a single eigenmode of that stencil, so one conjugate-gradient step from zero solves the discrete system exactly in exact arithmetic.

How a right-angle conducting corner shares an induced charge

Two grounded conducting planes meet at a right angle, occupying the faces \(x=0,\ y>0\) and \(y=0,\ x>0\). A long uniformly charged thin filament of line charge \(\lambda>0\) runs parallel to their intersection through position \((a,b)\), with \(a,b>0\). The vacuum region is the first quadrant. Quantities below are per unit length along the filament; its radius is negligible compared with both distances to the metal. Find the force on the filament and how the grounded faces divide the induced charge. Knowing that the two metal faces have identical voltage does not tell us how much charge each supplies.

Construct a potential that satisfies both metal faces

For an isolated line, Gauss's law gives

\[E_R=\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon_0R);\]

integration gives

\[V=-[\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon_0)]\ln(R/R_0).\]

A single line's potential has no zero reference at infinity. Our image system will have zero total line charge, so all arbitrary \(R_0\) factors cancel. Reflect the real source in the vertical face and reverse its sign. Reflect it independently in the horizontal face and reverse its sign again. Neither added source can be left without its own partner at the other face. The fourth line at \((-a,-b)\) must be positive. Denote distances to the real and image lines by \(R_{++},R_{-+},R_{+-},R_{--}\) following their coordinates. Then

\[V=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac{R_{-+}R_{+-}}{R_{++}R_{--}} .\]

At \(x=0\) distances cancel pairwise in the ratio, and the same happens at \(y=0\). The solution tends to zero at large distance and its only singularity in the physical domain is at the real filament. The grounded Dirichlet boundary and appropriate behaviour at the corner and infinity fix the field uniquely. If the faces were instead free to hold different voltages, this particular superposition alone would not solve their boundary conditions.

Read the signed force from three image fields

At the source position remove its own singular field. The vertical reflection attracts horizontally with field \(-\lambda/(4\pi\epsilon_0a)\); the horizontal reflection attracts vertically by the corresponding expression with \(b\). The doubly reflected positive image repels along \((a,b)\), partly compensating both attractions. Multiplying by \(\lambda\) gives

\[\frac{\mathbf F}{L}=\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\left(-\frac1a+\frac{a}{a^2+b^2}\right)\hat{\mathbf x}+\left(-\frac1b+\frac{b}{a^2+b^2}\right)\hat{\mathbf y}\right].\]

In the symmetric case \(a=b\) the two components are equal. Each is exactly half as large as the attraction to its corresponding isolated face. The second grounded conductor changes the charge distribution of the first; simply adding two single-plane forces would overestimate the force. If \(a\gg b\), the horizontal force towards the remote vertical wall decays as \(b^2/a^3\), while the downward force approaches its single-plane value. The charge and its nearest opposite image look more like a dipole to the distant face.

Find the charge split by two independent routes

On the bottom face the surface normal from metal into vacuum is \(+\hat{\mathbf y}\). Computing \(-\epsilon_0\partial_y V(x,0^+)\) gives

\[\sigma_{\rm bottom}(x)=-\frac{\lambda b}{\pi}\left[\frac1{(x-a)^2+b^2}-\frac1{(x+a)^2+b^2}\right],\quad x>0 .\]

The second term reduces the charge near the corner and forces the density to vanish there. Integration from zero to infinity uses the antiderivative \(b^{-1}\arctan[(x\mp a)/b]\). The two terms give \(-2\lambda\arctan(a/b)/\pi\). Exchanging \(a,b\) gives the vertical face charge. Their sum is \(-\lambda\) because the two acute arctangents add to \(\pi/2\). For \(a=20\) mm and \(b=10\) mm the bottom and vertical faces receive respectively 70.5% and 29.5% of the induced negative charge. The closer face collects the greater magnitude, although both are perfectly grounded.

A reciprocity argument provides a check with no surface differentiation. Remove the filament and solve an auxiliary problem with the vertical face at 1 V and the bottom at 0 V. In polar coordinates the bounded angular solution is \(w=2\theta/\pi\) after division by 1 V, because its second angular derivative vanishes and its face values agree. The singular junction between ideal differently biased electrodes belongs only to this auxiliary problem. The induced charge per length on the real vertical face is \(-\lambda w(a,b)=-2\lambda\arctan(b/a)/\pi\). This reproduces the integral result and interprets the measured charge split as an angular coordinate.

Anticipate the field near a corner before refining a mesh

Empty space immediately around the wedge vertex contains no real source if \(a,b>0\). In planar polar coordinates a separated harmonic function vanishing on the two faces has form \(r^\nu\sin(\nu\theta)\), with \(\nu=m\pi/\alpha\) for opening angle \(\alpha\) and positive integer \(m\). For the first-quadrant region \(\alpha=\pi/2\), the leading generic term is \(r^2\sin(2\theta)\). Its field tends to zero linearly with distance from the vertex. Expanding the explicit bottom-face density above around \(x=0\) indeed gives a term proportional to \(x\), providing a local check on the image construction.

It is easy to transfer an intuition about sharp metal tips to the wrong side of the metal. A conducting projection surrounded by a vacuum angle greater than \(\pi\) has a leading exponent below unity. Differentiating its potential then gives a field that can diverge as the ideal corner is approached. The inside corner of the two grounded planes in this problem has the opposite local behaviour. Mesh refinement near either corner should be informed by which opening angle belongs to the field domain.

Infer what two electrode meters can measure

The induced vertical charge accounts for exactly one quarter of the total magnitude. A second experiment scales both source coordinates by a common factor of two. Determine the locus of the original source, the new charge split and the force ratio. Which observation could distinguish the two radii?

Derive the result and check it independently

The auxiliary potential gives

\[\theta=\pi/8=22.5^\circ.\]

Hence \(b/a=\tan(\pi/8)=\sqrt2-1\) and any distance along that ray gives the same ideal charge split. Doubling \(a,b\) preserves that ratio. In the force formula every term scales inversely with length, so both force components halve at unchanged \(\lambda\). A force measurement or a resolved surface charge profile can distinguish the distances. Integrating each face first discarded that spatial information. In a numerical solution, compare both electrode integrals and the local force; matching a global charge split alone leaves the distance scale untested.

Trade unknown surface charge for known potential

A point charge held above a large grounded metal plane attracts charge towards the region beneath it. The induced surface density is nonuniform and initially unknown. Direct integration of Coulomb’s law would therefore introduce the very quantity one wants to determine. In a boundary-value formulation the potential of the metal is known instead. Assume a perfectly conducting plane at \(z=0\), vacuum above it and a positive point charge \(q\) at \(z=a>0\). Grounding fixes the plane at zero potential and allows charge to enter or leave it.

Away from the point source the potential obeys Laplace’s equation, and at the charge its singular behaviour is the Coulomb potential. These properties with the boundary values determine a unique solution. To see why, subtract two candidate solutions with the same charges and Dirichlet boundary conditions. Their difference \(w\) has zero Laplacian and vanishes on the boundary. Integrating \(\nabla\cdot(w\nabla w)=|\nabla w|^2+w\nabla^2w\) over the domain gives zero net surface term, including the limit at infinity for appropriately decaying fields. Hence the integral of \(|\nabla w|^2\) vanishes and \(w=0\) throughout.

Construct the image

Put a fictitious charge \(-q\) at \(z=-a\). At any point on the plane its distance equals the distance to the actual charge, so the two potentials cancel pointwise. In the upper half-space the fictitious source lies outside the solution domain. The combined potential has the correct single singularity, is zero on the metal and decays at infinity. By uniqueness it is the upper-half-space solution. With \(s\) the horizontal distance from the projection of the charge,

\[V(s,z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2+(z-a)^2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2+(z+a)^2}}\right].\]

It is important to keep the domain in view. The expression continued below \(z=0\) describes an auxiliary mathematical problem. Inside the real conductor electrostatic equilibrium gives a constant potential and zero field. The fictitious charge is a way to reproduce the valid exterior data, rather than a literal localisation of the induced charge.

Recover the induced charge and force

The field just outside a conducting surface has no tangential component. Its outward normal component is \(\sigma_s/\epsilon_0\) because the field inside is zero. Differentiate the potential with respect to height and set \(z=0^+\) to obtain

\[\sigma_s(s)=-qa/[2\pi(s^2+a^2)^{3/2}].\]

Integrating over rings \(2\pi s\,ds\) from zero to infinity gives total induced charge \(-q\). A nearby grounded conductor connected to a reservoir can supply that total charge even though its potential remains fixed.

At the position of the real charge its own singular field is excluded when calculating force. The induced field equals that of the image at distance \(2a\), so the attraction has magnitude \(q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a^2)\). Integrating the force from infinity gives interaction energy \(-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a)\). Simply multiplying the image potential at the charge by \(q\) would double the magnitude because the induced distribution adjusts as the charge moves. The work integral automatically includes the changing response.

Quantify the localisation

Take \(q=1.0\) nC and \(a=20\) mm. The force magnitude is \(5.62\) µN. The surface density directly beneath the charge is \(-q/(2\pi a^2)=-0.398\) µC/m². Within a disk of radius \(S\) the integral gives \(-q[1-a/\sqrt{S^2+a^2}]\). Setting the fraction in brackets equal to one half yields

\[S=\sqrt3\,a=34.6\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

The density peaks directly below the charge, but a disk wider than its height is needed to collect half the total.

Bring the charge twice as close while keeping \(q\) fixed. Both the peak density and the attraction quadruple, while the radius containing half the induced charge halves. This spatial prediction is sensitive to finite plate size. A real plate with a radius comparable to \(a\) cannot reproduce the infinite-plane surface distribution far from the centre, even when the potential at its surface is tightly controlled.

Only the half-space accessible to the physical charge enters the field-energy comparison. Extending the mathematical solution through the grounded boundary introduces a second singular source whose mechanical motion has no counterpart in the laboratory. This is especially important when computing energy by moving the real charge toward the plate.

How a patterned electrode is smoothed by distance

A grounded plane is one boundary condition. Another laboratory problem specifies a different potential at each position along a plane, using many narrow electrodes separated by small insulating gaps. Model the ideal limit by a prescribed function \(V(x,0)\) in vacuum occupying \(z>0\), uniform along \(y\). Away from charges electrostatic potential obeys \(\partial_x^2V+\partial_z^2V=0\). First impose \(V(x,0)=V_k\cos(kx)\) with \(k>0\) and require decay at large \(z\). Separability suggests \(V(x,z)=V_k\cos(kx)Z(z)\). Then

\[Z''-k^2 Z=0.\]

Discard the growing exponential and set \(Z(0)=1\).

\[V(x,z)=V_k e^{-kz}\cos(kx),\]
\[E_z=kV_ke^{-kz}\cos(kx).\]

Each sinusoidal boundary component is transmitted with a factor \(e^{-kz}\). By linearity a general periodic pattern decomposes into such harmonics, each decaying according to its own wavenumber. The constant component fixes the voltage reference or background field separately. Smoothing with height follows directly from Laplace's equation in a source-free half-space. It places a physical limit on how well a distant probe can resolve narrow electrode features even before instrument noise is considered.

Predict a non-sinusoidal surface measurement

Let a fabricated pattern have period \(d=4.00\) mm with a fundamental voltage amplitude of 2.00 V and a third harmonic of 0.600 V, both cosine waves with maxima at \(x=0\). At height \(z=0.500\) mm the attenuation exponents are

\[(2\pi/d)z=0.785,\]
\[3(2\pi/d)z=2.356.\]

Hence the voltage amplitudes there are

\[2.00e^{-0.785}=0.912\,\mathrm{V},\]
\[0.600e^{-2.356}=0.0569\,\mathrm{V}.\]

The third-to-first amplitude ratio has fallen from 0.300 at the metal to 0.0624.

A probe sensitive instead to the normal field differentiates in \(z\) and multiplies each voltage component by its wavenumber. At the same height the fundamental contributes \(1.43\times10^3\) V/m and the third harmonic contributes \(268\) V/m at the shared maximum. Reporting a field ratio as though it were a voltage ratio would therefore exaggerate the surviving high spatial frequencies by a factor of three. Inverting the voltage law to recover the fabrication pattern multiplies measurement noise by \(e^{kz}\). Repeating a scan at two known heights allows a direct test: the ratio for each Fourier component should follow its own exponential independently of the unknown electrode voltage. A ground plane placed nearby on the other side would change the far boundary condition and require a different transfer factor.

For the same apparatus, suppose detector noise permits relative voltage harmonics down to one percent of their surface amplitudes. A harmonic remains individually detectable only if \(e^{-kz}>0.01\). At a height of 0.500 mm this requires spatial wavelength greater than \(2\pi z/\ln100=0.682\) mm at that noise level. The limit is a measurement criterion, not a strict cutoff in Laplace's equation: every finite wavelength has a nonzero transfer at finite height. An improved signal-to-noise ratio extends the accessible range only logarithmically. Reducing the probe height can therefore improve recovery more effectively than merely accumulating many additional scans.

Which electrode details reach a remote probe?

Set the potential on an infinite plane to a spatial sinusoid of wavelength \(L/n\), with no free charge above it. Separation in Laplace’s equation gives an exponentially decaying height envelope \(e^{-2\pi n y/L}\) for the bounded solution.
