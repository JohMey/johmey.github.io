What the two unused current terminals buy us

Place four point contacts on a straight line on the top of a uniform conducting sample. Drive current \(+I\) into the first contact and remove it at the fourth. Attach an ideal high-impedance voltmeter to the second and third contacts. Equal probe spacing is called \(s\); the sample thickness is \(h\). Record \(V=\phi_2-\phi_3\) with the sign convention that positive conventional current flows toward lower electric potential. We seek the resistance of the actual measurement \(V/I\), rather than the terminal resistance between the current contacts.

The voltage lost across a current contact can be large because all the drive current passes through it. That loss does not enter the ideal difference between the inner probe potentials. Likewise a contact resistance in series with an ideal voltmeter has zero \(IR\) drop. The separation does not eliminate all geometric uncertainties. A voltage contact with appreciable area can average the field or perturb it, a voltmeter with finite input impedance draws a small current, and an edge in the conducting specimen redirects current. Derive first what an ideal experiment would do before introducing corrections for a manufactured sample.

In the ohmic, low-frequency limit \(\mathbf J=-\sigma\nabla\phi\) with constant scalar conductivity \(\sigma=1/\rho\). Conservation in the conducting interior means \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\). A point current lead on an otherwise insulating surface is a boundary flux. Unlike a prescribed voltage, it fixes the strength of a source without fixing its absolute potential. Only currents and differences should survive an arbitrary additive constant. This will be a useful test when we introduce infinitely many reflected current sources.

A spreading experiment in three dimensions

First make the sample so thick that the lower face is inaccessible to the current on the scale of the probes. Around a point input on the surface of a conducting half-space consider a hemisphere of radius \(r\). All its outward current is \(I\); rotational symmetry plus reflection across the ideal upper plane produces a radial field of magnitude \(J=I/(2\pi r^2)\). Integrate \(E_r=\rho J=-d\phi/dr\) inward from infinity. The surface potential relative to infinity is \(\rho I/(2\pi r)\). Subtract a second copy centred on the sink.

At probe 2 the distances from source and sink are \(s\) and \(2s\), respectively. Its potential is \(+\rho I/(4\pi s)\); symmetry gives the opposite sign at probe 3. Therefore

\[R_{\rm bulk}=\frac{V}{I}=\frac{\rho}{2\pi s}.\]

A bulk resistivity in ohm metres can be extracted by multiplying the transfer resistance by \(2\pi s\) in metres. The inverse spacing is not a contact effect. It is the result of spreading current over an area that grows quadratically with radius. In particular, doubling \(s\) halves the measured resistance of a sufficiently thick sample. This scaling is a check independent of the calibration of a fixed current source as long as it remains stable across both measurements.

Confine the same current to a thin sheet

If current rapidly becomes uniform through a thin specimen, the spreading area is instead the circumference of a circle times \(h\). Thus \(J_r=I/(2\pi r h)\). The integral of \(1/r\) is logarithmic and contains an arbitrary reference length. A single source in an infinite sheet cannot be assigned zero potential at infinity by a finite constant. The source–sink pair poses no difficulty because the reference cancels from the potential difference. With \(R_s=\rho/h\) and source and sink distances \(r_+\) and \(r_-\), the sheet potential can be written as

\[\phi=\frac{I R_s}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_-}{r_+},\]
\[R_{\rm thin}=\frac{R_s\ln 2}{\pi}.\]

At the two inner contacts the ratios are two and one half. The logarithms are equal and opposite, which supplies the extra factor of two. Doubling the probe spacing now leaves the measured transfer resistance unchanged. The name ohms per square refers to \(R_s\), the resistance across opposite complete edges of a square with uniform flow; a four-point probe is a different arrangement and its resistance is smaller by \(\ln 2/\pi\). Attaching the word square to a current probe reading without this conversion would produce a systematic error.

Reflect the current from an insulating lower face

A real film has an upper surface and a back surface. Assume both prevent normal current except at the upper contacts. Reflecting a source with the same sign across an insulating plane cancels the normal derivative there. Continuing the reflection between both parallel faces generates current images displaced vertically by \(2nh\). A grounded conducting backing would impose a different boundary condition and cannot be represented by these signs. On the top surface the half-space spreading result therefore acquires a sequence of image distances. Define \(z=s/h\). After subtracting the source and sink contributions at the inner probes one obtains

\[\frac{h}{\rho}\frac{V}{I}=\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{1}{2z}+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{z^2+4n^2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{4z^2+4n^2}}\right)\right].\]

Every term in the remaining sum is positive: a nearby reflecting back increases the measured resistance relative to the infinite bulk expression. Individual absolute image potentials have slowly diverging harmonic tails; the paired differences converge. To see why, use \(1/a-1/b=(b^2-a^2)/[ab(a+b)]\). With the two square roots above the numerator is exactly \(3z^2\), independent of \(n\), while the denominator grows as \(n^3\). This form suppresses subtractive cancellation and makes an image-cutoff refinement test meaningful.

The thin-film limit can also be read from the sum without the original sheet argument. For large \(z\) the image spacing is small compared with the distances. Replace the sum over positive and negative images by an integral along the vertical coordinate with density \(1/(2h)\). Integrating the difference of two inverse square roots gives the difference of two inverse hyperbolic sines. At a large upper limit only the ratio of the lateral distances survives. Their ratio is two, returning \(\ln 2\). This second derivation identifies thickness averaging as a long-distance limit of a three-dimensional field, rather than a separate electrical transport law.

Take \(\rho=2.0\times10^{-5}\) ohm m, \(h=20\) micrometres and \(s=80\) micrometres. The sheet resistance is 1.00 ohm per square. For a 5.0 mA current the thin-sheet formula predicts \(V=1.1032\) mV. Summing the images changes this prediction by only a few parts per million at \(s/h=4\). A bulk interpretation would instead give \(2\pi s V/I\simeq1.11\times10^{-4}\) ohm m, more than five times the true resistivity. Both conversions have the right dimensions and both produce a positive, plausible laboratory number. Their different response to spacing supplies the experimental distinction.

Tutorial on offsets, symmetry and geometry

Current reversal at a fixed spacing gives +1.112 mV at +5.00 mA and −1.088 mV at −5.00 mA. Find the resistive signal and the current-independent offset. Assuming a thin sample, infer the sheet resistance.

Work through the solution

Half the difference is 1.100 mV. Half the sum is the offset, 0.012 mV. The transfer resistance is 0.2200 ohm. Multiplication by π/ln 2 gives about 0.997 ohm per square. If the temperature changes during the reversal cycle, a drifting thermoelectric offset may survive this subtraction.

The current contacts are shifted outward so that the two inner probes have distances \(a,b\) from the left source and \(b,a\) from the right sink, with \(b>a\). Derive the thin-sheet conversion.

Work through the solution

The two inner potentials are opposite and have magnitude \(IR_s\ln(b/a)/(2\pi)\). The differential resistance is \(R_s\ln(b/a)/\pi\). Equal neighbouring spacings give \(b/a=2\). The sign of the measured voltage reverses if the two potential leads are exchanged, while the physical sheet resistance remains positive.

Why can current and voltage terminal pairs be interchanged in an ideal ohmic specimen, and what is a useful check if their readings differ?

Work through the solution

The linear scalar conduction operator with insulating external walls is reciprocal. Integrating the two field equations against the potential of the other experiment, then subtracting, leaves only terminal terms; symmetry of the conductivity cancels the interior contribution. This equates the two transfer resistances using consistent orientations. Check damping of instrument transients, finite voltage input current and polarity reversal before assigning a difference to the material. A magnetic-field-induced antisymmetric conductivity requires a different reciprocity comparison.
