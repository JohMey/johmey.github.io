Specify all the boundaries before searching for a formula

A long rectangular cross-section occupies \(0<x<a,\ y>0\). The side walls are grounded, the bottom edge carries a prescribed voltage \(f(x)\), and the potential decays as \(y\) grows. Physically the bottom edge may be an array of narrow driven electrodes separated by insulating gaps. The idealisation prescribes voltage in the limit of small gaps. There is no free charge in the interior and the dielectric is homogeneous. The problem is thus

\[V_{xx}+V_{yy}=0.\]

A single field line or Gaussian surface does not tell us how a complicated \(f\) propagates upwards.

Try one product \(X(x)Y(y)\). Dividing the equation by the product gives

\[X''/X=-Y''/Y.\]

Each side depends on a different variable, so both must be constant. Write the first constant as \(-k^2\). Testing zero and positive values for \(X''/X\) yields only the trivial solution satisfying both grounded sides. The negative choice yields

\[X=A\sin kx+B\cos kx.\]

The left wall sets \(B=0\); the right wall requires

\[k_n=n\pi/a,\]
\[n=1,2,\ldots.\]

For a fixed \(n\), \(Y\) is a sum of growing and decaying exponentials. The far boundary eliminates the growing branch.

Determine the coefficients by asking one question per mode

No individual mode can fit general data at the base. Add the admissible products and enforce the bottom voltage. Multiply by \(\sin(m\pi x/a)\) and integrate over the width. Products of distinct sine modes average to zero, while the squared sine integrates to \(a/2\). These measurements recover independent coefficients:

\[b_n=\frac2a\int_0^a f(x)\sin(n\pi x/a)\,dx,\]
\[V(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin(n\pi x/a)e^{-n\pi y/a}.\]

Physically every extra oscillation across the base shortens the vertical penetration length. For \(a=40\) mm the first mode decays by a factor \(e\) every 12.7 mm; the third takes only 4.24 mm. Complicated boundary structure can leave a very simple interior field.

Worked cancellation that moves with height

Drive the base with \(f=3\sin(\pi x/a)+3\sin(3\pi x/a)\) volts. At the centre the two boundary voltages cancel since \(\sin(3\pi/2)=-1\). Above it the voltage becomes \(3(e^{-t}-e^{-3t})\) with \(t=\pi y/a\). Differentiate to locate its maximum: \(-e^{-t}+3e^{-3t}=0\) gives

\[t=\frac12\ln3.\]

Thus \(y=0.175a\) and the reading reaches \(2/\sqrt3=1.155\) V, even above a point set to zero volts. A full solution at the centre depends on the surrounding boundary data.

Predict the influence of a finite electrode

Place a 5 V electrode on the central half of the base and ground the remainder. Direct integration yields

\[b_n=\frac{10\ {\rm V}}{n\pi}\big[\cos(n\pi/4)-\cos(3n\pi/4)\big].\]

Even modes vanish by reflection symmetry. The first two nonzero coefficients are \(b_1=4.50\) V and \(b_3=-1.50\) V. At \(x=a/2,y=a/4\), their combined central reading is

\[4.50e^{-\pi/4}+1.50e^{-3\pi/4}=2.19\,\mathrm{V}.\]

A conservative bound on the discarded tail uses \(|b_n|\le20/(n\pi)\) V. Summing a geometric bound from \(n=5\) gives a maximum error of \(20e^{-5\pi/4}/[5\pi(1-e^{-\pi/4})]\approx0.046\) V. Away from the base this is an inexpensive error certificate.

At the electrode edge itself the series approaches the midpoint of the voltage jump and overshoots nearby when truncated. A real electrode has a gap and finite edge radius. Estimating the maximum surface field from this discontinuous idealisation makes the result depend on the chosen resolution. An interior voltage at a specified positive height is much better conditioned.

Change a physical boundary and rebuild the mode

An enclosure of finite height \(H\) with a grounded lid needs a mode which vanishes at \(H\) and equals one at the base. Solving the same vertical ODE now gives

\[Y_n=\sinh[k_n(H-y)]/\sinh(k_nH).\]

The transverse eigenvalues stay fixed because the side walls have not moved. For \(H=a/2\) and \(y=H/2\), the fundamental is transmitted with factor \(\sinh(\pi/4)/\sinh(\pi/2)=0.377\) instead of the open-strip value \(e^{-\pi/4}=0.456\). This difference is a way to diagnose an unmodelled nearby conductor.

Move a measurement plane away from patterned electrodes

The first boundary harmonic is fixed at 3 V. Set the third to 3 V as well. At the midpoint the boundary values cancel; 0.18 strip widths above it the two terms leave about 1.15 V. Follow the two curves to see which boundary details survive propagation into the gap.

How accurately could the third harmonic be recovered?

At \(y=a/2\) an instrument can resolve a 10 mV Fourier amplitude. How large must the third harmonic at the base be? Compare the fundamental.

Work through the solution

Third-mode attenuation is \(e^{-3\pi/2}=0.00898\), so about \(1.11\) V is needed at the boundary. The fundamental needs only \(0.010e^{\pi/2}=0.0481\) V. Back-propagating a noisy field magnifies its finest scales first. Improving the number of model harmonics cannot compensate for a missing signal.

Check the energy of one harmonic

The base drives \(b\sin(kx)\) for one admissible \(k\). Integrate the electric energy per unit length perpendicular to the strip and check its dimensions.

Work through the solution

The two field components are \(-kb\cos(kx)e^{-ky}\) and \(kb\sin(kx)e^{-ky}\), so their squares sum to \(k^2b^2e^{-2ky}\). Integrating \(\epsilon E^2/2\) across \(a\) and upwards to infinity gives \(U'=\epsilon a k b^2/4\), in J/m. At fixed boundary amplitude a shorter wavelength stores more energy near the base despite penetrating less far.
