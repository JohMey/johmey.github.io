How a magnet produces an exterior field

Add the fields of tiny current loops

A small current loop produces a dipole vector potential \(\mu_0\mathbf m\times\hat{\mathbf R}/(4\pi R^2)\) far from that loop. Coarse-grain a material into a dipole moment per volume \(\mathbf M\), in A/m. With \(\nabla'(1/R)=\hat{\mathbf R}/R^2\), the potential becomes an integral of \(\mathbf M\times\nabla'(1/R)\). Apply the curl product rule to \(\nabla'\times(\mathbf M/R)\), then integrate the curl over the body. For outward normal \(\mathbf n\) this gives

\[\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left[\int_\Omega\frac{\nabla'\times\mathbf M}{R}\,d^3r'+\oint_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathbf M\times\mathbf n}{R}\,dS'\right].\]

Comparison with the vector potential of conduction currents identifies \(\mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M\) and \(\mathbf K_b=\mathbf M\times\mathbf n\). These currents are a representation of the material dipoles. Neighbouring equal loop currents cancel where they share a boundary. At the outer surface a current remains unpaired. The curl measures how cancellation fails within a nonuniform specimen.

Worked magnetised cylinder

Let a very long cylinder of radius \(a\) have uniform axial \(M_0\hat{\mathbf z}\). Its bound volume current vanishes. Its side carries \(K_\phi=M_0\), and its flat end faces carry none in this model. The remaining current acts like a long solenoid. The internal field far from the ends is \(B_z=\mu_0 M_0\), while the exterior field tends to zero in the infinite-length limit. With \(M_0=4.0\times10^4\) A/m this gives 50.3 mT. A finite specimen has a return field near and beyond its ends; the far field of a finite magnet must eventually be dipolar.

What a loop linking a winding actually counts

Subtracting \(\mathbf J_b\) from magnetostatic Ampère’s law gives \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_{\rm free}\) with \(\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu_0-\mathbf M\). A rectangular loop across an interface yields \(\mathbf n\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_{\rm free}\), while \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\) guarantees continuous normal \(B\). In a region free of conduction currents and with suitable topology one can introduce a magnetic scalar potential \(\mathbf H=-\nabla\phi_m\). Its divergence follows from \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf H=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf M\). This alternative calculation uses the same magnetisation as the current description. Both predict identical \(\mathbf B\).

Derive the demagnetising field of a sphere

Put a sphere of radius \(a\) with fixed uniform \(\mathbf M=M\hat{\mathbf z}\) in vacuum and assume no applied field. Inside and outside, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf M=0\). The angular structure at the surface is \(M\cos\theta\), so use \(\phi_i=C r\cos\theta\) and \(\phi_o=C a^3\cos\theta/r^2\). They are harmonic, regular at the origin or at infinity, and continuous at \(a\); continuity ensures equal tangential \(H\). At the surface their outward fields are \(H_{i,r}=-C\cos\theta\) and \(H_{o,r}=2C\cos\theta\). With normal \(B\) continuous, \(2C=-C+M\), hence

\[\mathbf H_i=-\tfrac13\mathbf M,\]
\[\mathbf B_i=\tfrac23\mu_0\mathbf M .\]

The factor \(1/3\) encodes specimen shape. A very long axial cylinder with the same magnetisation has almost zero internal \(H\) far from its ends. The sphere’s exterior scalar potential corresponds to a dipole moment equal to magnetisation times volume.

Worked susceptibility seen from outside

For a reversible linear sphere with \(M=\chi H_i\) in a uniform applied field \(H_0\), superposition adds the applied field and the demagnetising contribution: \(H_i=H_0-M/3\). Solving yields

\[M/H_0=\chi/(1+\chi/3).\]

If a moment measurement gives \(M/H_0=1.50\), the intrinsic susceptibility is 3.0. As \(\chi\) grows, \(M/H_0\) approaches 3, and the inversion becomes very sensitive to moment uncertainty. Using \(B=\mu H\) without specifying where the field is measured can conceal this shape dependence.

Separate reversible energy from cycle loss

When ferromagnetic domains rearrange, \(B\) can depend on the prior excursion of \(H\). A single constant susceptibility then cannot describe an entire cycle. In a slow, approximately uniform closed magnetic circuit the source supplies incremental energy density \(H\,dB\). Returning to the initial magnetic state leaves the signed cycle integral \(\oint H\,dB\); its dissipated energy per volume is the positive area for the driven loop orientation. A measured loop area of 180 J/m³ at 50 cycles/s would dissipate 9.0 kW/m³, or 0.090 W in \(10\) cm³. Eddy-current loss can also enlarge a measured dynamic loop, so a rate sweep helps interpret that result.

Infer material response from an external field

The sphere reference curve gives an apparent susceptibility of 2.25 at intrinsic susceptibility 9. The adjustable ellipsoid starts at N = 0.33, giving 2.27. Try smaller demagnetising factors for elongated ellipsoids magnetised along their long axes. Determine when an apparent response would severely underestimate the intrinsic one.

Test a surface-current picture

What current remains if magnetisation falls towards the rim?

Take a long cylinder with \(M_z=M_0(1-s^2/a^2)\) inside. Find the azimuthal bound currents and the field on its axis.

Work through the solution

Cylindrical curl gives

\[J_{b,\phi}=-dM_z/ds=2M_0s/a^2.\]

At \(s=a\) the surface current is zero. Regard a shell \(ds\) as a small solenoidal sheet \(K=J_{b,\phi}ds\). At the axis all shells contribute, giving \(B_z(0)=\mu_0\int_0^a J_{b,\phi}\,ds=\mu_0M_0\). Far from the ends the radial profile inside is \(B_z(s)=\mu_0M_z(s)\).

Invert a nearly saturated shape response

If \(M/H_0=2.70\) for a linear sphere, what is \(\chi\)? Estimate how an absolute uncertainty of 0.03 in the measured ratio propagates.

Work through the solution

Writing \(r=M/H_0\), one finds

\[\chi=r/(1-r/3)=27.\]

Its derivative is \((1-r/3)^{-2}=100\), so the local uncertainty is about 3. Distinguishing two high susceptibilities requires precise specimen geometry and much better relative accuracy than the measured moment alone suggests.

From microscopic moments to measured magnetisation · Magnetism, induction and field dynamics

A magnetised sample can make a compass turn without a power supply attached. The macroscopic theory assigns it bound currents in its bulk and on its surface. These are calculated sources for the magnetic field. We will reconstruct them from magnetic moments, predict signals from finite samples, and ask what an exterior field measurement can identify.
