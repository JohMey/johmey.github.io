From first principles

How turns, permeability and a gap set the flux

Wind \(N\) turns around a closed magnetic core and drive a steady current \(I\). Use a core of length \(\ell_c\) and constant cross-section \(A\), interrupted by an air gap of length \(g\). Assume a linear core of permeability \(\mu_c\), a nearly uniform field through each cross-section and negligible leakage and fringing. Ampère’s law counts the linked current along a loop. Flux continuity through a thin pillbox at the interface gives a common normal \(B\):

\[H_c\ell_c+H_g g=NI,\]
\[B=\mu_c H_c=\mu_0 H_g.\]

The material relation \(B=\mu H\) is experimental input. Insert each material’s field into the loop balance, then multiply by area to obtain the flux \(\Phi=BA\):

\[\Phi\left(\frac{\ell_c}{\mu_c A}+\frac{g}{\mu_0 A}\right)=NI .\]

The bracket is the reluctance. Each turn links the same flux, so the flux linkage is \(N\Phi\). For a linear core the coil inductance follows from flux linkage per current:

\[L=\frac{N\Phi}{I}=\frac{N^2 A}{\ell_c/\mu_c+g/\mu_0}.\]

A gap can control \(L\) even when it occupies little of the magnetic path. Compare \(g/\mu_0\) with \(\ell_c/\mu_c\) before neglecting it. If the core saturates, the field-to-current ratio changes with the operating current.

Derivation and applications

Derive the inductance measured by a small alternating signal

A winding on a biased core can give different answers to two seemingly ordinary inductance measurements. Consider a narrow toroid with \(N\) turns, uniform cross-section \(A\), core path length \(\ell\) and one short air gap \(g\). Assume negligible leakage, fringing and hysteresis. Allow the core law \(H_c(B)\) to be nonlinear but single-valued. Flux conservation makes the induction \(B\) common to gap and core, while Ampère's law adds their distinct field integrals.

\[NI=\ell H_c(B)+\frac g{\mu_0}B,\]
\[\Lambda=NAB.\]

Here \(\Lambda\) is flux linkage. A slowly varying current superposed on a constant bias probes only a small interval of this curve. Differentiate the circulation equation about the operating induction \(B_0\) and define the differential permeability \(\mu_d=(dH_c/dB)^{-1}\) evaluated there.

\[N\,dI=\left(\frac{\ell}{\mu_d}+\frac g{\mu_0}\right)dB,\]
\[L_d=\frac{d\Lambda}{dI}=\frac{N^2A}{\ell/\mu_d+g/\mu_0}.\]

The induced terminal voltage after subtracting winding resistance is \(d\Lambda/dt\). Thus the locally measured inductance is the slope. A DC current and a flux measurement instead give the chord joining the bias point to the origin. Writing \(\mu_s=B_0/H_c(B_0)\) makes that expression

\[L_s=\frac{\Lambda_0}{I_0}=\frac{N^2A}{\ell/\mu_s+g/\mu_0}.\]

These definitions coincide for a straight constitutive curve through the origin. A large air gap also makes the two estimates converge because the same linear air term then dominates each denominator.

Worked example

Predict the response to a sinusoidal probe

Take \(N=100\), \(A=100\) mm², \(\ell=100\) mm and \(g=0.500\) mm. At the selected bias suppose \(\mu_d=200\mu_0\) and \(\mu_s=1000\mu_0\). The incremental effective gap is \(0.500+100/200=1.00\) mm, giving \(L_d=1.26\) mH. The chord yields \(2.09\) mH. A 1.00 kHz current of 10.0 mA peak therefore produces a reactive voltage of \(2\pi f L_d I=79.0\) mV peak, provided the excursion stays within the nearly constant local slope.

Second application

Infer the material slope from a voltage ramp

A second winding has \(N=80\), \(A=150\) mm², \(\ell=120\) mm and \(g=0.200\) mm. An inductive voltage of 96.0 mV during a 40.0 A/s ramp gives \(L_d=2.40\) mH. Reversing the slope formula yields \(\ell/(\mu_d/\mu_0)=\mu_0 N^2A/L_d-g=0.303\) mm, so \(\mu_d/\mu_0\simeq396\). The positive residual is a check on subtracting the gap contribution before attributing the measured slope to the core.

Specify the path taken by the field

A toroidal winding wraps a ring of magnetic material. Large permeability can guide flux around the ring. Cutting a small gap into it can greatly reduce inductance. To understand this change we need to distinguish the field produced by free current from the magnetic response of the material. Write \(\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M)\), where \(\mathbf M\) is magnetic moment per unit volume. For a linear isotropic material, \(\mathbf B=\mu_0\mu_r\mathbf H\). The approximation of a single constant \(\mu_r\) requires operation below strong saturation and negligible hysteresis over the field excursion.

Treat the cross-section as narrow compared with the mean radius and assume tightly distributed turns. Let the material path length be \(\ell_c\), gap length \(g\) and common area \(A\). Neglect stray flux and fringing as a first approximation when the gap is short compared with the transverse dimensions. These choices reduce a spatially varying field to one representative flux through each cross-section. They do not imply equal values of \(H\) in the two materials.

Calculate a coil field without an assumed magnetic path

Ampère's circulation law simplifies when symmetry makes the field uniform along a useful closed path. For a circular wire loop in free space, symmetry does not supply such a path through its aperture. In vacuum a steady thin-wire current instead contributes according to Biot–Savart, \(d\mathbf B=(\mu_0 I/4\pi)d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R/R^3\), where \(\mathbf R\) points from the wire element to the observation point. The formula assumes a complete steady circuit. For a laboratory coil the distant return leads should contribute little to the region being observed.

Take a circular loop of radius \(a\) and observe on its axis at coordinate \(z\). All wire elements share distance \(\sqrt{a^2+z^2}\). Their transverse contributions cancel in opposite pairs. The axial component of \(d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R\) is \(a\,d\ell\), and \(\oint d\ell=2\pi a\). For \(N\) tightly spaced turns the sum yields

\[B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0NIa^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.\]

A 50-turn coil of radius 40.0 mm carrying 0.800 A produces 0.628 mT at the centre. One radius away the ratio is \(2^{-3/2}=0.354\), giving 0.222 mT. Far along the axis the field falls as the inverse cube and is proportional to the magnetic moment \(NI\pi a^2\). Such a falloff is absent from a lumped magnetic circuit with a single prescribed uniform field. Which model is appropriate depends on whether a core guides most of the flux or the winding produces a field through open space.

Apply the two circulation and flux laws

Magnetic flux continuity follows from \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\). With negligible leakage, the same \(\Phi=BA\) crosses the core and the gap. Ampère’s law around the ring gives \(H_c\ell_c+H_g g=NI\) for \(N\) turns carrying current \(I\). Substitute \(H_c=B/(\mu_0\mu_r)\) and \(H_g=B/\mu_0\) to find

\[B=\frac{\mu_0NI}{g+\ell_c/\mu_r},\qquad \Phi=\frac{NI}{\ell_c/(\mu_0\mu_r A)+g/(\mu_0 A)}.\]

Each denominator contribution in the flux expression is called a reluctance. The sum follows from the distinct magnetic potential drops along one flux path. A gap as short as \(\ell_c/\mu_r\) accounts for half the total circulation even though it occupies only a small fraction of the length. For a ring of appreciable radial width, the ungapped toroidal field varies as \(1/r\) and flux should be integrated over the cross-section.

Link field energy to an electrical measurement

A coil’s flux linkage is \(N\Phi\) when each turn links the same flux. For a fixed, linear geometry \(L=N\Phi/I\). The voltage needed to raise the current is \(v=L\,dI/dt\) in the passive sign convention, omitting winding resistance. The power delivered to the magnetic field is \(vi\). Integrating as the current grows gives stored energy \(W=LI^2/2\). Equivalently, integrating \(B^2/(2\mu)\) over each region apportions this energy between the gap and the core.

Since the same \(B\) passes through both regions but their permeabilities differ, a unit volume of gap stores \(\mu_r\) times the energy of the same volume of core in this model. The ratio of total gap energy to total core energy is \(g\mu_r/\ell_c\). If permeability depends on field, a small-signal inductance measured around a bias is the derivative \(d(N\Phi)/dI\). It can differ from the ratio \(N\Phi/I\) inferred from a single point.

Estimate a coil with a machined gap

Choose \(N=100\), \(I=0.40\) A, \(\ell_c=0.20\) m, \(\mu_r=2000\), \(A=1.0\) cm² and \(g=0.50\) mm. The effective length in the field denominator is \(0.50+0.10=0.60\) mm. Thus \(B=0.0838\) T, \(\Phi=8.38\) µWb and \(L=2.09\) mH. The gap contributes five sixths of the circulation and stores five sixths of the \(0.168\) mJ total energy.

Closing the gap at the same current would predict \(0.503\) T from the linear law. The calculation itself supplies a reason to consult the real material curve before accepting that number. A measured inductance that departs from the prediction may reflect core saturation, imperfect contact at the joint or fringing that changes the effective gap area. Measuring a small current sweep at several gap widths helps distinguish material response from the geometry of a single fixed gap.

The current-to-flux ratio and the incremental response need not agree near magnetic saturation. If \(\lambda\) is the linked flux, a slow voltage measurement detects \(d\lambda/dt=(d\lambda/dI)\dot I\), so small AC perturbations about a DC operating point report the local slope. In contrast, taking a single ratio \(\lambda/I\) effectively joins that point to the origin. The two are equal in the linear magnetic-circuit model derived above. Deliberately sweeping the bias provides an operational way to find where that model stops describing the core.

Two windings sharing a flux path

A second coil wound on the same magnetic core allows the flux model to predict mutual induction. Choose both positive winding directions so currents create flux around the core in the same sense. In a linear core of reluctance \(\mathcal R\) with negligible leakage, the magnetic flux is \(\Phi=(N_1I_1+N_2I_2)/\mathcal R\). Each turn links that same flux. Multiplying by the number of turns in winding 1 or 2 gives flux linkages \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\), hence

\[\lambda_1=L_1I_1+MI_2,\qquad \lambda_2=MI_1+L_2I_2,\qquad M=\frac{N_1N_2}{\mathcal R},\quad L_i=\frac{N_i^2}{\mathcal R}.\]

Equality of the two mutual coefficients expresses reciprocity in this linear, stationary arrangement. With imperfect flux linkage one often writes \(M=k_c\sqrt{L_1L_2}\). The stored energy \(W=(L_1I_1^2+2MI_1I_2+L_2I_2^2)/2\) must be nonnegative for arbitrary currents, requiring \(|M|\le\sqrt{L_1L_2}\). In the perfectly coupled limit opposing ampere-turns can cancel the common core flux. Leakage and winding resistance then control the small residual voltages seen in real instruments.

Open the secondary so that \(I_2=0\). Faraday's law in the passive terminal-voltage convention gives \(v_i=d\lambda_i/dt=N_i\,d\Phi/dt\). Their voltage ratio is the turns ratio, regardless of sinusoidal amplitude, provided winding drops are negligible. The input current still has to build the necessary flux. Inferring it requires the primary magnetizing inductance, which the turns ratio alone cannot supply.

Set a sinusoidal operating point

Use \(N_1=400\), \(N_2=100\), core area \(A=4.00\) cm² and linear reluctance \(\mathcal R=1.00\times10^6\) A/Wb. Then \(L_1=0.160\) H, \(L_2=0.0100\) H and \(M=0.0400\) H. Drive the primary with 8.00 V RMS at 100 Hz with secondary open. Integrating the sinusoidal voltage gives peak flux \(\Phi_{\rm pk}=\sqrt2V_{\rm rms}/(2\pi fN_1)=45.0\) μWb. The predicted peak flux density is \(0.113\) T, secondary voltage \(2.00\) V RMS and magnetizing current \(V_{\rm rms}/(2\pi fL_1)=79.6\) mA RMS.

At unchanged voltage but half the frequency, voltage ratio is unchanged while peak flux density and current double in this model. A measured current waveform that develops sharp peaks at low frequency can thus signal core saturation before any dramatic change in the nominal turns ratio. A fixed DC terminal voltage on an ideal resistance-free winding would instead keep changing the flux in time, outside the assumed sinusoidal operating condition.

A short electrical pulse can determine the sense of the windings before a sinusoidal test. Mark one terminal on each coil and increase primary current through the first marked terminal. The sign of the measured open-secondary pulse with respect to its marked terminal identifies whether the two positive winding conventions reinforce or oppose one another. Reversing one winding changes the sign of the mutual coefficient and of that pulse but leaves both self-inductances and the magnitude of the voltage ratio unchanged. A sign convention overlooked here becomes important when combining two coils in series, because their mutual induced voltages can either add or subtract.

Winding resistance also introduces voltage even at steady current, so a four-wire resistance measurement helps separate ohmic voltage from linkage changes during a slow pulse.

When does an air gap dominate a magnetic circuit?

A 0.25 m core with uniform cross-section carries 100 ampere-turns. In the linear, negligible-leakage model, the core and air gap share the same \(B\). Their magnetomotive drops add, giving \(B=\mu_0NI/(\ell/\mu_r+g)\).
