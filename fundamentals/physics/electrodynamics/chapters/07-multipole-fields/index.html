From first principles

Why far-field measurements resolve moments in order

Place a static charge distribution inside a ball of radius \(a\). A distant detector at \(\mathbf r=r\hat{\mathbf n}\), \(r\gg a\), sums contributions with very similar travel distances. Begin with Coulomb’s potential, and factor the common \(r\) out of the denominator:

\[V(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\int \rho(\mathbf r')\left(1-2\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf r'}{r}+\frac{|\mathbf r'|^2}{r^2}\right)^{-1/2}\,dV'.\]

For a small dimensionless \(z\),

\[(1+z)^{-1/2}=1-z/2+3z^2/8+\cdots.\]

Keeping terms through first order in \(a/r\) takes the detector direction outside the second integral:

\[V(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac{Q}{r}+\frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf p}{r^2}+\cdots\right),\]
\[Q=\int\rho\,dV',\]
\[\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho\,dV'.\]

In a neutral sample the leading coefficient vanishes exactly. The dipole term distinguishes viewing directions and gains one extra inverse power of distance. The electric field is the negative spatial gradient, so its monopole and dipole pieces fall as \(r^{-2}\) and \(r^{-3}\). An inversion-symmetric neutral distribution also has zero dipole and requires the next, quadrupole term. The omission of higher terms is controlled by source size relative to observation distance.

Derivation and applications

Translate the origin of a multipole expansion

A tabulated dipole moment can change when the coordinate origin moves. To track what a distant measurement actually fixes, consider a bounded static distribution of point charges \(q_j\) in vacuum. Let \(\mathbf r_j\) denote their positions relative to origin \(O\), and observe at \(\mathbf r=r\mathbf n\) with \(r\) much larger than every source distance. Expand the reciprocal distance in the Coulomb potential to first order in each source coordinate.

\[\frac{1}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j|}=\frac1r+\frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_j}{r^2}+O\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf r_j|^2}{r^3}\right).\]

The coefficients of the first two terms are total charge \(Q\) and dipole moment \(\mathbf p\). Move the origin by vector \(\mathbf a\) without moving the apparatus. Subtract \(\mathbf a\) from every source coordinate and sum the moments again.

\[Q=\sum_j q_j,\]
\[\mathbf p'=\sum_jq_j(\mathbf r_j-\mathbf a)=\mathbf p-Q\mathbf a .\]

A neutral system consequently has an origin-independent dipole. For a charged system the observation vector also changes. To the same order, \(r'=|\mathbf r-\mathbf a|\) and the monopole at the new origin contributes precisely the apparent dipole that was removed from the source moment.

\[\frac Q{r'}+\frac{\mathbf n'\cdot\mathbf p'}{r'^2}=\frac Qr+\frac{Q\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a+\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf p-Q\mathbf a)}{r^2}+O(r^{-3}).\]

Multiplying by \(k=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\) restores voltage units. The cancellation holds for a distant observer relative to both origins. A finite-order numerical truncation can still differ at the first omitted order.

Worked example

Choose the centre of charge for two positive beads

Charges 2.00 nC at \(x=0\) and 1.00 nC at \(x=30.0\) mm have \(Q=3.00\) nC and \(p_x=30.0\) pC m. The shift \(a=p_x/Q=10.0\) mm sets the new dipole to zero. At the physical position \(x=1.00\) m the shifted monopole predicts

\[kQ/0.990=27.24\,\mathrm{V}.\]

The exact two-charge expression \(k(2.00+1.00/0.970)10^{-9}\) gives 27.24 V to that precision. Higher moments explain the small residual.

Second application

Locate a neutral dipole from an axial voltage

Two opposite charges of magnitude 1.50 nC are separated by 8.00 mm. Their dipole moment is 12.0 pC m, pointing towards the positive charge. At a distance of 0.400 m from the midpoint on the positive axis, the leading voltage is

\[kp/r^2=0.674\,\mathrm{V}.\]

Moving the coordinate origin 20 mm towards the positive charge leaves the moment at 12.0 pC m because \(Q=0\). Higher terms about the shifted origin account for the change in the numerical observer distance.

How far away is far enough to treat two charges as a dipole?

Take a positive and a negative charge, each of magnitude 1 nC, a distance \(2a\) apart. Observe on the positive axis at \(z>a\). Choose a tolerance of one per cent before looking at the graph and predict the required distance.

Superposition gives

\[V=kq[(z-a)^{-1}-(z+a)^{-1}]=2kqa/(z^2-a^2).\]

Replacing the denominator by \(z^2\) gives the dipole term. Dividing its difference from the exact expression by the exact expression leaves exactly \((a/z)^2\) on this axis.

The one-per-cent distance is \(10a\). Use two separations to check it on the curves. If both charges are doubled, explain separately what happens to the absolute error and to the required distance for a relative tolerance.

Reconstruct the orientation of a quadrupole from six voltage probes

Suppose a small charge distribution is confined near a known origin and has zero total charge and zero electric dipole moment. A device surrounds it with probes at a common radius \(R\) much larger than the source diameter. Each probe reports the electrostatic potential relative to infinity. Axial measurements are easy to interpret when the source is known to share the instrument's axes. Here the internal orientation is unknown. The task is to infer a tensor from a set of readings, predict another reading, and identify which unseen source features the data leave undetermined.

Extract the angular form from the Coulomb integral

For a source coordinate \(\mathbf s\) and observation direction \(\mathbf n\), expansion of the Coulomb kernel at \(\mathbf r=R\mathbf n\) gives \(R^{-1}+(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s)/R^2+[3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s)^2-s^2]/(2R^3)+\cdots\). The first two terms integrate to zero by our source assumptions. Define

\[Q_{ij}=\int\rho(\mathbf s)(3s_i s_j-s^2\delta_{ij})\,d^3s,\]
\[V(R\mathbf n)=\frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_0R^3}\mathbf n^T Q\mathbf n+O(R^{-4}).\]

This convention absorbs the factor of three into \(Q\); a tensor defined using the raw second moment would need a trace removal and a different numerical factor. The stated tensor is symmetric. Its trace vanishes identically because \(3s^2-s^2\delta_{ii}=0\) in three spatial dimensions. Thus the six independent entries of a general symmetric matrix reduce to five. The scalar \(\mathbf n^TQ\mathbf n\) is the quadrupole measured along the chosen direction, in C m². It has the same value for opposite probe directions.

Construct an invertible set of probe directions

Let each calibrated reading be \(w(\mathbf n)=8\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 V(R\mathbf n)\). Along the three Cartesian axes, \(w_x=Q_{xx}\), \(w_y=Q_{yy}\), \(w_z=Q_{zz}\). Their sum should vanish for a pure quadrupole. To obtain an off-diagonal component, point a probe at \(\mathbf n_{xy}=(\hat{\mathbf x}+\hat{\mathbf y})/\sqrt2\). Expanding the quadratic form gives

\[w_{xy}=(Q_{xx}+Q_{yy})/2+Q_{xy}.\]

Two analogous diagonal-of-a-plane directions give \(Q_{xz},Q_{yz}\). Six measurements determine the five independent values with one trace check. More directions provide independent residuals for noise or the influence of unmodelled moments.

For a concrete observation, report readings in units of \(Q_*=1.00\times10^{-14}\) C m². Suppose the axial readings are \(0.50,0.50,-1.00\), while the positive bisectors of \(xy,xz,yz\) give \(2.00,-0.25,-0.25\). The trace check passes. The reconstructed off-diagonal entries are \(Q_{xy}=1.50Q_*\) and \(Q_{xz}=Q_{yz}=0\). The \(xy\) block has eigenvectors \((1,1,0)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1,0)/\sqrt2\) with eigenvalues \(2Q_*,-Q_*\). The \(z\) axis also has eigenvalue \(-Q_*\). The source's principal symmetry axis lies on the positive \(xy\) bisector in this quadrupolar description. Rotating the other two axes within their degenerate plane yields the same tensor.

Predict a reading, then test the radial dependence

For \(\mathbf n=(1,1,1)/\sqrt3\), the diagonal contribution is the trace divided by three and vanishes. The off-diagonal contribution is \(2Q_{xy}/3=Q_*\). At \(R=0.10\) m the corresponding predicted potential is

\[Q_*/(8\pi\epsilon_0 R^3)=44.9\,\mathrm{mV}.\]

Put a second independently calibrated probe on the same ray at 0.20 m. Its quadrupole potential should be one eighth of that value, 5.62 mV. This is a new prediction using both an angle and a radius absent in the reconstruction.

Measure also at the opposite direction. Their difference cancels the quadrupole and all even angular orders while retaining odd moments, which can expose a residual dipole or octupole. Their average cancels the odd contributions but can still contain the monopole and higher even orders. Multiplying by \(R^3\) and seeking a common plateau over several radii is stronger than a single pairwise symmetry test. Because voltage signal weakens as \(R^{-3}\), a distant probe can reduce truncation bias yet amplify instrumental error when reconstructing \(Q\).

Estimate which principal axis is actually determined by the measurements

For a real symmetric \(xy\) block, an eigenaxis angle \(\varphi\) obeys \(\tan(2\varphi)=2Q_{xy}/(Q_{xx}-Q_{yy})\). Evaluate it using the signed numerator and denominator to select the appropriate quadrant. Perturbing our measured difference \(Q_{xx}-Q_{yy}\) from zero to \(+0.10Q_*\), while leaving \(Q_{xy}=1.50Q_*\), shifts the inferred principal axis from \(45^\circ\) to about \(44.0^\circ\). The large separation between the exceptional eigenvalue and the two smaller ones stabilises this axis.

Within the plane belonging to the two equal negative eigenvalues, even a very small measurement perturbation can pick out almost any pair of apparent principal axes. That numerical rotation does not establish two new physical directions in the source. Fit the unique axis and the nearly degenerate subspace separately, and compare changes in predicted probe voltages under plausible uncertainty. A common 1% radius calibration error also changes the inferred magnitude by approximately 3% because of the \(R^3\) factor, but leaves the inferred angular orientation unchanged if it affects every probe equally.

Identify what the reconstruction cannot see

All six readings receive the same unknown voltage offset. Can the trace check remove it? Would that procedure distinguish a meter offset from an actual nonzero monopole? What changes if the absolute reference is reliable but the source may also have a dipole?

Derive the result and check it independently

At a common radius a uniform voltage offset adds the same constant to each rescaled \(w\). A genuine quadrupole has zero axial sum, so under the stated zero-charge assumption the mean of the three axial readings estimates that common offset. Subtract it from every reading before the angular inversion. An actual monopole contributes the same angular offset on a single sphere and cannot be distinguished from an instrumental zero by this operation. Measurements at multiple radii separate a constant voltage offset, a \(1/R\) monopole and a \(1/R^3\) quadrupole, provided the potential reference stays stable when moving the probes.

A dipole is odd under reversal of the observation direction. Pairing each measurement with its antipodal partner and averaging first removes its contribution exactly in the multipole expansion. After that, fit the symmetric traceless tensor and predict more withheld directions. Distinct microscopic charge arrangements can share all five adjusted moments; the measured tensor describes the distant field to this order and generally does not identify a unique arrangement of internal charges.

Reading a charge distribution from far away

The radiation of an oscillating dipole was described through its time-dependent moment. A static distribution also has a hierarchy of moments. Consider fixed charges \(q_a\) inside a sphere of radius \(a\) and an observation point \(\mathbf r=r\mathbf n\) in vacuum. Measuring the complete charge map from far away would require extraordinary precision. Two distributions sharing their first few moments produce nearly the same voltage there. We can quantify which differences survive.

Superposition gives the exact potential as a sum of \(q_a/(4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_a|)\). Factor out \(r\) and expand \((1+x)^{-1/2}\) with \(x=-2\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a/r+r_a^2/r^2\). Keep both the first-order contribution from \(r_a^2\) and the squared contribution from \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a\). They have the same order in source size.

\[\frac{1}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_a|}=\frac1r+\frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a}{r^2}+\frac{3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a)^2-r_a^2}{2r^3}+O(a^3/r^4).\]

The total charge multiplies the first term. The second depends on the dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\sum q_a\mathbf r_a\). Translation of the coordinate origin by \(\mathbf d\) changes the dipole to \(\mathbf p-Q\mathbf d\). A neutral system therefore has an origin-independent dipole. When charge and dipole vanish, the leading quadrupole is likewise independent of the chosen origin.

From potential to a vector measurement

For an axial dipole the leading potential is \(p\cos\theta/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). A probe measures a force per unit charge, requiring both radial and angular derivatives. The angular gradient contains \(1/r\) because the physical arc length is \(r\,d\theta\). This produces

\[E_r=\frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3},\]
\[E_\theta=\frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3}.\]

A voltage reading of zero on the equatorial plane thus coexists with a nonzero transverse field. The vector result also supplies a laboratory diagnostic. Doubling distance reduces an ideal dipole field by eight, while a residual net charge produces a fourfold reduction. A small net charge can dominate sufficiently far from a nominally neutral device.

A quadrupole on the bench

Put \(+1.0\) nC at \(z=\pm1.0\) cm and \(-2.0\) nC at the origin. Charge, dipole and all odd moments vanish. On the positive axis the exact result simplifies before expansion,

\[V(z)=kq\left(\frac1{z-a}+\frac1{z+a}-\frac2z\right)=\frac{2kqa^2}{z(z^2-a^2)},\qquad z>a .\]

At \(z=0.100\) m the leading term \(2kqa^2/z^3\) gives \(1.798\) V. The exact value is \(1.816\) V, so the relative error referred to the exact value is

\[a^2/z^2=1.0\%.\]

In the equatorial plane the same arrangement gives \(2kq[1/\sqrt{r^2+a^2}-1/r]\), with a negative leading voltage of half the axial magnitude. Along directions where the angular factor of a leading moment vanishes, a relative-error estimate based on that term alone loses meaning. Compare absolute errors there or retain the next surviving moment.

A simple way to detect an experimental origin error is to compare voltages on opposite sides at equal nominal distances. For a pure axial dipole these voltages have equal magnitude and opposite signs. Translating either probe along the axis changes the magnitude because of the radial gradient. An apparent even contribution can then be caused by mismatched distances as easily as by a physical quadrupole. Reverse the source orientation while leaving the two probes fixed to separate a geometric offset from a source component that retains its sign.

A field gradient exerts a net force on a neutral dipole

Far-field multipole moments also determine how a localized source responds to an imposed field that varies little across it. Consider two charges \(\pm q\) separated by a small vector \(\mathbf d\) pointing from negative to positive charge. Their dipole moment is

\[\mathbf p=q\mathbf d.\]

Expand the external potential around their midpoint \(\mathbf R\). The constant contributions cancel, leaving interaction energy \(q[V(\mathbf R+\mathbf d/2)-V(\mathbf R-\mathbf d/2)]\simeq-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E(\mathbf R)\). The next terms are negligible when the external field varies on a scale much longer than the separation.

For a fixed dipole orientation the force follows by taking the gradient with respect to the midpoint. In components \(F_i=p_j\partial_i E_j\). The electrostatic field is curl free, so this equals \(p_j\partial_jE_i\), the difference of the forces on the two ends obtained by direct expansion. A uniform field consequently exerts torque \(\mathbf p\times\mathbf E\) but no net force on a fixed neutral dipole.

An induced dipole changes its magnitude as the field is raised. For a linear polarizable object with \(\mathbf p=\alpha\mathbf E\), the work integrated over field buildup gives energy \(U=-\alpha E^2/2\). The one-half also follows by adding the internal energy cost \(p^2/(2\alpha)\) to the coupling \(-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E\) and minimizing over \(\mathbf p\). The force is then \(\mathbf F=(\alpha/2)\nabla E^2\) for real constant \(\alpha\) in a static field. Simply substituting \(\alpha E\) into the permanent-dipole energy before differentiating would double the answer.

Predict a force on a dielectric bead

A neutral bead in vacuum has static polarizability \(\alpha=4.00\times10^{-21}\) C m²/V. Locally on a symmetry axis the field is \(E_z=2.00\times10^4\) V/m and its axial gradient is \(3.00\times10^6\) V/m². Assume the bead is much smaller than the variation length \(E/|\partial_zE|=6.67\) mm and other field components vanish on that axis. The induced moment is \(8.00\times10^{-17}\) C m. Its axial force is \(\alpha E_z\partial_z E_z=2.40\times10^{-10}\) N, directed toward increasing squared field.

If the field amplitude of the electrodes is doubled without changing their geometry, both field and gradient double, so the force rises fourfold. A fixed, mechanically aligned permanent dipole would show only a twofold force increase. This scaling and a reversal of electrode polarity offer practical discrimination between the two models. Near a field zero an induced dipole and its leading force both vanish; finite-size and higher-moment effects must be considered if a measurable force persists there.

The validity condition on field gradients can be phrased in terms of an observable length scale for a real bead. Moving it by a fraction of a millimetre along the axis and measuring its induced moment probes the local derivative of field. A moment tracking field linearly while force follows its squared-field gradient supports the same polarizability in both tests. If the bead size becomes comparable to the variation length, different parts of it experience different applied fields. Higher induced moments and local field distortion then enter the force balance, so one scalar dipole polarizability is no longer sufficient.
