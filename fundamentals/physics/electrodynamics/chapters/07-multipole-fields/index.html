Reading a charge distribution from far away

The radiation of an oscillating dipole was described through its time-dependent moment. A static distribution also has a hierarchy of moments. Consider fixed charges \(q_a\) inside a sphere of radius \(a\) and an observation point \(\mathbf r=r\mathbf n\) in vacuum. Measuring the complete charge map from far away would require extraordinary precision. Two distributions sharing their first few moments produce nearly the same voltage there. We can quantify which differences survive.

Superposition gives the exact potential as a sum of \(q_a/(4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_a|)\). Factor out \(r\) and expand \((1+x)^{-1/2}\) with \(x=-2\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a/r+r_a^2/r^2\). Keep both the first-order contribution from \(r_a^2\) and the squared contribution from \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a\). They have the same order in source size.

\[\frac{1}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_a|}=\frac1r+\frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a}{r^2}+\frac{3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_a)^2-r_a^2}{2r^3}+O(a^3/r^4).\]

The total charge multiplies the first term. The second depends on the dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\sum q_a\mathbf r_a\). Translation of the coordinate origin by \(\mathbf d\) changes the dipole to \(\mathbf p-Q\mathbf d\). A neutral system therefore has an origin-independent dipole. When charge and dipole vanish, the leading quadrupole is likewise independent of the chosen origin.

From potential to a vector measurement

For an axial dipole the leading potential is \(p\cos\theta/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). A probe measures a force per unit charge, requiring both radial and angular derivatives. The angular gradient contains \(1/r\) because the physical arc length is \(r\,d\theta\). This produces

\[E_r=\frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3},\qquad E_\theta=\frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3}.\]

A voltage reading of zero on the equatorial plane thus coexists with a nonzero transverse field. The vector result also supplies a laboratory diagnostic. Doubling distance reduces an ideal dipole field by eight, while a residual net charge produces a fourfold reduction. A small net charge can dominate sufficiently far from a nominally neutral device.

A quadrupole on the bench

Put \(+1.0\) nC at \(z=\pm1.0\) cm and \(-2.0\) nC at the origin. Charge, dipole and all odd moments vanish. On the positive axis the exact result simplifies before expansion,

\[V(z)=kq\left(\frac1{z-a}+\frac1{z+a}-\frac2z\right)=\frac{2kqa^2}{z(z^2-a^2)},\qquad z>a .\]

At \(z=0.100\) m the leading term \(2kqa^2/z^3\) gives \(1.798\) V. The exact value is \(1.816\) V, so the relative error referred to the exact value is \(a^2/z^2=1.0\%\). In the equatorial plane the same arrangement gives \(2kq[1/\sqrt{r^2+a^2}-1/r]\), with a negative leading voltage of half the axial magnitude. Along directions where the angular factor of a leading moment vanishes, a relative-error estimate based on that term alone loses meaning. Compare absolute errors there or retain the next surviving moment.

A simple way to detect an experimental origin error is to compare voltages on opposite sides at equal nominal distances. For a pure axial dipole these voltages have equal magnitude and opposite signs. Translating either probe along the axis changes the magnitude because of the radial gradient. An apparent even contribution can then be caused by mismatched distances as easily as by a physical quadrupole. Reverse the source orientation while leaving the two probes fixed to separate a geometric offset from a source component that retains its sign.

A field gradient exerts a net force on a neutral dipole

Far-field multipole moments also determine how a localized source responds to an imposed field that varies little across it. Consider two charges \(\pm q\) separated by a small vector \(\mathbf d\) pointing from negative to positive charge. Their dipole moment is \(\mathbf p=q\mathbf d\). Expand the external potential around their midpoint \(\mathbf R\). The constant contributions cancel, leaving interaction energy \(q[V(\mathbf R+\mathbf d/2)-V(\mathbf R-\mathbf d/2)]\simeq-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E(\mathbf R)\). The next terms are negligible when the external field varies on a scale much longer than the separation.

For a fixed dipole orientation the force follows by taking the gradient with respect to the midpoint. In components \(F_i=p_j\partial_i E_j\). The electrostatic field is curl free, so this equals \(p_j\partial_jE_i\), the difference of the forces on the two ends obtained by direct expansion. A uniform field consequently exerts torque \(\mathbf p\times\mathbf E\) but no net force on a fixed neutral dipole.

An induced dipole changes its magnitude as the field is raised. For a linear polarizable object with \(\mathbf p=\alpha\mathbf E\), the work integrated over field buildup gives energy \(U=-\alpha E^2/2\). The one-half also follows by adding the internal energy cost \(p^2/(2\alpha)\) to the coupling \(-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E\) and minimizing over \(\mathbf p\). The force is then \(\mathbf F=(\alpha/2)\nabla E^2\) for real constant \(\alpha\) in a static field. Simply substituting \(\alpha E\) into the permanent-dipole energy before differentiating would double the answer.

Predict a force on a dielectric bead

A neutral bead in vacuum has static polarizability \(\alpha=4.00\times10^{-21}\) C m²/V. Locally on a symmetry axis the field is \(E_z=2.00\times10^4\) V/m and its axial gradient is \(3.00\times10^6\) V/m². Assume the bead is much smaller than the variation length \(E/|\partial_zE|=6.67\) mm and other field components vanish on that axis. The induced moment is \(8.00\times10^{-17}\) C m. Its axial force is \(\alpha E_z\partial_z E_z=2.40\times10^{-10}\) N, directed toward increasing squared field.

If the field amplitude of the electrodes is doubled without changing their geometry, both field and gradient double, so the force rises fourfold. A fixed, mechanically aligned permanent dipole would show only a twofold force increase. This scaling and a reversal of electrode polarity offer practical discrimination between the two models. Near a field zero an induced dipole and its leading force both vanish; finite-size and higher-moment effects must be considered if a measurable force persists there.

The validity condition on field gradients can be phrased in terms of an observable length scale for a real bead. Moving it by a fraction of a millimetre along the axis and measuring its induced moment probes the local derivative of field. A moment tracking field linearly while force follows its squared-field gradient supports the same polarizability in both tests. If the bead size becomes comparable to the variation length, different parts of it experience different applied fields. Higher induced moments and local field distortion then enter the force balance, so one scalar dipole polarizability is no longer sufficient.
