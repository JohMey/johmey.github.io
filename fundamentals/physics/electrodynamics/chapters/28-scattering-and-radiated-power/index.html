Why a small transparent sphere scatters light

A particle can redistribute light without consuming it. The calculation starts with an electrostatic boundary-value problem and ends with power crossing a faraway sphere. Connecting the two limits predicts a wavelength dependence that can be tested with colour.

Find the induced dipole by matching two potentials

Take a sphere of radius \(a\) and relative permittivity \(\epsilon_r\) in vacuum. Its size must be small compared with the wavelength everywhere, including inside the particle. Across its diameter, the incident wave then looks almost like a uniform electric field \(E_0\hat{\mathbf z}\). Spherical symmetry and the applied field single out the angular factor \(\cos\theta\). Choose \(\phi_{\rm in}=-Cr\cos\theta\) and \(\phi_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+D\cos\theta/r^2\). Continuity of tangential electric field is equivalent here to continuity of potential. With zero free surface charge, the normal displacement is also continuous. At \(r=a\),

\[C=E_0-D/a^3,\]
\[\epsilon_r C=E_0+2D/a^3.\]

Eliminate \(C\) to obtain

\[D=a^3E_0(\epsilon_r-1)/(\epsilon_r+2).\]

The coefficient of the dipole potential is \(p/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\). Hence \(p=\alpha_0 E_0\) with \(\alpha_0=4\pi\epsilon_0a^3(\epsilon_r-1)/(\epsilon_r+2)\). The internal field \(3E_0/(\epsilon_r+2)\) explains the denominator. For \(\epsilon_r=1\) the disturbance vanishes. In the perfectly polarizable limit the internal field tends to zero.

Match a small dipole to an outgoing wave

Use complex amplitudes with time dependence \(e^{-i\omega t}\). The far electric field is \((k^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 r)[(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf p)\times\hat{\mathbf n}]e^{ikr}\), \(k=\omega/c\). Squaring its amplitude and multiplying by \(\epsilon_0 c/2\) gives the mean intensity. Divide by the incident intensity \(\epsilon_0c|E_0|^2/2\). The differential scattering cross section is then \(k^4|\alpha|^2\sin^2\theta/(16\pi^2\epsilon_0^2)\), with \(\theta\) measured from the dipole axis. Integrating the angular factor gives \(8\pi/3\):

\[\sigma_{\rm sca}=\frac{k^4|\alpha|^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0^2}\simeq\frac{8\pi}3 k^4a^6\left|\frac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}\right|^2 .\]

The sixth power originates in the coherent addition of dipoles throughout a volume proportional to \(a^3\). The fourth power in frequency comes from the second time derivative of the dipole in the radiative electric field. A cross section has units of area and measures removed power divided by incident intensity.

Where the scattered energy comes from

The incident field does work at mean rate \(\langle\mathbf E\cdot\dot{\mathbf p}\rangle=(\omega/2)\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)|E_0|^2\). Dividing by incident intensity yields

\[\sigma_{\rm ext}=k\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)/\epsilon_0.\]

A strictly real electrostatic \(\alpha_0\) would predict no extracted power while predicting positive scattered power. The static calculation captured the leading dipole amplitude but omitted its small radiation-induced phase delay. For a lossless electric dipole its leading radiative correction satisfies

\[\frac1\alpha=\frac1{\alpha_0}-i\frac{k^3}{6\pi\epsilon_0}.\]

Indeed \(\operatorname{Im}\alpha=k^3|\alpha|^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0)\), making extinction equal to scattering. In an absorbing particle their difference is absorbed power per incident intensity. Finite-size dispersion and higher multipoles require additional terms once \(ka\) grows.

Size, colour and the limits of inference

A particle doubles in radius

For dilute spheres of the same weakly dispersive material, doubling \(a\) gives \(2^6=64\) times the scattered power per particle at fixed incident intensity. If the total volume of dielectric is held fixed, the number of spheres falls by eight. The whole sample then scatters eight times the power, provided phases between different particles are effectively uncorrelated and multiple scattering is negligible. The two questions describe different samples.

A frequency-independent contrast

For \(a=20\) nm, \(\epsilon_r=2.25\) and vacuum wavelength \(600\) nm, \(ka=0.209\) and \((\epsilon_r-1)/(\epsilon_r+2)=0.2941\). The leading cross section is \(5.58\times10^{-19}\) m², around \(4.4\times10^{-4}\) of the geometric area. At \(450\) nm the model predicts 3.16 times that cross section. A large discrepancy can indicate dispersion, additional multipoles, or a distribution of particle sizes.

Compare the scattering of several particle sizes

For radius 20 nm, relative permittivity 2.25 and wavelength 600 nm, the predicted cross section is about 0.56 nm². Halve the radius and estimate the drop before reading the plot. The curve uses a frequency-independent dielectric contrast.

Estimate a radius ratio

Two dilute suspensions have the same number density, composition and optical path length. In the single-scattering regime one produces twice the scattered intensity at the same angle. Infer the ratio of particle radii.

Work through the solution

The proportionality to \(a^6\) gives

\[a_2/a_1=2^{1/6}=1.122.\]

A 12.2% change in radius doubles the signal. If counts or material contrasts are unknown, the same intensity change has multiple explanations.

Recover Thomson scattering

A free electron is driven by a weak field. Use \(m\ddot x=-eE_0e^{-i\omega t}\) to find its dipole and cross section. Why does the wavelength law change?

Work through the solution

The displacement amplitude is \(eE_0/(m\omega^2)\). Multiplying by electronic charge gives

\[\alpha=-e^2/(m\omega^2).\]

The \(\omega^{-4}\) in \(|\alpha|^2\) cancels \(k^4\). The cross section becomes \((8\pi/3)[e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0mc^2)]^2\). This classical approximation assumes nonrelativistic electron motion and negligible recoil.
