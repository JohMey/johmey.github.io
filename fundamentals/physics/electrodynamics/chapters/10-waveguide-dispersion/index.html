Fields constrained by metal walls

Can a hollow metal tube carry an arbitrarily slowly varying electromagnetic wave? Take perfectly conducting walls, a uniform vacuum interior, width \(a\) along \(x\), height \(b\) along \(y\) and translation symmetry along \(z\). Tangential electric field must vanish on each wall. In free space a plane wave can have uniform transverse fields. Inside a hollow simply connected guide a nonzero transverse electrostatic potential difference between separate conductors cannot be maintained. The lowest guided pattern consequently has transverse variation.

For the simplest transverse-electric mode set \(E_y=E_m\sin(\pi x/a)\exp[i(\beta z-\omega t)]\), taking real parts for physical fields. At the side walls \(E_y\) is tangential and the sine vanishes. At the top and bottom the electric field is normal, as allowed by surface charge. Its zero divergence and accompanying magnetic field follow from Maxwell’s equations. In particular the curl includes an \(x\) derivative, giving a longitudinal magnetic component and the conventional label TE\(_{10}\).

Separate transverse and longitudinal wavenumbers

Substitute the field into the vacuum wave equation. The two spatial derivatives contribute \(-(\pi/a)^2\) and \(-\beta^2\), while the time derivative contributes \(-\omega^2/c^2\). Hence

\[\beta^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^2,\qquad f_c=\frac{c}{2a}.\]

Below cutoff the longitudinal dependence is exponential. In a long uniform section the bounded solution decays along the guide. A finite closed-off section can also have a growing exponential determined by reflections at its far end. Above cutoff the phase velocity is \(\omega/\beta\), while differentiating the dispersion relation gives the narrowband envelope velocity \(d\omega/d\beta=c^2\beta/\omega\). Their product is \(c^2\). Phase fronts move faster than \(c\) because the fixed transverse field variation contributes to total wavenumber. A modulation envelope in this lossless guide propagates more slowly than \(c\).

Choose a usable frequency band

For a guide with \(a=22.86\) mm and \(b=10.16\) mm, the first cutoff is \(6.56\) GHz. Higher-order patterns have transverse wavenumber squared \((m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2\). The next candidate cutoffs are \(13.11\) GHz for TE\(_{20}\) and \(14.75\) GHz for TE\(_{01}\). Consequently a 10.0 GHz drive supports one propagating mode in this ideal guide.

At 10.0 GHz the group velocity is \(c\sqrt{1-(6.56/10)^2}=0.755c\). A 2.00 m section delays a narrow envelope by \(8.84\) ns. Its guided wavelength is \(c/[f\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}]=39.7\) mm, exceeding the 30.0 mm free-space wavelength. Near cutoff the group velocity varies rapidly across a finite bandwidth, so quoting one envelope delay can conceal pulse distortion. Separately, real metal walls introduce attenuation and bends can excite evanescent modes even in the quoted single-mode band.

A practical way to measure the guided wavelength is to create a partial reflection at the termination. The forward and backward fields interfere and their stationary intensity maxima are separated by half the guided wavelength. At the example frequency the spacing should be about 19.9 mm. That measurement tests the propagation constant at one frequency. To test the group velocity, launch a short modulated burst or measure how transmitted phase changes between two nearby frequencies.

Close a guide to make a cavity

Placing conducting end plates at \(z=0\) and \(z=L\) turns a propagating guide mode into a standing field with discrete frequencies. For the TE pattern of the previous derivation, the electric field has only \(E_y\) and is tangential to both end plates. The conductor therefore requires it to vanish there. A consistent mode has \(E_y\propto\sin(\pi x/a)\sin(p\pi z/L)\) with positive integer \(p\). The transverse electric field still points normal to the walls at fixed \(y\), satisfying the original conditions. Combining longitudinal and transverse wavenumbers in the wave equation gives

\[f_{10p}=\frac c2\sqrt{\frac1{a^2}+\frac{p^2}{L^2}} .\]

The lowest such cavity resonance lies above the guide's cutoff even though no net power travels through the end plates. In a standing mode electric and magnetic energies exchange, and cycle-average net axial transport vanishes. Increasing cavity length at fixed \(p\) brings the frequency progressively nearer the guide cutoff. Conversely, if several longitudinal nodes fit into a short cavity, the longitudinal contribution can dominate the resonance frequency.

Weak finite conductivity or an output coupler causes stored energy to leave the mode. Define \(Q=\omega U/P_{\rm loss}\) with \(U\) the cycle-averaged stored energy and \(P_{\rm loss}\) the average dissipated or extracted power. When \(Q\) is approximately constant after switching off the drive, \(dU/dt=-\omega U/Q\) gives an energy decay time \(Q/\omega\). A field-amplitude measurement follows the square root of energy and consequently has twice that time constant.

Identify a resonance and estimate a loaded quality factor

Choose \(a=30.0\) mm, \(L=40.0\) mm and cavity height 12.0 mm. The lowest member of this family has \(f_{101}=(c/2)\sqrt{(1/0.0300)^2+(1/0.0400)^2}=6.25\) GHz using \(c=3.00\times10^8\) m/s. The parent guide’s cutoff is 5.00 GHz. Raising longitudinal index to two gives \(f_{102}=9.01\) GHz. Measured field nodes along the cavity distinguish these patterns from other possible modes near the same frequency.

After terminating the drive at the first resonance, suppose a weak probe's voltage amplitude falls by a factor \(e\) in 2.00 μs. Then \(Q=\omega\tau_{\rm amplitude}/2=\pi f\tau_{\rm amplitude}=3.93\times10^4\). The corresponding full resonance width is approximately \(f/Q=159\) kHz for a single weakly damped mode. A fit to detected power would return a 1.00 μs time constant instead. Increasing the probe coupling shortens the measured lifetime, so this ring-down gives the loaded quality factor. Measurements over several coupling strengths help separate internal wall losses from energy deliberately extracted by the probe.

The energy decay also predicts how much source power is needed to sustain a measured field. For example, if the resonant cavity in this measurement stores \(2.00\) nJ, total time-averaged loss is \(U/\tau_U=2.00\) mW. Source power actually coupled into the mode must replenish that loss in steady state. The source's forward power can be larger because some incident energy reflects at the coupling port. An independently measured coupling efficiency is therefore needed before converting a power-meter reading at the source into a stored field or a wall heating rate.

Read a waveguide delay near cutoff

In a lossless uniform guide with one propagating mode, \(\beta^2=(\omega^2-\omega_c^2)/c_m^2\). Compute \(v_p=\omega/\beta\) and \(v_g=d\omega/d\beta\). The bandwidth of a pulse must remain inside this propagating band for its group-delay estimate to be useful.
