From first principles

Why a conducting guide cuts off long waves

In vacuum, take a curl of Faraday’s law and substitute the source-free Ampère–Maxwell law. Use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0\). Each Cartesian electric-field component then obeys

\[\nabla^2 E_y=c^{-2}\partial_t^2 E_y.\]

Consider a rectangular guide with perfectly conducting walls at \(x=0,a\). Take a TE mode with \(E_y\) independent of \(y\) and a travelling factor \(e^{i(k_z z-\omega t)}\). The tangential electric field must vanish on the two side walls, so the lowest nonzero profile is \(\sin(\pi x/a)\). Differentiating that sine twice contributes \(-(\pi/a)^2\); differentiating the travelling factors gives

\[k_z^2=\omega^2/c^2-(\pi/a)^2,\]
\[f_c=\frac{c}{2a}.\]

The boundary condition consumes part of the squared wave number in the transverse direction. Below \(f_c\), \(k_z\) is imaginary and the solution bounded far down the guide decays. Above cutoff it is real. Differentiating the dispersion relation gives the speed of a narrowband envelope,

\[d\omega/dk_z=c^2k_z/\omega.\]

Near cutoff it can be small even though the field between the metal walls still obeys the vacuum wave equation.

Background · Wave impedance
\[Z=E_t/H_t\]

The ratio has units of ohms. The field direction and mode determine the relevant tangential components. In a guide it can differ from the intrinsic impedance of the filling material.

Derivation and applications

Measure penetration below waveguide cutoff

A guided TE mode can have nonzero field past a junction even below its propagation threshold. Consider the fundamental TE mode of an air-filled rectangular guide with broad dimension \(a\) and perfectly conducting walls. Let the section beyond the junction be uniform and extend far enough that there is no appreciable returning field. Its standing transverse profile fixes the transverse wavenumber at \(\pi/a\). Inserting this profile into the homogeneous wave equation leaves an ordinary equation for longitudinal amplitude \(F(z)\).

\[\frac{d^2F}{dz^2}+\left[\left(\frac{2\pi f}{c}\right)^2-\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^2\right]F=0,\]
\[f_c=\frac{c}{2a}.\]

Below \(f_c\) the bracket is negative. Define its negative square root in magnitude as the positive decay coefficient \(\alpha\). Of the two exponential solutions only the one decaying towards the remote end satisfies the assumed boundary condition.

\[\alpha=\frac{\pi}{a}\sqrt{1-(f/f_c)^2},\]
\[\frac{F(z)}{F(0)}=e^{-\alpha z}.\]

Suppose two identical weak probes respond to local squared electric-field amplitude at identical transverse coordinates. Forming their ratio cancels the unknown excitation and the transverse mode profile. Their readings in decibels then supply a slope from which cutoff can be recovered.

\[\frac{S(z_2)}{S(z_1)}=e^{-2\alpha(z_2-z_1)},\]
\[10\log_{10}\frac{S(z_2)}{S(z_1)}=-\frac{20\alpha}{\ln10}(z_2-z_1).\]

A single decaying mode in a lossless semi-infinite guide stores reactive energy and has zero mean axial power. These probe readings track a local field. In a short section bounded by a second discontinuity, both exponentials can be required and axial power can pass through the combination.

Worked example

Predict the depth dependence of a probe signal

For \(a=30.0\) mm and \(c=3.00\times10^8\) m/s, \(f_c=5.00\) GHz. At 4.00 GHz the decay coefficient is \((\pi/0.0300)(0.600)=62.8\) m⁻¹. Over 20.0 mm the field amplitude falls to \(e^{-1.257}=0.285\) of its initial value. A square-law probe reads \(0.0810\) of its initial signal, corresponding to a drop of 10.9 dB.

Second application

Recover the guide width from two readings

At 6.00 GHz two identical probes 10.0 mm apart have signal ratio 0.250. Their field ratio is 0.500 and \(\alpha=\ln2/0.0100=69.3\) m⁻¹. The vacuum wavenumber is \(2\pi f/c=125.7\) m⁻¹, so \(\pi/a=\sqrt{125.7^2+69.3^2}=143.5\) m⁻¹. This gives \(a=21.9\) mm and cutoff 6.85 GHz, above the measuring frequency as required.

What changes at the cutoff of a rectangular guide?

For the lowest transverse electric mode of an empty perfectly conducting rectangular guide, the wider side sets \(k_c=\pi/a\). Move the width while holding the source frequency fixed. Predict where axial propagation becomes possible.

Separation of the transverse and longitudinal coordinates gives

\[k_z^2=(2\pi f/c)^2-k_c^2.\]

Above \(f_c=c/(2a)\) the positive square root is the phase constant. Below it the magnitude of the imaginary root is the spatial decay rate. Both have units of inverse length and refer to different solutions.

Use 3 cm and 4 GHz. Read the attenuation constant and infer the distance for a factor \(e\) drop in amplitude. Widen the guide until 4 GHz propagates. Locate the boundary with the algebra and then with the operating-point marker.

How a narrow waveguide lengthens a pulse even with perfectly conducting walls

Launch a short microwave pulse into an empty, straight rectangular guide of width \(a\), with only its lowest transverse electric mode excited. The walls are ideal conductors and the receiver is matched, so there is neither Ohmic loss nor a reflected wave to explain any change in its shape. Yet a received pulse can be longer and lower in peak amplitude than the transmitted one. Group delay explains the arrival of the pulse's centre. To explain the altered width we must also find how that delay varies within its frequency band.

Follow the phase of each frequency separately

For the dominant mode the longitudinal propagation constant above cutoff is \(k(\omega)=c^{-1}\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_c^2}\), with \(\omega_c=\pi c/a\). The transverse mode shape is set by the walls; the frequency dependence below comes from its longitudinal wavenumber. With convention \(e^{ikz-i\omega t}\), each spectral component accumulates phase \(k(\omega)L\) in a length \(L\). Let \(\Omega=\omega-\omega_0\) be detuning around the positive carrier. For a spectrum concentrated well above cutoff, expand

\[k(\omega_0+\Omega)=k_0+k'_0\Omega+\tfrac12 k''_0\Omega^2+\cdots,\]
\[k'_0=\frac{\omega_0}{c\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\omega_c^2}},\]
\[k''_0=-\frac{\omega_c^2}{c(\omega_0^2-\omega_c^2)^{3/2}} .\]

The term \(k'_0L\) shifts every frequency by the same first-order group delay. The negative \(k''_0\) means higher frequencies have a smaller delay than lower frequencies. Thus the fast and slow parts of the spectrum separate in time even if the magnitude of each transmitted Fourier coefficient is unchanged. The expansion also predicts its own failure near cutoff, where the higher derivatives increase and some input spectral components may fall below cutoff altogether.

Carry a Gaussian envelope through the Fourier integral

Choose analytic input \(E^+(0,t)=E_p e^{-t^2/(2T_0^2)}e^{-i\omega_0t}\), with the physical field given by its real part. The spectral envelope is proportional to \(e^{-T_0^2\Omega^2/2}\). Multiply by the propagation phase and set \(s=t-k'_0L\). Completing the square in the inverse transform requires integrating \(\exp[-(T_0^2-i k''_0L)\Omega^2/2-i\Omega s]\). The result, apart from the carrier, is

\[A(L,t)=\frac{E_p T_0}{\sqrt{T_0^2-i k''_0 L}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-k'_0L)^2}{2(T_0^2-i k''_0 L)}\right].\]

Taking the magnitude gives \(T_L=T_0\sqrt{1+(k''_0L/T_0^2)^2}\), using the same width convention as the input. The magnitude peak ratio is \(\sqrt{T_0/T_L}\). The factor compensates the broadening so that the time integral of \(|A|^2\) stays fixed. The complex exponent also contains a quadratic phase in \(s\); thus the instantaneous frequency varies across the outgoing envelope. A power meter sees the broader envelope, while a phase-sensitive receiver can test the chirp. One should distinguish an amplitude width \(T\) in \(e^{-t^2/(2T^2)}\) from an intensity full width at half maximum \(2\sqrt{\ln2}\,T\).

Predict a flight time and time spread independently

Take \(a=20.0\) mm, \(L=4.00\) m and \(f_0=9.00\) GHz. Then \(f_c=7.495\) GHz, \(v_g=1/k'_0=0.554c\) and mean arrival delay is \(24.1\) ns. The dispersion coefficient is \(k''_0=-0.241\) ns²/m. For \(T_0=1.00\) ns the width grows to \(1.39\) ns and the magnitude peak falls to about 85% of its input value. The guided carrier advances in phase at speed greater than \(c\), but the group arrival and the causal front cannot be inferred from that phase speed.

Check the frequency sorting by evaluating \(L/v_g\) independently at two nearby frequencies. For weak bandwidth, their delay separation per angular-frequency separation must approach \(k''_0 L\). A direct numerical inverse transform provides a further test: sample the input spectrum densely enough to resolve the rapidly varying phase, multiply by \(e^{ik(\omega)L}\), and evaluate the time signal. Refine both bandwidth and frequency spacing. If the exact transform remains asymmetric after convergence, the quadratic expansion has reached its limit even when the solver itself has converged.

Estimate the frequency band in which a single dispersion coefficient suffices

The neglected cubic term in accumulated phase is \(Lk'''_0\Omega^3/6\). Differentiating the expression for \(k''\) yields

\[k'''_0=3\omega_0\omega_c^2/[c(\omega_0^2-\omega_c^2)^{5/2}].\]

Relative to the quadratic term its magnitude is \(\omega_0|\Omega|/(\omega_0^2-\omega_c^2)\). At a typical spectral detuning of \(1/T_0\) this ratio is about 0.058 for the 9 GHz example. The ratio doubles at twice that detuning, although the spectral amplitude there is already smaller. Testing the accumulated cubic phase itself is also necessary if the guide is sufficiently long.

Thus a nominally narrow fractional bandwidth \(\Delta f/f_0\) can still give a poor quadratic approximation near cutoff, where the smaller scale is \(f_0-f_c\). The analytic Gaussian also has arbitrarily early and late tails. Its fitted peak delay describes a prepared modulation and cannot time the first arrival of new information. A sharply switched source contains frequencies far outside the fitted Gaussian band, including components whose propagation approaches the vacuum light speed.

Test how close a measurement can approach cutoff

A second run uses \(f_0=8.00\) GHz, \(T_0=2.00\) ns and the same guide length. Estimate delay and width. How could a measured lower peak be distinguished from attenuation, filtering at cutoff or a reflection?

Derive the result and check it independently

At 8.00 GHz one finds \(v_g=0.350c\), \(k''_0=-1.36\) ns²/m, delay \(38.2\) ns and \(T_L=2\sqrt{1+(5.45/4)^2}=3.38\) ns. Measure the magnitude of the frequency-domain transmission after correcting for port response. Pure dispersion changes phase while preserving each above-cutoff spectral magnitude in this model. A systematic magnitude reduction over the band indicates losses or coupling, a sharp suppression near the cutoff indicates spectral filtering, and a periodic ripple or time-separated echo suggests a reflection.

A second guide length supplies another check: delay varies linearly with \(L\) and \(T_L^2-T_0^2\) varies quadratically at fixed band and input width. If only a short time window is recorded, a broader pulse can be partly clipped and look as if transmission lost energy. Extend the integration window before evaluating that claim.

Fields constrained by metal walls

Can a hollow metal tube carry an arbitrarily slowly varying electromagnetic wave? Take perfectly conducting walls, a uniform vacuum interior, width \(a\) along \(x\), height \(b\) along \(y\) and translation symmetry along \(z\). Tangential electric field must vanish on each wall. In free space a plane wave can have uniform transverse fields. Inside a hollow simply connected guide a nonzero transverse electrostatic potential difference between separate conductors cannot be maintained. The lowest guided pattern consequently has transverse variation.

For the simplest transverse-electric mode set \(E_y=E_m\sin(\pi x/a)\exp[i(\beta z-\omega t)]\), taking real parts for physical fields. At the side walls \(E_y\) is tangential and the sine vanishes. At the top and bottom the electric field is normal, as allowed by surface charge. Its zero divergence and accompanying magnetic field follow from Maxwell’s equations. In particular the curl includes an \(x\) derivative, giving a longitudinal magnetic component and the conventional label TE\(_{10}\).

Separate transverse and longitudinal wavenumbers

Substitute the field into the vacuum wave equation. The two spatial derivatives contribute \(-(\pi/a)^2\) and \(-\beta^2\), while the time derivative contributes \(-\omega^2/c^2\). Hence

\[\beta^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^2,\]
\[f_c=\frac{c}{2a}.\]

Below cutoff the longitudinal dependence is exponential. In a long uniform section the bounded solution decays along the guide. A finite closed-off section can also have a growing exponential determined by reflections at its far end. Above cutoff the phase velocity is \(\omega/\beta\), while differentiating the dispersion relation gives the narrowband envelope velocity \(d\omega/d\beta=c^2\beta/\omega\). Their product is \(c^2\). Phase fronts move faster than \(c\) because the fixed transverse field variation contributes to total wavenumber. A modulation envelope in this lossless guide propagates more slowly than \(c\).

Choose a usable frequency band

For a guide with \(a=22.86\) mm and \(b=10.16\) mm, the first cutoff is \(6.56\) GHz. Higher-order patterns have transverse wavenumber squared \((m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2\). The next candidate cutoffs are \(13.11\) GHz for TE\(_{20}\) and \(14.75\) GHz for TE\(_{01}\). Consequently a 10.0 GHz drive supports one propagating mode in this ideal guide.

At 10.0 GHz the group velocity is

\[c\sqrt{1-(6.56/10)^2}=0.755c.\]

A 2.00 m section delays a narrow envelope by \(8.84\) ns. Its guided wavelength is \(c/[f\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}]=39.7\) mm, exceeding the 30.0 mm free-space wavelength. Near cutoff the group velocity varies rapidly across a finite bandwidth, so quoting one envelope delay can conceal pulse distortion. Separately, real metal walls introduce attenuation and bends can excite evanescent modes even in the quoted single-mode band.

A practical way to measure the guided wavelength is to create a partial reflection at the termination. The forward and backward fields interfere and their stationary intensity maxima are separated by half the guided wavelength. At the example frequency the spacing should be about 19.9 mm. That measurement tests the propagation constant at one frequency. To test the group velocity, launch a short modulated burst or measure how transmitted phase changes between two nearby frequencies.

Close a guide to make a cavity

Placing conducting end plates at \(z=0\) and \(z=L\) turns a propagating guide mode into a standing field with discrete frequencies. For the TE pattern of the previous derivation, the electric field has only \(E_y\) and is tangential to both end plates. The conductor therefore requires it to vanish there. A consistent mode has \(E_y\propto\sin(\pi x/a)\sin(p\pi z/L)\) with positive integer \(p\). The transverse electric field still points normal to the walls at fixed \(y\), satisfying the original conditions. Combining longitudinal and transverse wavenumbers in the wave equation gives

\[f_{10p}=\frac c2\sqrt{\frac1{a^2}+\frac{p^2}{L^2}} .\]

The lowest such cavity resonance lies above the guide's cutoff even though no net power travels through the end plates. In a standing mode electric and magnetic energies exchange, and cycle-average net axial transport vanishes. Increasing cavity length at fixed \(p\) brings the frequency progressively nearer the guide cutoff. Conversely, if several longitudinal nodes fit into a short cavity, the longitudinal contribution can dominate the resonance frequency.

Weak finite conductivity or an output coupler causes stored energy to leave the mode. Define \(Q=\omega U/P_{\rm loss}\) with \(U\) the cycle-averaged stored energy and \(P_{\rm loss}\) the average dissipated or extracted power. When \(Q\) is approximately constant after switching off the drive, \(dU/dt=-\omega U/Q\) gives an energy decay time \(Q/\omega\). A field-amplitude measurement follows the square root of energy and consequently has twice that time constant.

Identify a resonance and estimate a loaded quality factor

Choose \(a=30.0\) mm, \(L=40.0\) mm and cavity height 12.0 mm. The lowest member of this family has \(f_{101}=(c/2)\sqrt{(1/0.0300)^2+(1/0.0400)^2}=6.25\) GHz using \(c=3.00\times10^8\) m/s. The parent guide’s cutoff is 5.00 GHz. Raising longitudinal index to two gives \(f_{102}=9.01\) GHz. Measured field nodes along the cavity distinguish these patterns from other possible modes near the same frequency.

After terminating the drive at the first resonance, suppose a weak probe's voltage amplitude falls by a factor \(e\) in 2.00 μs. Then

\[Q=\omega\tau_{\rm amplitude}/2=\pi f\tau_{\rm amplitude}=3.93\times10^4.\]

The corresponding full resonance width is approximately \(f/Q=159\) kHz for a single weakly damped mode. A fit to detected power would return a 1.00 μs time constant instead. Increasing the probe coupling shortens the measured lifetime, so this ring-down gives the loaded quality factor. Measurements over several coupling strengths help separate internal wall losses from energy deliberately extracted by the probe.

The energy decay also predicts how much source power is needed to sustain a measured field. For example, if the resonant cavity in this measurement stores \(2.00\) nJ, total time-averaged loss is

\[U/\tau_U=2.00\,\mathrm{mW}.\]

Source power actually coupled into the mode must replenish that loss in steady state. The source's forward power can be larger because some incident energy reflects at the coupling port. An independently measured coupling efficiency is therefore needed before converting a power-meter reading at the source into a stored field or a wall heating rate.

Read a waveguide delay near cutoff

In a lossless uniform guide with one propagating mode,

\[\beta^2=(\omega^2-\omega_c^2)/c_m^2.\]

Compute \(v_p=\omega/\beta\) and \(v_g=d\omega/d\beta\). The bandwidth of a pulse must remain inside this propagating band for its group-delay estimate to be useful.
