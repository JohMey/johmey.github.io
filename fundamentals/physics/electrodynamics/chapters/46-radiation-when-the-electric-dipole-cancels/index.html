Radiation when the electric dipole cancels

Two optical dipoles can cancel in the long-wavelength electric-dipole approximation and still radiate. Expanding their source phase shows which moments survive, where the remaining light goes, and how a small asymmetry restores a different angular pattern.

Keep the source phase inside the radiation integral

For a localised harmonic current \(\mathbf J(\mathbf x')e^{-i\omega t}\), take a detector far compared with source size and wavelength. In the outgoing potential set \(1/R\simeq1/r\), while retaining \(kR\simeq kr-k\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x'\), where \(\mathbf n=\mathbf r/r\). This approximation requires the quadratic path phase across the source to be negligible. The radiative electric field is proportional to the transverse part of the current integral

\[\mathbf C(\mathbf n)=\int\mathbf J(\mathbf x')e^{-ik\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x'}\,d^3x',\]
\[\mathbf E_{\rm rad}=\frac{i\mu_0\omega e^{ikr}}{4\pi r}[\mathbf C-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf C)] .\]

For small source size \(a\) with \(ka\ll1\), expand the exponential. Continuity and integration by parts give \(\int J_i\,d^3x=\dot p_i\) for electric dipole \(\mathbf p=\int\rho\mathbf x\,d^3x\). The first correction is \(-ik n_j\int J_i x_j\,d^3x\). Split this integral into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts. Define \(Q_{ij}=\int\rho x_i x_j\,d^3x\) and \(\mathbf m=\frac12\int\mathbf x\times\mathbf J\,d^3x\). Differentiating \(Q_{ij}\) and using charge conservation yields

\[\int J_i x_j\,d^3x=\tfrac12\dot Q_{ij}-\epsilon_{ijk}m_k,\]
\[\mathbf C=\dot{\mathbf p}-\frac{ik}2\dot Q\,\mathbf n+ik\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf m+\cdots .\]

The trace of \(Q\) contributes a vector along \(\mathbf n\); the far-field transverse projection removes it. Thus only its traceless electric quadrupole part can radiate. The antisymmetric contribution is the magnetic dipole. Both occur at the next order in this source-phase expansion. Their importance depends on source symmetry and on the actual sizes of their transition moments.

Construct an electric quadrupole with two displaced dipoles

Put narrow identical \(z\)-directed electric dipoles at \(z=\pm d/2\) and drive them with moments \(+p\) and \(-p\), perfectly out of phase. Their total electric dipole is zero. The currents are collinear with their displacement, so this geometry also gives no net magnetic dipole. In direction \(\theta\) to the axis the single-dipole field has factor \(\sin\theta\). The additional two-source factor is \(e^{-ikd\cos\theta/2}-e^{ikd\cos\theta/2}\). In units of the equatorial intensity of one dipole the complete pattern is

\[F(\theta)=4\sin^2\theta\,\sin^2\!\left(\tfrac12kd\cos\theta\right)\simeq(kd)^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta .\]

This has nodes on the axis from transversality and in the equatorial plane from source cancellation. The leading lobes peak at \(45^\circ\) and \(135^\circ\). Integrating over solid angle, \(\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u^2)u^2\,du=4/15\); the single-dipole integral is \(4/3\). The pair-to-single total-power ratio is \((kd)^2/5\) at leading order. A fixed single-dipole amplitude is the reference; normalising each curve to its own maximum would hide this suppression.

Relate the symmetry argument to optical transitions

In the long-wavelength quantum interaction the electric-dipole term contains a matrix element of position, odd under spatial inversion. Between two states of the same definite parity, the integrand for an electric dipole moment changes sign on inversion and integrates to zero. The electric quadrupole contains two position factors and has even parity. The magnetic dipole is an axial vector and also has even parity. Either next-order channel can therefore satisfy parity between such states, subject to angular-momentum and other selection rules. Classical source symmetries illuminate the long-wavelength expansion; actual atomic emission rates require the relevant quantum matrix elements and state populations.

Estimate the strength and detect imperfect cancellation

An optical-scale separation

For \(d=20\) nm and \(\lambda=600\) nm, \(kd=0.2094\). The leading integrated pair-to-single power ratio is \(0.00877\), under 1%. In the \(45^\circ\) direction the dimensionless intensity is \(4(1/2)\sin^2[kd/(2\sqrt2)]\simeq0.01095\). Tripling \(d\) at fixed wavelength should initially give roughly nine times the power; check the exact sine before applying that extrapolation.

An equatorial leak

Reduce the opposing dipole magnitude to \((1-\delta)p\). Expanding the squared two-source factor gives \(\delta^2+4(1-\delta)\sin^2(kd\cos\theta/2)\). At the equator the second term is zero, leaving intensity fraction \(\delta^2\) relative to one dipole. A measured fraction 0.0025 corresponds to a 5% amplitude imbalance if this is the only imperfection. A phase mismatch can also fill the node, so the observation cannot distinguish both mechanisms without additional phase information.

Break the symmetry of an optical dipole pair

Compare a coherent two-source calculation against its first long-wavelength approximation. At d/λ = 0.035, equal and opposite dipoles emit about 1% of the power of a single dipole. Introduce a 5% amplitude mismatch and check the equatorial node. Then increase spacing to test the limit of the expansion.

Separate the amplitude and power scaling

For the symmetric pair suppose the separation is halved with the two moments and frequency unchanged. Predict the leading power change at \(45^\circ\) and at \(90^\circ\). Where is a ratio meaningful?

Work through the solution

At \(45^\circ\) the leading field is proportional to \(kd\), so the intensity drops by a factor of four. At \(90^\circ\) the exact two-source phase difference is zero for every spacing. Both intensities vanish there and their ratio is undefined. A finite measurement at that angle probes symmetry breaking or background light.

Compare with a closed current loop

A small circular loop carries uniform oscillating current \(I\) and has area \(A\). Identify its leading moment and the directions in which its radiation vanishes.

Work through the solution

The net electric dipole of this ideal divergence-free closed current is zero. Its magnetic dipole is \(IA\hat{\mathbf n}_{\rm loop}\). The radiation term contains \(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m\), hence vanishes along the loop axis and peaks transverse to it. At leading order the loop can radiate strongly in its equatorial plane, unlike the collinear electric-quadrupole pair.
