From first principles

From a short cable segment to two travelling waves

Consider voltage \(V(z,t)\) from inner conductor to return, and current \(I(z,t)\) towards increasing \(z\). A short, uniform, lossless line segment of length \(\Delta z\) stores charge \(C'\Delta z\,V\) and links magnetic flux \(L'\Delta z\,I\). No current leaks through the dielectric. The current difference must charge the segment, and the voltage drop must drive its changing flux:

\[I(z)-I(z+\Delta z)=C'\Delta z\,\partial_t V,\]
\[V(z)-V(z+\Delta z)=L'\Delta z\,\partial_t I .\]

Divide by \(\Delta z\) and take its limit. Differentiate the voltage equation once more in space and eliminate the current derivative using the charge balance:

\[\partial_z V=-L'\partial_t I,\]
\[\partial_z I=-C'\partial_t V,\]
\[\partial_z^2 V=L'C'\partial_t^2 V .\]

For a forward wave \(V=F(t-z/v)\), comparing second derivatives gives

\[v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}.\]

Substituting in the first-order relation shows the forward current is

\[I=V/Z_0,\]

with \(Z_0=vL'=\sqrt{L'/C'}\) for the travelling part. A backward wave reverses that current sign. The line can therefore have a finite characteristic impedance even when its conductors dissipate no heat.

Derivation and applications

Sum repeated returns on a pulse transmission line

A step applied to a finite line can produce a staircase at the load. Model a uniform lossless transmission line with real characteristic impedance \(Z_0\), one-way travel time \(\tau\), resistive termination \(R_L\) and a source step \(V_s\) in series with resistance \(R_s\). Initially the line is unenergised. Until a reflection comes back, the source sees the characteristic impedance, giving the launched voltage \(V_+\). On reaching either termination an incident voltage wave produces a reflected fraction set by that termination.

\[V_+=V_s\frac{Z_0}{R_s+Z_0},\]
\[\Gamma_L=\frac{R_L-Z_0}{R_L+Z_0},\]
\[\Gamma_s=\frac{R_s-Z_0}{R_s+Z_0}.\]

For the source reflection, keep its step height fixed. The incremental wave therefore encounters just \(R_s\). The first arriving step at the load changes its voltage by incident plus reflected amplitudes. Each further round trip multiplies the corresponding incident step by \(\Gamma_L\Gamma_s\). Index the first arrival by \(n=0\).

\[\Delta V_{L,n}=(1+\Gamma_L)V_+(\Gamma_L\Gamma_s)^n,\]
\[t_n=(2n+1)\tau .\]

The voltage after the \(n\)th arrival is the partial sum of these steps. Provided the magnitude of the round-trip multiplier is below unity, the series converges. Substitution of the two voltage reflection coefficients recovers the expected DC divider exactly.

\[V_{L,\infty}=\frac{(1+\Gamma_L)V_+}{1-\Gamma_L\Gamma_s}=\frac{V_sR_L}{R_s+R_L}.\]

Successive steps alternate when the reflection coefficients have opposite signs. The spacing of changes measures travel time even when delays in the measuring electronics obscure the absolute launch time.

Worked example

Predict overshoot at a resistive receiver

Use \(V_s=3.00\) V, \(R_s=25.0\) Ω, \(Z_0=50.0\) Ω, \(R_L=100\) Ω and \(\tau=20.0\) ns. The launched step is 2.00 V. The load and source coefficients are \(+1/3\) and \(-1/3\). The receiver rises to 2.667 V at 20.0 ns, then falls by \(2.667/9=0.296\) V at 60.0 ns. Its eventual 2.400 V follows from the divider. Measuring only the first plateau would overestimate the steady voltage.

Second application

Read the source resistance from staircase increments

A separate 75.0 Ω line has a 225 Ω termination. Two consecutive load increments are \(+0.800\) V and \(+0.0800\) V, 50.0 ns apart. Their ratio is \(0.100=\Gamma_L\Gamma_s\) and \(\Gamma_L=0.500\), yielding \(\Gamma_s=0.200\). Inverting gives

\[R_s=75.0(1.20/0.800)=112.5\,\Omega.\]

The one-way time is 25.0 ns, corresponding to 5.00 m at a measured propagation speed of \(2.00\times10^8\) m/s.

Problem sheet 6 · Can impedance identify a dielectric by itself?

Read the model setup and preparatory problems

Watch a pulse reflect from a load

A lossless metre of transmission line has speed \(2.0\times10^8\) m/s and characteristic impedance 50 Ω. Launch a Gaussian voltage pulse from a matched source. It peaks at the source at 3 ns. Predict when it peaks at the far end and when the return reaches the source.

At the load, voltage adds and currents subtract. Substituting \(V_L=Z_L I_L\) gives

\[\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0).\]

An observer at position \(x\) sees the incident shape delayed by \(x/v\), and the reflected shape by \((2L-x)/v\). This supplies an independent travel-time check for the moving curves.

Sweep time past 8 ns and 13 ns. At a short, explain why zero load voltage coexists with a finite reflected pulse. At a matched termination, trace the energy carried by the incident pulse.

A cable can attenuate a pulse without distorting its shape

A laboratory sends two pulses down a long uniform cable. A shorter pulse has more high-frequency content, so it is often expected to distort more. That expectation implicitly assumes a particular frequency dependence of the propagation constant. We will derive a case in which an entire waveform is simply delayed and reduced in amplitude, even though the line converts energy to heat. The calculation also identifies when adding a second loss mechanism changes the phase response and how to distinguish pure attenuation from imperfect matching at the ends.

Give both energy-storage mechanisms their own losses

Let \(L',C'\) be the series inductance and shunt capacitance per length. Include a series resistance \(R'\) per length and a shunt conductance \(G'\) per length. Assume that these four coefficients are positive constants over the pulse band, the line is uniform, and one transverse electromagnetic mode suffices. Charge and voltage balances on a short segment give

\[-\partial_zV=R'I+L'\partial_t I,\]
\[-\partial_zI=G'V+C'\partial_t V .\]

With time convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\) and a forward spatial dependence \(e^{-\Gamma z}\), their product and ratio yield

\[\Gamma^2=(R'-i\omega L')(G'-i\omega C'),\]
\[Z_{\rm line}^2=(R'-i\omega L')/(G'-i\omega C').\]

Choose the forward branch \(\Gamma=\alpha-i\beta\), \(\alpha\geq0,\beta\geq0\), with the impedance branch satisfying the original first-order equations for a forward wave. In general both amplitude and phase change differently for different frequencies. A matched receiver must account for the actual line impedance rather than just a nominal lossless value.

Find the exact condition for undistorted propagation

Suppose \(R'/L'=G'/C'=\nu\), with units of inverse seconds. Both complex factors then share \(\nu-i\omega\), so \(\Gamma=\sqrt{L'C'}(\nu-i\omega)\) and \(Z_{\rm line}=\sqrt{L'/C'}\) is real and frequency independent. Define \(u=1/\sqrt{L'C'}\) and \(\alpha=\nu/u\). The forward transfer function over distance \(z\) multiplies each voltage component by \(e^{-\alpha z}e^{+i\omega z/u}\). Fourier inversion thus gives

\[V(z,t)=e^{-\alpha z}V(0,t-z/u),\]
\[I(z,t)=V(z,t)/Z_{\rm line}.\]

The delay sign can be checked by inserting the result in the time-domain equations. In fact \(V\) satisfies

\[\partial_z^2V=L'C'(\partial_t+\nu)^2V.\]

One-way solutions satisfy the first-order propagation operator \(\partial_z+u^{-1}(\partial_t+\nu)\). Direct substitution gives zero for any differentiable input shape. Piecewise smooth pulses follow by continuity or by their Fourier representations. This is the Heaviside distortionless condition. It permits attenuation; it is a relationship between two loss-to-storage ratios.

Verify that the missing power becomes heat

Multiply the two original equations by \(I\) and \(V\) respectively, then add. The product rule yields

\[-\partial_z(VI)=R'I^2+G'V^2+\partial_t\!\left(\frac12L'I^2+\frac12 C'V^2\right).\]

The first two terms are positive local heating rates per length. For a forward pulse \(I=V/Z_{\rm line}\). At the matched loss ratios,

\[R'/Z_{\rm line}^2=G'.\]

Conductor heating and dielectric leakage heating are therefore equal at fixed local voltage, although the resistances of the two physical paths play different roles. Integrating over all time removes the storage derivative if the line begins and ends quiet. The pulse energy crossing each section decays as \(e^{-2\alpha z}\), the square of its voltage attenuation. This provides a local and an integrated measurement of the same physical loss.

Take \(L'=0.25\) μH/m and \(C'=100\) pF/m. Then \(u=2.00\times10^8\) m/s and \(Z_{\rm line}=50.0\ \Omega\). For \(R'=0.050\ \Omega/\mathrm m\), choose \(G'=20.0\) μS/m, making both ratios \(2.00\times10^5\) s⁻¹. A 100 m line delays every part of a matched pulse by \(0.500\) μs and multiplies its voltage by \(e^{-0.10}=0.905\). Its transmitted energy is 81.9% of the launched energy, leaving 18.1% as loss along the line. A pulse measured to be ten per cent lower in voltage has therefore lost more than ten per cent of its transported energy.

Show why a constant attenuation and a linear phase are restrictive

The sufficient condition can also be obtained as a necessity if exact distortionless propagation is required over a continuous frequency range in this constant-coefficient model. Suppose \(\Gamma=\alpha-i b\omega\) with constant attenuation and delay per length \(b\). Squaring and matching constant, linear and quadratic terms against \((R'-i\omega L')(G'-i\omega C')\) yields \(\alpha^2=R'G'\), \(b^2=L'C'\) and \(2\alpha b=R'C'+L'G'\). Eliminating \(\alpha,b\) leaves \((R'C'-L'G')^2=0\). A line with only series resistance and zero shunt loss cannot satisfy that exact identity unless it is lossless altogether.

This test uses the functional dependence on frequency, rather than one measured attenuation value. In a real cable the skin effect makes \(R'\) increase with frequency, and a dielectric may have frequency-dependent polarisation loss. The two traces from two cable lengths therefore test a specified band of operation. An approximately straight phase line over a limited band can be entirely consistent with the measurements while extrapolating that fit to a much shorter pulse gives appreciable waveform error.

Estimate the effect of loss mismatch

A second cable has the same \(R',L',C'\) but negligible \(G'\). Does weak loss recover the same exact propagation law? Derive its leading high-frequency attenuation and give a procedure for detecting the missing exact cancellation.

Derive the result and check it independently

When \(\omega L'\gg R'\) and \(\omega C'\gg G'\), expand the square root around its lossless form. To first order the attenuation is \(\alpha\simeq[R'/Z_{\rm lossless}+G'Z_{\rm lossless}]/2\), with \(Z_{\rm lossless}=\sqrt{L'/C'}\); the phase is approximately \(\omega/u\). With negligible \(G'\) the asymptotic attenuation is \(0.00050\) m⁻¹. This is an approximation over a band. At sufficiently low frequency \(R'\) competes strongly with \(\omega L'\); then \(\Gamma\) behaves like the square root of frequency and the waveform distorts.

Send the same calibrated pulse through two lengths, compare complex spectra and form their ratio to suppress the source response. Under distortionless propagation the logarithm of its magnitude is constant across frequency and its unwrapped phase is linear. Periodic ripples can indicate connector reflections. Time-gating a clean first arrival, or matching the relevant band and checking a second cable length, helps distinguish distributed distortion from an echo. A single rise-time reading cannot by itself identify the underlying loss mechanism.

A line resolves the travel time

A cable can store energy all along its length. When a change of source voltage travels an appreciable fraction of the cable during its rise time, a single lumped capacitor cannot encode both its arrival and its reflection. Model a uniform two-conductor line using inductance \(L'\) and capacitance \(C'\) per unit length. Omit conductor resistance and dielectric leakage initially. Let \(V(z,t)\) be voltage between conductors and \(I(z,t)\) current toward increasing \(z\).

A short section \(dz\) experiences an inductive voltage difference \(L'dz\,\partial_t I\). Its net incoming current charges \(C'dz\) at the rate \(\partial_t V\). Accounting for the positive current direction gives the pair

\[\partial_z V=-L'\partial_t I,\]
\[\partial_z I=-C'\partial_t V.\]

Differentiating once more gives wave equations with \(v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}\). Substitution of a travelling waveform shows that a forward wave has \(I_+=V_+/Z_0\), while a backward wave has \(I_-=-V_-/Z_0\), where \(Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}\). The characteristic impedance thus describes the voltage-to-current ratio of one travelling component. A resistance meter at the end of a finite DC cable ordinarily measures something different.

How the load launches a return wave

At a resistive termination \(R_L\) the total voltage equals \(R_L\) times total current. Write \(V_-= \Gamma V_+\) and use the opposite current signs above. Rearrangement gives

\[\Gamma=\frac{R_L-Z_0}{R_L+Z_0},\]
\[V_L=(1+\Gamma)V_+ .\]

An open end doubles voltage and cancels current. A shorted end cancels voltage and doubles current. With positive real \(Z_0\) the reflected fraction of incoming wave power is \(\Gamma^2\). A voltage reflection of either sign carries energy back toward the source. A matched resistor absorbs all the incident energy, a condition also useful for interpreting the first step in a time-domain voltage trace.

Locate and characterize a mismatch

A line with \(L'=250\) nH/m and \(C'=100\) pF/m has \(v=2.00\times10^8\) m/s and \(Z_0=50.0\) Ω. A 2.00 V source step with series resistance 50.0 Ω launches 1.00 V into a 30.0 m cable. Initially the source sees the characteristic impedance because no reflection has had time to return. At 150 ns the leading edge reaches a 150 Ω load, where \(\Gamma=0.500\). The load rises to 1.50 V and draws 10.0 mA.

The return step of 0.50 V reaches the source at 300 ns. The source resistance is matched, so it absorbs the returning wave with no further reflection and the source-end voltage becomes 1.50 V. The result agrees with the final DC divider. A measured return time locates the discontinuity through \(d=vt_{\rm return}/2\), while its signed amplitude identifies an impedance increase or decrease. Distributed losses must be estimated before using this amplitude ratio on a long real cable.

Changing the source resistance leaves the first arrival time unchanged while altering the incident amplitude. It also changes what happens when the first reflection returns. At a source whose internal resistance differs from the line impedance, part of that returning pulse is launched outward again. Repeated round trips then generate a staircase of voltages separated by twice the propagation time.

Match unequal impedances with a finite line segment

A resistive load can be matched at a selected frequency even when its resistance differs from the feeding line’s characteristic impedance. Insert a uniform lossless section of impedance \(Z_t\) and length \(\ell\) between the original line \(Z_0\) and the load \(R_L\). Inside the added section decompose voltage into forward and backward waves. With the load plane at \(z=0\), a convenient phasor convention gives

\[V(z)=V_+e^{-i\beta z}+V_-e^{i\beta z},\]
\[I(z)=(V_+e^{-i\beta z}-V_-e^{i\beta z})/Z_t.\]

At \(z=0\) use \(V(0)/I(0)=R_L\) to remove the unknown reflection coefficient.

Evaluate the ratio at the input plane \(z=-\ell\). Collecting the exponentials into cosines and sines gives

\[Z_{\rm in}=Z_t\frac{R_L\cos(\beta\ell)+iZ_t\sin(\beta\ell)}{Z_t\cos(\beta\ell)+iR_L\sin(\beta\ell)}.\]

For an electrically short segment the input simply approaches the load. At a quarter wavelength \(\beta\ell=\pi/2\) and \(Z_{\rm in}=Z_t^2/R_L\). Setting this equal to \(Z_0\) gives

\[Z_t=\sqrt{Z_0R_L}.\]

Reflections still occur inside the added section, but their summed contribution at its input cancels at the design frequency. This is a phase-dependent match, so its behaviour under a suddenly applied step differs from a resistor matched at every frequency.

Design a section and test its bandwidth

A 50.0 Ω source line drives a 100 Ω resistor at 100 MHz. Available cable has phase speed \(2.00\times10^8\) m/s. The required matching impedance is \(70.7\) Ω and the length is

\[v/(4f)=0.500\,\mathrm{m}.\]

At design frequency the input impedance equals 50.0 Ω, whereas directly attaching the resistor would produce reflection amplitude \((100-50)/(100+50)=1/3\) and reflect 11.1 percent of the incident power.

To test the frequency limit without dividing by a nearly infinite tangent, evaluate the sine–cosine form at 50.0 MHz. The electrical length is \(\pi/4\), so the trigonometric factors cancel. Substituting the three real impedances gives \(Z_{\rm in}=66.7-23.6i\) Ω. The feeding-line reflection magnitude \(|(Z_{\rm in}-50)/(Z_{\rm in}+50)|\) is about \(0.243\), reflecting \(5.9\) percent of incident power. At 200 MHz the section is half a wavelength and the input again equals the bare load. A frequency sweep should therefore show a matched centre frequency and increasing reflected power away from it. Loss or an appreciably reactive load changes both the depth and position of that minimum.

A frequency-domain match can also be understood through a short series of time-domain reflections. The impedance first rises from 50.0 to 70.7 ohms and then rises again from 70.7 to 100 ohms. The two interface reflections have the same sign when considered locally. At a quarter wavelength, propagation through the matching section and back adds a half-cycle phase, making the returning contributions cancel after all internal reflections are summed. An abrupt step arrives at those interfaces at different times, so the earliest observed reflected step need not vanish. A narrowband sinusoid and a broadband step perform different tests of the same lossless device.

An actual connector of finite electrical length introduces another discontinuity that may be visible in either test.

Recover the load from a reflected pulse

Send a voltage step down a lossless line into a real load. Continuity gives

\[\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0).\]

Use the original wave impedance for both incident and backward power, which makes reflected power scale as \(\Gamma^2\).
