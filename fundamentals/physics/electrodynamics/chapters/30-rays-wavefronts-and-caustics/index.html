From wavefronts to rays, focusing and caustics

A ray is a prediction extracted from the phase of a wave. Following its amplitude at the same time reveals the point at which a ray calculation loses its validity. A gently graded transparent material gives a solvable example of both effects.

Derive the phase and transport equations together

In a lossless isotropic dielectric with a slowly varying refractive index \(n(\mathbf r)\), consider one scalar polarisation component satisfying \(\nabla^2\psi+k_0^2 n^2\psi=0\), where \(k_0=\omega/c\). This scalar description works when vector-polarisation coupling is negligible over the scales of interest. Set \(\psi=A(\mathbf r)e^{ik_0S(\mathbf r)}\) with a slowly varying real amplitude. Differentiation twice gives

\[0=\nabla^2 A+i k_0[2\nabla A\cdot\nabla S+A\nabla^2S]+k_0^2 A(n^2-|\nabla S|^2).\]

If the material and amplitude vary little over a wavelength, first equate the leading \(k_0^2\) term to zero. At the next order the \(k_0\) term vanishes. The two results are the eikonal equation and transport equation:

\[|\nabla S|^2=n^2,\]
\[\nabla\cdot(A^2\nabla S)=0 .\]

The first fixes the optical length accumulated between neighbouring wavefronts. The second transports energy down a thin ray tube. Across a tube of area \(\mathcal A\) perpendicular to the phase gradient, \(A^2 n\mathcal A\) is constant to this order. In a uniform medium a spherical tube grows as \(r^2\), giving \(A\propto1/r\), consistent with the outgoing wave.

Follow the normal to a wavefront

Choose arclength \(s\) along the ray and \(\hat{\mathbf t}=\nabla S/n\). In the eikonal equation take a spatial derivative. Since \(\nabla S\) is a gradient, \((\nabla S\cdot\nabla)\nabla S=\nabla(|\nabla S|^2)/2=n\nabla n\). Divide by \(n\). The ray equation is

\[\frac{d}{ds}(n\hat{\mathbf t})=\nabla n .\]

A transverse increase of index bends the ray toward larger optical index. If \(n\) depends only on the coordinate normal to layers, the component of \(n\hat{\mathbf t}\) parallel to them stays constant. Thus \(n\sin\theta\) is conserved for an angle measured from the normal, recovering Snell's law. The same equation follows by requiring stationary optical length \(\int n\,ds\) when varying the path with fixed endpoints.

Solve a graded-index profile

For paraxial rays travelling close to \(+z\), take \(n(y)\simeq n_0(1-g^2y^2/2)\). Assume \(|gy|\ll1\) and \(|dy/dz|\ll1\). Then \(ds\simeq dz\), \(\hat t_y\simeq y'\), and \(d(ny')/dz\simeq n_0y''\) to leading order. Since \(\partial_y n=-n_0g^2y\), the trajectory solves \(y''+g^2y=0\). Initial height \(y_0\) and slope \(s_0\) give

\[y(z)=y_0\cos(gz)+\frac{s_0}{g}\sin(gz),\]
\[y'(z)=-g y_0\sin(gz)+s_0\cos(gz).\]

For a collimated bundle, all \(s_0=0\). The transverse magnification \(\partial y/\partial y_0=\cos(gz)\) vanishes simultaneously for all rays at \(z_f=\pi/(2g)\). This is a caustic of the ray description. In this planar example the ray density grows as \(1/|\cos(gz)|\). Its divergence signals that the neglected \(\nabla^2 A\) becomes important. The underlying wave stays finite and develops diffraction near the focus.

A focus and a reconstructed gradient

Refocus an off-axis bundle

Take \(g=20\ {\rm m^{-1}}\), initial heights between \(-1\) and \(+1\) mm and zero slopes. The predicted first focus is \(78.5\) mm downstream. The extreme rays acquire slopes of magnitude \(0.020\) at the focus, well within the paraxial regime. Tilt every initial ray by 0.010 rad. At \(z_f\) the common height shifts to \(s_0/g=0.50\) mm. The focal length is insensitive to this common tilt in the parabolic approximation.

Estimate the index from a photograph

Two successive crossings of initially parallel symmetric rays are 120 mm apart. Crossings occur at \(gz=\pi/2+m\pi\), so \(g=\pi/0.120=26.2\ {\rm m^{-1}}\). At a height of 2 mm, the predicted fractional index decrease from the central index is \(g^2y^2/2=1.37\times10^{-3}\). Resolving this small contrast through repeated bending can be easier than resolving it through a single refraction measurement.

Focus and tilt a bundle of paraxial rays

At gradient 20 m⁻¹, five parallel rays meet after 78.5 mm. A common tilt of 10 mrad displaces their meeting point by 0.50 mm. Follow the bundle through a crossing and decide why the geometric density ceases to predict a finite intensity there.

Problem sheet · optical path and diffraction

A travel-time check

A ray passes through two homogeneous layers of thicknesses \(a,b\), indices \(n_1,n_2\), with lateral endpoint separation \(D\). Write travel time as a function of crossing coordinate \(x\). Recover the interface condition.

Work through the solution

The time is \(T(x)=[n_1\sqrt{a^2+x^2}+n_2\sqrt{b^2+(D-x)^2}]/c\). The derivative vanishes when \(n_1 x/\sqrt{a^2+x^2}=n_2(D-x)/\sqrt{b^2+(D-x)^2}\). The two fractions are the sines of the angles to the interface normal. Each segment's length has positive second derivative, making this stationary path a local minimum in this geometry.

Estimate the diffraction scale

A circular pupil of numerical aperture 0.030 focuses vacuum light of wavelength 600 nm. Estimate the first dark-ring radius and compare it with a ray focus.

Work through the solution

For a uniformly illuminated circular aperture in the paraxial limit the first Airy minimum is \(0.61\lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}\), giving \(12.2\) μm. Different pupil shapes change the numerical factor. The wavelength dependence is absent from the ray crossing, so a ray trace alone cannot distinguish two experiments with identical geometry but different wavelengths.
