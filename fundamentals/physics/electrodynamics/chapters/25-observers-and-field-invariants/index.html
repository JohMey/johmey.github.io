Changing observers and classifying electromagnetic fields

Can an observer move so that a measured electric field disappears? Two numbers constructed from the fields settle the question before a velocity is chosen. The moving observer's force measurements explain why the classification works.

Measure fields at the same event

Take two inertial laboratories with parallel axes. The primed laboratory moves with constant velocity \(v\hat{\mathbf x}\) relative to the unprimed one. Compare fields at one spacetime event. The position labels and times assigned to that event differ by \(x'=\gamma(x-vt)\) and \(t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2)\), where \(\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}\). Comparing both experiments at identical numerical position and time labels would usually compare different events.

How can a primed observer measure \(E'_y\)? Release a probe initially at rest in the primed frame. In the unprimed frame the probe has velocity \(v\hat{\mathbf x}\), so its transverse force is \(q(E_y-vB_z)\). The transverse momentum \(p_y\) is invariant under this boost, but along the probe's instantaneous trajectory \(dt'=dt/\gamma\). Thus \(dp'_y/dt'=\gamma dp_y/dt\), and \(E'_y=\gamma(E_y-vB_z)\). The other transverse component is \(E'_z=\gamma(E_z+vB_y)\). Longitudinal electric field is unchanged. A probe with adjustable primed velocity can also distinguish the magnetic force. The complete pair of rules is

\[\mathbf E'_\parallel=\mathbf E_\parallel,\]
\[\mathbf B'_\parallel=\mathbf B_\parallel,\]
\[\mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E_\perp+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),\]
\[\mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf B_\perp-\mathbf v\times\mathbf E/c^2).\]

Which differences survive a boost?

Examine perpendicular laboratory fields \(E_y=E\), \(cB_z=b\). In their common units the transformed pair is \(e'=\gamma(E-\beta b)\),

\[b'=\gamma(b-\beta E).\]

Squaring and subtracting cancels the cross terms. The factor \(\gamma^2(1-\beta^2)\) is unity. In a general orientation the parallel parts remain unchanged and the other pair of transverse components cancels in the same manner. The scalar product also remains unchanged. The two invariants are

\[I_1=|\mathbf E|^2-c^2|\mathbf B|^2,\]
\[I_2=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B .\]

If \(I_2\neq0\), neither field can be transformed away. For nonzero orthogonal fields with \(I_1<0\), choose \(\mathbf v=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). Its speed is \(E/B<c\), and it cancels \(\mathbf E'\). If \(I_1>0\) choose \(\mathbf v=c^2\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/E^2\) to cancel \(\mathbf B'\). For nonzero orthogonal fields of equal strength in common units the required speed would be \(c\). A plane wave illustrates this null case. A zero field also has both invariants zero, so both vanishing invariants alone do not prove that a wave is present.

Two changes of observer

An observer travelling with drifting charges

Let \(E_y=1.80\times10^5\) V/m and \(B_z=1.00\) mT. The cancelling velocity is \(+1.80\times10^8\) m/s along \(x\), approximately \(0.600c\). Then \(B'_z=B_z\sqrt{1-E^2/(cB_z)^2}\simeq0.800\) mT. A charge moving with that primed laboratory feels zero force at the chosen event. In the laboratory, the electric and magnetic terms cancel directly. The statements concern one probe velocity; charges moving differently in the primed frame can experience magnetic force.

Devices following a light pulse

A plane pulse travelling in \(+x\) has \(E_y=cB_z\). A detector moving along \(+x\) measures

\[E'_y/E_y=\gamma(1-\beta)=\sqrt{(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)}.\]

At \(\beta=0.60\) this is \(1/2\), and the intensity ratio is \(1/4\). Transform a phase \(kx-\omega t\) using \(k=\omega/c\). Its frequency receives the same factor \(1/2\). Both detectors obtain \(E'/B'=c\).

Why a neutral wire can have a charge density

For enclosed charge to agree between inertial descriptions, the charge and current densities must form the four-current \(J^\mu=(c\rho,\mathbf J)\). A boost gives

\[\rho'=\gamma(\rho-vJ_x/c^2),\]
\[J'_x=\gamma(J_x-v\rho).\]

For equal positive and negative charge densities producing a laboratory current, \(\rho=0\) but \(J_x\neq0\). In the moving laboratory the densities do not cancel. The change comes from how a simultaneous spatial slice intersects the worldlines of moving charge. Dividing a fixed list of particles by a contracted segment while keeping its endpoint events fixed would miscount particles crossing either endpoint.

Find a frame that cancels a field by varying its velocity

The axes are oriented as in the crossed-field derivation. For perpendicular fields E = 90 and cB = 150 kV/m, a boost at 0.60c cancels E and leaves cB′ = 120 kV/m. Exchange the two inputs and predict which field can now vanish.

Problem sheet · classification and measurements

Classify before calculating

Take a field with \(\mathbf E=E_0\hat{\mathbf y}\) and \(c\mathbf B=2E_0\hat{\mathbf y}+E_0\hat{\mathbf z}\). Could either field disappear? Explain why consulting the difference of squares alone gives incomplete information.

Work through the solution

The magnetic magnitude is \(\sqrt{5}E_0/c\), making \(I_1=-4E_0^2\). However \(I_2=2E_0^2/c\neq0\). A frame with either field zero would have \(I_2=0\), contradicting its observed invariant value.

A density reconstructed from a current

A laboratory measures \(\rho=0\) and \(J_x=9.0\) A/m². A second lab moves at \(0.60c\). Determine its current density and charge density, including signs.

Work through the solution

Here \(\gamma=1.25\). The transformed current is \(+11.25\) A/m². The transformed density is \(-1.25(0.60)(9.0)/c=-2.25\times10^{-8}\) C/m³. Inserting these into \(c^2\rho'^2-J_x'^2\) returns \(-81\) in squared current-density units, a check independent of sign conventions in the voltage probes.

Practicum · reconstruct a crossed-field trajectory from two clocks

A positron initially rests in a laboratory with uniform crossed fields \(\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}\),

\[\mathbf E=E\hat{\mathbf y},\]

with \(B=10.0\) mT and \(E/(cB)=0.600\). Ignore radiation losses and edge effects. Predict its maximum speed, arrival point after one cycle, and energy gain halfway through the cycle. Work by transforming whole events between observers. A velocity transformation alone cannot predict the travel time.

Choose the frame in which the trajectory is simplest

Find a frame with zero electric field, transform the initial particle velocity, and derive the orbit radius and frequency in that frame. Define what a “cycle” means for this measurement.

Follow the calculation and check the result

Boost along \(+x\) at \(v_d=E/B=0.600c\) with \(\gamma_d=1.25\). Then \(E'_y=0\) and \(B'_z=B/\gamma_d=8.00\) mT. Initially the particle has \(v'_x=-v_d\), so its primed energy is constant at \(\gamma_dmc^2\). With positive charge \(e\), its initial magnetic force points towards \(+y\). Defining \(\Omega'=eB'/(\gamma_dm)\) and \(R'=\gamma_dm v_d/(eB')\), one obtains

\[x'=-R'\sin(\Omega't'),\]
\[y'=R'[1-\cos(\Omega't')].\]

Differentiating verifies the initial velocity and yields the rotating transverse acceleration. The primed radius is approximately 0.160 m and the primed period \(T'=2\pi/\Omega'\) is 5.58 ns. One cycle means two successive events at which the primed particle returns to its initial location and velocity. In the laboratory these events occur at different positions.

Measure time and the maximum lab speed

Transform the coordinates of the first return event to the laboratory. At what part of its orbit does the particle reach its maximum lab speed? Why is it incorrect to use a single constant \(\gamma_d\) to convert every subinterval along the curved path?

Follow the calculation and check the result

For a full primed period \(\Delta x'=0\). The inverse Lorentz transformation gives

\[\Delta t=\gamma_dT'=6.98\,\mathrm{ns},\]
\[\Delta x=\gamma_dv_dT'=1.26\,\mathrm{m}.\]

Hence the average lab drift is \(0.600c\) along \(+x\). For an arbitrary subinterval the formula is instead \(\Delta t=\gamma_d(\Delta t'+v_d\Delta x'/c^2)\). Away from rest or half-period endpoints its second term generally contributes.

The primed momentum has largest positive \(x'\) component at half a period, when \(v'_x=v_d\). Then \(v_x=2v_d/(1+v_d^2/c^2)=0.88235c\), \(v_y=0\). The lab energy transformation \(\mathcal E=\gamma_d(\mathcal E'+v_dp'_x)\) confirms that this is the energy maximum and hence the speed maximum. At the end of a period the particle returns to laboratory rest even though the apparatus has seen a net displacement.

Check the energy with an independent voltage measurement

At half a cycle compute the work performed by the actual laboratory electric field from the displacement alone. Compare with the relativistic kinetic energy and explain how it can be zero over a whole cycle.

Follow the calculation and check the result

At the top of the primed orbit \(y'=2R'\). A boost along \(x\) leaves \(y=2R'\) unchanged. The electric work is \(eE(2R')=2\gamma_d^2\beta_d^2mc^2=1.125mc^2\), or about 575 keV. The lab maximum speed gives \(\gamma_{\max}=(1+\beta_d^2)/(1-\beta_d^2)=2.125\), so its kinetic energy is the same \(1.125mc^2\). The magnetic force performs zero work throughout. During the second half the particle moves back down in \(y\), and the electric field removes exactly this kinetic energy.

At a fixed laboratory event a numerical trajectory can be checked against both \(\mathcal E-mc^2=eEy\) and the parametric orbit. Comparing the two paths at equal numerical values of \(t\) and \(t'\) would compare different events and could create apparent errors in both period and speed. At each successive cusp the lab velocity momentarily vanishes; the motion on either side reverses its transverse component. The cusp spacing supplies an additional check on the drift distance.
