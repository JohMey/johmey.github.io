Energy, momentum and a charged particle’s worldline

A field may turn the momentum, change its magnitude, or do both. Beginning with a measurable clock carried by the particle gives a force law that remains well behaved even when increasing momentum produces little visible change in speed.

Use ticks of a comoving clock

A massive particle has a timelike worldline. In a laboratory with spacetime coordinates \(x^\mu=(ct,\mathbf x)\), the invariant interval along its path gives \(c^2d\tau^2=c^2dt^2-d\mathbf x^2\), so \(d\tau=dt/\gamma\),

\[\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.\]

Its four-velocity \(u^\mu=dx^\mu/d\tau=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)\) has fixed squared norm \(c^2\). Multiplication by the measured rest mass gives four-momentum \(p^\mu=(\mathcal E/c,\mathbf p)\), with \(\mathcal E=\gamma mc^2\),

\[\mathbf p=\gamma m\mathbf v.\]

Squaring and differentiating at fixed rest mass gives

\[\mathcal E^2=m^2c^4+p^2c^2,\]
\[d\mathcal E=\frac{c^2\mathbf p}{\mathcal E}\cdot d\mathbf p=\mathbf v\cdot d\mathbf p .\]

Use \(\dot{\mathbf p}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\). Taking its scalar product with \(\mathbf v\) shows \(d\mathcal E/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\). A uniform magnetic field changes momentum direction while conserving its magnitude and energy. Resolving a general force along and across the velocity gives

\[F_\parallel=\gamma^3 m a_\parallel,\]
\[F_\perp=\gamma m a_\perp.\]

These factors follow from differentiating \(\gamma\mathbf v\), without assigning the particle a changing rest mass.

Integrate motion in a constant electric field

Choose a positive charge initially at rest with \(E\) along \(+x\), and neglect other forces. A negative charge follows the reflected trajectory. Define \(s=qEt/(mc)\). Lab momentum grows linearly, \(p=qEt\). Substitute it into the energy relation and use \(v=c^2p/\mathcal E\). Integration with \(x(0)=0\) gives

\[\gamma=\sqrt{1+s^2},\]
\[\frac vc=\frac{s}{\sqrt{1+s^2}},\]
\[x=\frac{mc^2}{qE}\left(\sqrt{1+s^2}-1\right).\]

Multiplying the distance by \(qE\) recovers kinetic energy \(\mathcal E-mc^2\). A second check comes from the clock. Integrating \(d\tau=dt/\sqrt{1+s^2}\) gives

\[\tau=(mc/qE)\operatorname{arsinh}s.\]

With rapidity \(\eta=qE\tau/(mc)\),

\[s=\sinh\eta,\ \gamma=\cosh\eta.\]

The comoving proper acceleration is \(qE/m\) even while the laboratory acceleration decreases as \(\gamma^{-3}\). Expanding at early times yields

\[v=qEt/m+O(t^3),\]
\[x=qEt^2/(2m)+O(t^4).\]

Read an energy from the curvature of a track

In a uniform magnetic field along \(z\), longitudinal momentum and energy remain fixed. For transverse motion the change in momentum direction has magnitude \(p_\perp v_\perp/r\). Equating it with \(|q|v_\perp B\) gives

\[r=p_\perp/(|q|B).\]

The angular frequency is \(\omega_c=|q|B/(\gamma m)\), and the longitudinal distance travelled per turn is \(2\pi p_\parallel/(|q|B)\). The sign of the charge determines the sense of rotation for a specified field direction. Radius alone measures transverse momentum. To reconstruct total momentum from a helical path one must also measure the pitch.

Reconstruct two ideal laboratory observations

When does the classical speed fail?

For an electron starting at rest in a uniform field of magnitude 100 kV/m, the momentum magnitude reaches \(mc\) after \(mc/(eE)=17.0\) ns. A Newtonian extrapolation then predicts speed \(c\). The relativistic result is \(c/\sqrt2=0.707c\), kinetic energy \((\sqrt2-1)mc^2=212\) keV and travel distance \(2.12\) m. The potential difference over this distance is 212 kV, giving the same kinetic energy independently. These are ideal constant-field values for a prescribed test particle.

A helical track with a missing component

In \(B=0.20\) T a singly charged particle has circular projection of radius \(5.0\) mm and advances \(20\) mm along the field per revolution. Using elementary charge \(e\), one obtains \(p_\perp=1.60\times10^{-22}\) kg m/s and \(p_\parallel=1.02\times10^{-22}\) kg m/s. For an electron \(p/(mc)=0.696\), \(\gamma\simeq1.22\), and the kinetic energy is \(112\) keV. Assuming the track lay entirely in a transverse plane would underestimate its kinetic energy. A heavier singly charged particle with the same track has the same momentum but a different kinetic energy.

Compare a particle clock with a lab timer

In a 100 kV/m uniform field, an initially stationary electron reaches about 0.706c after 17 ns lab time, while its own clock advances roughly 15 ns. Identify where the Newtonian extrapolation crosses the light-speed line and inspect the kinetic energy at that time.

Problem sheet · frame and trajectory checks

Measure the elapsed proper time

For the electric trajectory starting from rest, let the final momentum be \(2mc\). Find the ratio of elapsed proper time to lab time, and compare it with the instantaneous final \(1/\gamma\).

Work through the solution

Since \(s=2\), the elapsed-time ratio is

\[\operatorname{arsinh}(2)/2=0.722.\]

At the endpoint \(1/\gamma=1/\sqrt5=0.447\). The particle was slower earlier. Applying its final clock rate to the entire trajectory would discard its acceleration history.

Predict an orbit in crossed fields

Take perpendicular fields with \(E/(cB)=0.50\) and a particle initially at rest in the laboratory. Describe its motion in the frame moving at \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\), and recover its average transverse drift direction.

Work through the solution

The boost speed is \(0.50c\). The primed electric field vanishes, the magnetic field is \(B/\gamma_d\) and the initial particle velocity is opposite the boost. Its primed motion is circular with constant speed. After every complete primed period its primed position returns to its starting value. Transforming the displacement between successive returns gives \(\Delta x/\Delta t=v_d\) along the drift direction, independently of the sign of charge. Opposite signs reverse the sense of the primed circle.
