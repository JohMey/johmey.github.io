Replace an instantaneous spring constant by a delayed response

A static dielectric constant is measured after the material has relaxed. At optical frequencies the bound charges have inertia and damping. Model a density \(N\) of independent bound charges of mass \(m\), charge \(q\), natural angular frequency \(\omega_0\) and damping rate \(\gamma>0\). A weak field gives a displacement satisfying \(m\ddot x+m\gamma\dot x+m\omega_0^2x=qE(t)\). The resulting polarisation is \(P=Nqx\). This model assumes small displacements and an isotropic response. A crystal with several resonances needs additional modes and oscillator strengths.

For a sinusoidal field with the convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\), differentiation multiplies a phasor by \(-i\omega\). Dividing the equation by its dynamic stiffness gives

\[\chi(\omega)=\frac{P}{\epsilon_0E}=\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\]
\[\omega_p^2=\frac{Nq^2}{m\epsilon_0}.\]

Rationalise the denominator using \(A=\omega_0^2-\omega^2,\ B=\gamma\omega\). Then \(\chi'=\omega_p^2A/(A^2+B^2)\) and \(\chi''=\omega_p^2B/(A^2+B^2)\). The real part changes sign across the resonance. The imaginary part stays positive at positive frequencies with our convention. In the low-frequency limit the static spring law reappears, \(\chi(0)=\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2\).

Decide which part of the response dissipates work

The field delivers power density \(E\dot P\) to the bound charges. The polarisation current phasor is \(-i\omega\epsilon_0\chi E\). For peak-amplitude phasors the cycle average of two real oscillating quantities is half the real part of one phasor times the complex conjugate of the other. Thus

\[\langle E\dot P\rangle=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\chi''|E|^2 .\]

This result also follows by multiplying the oscillator equation by \(N\dot x\). The kinetic and spring energy changes integrate to zero over one steady period, leaving \(Nm\gamma\langle\dot x^2\rangle\) as heat. Positive loss therefore has a mechanical interpretation independent of any phasor notation. Changing to an \(e^{+i\omega t}\) convention reverses the sign attached to the imaginary part, while the heat remains positive.

Worked response at resonance

Take \(\omega_p^2=0.20\,\omega_0^2\) and \(\gamma=0.10\,\omega_0\). At \(\omega=\omega_0\), \(A=0\) and \(\chi=2i\). If \(\omega_0=2.0\times10^{12}\) rad/s and \(E=100\) V/m peak, the absorbed power density is \(1.77\times10^5\) W/m³. This is local power where the field has that amplitude. Applying it throughout a thick absorbing slab would neglect attenuation of the field with depth.

Recover the actual lengths and speeds in a material

For relative permeability one and a plane wave \(e^{ikz-i\omega t}\), \(k=(\omega/c)\sqrt{1+\chi}\). Choose \(n=n'+i\kappa\) with \(\kappa\ge0\) for a passive medium. The field amplitude decays as \(e^{-\omega\kappa z/c}\) and intensity as \(e^{-2\omega\kappa z/c}\). At the resonance of the previous example, \(1+\chi=1+2i\). Equating real and imaginary parts of the squared index gives

\[n'^2-\kappa^2=1,\]
\[2n'\kappa=2,\]
\[n'=\sqrt{\tfrac12(\sqrt5+1)},\]
\[\kappa=\sqrt{\tfrac12(\sqrt5-1)} .\]

Here \(n'=1.272,\ \kappa=0.786\). At the stated angular frequency the intensity attenuation length \(c/(2\omega\kappa)=95.3\,\mu{\rm m}\). A finite slab also reflects at its surfaces. Inferring bulk loss from transmitted power alone requires accounting for those reflections.

For a narrow packet, expand the real propagation constant around its carrier: \(k'(\omega)\simeq k'_c+(\omega-\omega_c)\,dk'/d\omega\), where \(k'\) here denotes the real part of \(k\). The carrier phase moves at \(\omega_c/k'_c\). If attenuation and curvature vary little across the packet bandwidth, the envelope delay over length \(L\) is \(L\,dk'/d\omega\). Strong frequency-selective loss can reshape the peak so much that tracking it no longer measures the arrival of new information.

Link the two parts of susceptibility through causality

A causal linear material obeys \(P(t)=\epsilon_0\int_0^\infty h(\tau)E(t-\tau)\,d\tau\). Its susceptibility for our convention is \(\int_0^\infty h(\tau)e^{i\omega\tau}d\tau\). For a decaying response this expression is analytic when \({\rm Im}\,\omega>0\). Closing a contour in that half plane relates real and imaginary components by principal-value integrals. For a response decaying at large frequency one useful form is

\[\chi'(\omega)=\frac2\pi\,{\cal P}\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega'\chi''(\omega')}{\omega'^2-\omega^2}\,d\omega'.\]

Thus a narrow absorption band contributes to index variation at frequencies where the local absorption is small. A measurement over a short spectral window cannot uniquely evaluate the integral without information about loss outside it. The damped-oscillator poles lie below the real axis, as the causal convention requires.

Change the damping of a bound charge

Track the in-phase and out-of-phase parts of one oscillator response. With strength 0.50 and damping 0.20 in the plotted units, the absorptive part at resonance is 2.50. Compare that point with the frequency of maximum absorption as damping increases.

Use attenuation and loss as independent checks

Infer an extinction coefficient

After correcting for both surfaces, a liquid transmits 25% of its incident intensity in 0.50 mm at vacuum wavelength 1.00 µm. Find \(\kappa\) and the field-amplitude ratio.

Work through the solution

With \(T=\exp(-4\pi\kappa L/\lambda_0)\), \(\kappa=-\lambda_0\ln(0.25)/(4\pi L)=2.21\times10^{-4}\). The amplitude ratio is \(\sqrt T=0.50\). Using the amplitude decay law on intensity would overestimate \(\kappa\) by a factor of two.

Recover the conductivity limit

Set \(\omega_0=0\) in the charge model and express the response as \(J=\sigma E\). Find the low-frequency real conductivity.

Work through the solution

Using \(J=-i\omega P\) yields \(\sigma(\omega)=\epsilon_0\omega_p^2/(\gamma-i\omega)\). At \(\omega\ll\gamma\), \(\sigma\simeq\epsilon_0\omega_p^2/\gamma=Nq^2/(m\gamma)\). The absence of a restoring force lets charges drift steadily instead of settling to a finite static polarisation.
