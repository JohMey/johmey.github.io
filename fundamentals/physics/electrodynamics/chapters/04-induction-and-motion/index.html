Induction in moving circuits

From first principles

Follow a charge around a moving loop

A straight conducting bar of length \(\ell\) slides right at speed \(v\) on two fixed rails. Close the circuit at the left and apply a uniform magnetic field \(B\) out of the page. Orient the loop counterclockwise as seen by the reader. In a moving conductor the Lorentz force per unit charge contains both an electric field and a motional term. Its circulation defines the signed electromotive force:

\[\mathcal E=\oint_{C(t)}(\mathbf E+\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell .\]

In a static applied field the electric-field circulation vanishes. Only the right bar moves, and its motional force points down although positive circulation on the right edge points up. Its contribution is \(-B\ell v\). Independently, a bar at distance \(x\) encloses flux \(B\ell x\), so

\[\mathcal E=-B\ell v=-\frac{d}{dt}(B\ell x).\]

If instead the field varies while the bar moves, the fixed-path Faraday term adds \(-\ell x\dot B\). The two mechanisms combine by the product rule:

\[\mathcal E=-\ell x\dot B-B\ell\dot x=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}.\]

For a resistive circuit with negligible self-inductance, \(I=\mathcal E/R\). The negative sign predicts clockwise current during expansion and an opposing magnetic force on the bar. The mechanical work required to keep it moving supplies the resistive heating.

Derivation and applications

Close the mechanical power balance of a rotating coil

A rotating loop drives current through an inductive circuit even while the magnetic flux through the loop is reversing. Consider \(N\) tightly wound turns of area \(A\) rotating at imposed angular speed \(\omega\) in a uniform static field \(B\). Take the angle between the loop normal and the field to be \(\theta=\omega t\). Use a constant total circuit resistance \(R\) and self-inductance \(L\). Neglect brushes, friction and radiation. The external flux linkage and its time derivative give the motional source voltage

\[\Lambda_{\rm ext}=NBA\cos\theta,\]
\[e=-\frac{d\Lambda_{\rm ext}}{dt}=NBA\omega\sin\theta.\]

Self-induction supplies a second voltage that depends on the rate of change of current. After the initial transient dies away, the loop equation has a sinusoidal solution with peak current \(I_0\) and lag \(\phi\):

\[L\dot i+Ri=e,\]
\[I_0=\frac{NBA\omega}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L)^2}},\]
\[\tan\phi=\frac{\omega L}{R}.\]

The field torque along the rotation coordinate is \(i\,\partial\Lambda_{\rm ext}/\partial\theta\). An external motor balances that torque, so its instantaneous power equals \(e i\). Multiplying the loop equation by current now separates irreversible heating from reversible storage.

\[P_{\rm motor}=ei=Ri^2+\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12Li^2\right),\]
\[\langle P_{\rm motor}\rangle=\frac12 R I_0^2.\]

The storage term integrates to zero over a complete cycle, though for part of the cycle a falling current can return energy. The mean opposing torque magnitude follows by dividing mean mechanical power by the imposed angular speed.

Worked example

Measure the mean shaft torque

A teaching generator uses \(N=100\), \(A=20.0\) cm², \(B=0.200\) T and \(\omega=100\) rad/s. It produces 4.00 V peak. For \(R=3.00\) Ω and \(L=40.0\) mH, the reactance is 4.00 Ω and the impedance magnitude is 5.00 Ω. The 0.800 A peak current dissipates 0.960 W on average. The mean shaft torque required by the electrical load is therefore \(0.960/100=9.60\) mN m.

Second application

Read an inductance from the timing of a current maximum

In a separate apparatus the current maximum follows the voltage maximum by 2.50 ms at 50.0 Hz. The lag is \(2\pi(50.0)(0.00250)=\pi/4\), so \(\omega L=R\). With measured total resistance 12.0 Ω this gives \(L=12.0/(2\pi\,50.0)=38.2\) mH. A 6.00 V rms source would then deliver \(6.00^2/(2\times12.0)=1.50\) W of mean heating. Using only the resistance to infer current would double this power estimate.

Follow a charge around a circuit

A conducting rod slides on two rails in a static magnetic field. Although a flux meter sees the enclosed flux changing, a laboratory observer finds no time-varying applied magnetic field. The measured voltage comes from the force on charges carried with the moving rod. In a stationary loop beside an AC coil the situation is different: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field with nonzero circulation. A consistent treatment includes both mechanisms.

Choose a positive direction around a thin material circuit. The associated surface normal follows the right-hand rule. If a segment moves at velocity \(\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\), the electromotive force is the integral of \((\mathbf E+\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) around it. In the quasistatic circuit description, the drift motion along a thin wire adds no tangential magnetic force. The integral has units of work per charge. Its sign fixes the direction of the current for a passive resistance.

Recover the same fields from different potentials

Zero magnetic divergence permits \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\) in a contractible region, such as the interior of a ball. Combining this with Faraday's law yields \(\nabla\times(\mathbf E+\partial_t\mathbf A)=0\) and hence \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\). The scalar potential alone cannot represent a nonzero electric circulation around a stationary loop. Still, individual values of the potentials are not unique. For any smooth single-valued \(\chi\), take \(\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi\) and \(\phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi\). The curl of a gradient vanishes, and the two new derivative terms in \(\mathbf E'\) cancel, so both fields and the Lorentz force stay fixed.

Inside a long ideal solenoid assume \(\mathbf B=B(t)\mathbf e_z\) and axial symmetry. Choose \(\phi=0\) and \(\mathbf A=B(-y,x,0)/2\). The resulting azimuthal electric field has magnitude \(r|\dot B|/2\). Another allowed choice uses \(\chi=Bxy/2\), which gives \(\mathbf A'=(0,Bx,0)\) and \(\phi'=-\dot Bxy/2\). Computing only \(-\partial_t\mathbf A'\) would misleadingly give a field pointing along the \(y\) axis everywhere. Including the scalar gradient restores \((\dot By,-\dot Bx,0)/2\).

At \(x=0.0400\) m, \(y=0.0300\) m and \(\dot B=0.500\) T/s both calculations give \((0.00750,-0.0100,0)\) V/m. A circle of radius 50.0 mm encloses a flux growing at \(3.93\) mWb/s and has circulation \(-3.93\) mV in the positive sense around \(z\). The measured loop emf is gauge independent. A voltage measured along a specified open path in a changing magnetic field also depends on the routing of its leads.

Differentiate the linked flux

Let the loop boundary move during a short interval. Its magnetic flux changes both because \(\partial_t\mathbf B\) changes across the original surface and because the perimeter sweeps a thin ribbon. Faraday’s field equation relates the first contribution to \(-\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\). The oriented ribbon gives the second contribution \(-\oint(\mathbf v_{\rm wire}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\). Adding them gives \(\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt\). The flux rule holds here because the moving contour is the circuit being followed.

Take a rectangular loop in the horizontal plane with uniform field \(B\) upwards. Its right side is a rod of length \(\ell\), moving outwards at speed \(v\) so the upward flux increases. Positive circulation seen from above is counterclockwise. The induced electromotive force is therefore negative and the current is clockwise. Equivalently, on the moving rod \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\) points down the page. This local force gives the same sign as the global flux law.

Close the energy balance

For negligible self-inductance and total resistance \(R\), the magnitude is \(I=B\ell v/R\). A current-carrying rod experiences force \(\mathbf F=I\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B\), opposite to its velocity. Constant-speed operation needs a pulling force \(B\ell I\). Multiplying by speed gives \(P_{\rm mech}=B\ell Iv=I^2R\). The magnetic interaction redirects the transfer. The external pull supplies the energy dissipated as heat.

If the wire has appreciable self-inductance \(L\), its own flux changes when current changes. With constant applied speed the magnitude equation becomes \(L\,dI/dt+RI=B\ell v\) after the initial step in velocity. The current builds with time constant \(L/R\). At each instant the mechanical power balances Joule heating plus \(d(LI^2/2)/dt\). Predicting an immediate full drag force at the moment of starting would neglect the energy required to establish current.

Compare two measurements

Let \(B=0.40\) T, \(\ell=0.25\) m, \(v=3.0\) m/s and \(R=0.60\) Ω. The open-circuit emf magnitude is 0.300 V. Closing the circuit gives steady current 0.500 A and drag force 0.0500 N. Mechanical power and electrical heating both equal 0.150 W. A voltmeter with large internal resistance measures nearly the open-circuit value but draws little current, so it produces little braking. The induced voltage alone does not establish how much energy is transferred.

With \(L=0.12\) H the time constant is 0.20 s. At that time after closing, \(I=0.500(1-e^{-1})=0.316\) A. The mechanical power is 0.0948 W, of which 0.0599 W heats the resistor. The remaining 0.0349 W increases magnetic field energy. If the pulling force is removed entirely, speed can no longer be prescribed; the mechanical inertia must enter an additional equation. Under those conditions a single exponential computed at constant speed describes only part of the response.

A simple reversal checks all sign conventions in the experiment. Reverse the external magnetic field while keeping the prescribed velocity fixed. The induced voltage changes sign, and so does the steady current in a passive resistor. Their product in the electrical power is unchanged. The mechanical force depends on the product of current and magnetic field and therefore still opposes motion. The same circuit can operate as a motor if an independent supply forces current against its generator direction. The supply then contributes to the energy balance as a separate external port.

A changing flux can slow a moving magnet

Consider a small conducting loop of resistance \(R\) and inductance \(L\) fixed beside the path of an external magnet. Let \(x\) be the magnet's displacement and \(\Phi(x)\) its flux through the loop in a specified positive direction. Its motion supplies an electromotive force \(-\Phi'(x)\dot x\). Over a small region approximate the gradient by the constant \(K=\Phi'(x)\) and take the loop's self-inductance to be independent of position. The current equation is \(L\dot I+RI=-K\dot x\). It follows that a static magnet produces no sustained current in a resistive loop, even if its absolute magnetic flux is large.

Multiply the equation by \(I\). Inductive energy changes at rate \(d(LI^2/2)/dt\), and heat appears at rate \(RI^2\). Together these equal \(-KI\dot x\). Mechanical power supplied by the loop to the magnet is therefore \(KI\dot x\), giving electromagnetic force \(F=KI\). At sufficiently low frequency the inductive term is negligible and \(F\simeq-(K^2/R)\dot x\). A magnetic interaction has become an effective viscous damper through the combined action of induction and resistance.

For harmonic velocity with complex amplitude \(u\), the force amplitude is \(-K^2u/(R+i\omega L)\). Its component opposing velocity has effective damping \(c_{\rm em}=K^2R/(R^2+\omega^2L^2)\). The imaginary component returns stored energy during part of each cycle. Equating cycle-mean mechanical loss to mean resistor heating gives another way to verify which component really dissipates.

Compare motion below and above the electrical corner

Near a chosen working point let \(K=0.200\) Wb/m, \(R=2.00\) Ω, \(L=20.0\) mH and peak speed \(0.100\) m/s. The corner frequency in angular units is \(R/L=100\) rad/s. At \(\omega=10.0\) rad/s the induced emf amplitude is \(0.0200\) V and current amplitude is \(0.0200/\sqrt{2.00^2+0.200^2}=9.95\) mA. The average heat is \(RI_{\rm pk}^2/2=99.0\) μW. The low-frequency approximation gives \(K^2/R=0.0200\) N s/m and predicts \(100\) μW, sufficiently close here.

At \(200\) rad/s the same velocity amplitude gives current \(4.47\) mA and heat \(20.0\) μW. The smaller loss despite unchanged peak speed comes from a greater fraction of the emf supporting inductive voltage. At fixed nonzero frequency the limit \(R\to0\) eliminates dissipation while retaining induced current. The apparent divergence of the low-frequency approximation as resistance tends to zero is resolved because its assumption \(\omega L\ll R\) then fails. Measuring both force phase and heating provides independent tests of this simple localized flux model.

The approximation of constant flux gradient can itself be checked experimentally. Translate the magnet slowly through several known positions while measuring open-circuit emf and speed. Dividing the signed emf by the signed speed estimates the local gradient, without needing an absolute calibration of the magnet’s total flux. If the gradient changes sign across the motion, the induced current can have a distorted waveform even under perfectly sinusoidal displacement. The small-amplitude force response derived here should then be measured about a fixed mean position, or the full position-dependent gradient should be retained in the coupled electrical and mechanical equations.
