From first principles

How a dielectric surface determines its internal field

Put a uniform, isotropic dielectric sphere of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon\) in a uniform static field \(E_0\hat{\mathbf z}\) in vacuum. No free charge is supplied to the interface. Gauss’s law for the electric displacement therefore requires continuous normal \(D\), while electrostatic circulation requires continuous tangential \(E\). Write the potentials using the axial angular dependence \(\cos\theta\):

\[V_{\rm in}=-Ar\cos\theta,\]
\[V_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2.\]

These are harmonic functions. The interior expression is regular at the centre and the exterior correction decays at infinity. Continuity of potential at \(r=a\) gives

\[A=E_0-B/a^3.\]

Obtain the radial fields by differentiating both potentials with respect to radius and reversing their signs. Normal displacement continuity then gives a second independent equation:

\[\epsilon A=\epsilon_0(E_0+2B/a^3),\qquad (\epsilon+2\epsilon_0)A=3\epsilon_0E_0.\]

The interior field is uniform and has magnitude \(A\). The exterior correction is that of a dipole of moment \(4\pi\epsilon_0 B\) along the applied field. In a material indistinguishable from vacuum, \(A=E_0\) and the dipole vanishes. This limit checks both the radial derivative’s factor of two and the sign of the induced dipole.

Derivation and applications

Find the field inside a spherical cavity in a dielectric

A hollow pore samples the field inside a bulk insulator. A sphere makes the difference between the pore field and the surrounding macroscopic field analytically accessible. Let the host have static permittivity \(\epsilon_h\), the pore filling have permittivity \(\epsilon_c\), and their spherical interface have radius \(a\). Both media are linear, isotropic and homogeneous. There is no free interfacial charge. Far inside the host an applied uniform field \(E_\infty\) points along \(z\). Choose potentials with the same angular dependence as this forcing.

\[V_c=-Cr\cos\theta,\]
\[V_h=-E_\infty r\cos\theta+\frac{D}{r^2}\cos\theta .\]

The regular interior solution gives a uniform pore field of magnitude \(C\). The exterior term proportional to \(D\) is the decaying dipolar disturbance. Matching potential at \(r=a\) ensures the tangential field is continuous. Matching normal displacement uses \(E_r=-\partial_r V\) and introduces a factor of two in the exterior term.

\[C=E_\infty-\frac{D}{a^3},\]
\[\epsilon_c C=\epsilon_h\left(E_\infty+\frac{2D}{a^3}\right).\]

Eliminate the exterior coefficient with the first equation. The result depends on both permittivities even when the pore itself is empty. The radius controls the dipole term through its volume, while the uniform cavity field depends only on the contrast.

\[C=\frac{3\epsilon_h}{\epsilon_c+2\epsilon_h}E_\infty,\]
\[D=a^3\frac{\epsilon_c-\epsilon_h}{\epsilon_c+2\epsilon_h}E_\infty .\]

Equal permittivities recover a perfectly uniform field everywhere. A vacuum cavity in a host of very large positive permittivity has \(C/E_\infty\) approaching \(3/2\), with the far field inside the host held fixed.

Infer a pore filling from the measured field

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

In a separate host of relative permittivity 3.00, a calibrated probe finds a uniform cavity field equal to 0.750 of the host's distant field. Inverting the ratio gives

\[\epsilon_c/\epsilon_0=3(3.00)/0.750-2(3.00)=6.00.\]

The filling is more polarisable than the host. As a check, the exterior coefficient is

\[D/(a^3E_\infty)=(6-3)/(6+6)=0.250,\]

which yields the observed 0.750 field ratio by the potential condition alone.

Why a leaky sphere can reverse its drift in an alternating field

A neutral polarised particle can move toward a stronger electric field. If charge can also migrate through the particle and its surroundings, changing the frequency alone can reverse the leading gradient force. Calculate when the reversal exists.

Follow the boundary current through the interface

Take a sphere of radius \(a\) in a homogeneous medium. At frequencies for which its diameter is tiny compared with the wavelength and external field-variation length, the field near it can be treated quasistatically. Adopt time dependence \(e^{i\omega t}\). Continuity of total normal current involves \((\sigma+i\omega\epsilon)E_n=i\omega\epsilon^*E_n\) with \(\epsilon^*=\epsilon-i\sigma/\omega\). Matching the scalar potential and this boundary flux is algebraically the same sphere problem as in electrostatics, with the appropriate complex material ratios.

The dipole contrast \(K=(\epsilon_p^*-\epsilon_m^*)/(\epsilon_p^*+2\epsilon_m^*)\) has low-frequency limit \(K_0=(\sigma_p-\sigma_m)/(\sigma_p+2\sigma_m)\) and high-frequency limit \(K_\infty=(\epsilon_p-\epsilon_m)/(\epsilon_p+2\epsilon_m)\). Defining \(\tau=(\epsilon_p+2\epsilon_m)/(\sigma_p+2\sigma_m)\), multiplying numerator and denominator by the complex conjugate of the latter gives

\[\operatorname{Re}K=\frac{K_0+K_\infty(\omega\tau)^2}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.\]

The time-averaged gradient force in the ideal standing or locally in-phase field is \(2\pi\epsilon_m a^3\operatorname{Re}K\,\nabla E_{\rm rms}^2\), with \(\epsilon_m\) the real permittivity of the host. This prefactor uses rms electric field. A particle with positive real contrast is drawn towards increasing rms intensity. A crossover occurs only when the two limiting contrasts have opposite signs, in which case \(\omega_c=\sqrt{-K_0/K_\infty}/\tau\). If both contrasts vanish the particle is electrically matched in this model at all frequencies and no unique crossover can be inferred.

A coated inclusion can have zero external dipole and a finite internal field

A spherical particle of radius \(a\) and relative permittivity \(\epsilon_c\) is surrounded by a concentric shell extending to \(b\), with relative permittivity \(\epsilon_s\). The host has relative permittivity \(\epsilon_h\) and supports an imposed uniform electrostatic field \(E_0\hat{\mathbf z}\) far from the particle. All three materials are homogeneous, linear and isotropic, with positive permittivities and no free interfacial charge. Can one choose the amount of coating so that a distant probe sees exactly the undisturbed field, even though the core still changes the internal field? The answer requires matching fields at two interfaces under the same applied excitation.

Choose radial functions before assigning interface conditions

With axial symmetry and a uniform applied field, only the \(\ell=1\) sector is driven. Spherical boundaries preserve this angular dependence. The two radial solutions of Laplace's equation are \(r\) and \(r^{-2}\). Reject the divergent term in the solid core, keep both in the shell, and allow a decaying dipole in the host. Write

\[V_c=-Ar\cos\theta,\]
\[V_s=(-Br+C/r^2)\cos\theta,\]
\[V_h=(-E_0r+D/r^2)\cos\theta .\]

Here \(D\) is a potential coefficient, distinct from electric displacement \(\mathbf D\). The latter enters through continuity of its normal component \(\epsilon E_r\), while continuity of potential fixes the common tangential electric field at each radius. At \(r=a\) these two equations reduce to

\[A=B-C/a^3,\]
\[\epsilon_c A=\epsilon_s(B+2C/a^3).\]

Define \(m=(\epsilon_c-\epsilon_s)/(\epsilon_c+2\epsilon_s)\). Eliminating \(A\) gives

\[C=a^3mB,\]
\[A=(1-m)B.\]

This first interface problem determines how the core changes the mixture of regular and singular solutions in the shell, even before we choose a host.

Condense the inner sphere and shell into a surface response

Let \(f=(a/b)^3\) be the core volume fraction. At the outer surface the shell potential divided by \(-b\cos\theta\) is \(B(1-fm)\), and its normal displacement divided by \(\epsilon_0\cos\theta\) is \(\epsilon_sB(1+2fm)\). Their ratio defines the relative permittivity of a homogeneous sphere having the same boundary response to this excitation:

\[\epsilon_{\rm eq}=\epsilon_s\frac{1+2fm}{1-fm},\]
\[\frac{D}{b^3E_0}=\frac{\epsilon_{\rm eq}-\epsilon_h}{\epsilon_{\rm eq}+2\epsilon_h}.\]

The second relation follows by matching host potential and normal displacement at \(b\) just as for a single homogeneous sphere. It also checks limiting cases. At \(f=0\) the equivalent object has shell permittivity; at \(f=1\) substitution reduces it to \(\epsilon_c\). If the core and shell are identical, \(m=0\) and changing their nominal boundary does nothing.

To eliminate the exterior dipole choose \(\epsilon_{\rm eq}=\epsilon_h\), giving \(f=(\epsilon_h-\epsilon_s)/[m(\epsilon_h+2\epsilon_s)]\). A physical value requires

\[0\leq f\leq1;\]

for positive distinct permittivities the host must lie between the shell and core responses. An arbitrary mismatched shell cannot always cancel the polarisation of its core. The condition concerns a uniform applied field in electrostatics. A nearby point source drives multiple angular orders with different radial responses.

Read all three fields in a neutral inclusion

Choose \(\epsilon_c=8,\epsilon_s=2,\epsilon_h=3\). Then \(m=1/2\), \(f=2/7\), and \(a/b=0.6586\). Under the matched condition \(D=0\), so continuity at \(b\) demands \(B(1-fm)=E_0\), giving \(B=7E_0/6\) and \(A=7E_0/12\). The core still sees a uniform field only 58.3% as large as the host's undisturbed field. At \(b\) the shell-side radial electric field at the north pole is

\[B(1+2fm)=1.50E_0.\]

Multiplying by shell permittivity 2 recovers \(3E_0\), exactly the required host displacement. At the equator the tangential field agrees directly. These surface tests can reveal a wrong sign in \(C\) even if an exterior dipole fit accidentally vanishes.

For \(b=30\) μm in \(E_0=3000\) V/m, \(a=19.8\) μm and the voltage difference across the core diameter has magnitude \(2aA=69.2\) mV. The host would give \(2aE_0=119\) mV over the same distance. A far-field perturbation measurement alone cannot establish a particle's internal electric exposure. A small capacitive probe inserted into the core would perturb the solution, so a practical comparison must estimate that additional boundary disturbance.

Check the coating with a different spatial pattern

To test how specific the cancellation is, impose an axisymmetric \(\ell=2\) potential on a distant enclosing electrode. More generally the radial functions are \(r^\ell\) and \(r^{-\ell-1}\) for each angular order. Repeating the inner matching gives \(m_\ell=\ell(\epsilon_c-\epsilon_s)/[\ell\epsilon_c+(\ell+1)\epsilon_s]\) in place of \(m\). The outer surface now weighs the decaying term by \(f_\ell=(a/b)^{2\ell+1}\). The equivalent boundary permittivity for this order is \(\epsilon_s[1+(\ell+1)f_\ell m_\ell/\ell]/[1-f_\ell m_\ell]\). Setting \(\ell=1\) reproduces the expression derived directly above.

For the neutral-dipole geometry at \(\ell=2\), \(m_2=12/22\) and \(f_2=(2/7)^{5/3}\). These give an equivalent response near 2.36, clearly distinct from the host value of 3. A field-gradient experiment can thus reveal the inclusion even when the uniform-field exterior measurement gives an exact cancellation. This is a direct test of the assumption that the imposed field varies slowly on the scale of the coated sphere.

Quantify tolerance and distinguish neutrality from zero polarisation

Keep the outer radius fixed but let the manufactured core radius be 1% larger than the neutral value. Predict the sign and approximate size of the exterior dipole coefficient \(D/(b^3E_0)\). Why does summing all bound surface charges give a weaker check?

Derive the result and check it independently

A small fractional radius error gives \(\delta f\simeq3f(0.01)=0.00857\). Differentiate the equivalent permittivity:

\[d\epsilon_{\rm eq}/df=3\epsilon_s m/(1-fm)^2,\]

which here is

\[49/12=4.083.\]

Thus \(\delta\epsilon_{\rm eq}\simeq0.0350\). Near the matched point the dipole coefficient varies as \(\delta\epsilon_{\rm eq}/(3\epsilon_h)\), giving \(+0.00389\). The slightly increased high-permittivity core gives a weak dipole along the applied field. Inserting the actual perturbed radius in the full rational formula checks the linearisation.

Bound charge integrated over each complete spherical interface vanishes because its angular pattern is proportional to \(\cos\theta\). This is true for both matched and mismatched coatings. It guarantees absence of a monopole but says nothing about the dipole. A numerical verification should check continuity of radial displacement, tangential field and the exterior dipole coefficient separately; one integrated neutral charge reading cannot replace those tests.

An inclusion in a uniform field

Place a homogeneous dielectric sphere of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon\) in vacuum. Distant electrodes produce a static uniform field \(E_0\mathbf e_z\) before insertion. Take the dielectric to be linear, isotropic and free of mobile charge. Bound charge can develop at its surface even though the object is electrically neutral. The monopole measurement from the previous lecture consequently misses the material response. The dipole moment provides a measurable alternative.

In each constant-permittivity region Gauss’s law for \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) give Laplace’s equation. The background potential is \(-E_0r\cos\theta\), an angular pattern of degree one. A spherical interface does not couple different angular degrees. The allowed radial functions for this degree are \(r\) and \(r^{-2}\). Finiteness at the centre prohibits a singular interior term. Matching the imposed field far away fixes the growing exterior term.

\[V_{\rm in}=-Ar\cos\theta,\]
\[V_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2.\]

Solve at the surface

Continuity of voltage gives

\[A=E_0-B/a^3.\]

It also guarantees continuity of tangential field, as a tangential displacement compares points along the same surface potential. Normal displacement is continuous in the absence of free surface charge. Differentiating radially changes the sign and supplies a factor of two for the decaying term, so \(\epsilon A=\epsilon_0(E_0+2B/a^3)\). Elimination gives

\[A=\frac{3\epsilon_0}{\epsilon+2\epsilon_0}E_0,\]
\[B=a^3\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_0}{\epsilon+2\epsilon_0}E_0.\]

The uniform internal field polarizes the sphere along the field direction. Its bound surface charge is \(\sigma_b=(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)A\cos\theta\). Opposite hemispheres cancel in total charge. Their separation gives an induced dipole \(p=4\pi\epsilon_0B\). Equivalently integrate the uniform polarization over the volume. Both constructions give the same dipole, one from exterior voltage and one from microscopic charge displacement. When \(\epsilon=\epsilon_0\) the exterior perturbation vanishes. A very large positive permittivity suppresses the internal field and approaches the conductor result.

Predict a voltage difference

Take \(a=2.00\) mm, \(\epsilon=4\epsilon_0\) and \(E_0=30.0\) kV/m. The internal field is \(15.0\) kV/m, and \(B=1.20\times10^{-4}\) V m². Multiplication by \(4\pi\epsilon_0\) gives \(p=1.34\times10^{-14}\) C m. At an axial probe 10.0 mm from the centre the sphere increases voltage relative to the uniform-field baseline by \(B/r^2=1.20\) V. The opposite axial probe sees a shift of \(-1.20\) V. A differential measurement doubles the signal and removes a common voltage offset.

For the assumed uniform applied field the exterior dipole solution is exact at every point outside the sphere. A nearby field-producing electrode can spoil that simplification. A field that varies across the particle contains higher angular orders in its local expansion and their induced responses survive at the surface. In an experiment, repeating the measurement with a smaller sphere tests the predicted \(a^3\) law while reducing sensitivity to field curvature.

The assumption of real, constant permittivity is experimentally testable. A slowly varying applied field should give a dipole that follows the field with negligible lag. If the measured signal shifts in phase while frequency is raised, the response must be described by a frequency-dependent complex polarization. A static voltage estimate alone cannot supply that extra timescale.

Build an effective permittivity from many inclusions

A single dielectric sphere in a smooth applied field develops a dipole. A dilute suspension of such spheres can therefore alter the bulk capacitance of a sample. Suppose identical spheres of radius \(a\) and permittivity \(\epsilon_i\) occupy a small fraction \(f\) of a matrix with permittivity \(\epsilon_m\). The matrix is homogeneous, both materials are isotropic, and no conducting path or interfacial free charge is present. Each sphere sees nearly the matrix field when it is far from all neighbours. The single-sphere boundary calculation gives an excess dipole \(p=4\pi\epsilon_m a^3 b E_m\), with \(b=(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_m)/(\epsilon_i+2\epsilon_m)\).

The number of inclusions per unit sample volume is \(n=f/(4\pi a^3/3)\). At first order the excess polarization density is

\[np=3f\epsilon_m b E_m.\]

The difference between \(E_m\) and the sample-average field is itself first order in \(f\), so replacing one by the other in this correction changes only higher-order terms. Comparing average displacement with \(\epsilon_{\rm eff}E\) gives

\[\epsilon_{\rm eff}=\epsilon_m[1+3fb+O(f^2)] .\]

This is a dilute effective-medium prediction. The absolute radius cancels at fixed volume fraction because each dipole scales as \(a^3\) while number density scales as \(a^{-3}\). The cancellation requires particles small compared with the scale of the imposed field. Aggregation, closely spaced pairs and a large occupied fraction can change local fields and produce a nonlinear dependence on concentration.

Measure a composite using two capacitor readings

A parallel-plate measurement cell has electrode area \(25.0\) cm² and gap 1.00 mm. Filling it with matrix alone gives \(\epsilon_m=2.00\epsilon_0\), hence \(C_m=44.3\) pF after subtraction of stray capacitance. Disperse spheres with \(\epsilon_i=8.00\epsilon_0\) at \(f=0.0200\). The contrast factor is

\[(8-2)/(8+4)=0.500.\]

Consequently

\[\epsilon_{\rm eff}/\epsilon_m\simeq1+3(0.0200)(0.500)=1.030.\]

The expected reading is \(45.6\) pF, an increase of approximately \(1.33\) pF.

A second sample with the same volume fraction but twice the sphere radius should give the same leading result. A systematic change might indicate settling, aggregation, finite-gap effects or frequency-dependent interface polarization. In the equal-permittivity limit the contrast factor vanishes and no capacitive signal should remain. In the high-permittivity limit \(b\) approaches one, so the fractional increase saturates at approximately \(3f\) to first order. This bounded prediction gives a useful check when a simple arithmetic mixing rule appears to predict an arbitrarily large capacitance increase from an arbitrarily dilute concentration.

The hypothesis that the suspension is dilute can be tested without a microscopic field probe. Make a concentration series by adding material with known volume at fixed particle size. The predicted initial slope of relative capacitance versus volume fraction for the numerical pair is \(3b=1.50\). A linear fit restricted to the lowest concentrations estimates the contrast parameter and hence the inclusion permittivity if the matrix is known. Strong curvature at higher concentration then measures the breakdown of the independent-inclusion approximation. Extrapolating that curvature back as a new single-particle dielectric constant would blend collective and material effects.
