Radiation reaction and the energy stored near a charge

A charge can radiate at an instant when a proposed frictional force would do no work. To account for the energy one needs three measurements: mechanical work on the charge, power leaving in radiation and a reversible change in its nearby field.

A moving oscillator tests a friction hypothesis

For a point charge moving nonrelativistically in SI units define \(\tau=q^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0mc^3)\). The Larmor formula gives

\[P_{\rm rad}=m\tau a^2.\]

Suppose a charge reaches a turning point of a slow sinusoidal oscillation. Its velocity there is zero and its acceleration is maximal. Radiation carries positive power away, yet any finite force on the particle does zero mechanical work at that instant. Equating instantaneous mechanical loss to instantaneous radiated power cannot account for the turning point.

Integrate over a smooth acceleration history. The product rule gives

\[d(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v)/dt=\dot{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf v+a^2.\]

Rearranging shows that a local force \(\mathbf F_{\rm self}=m\tau\dot{\mathbf a}\) can recover the Larmor loss between endpoints where \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v\) agrees:

\[\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F_{\rm self}\cdot\mathbf v\,dt=m\tau[\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v]_{t_1}^{t_2}-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}P_{\rm rad}\,dt .\]

This identity motivates the nonrelativistic Abraham–Lorentz term. An integrated energy argument only fixes its work under the chosen endpoint conditions. Deriving the full local self-force requires analysing the near field, regularising the point-source singularity and identifying the measured particle mass. These assumptions delimit the classical effective equation.

Follow the energy that can return to the particle

Let \(K=mv^2/2\), \(E_S=-m\tau\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v\). Write \(m\mathbf a=\mathbf F_{\rm ext}+\mathbf F_{\rm self}\), dot with \(\mathbf v\), and use the product rule once more. The result is

\[\mathbf F_{\rm ext}\cdot\mathbf v=\frac{d}{dt}(K+E_S)+P_{\rm rad}.\]

The acceleration-dependent quantity \(E_S\), called the Schott energy, represents a reversible bound-field term after regularisation. Its sign depends on motion; it is a changeable contribution relative to an energy reference. For constant acceleration over a short nonrelativistic interval, the self-force vanishes, but \(dE_S/dt=-m\tau a^2\). The decrease supplies the radiated energy. Switching the acceleration on and off modifies the budget near the endpoints. Extending a constant-acceleration segment to infinite time would break the assumed slow-speed model.

Why taking the third-order equation literally fails

Take a constant mass and one Cartesian direction. With no external force the equation becomes \(a-\tau\dot a=0\), whose general solution includes \(a=Ae^{t/\tau}\). Standard position and velocity data leave \(A\) undetermined. This runaway is incompatible with a passive free particle. The prescription \(a(t)\to0\) after an applied force has ended removes that branch, but integrating backward gives

\[a(t)=\frac1{m\tau}\int_t^\infty e^{-(s-t)/\tau}F_{\rm ext}(s)\,ds .\]

The integral samples future forces. A discontinuous idealised force can give apparent preacceleration over order \(\tau\). For an electron \(\tau\simeq6.27\times10^{-24}\) s. On such scales an elementary classical point-charge description is inadequate. For smooth forcing varying over \(T\gg\tau\), instead use the leading equation \(a=F_{\rm ext}/m+O(\tau/T)\) on the right. To first order in \(\tau/T\),

\[m a=F_{\rm ext}+\tau\,\frac{dF_{\rm ext}}{dt}.\]

For a force depending on position and velocity, evaluate its total derivative along the leading-order motion. This reduced-order equation selects the smooth, perturbative dynamics without adding independent initial acceleration data.

A spectral width and a phase-resolved budget

Radiative damping of a weakly bound oscillator

Let \(F_{\rm ext}=-m\omega_0^2x\). Then \(\ddot x+\tau\omega_0^2\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0\) to first order. The amplitude falls with e-folding time \(2/(\tau\omega_0^2)\). At \(\omega_0=3.0\times10^{15}\) s⁻¹ the formal electron model gives \(35.4\) ns, with \(\tau\omega_0=1.88\times10^{-8}\). The weak-damping approximation is internally consistent. Actual atomic linewidths additionally depend on quantum matrix elements and other decay channels. The calculation describes a classical bound oscillator with a chosen restoring force.

The same energy viewed at two phases

Impose \(v=v_a\sin\omega t\), so \(a=\omega v_a\cos\omega t\) and \(\dot a=-\omega^2v_a\sin\omega t\). In units \(P_0=m\tau\omega^2v_a^2\), radiation power is \(\cos^2\omega t\) while mechanical self-force power is \(-\sin^2\omega t\). At phase zero all the instantaneous radiation is supplied by decreasing bound-field energy. A quarter period later the radiation vanishes at the corresponding retarded emission phase, but the self-force still removes mechanical energy. Over one period the integrated bound-field term vanishes, so the mean mechanical loss is \(P_0/2\).

Close the energy accounts at a chosen emission phase

The radiated power, the self-force work on the particle and the rate of change of bound-field energy sum to zero at every emission phase. With zero mean speed and phase zero, their dimensionless values are +1, 0 and −1. Shift the mean speed and decide which of the three readings must stay the same.

Problem sheet · how local is radiative friction?

Calculate a complete pulse budget

Take \(a(t)=a_0\sin(\pi t/T)\) for \(0<t<T\), zero elsewhere, with initial velocity zero. Calculate final speed, total radiated energy and the ratio of radiation to final kinetic energy.

Work through the solution

The velocity gain is \(2a_0T/\pi\). Integrating \(a^2\) yields

\[E_{\rm rad}=m\tau a_0^2T/2.\]

The final kinetic energy is \(2ma_0^2T^2/\pi^2\), so the ratio is \(\pi^2\tau/(4T)\). Both endpoints have zero acceleration. The time-integrated external work must therefore equal the sum of the final kinetic energy and radiated energy. Choose \(a_0T\ll c\) and \(T\gg\tau\) for this calculation.

Diagnose a spurious trajectory

A computation with no force doubles its acceleration every few multiples of the electron radiation-reaction time. Would closer timesteps necessarily remove the instability?

Work through the solution

The exact homogeneous third-order equation already has \(a=Ae^{t/\tau}\). A smaller step can track that unphysical branch more accurately. A model restricted to smooth weak radiation reaction instead evolves the reduced second-order equation. In free motion this gives \(a=0\) identically. Compare physical timescales and equations before interpreting a converged numerical trajectory as an observable.

Radiated power, mechanical work and energy limits · Waves, radiation and relativity

A charge can radiate while its speed is constant. A source can exchange energy with its nearby field before a pulse reaches a receiver. We will use the flux through a distant sphere, the work at the charge and the energy stored in a local field as three operationally distinct measurements. Agreement requires the relevant integration times and the range of validity of the particle model.
