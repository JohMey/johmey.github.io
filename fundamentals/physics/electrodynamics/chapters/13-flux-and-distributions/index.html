What would a very small instrument measure?

Imagine six square flux meters on the faces of an empty cube of side \(h\). Each one measures the field component along its outward normal. A uniform field produces equal and opposite readings on opposite faces. A field whose \(x\)-component grows towards the right gives a positive difference between those two readings. To leading order their difference is \(h^2[E_x(x+h/2)-E_x(x-h/2)]\). Adding the three pairs and dividing by volume gives

\[\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1}{h^3}\oint_{\partial V_h}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\partial_x E_x+\partial_y E_y+\partial_z E_z.\]

The limit exists for a smooth field. Tiling a region with cubes then cancels all the interior-face readings. Only its outer surface remains. This explains why a local divergence controls a global flux even for an irregular boundary. In spherical symmetry a sphere of radius \(r\) makes the analogous measurement \(4\pi r^2 E_r\). Taking the difference between two nearby concentric spheres and dividing by the shell volume gives

\[\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=r^{-2}d(r^2E_r)/dr.\]

The factor \(r^2\) records the changing area of the meter.

A circulation meter is a small oriented loop. For a rectangle in the \(xy\)-plane, positive traversal about \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\) compares \(E_y\) on its two vertical edges and \(E_x\) on its horizontal edges. Division by area gives

\[(\nabla\times\mathbf E)_z=\partial_xE_y-\partial_yE_x.\]

Neighbouring loops cancel shared edges, giving the surface-to-boundary rule known as Stokes’ theorem. Reversing the loop normal reverses the circulation. Curl measures local circulation density, even at a point where the vector itself happens to vanish.

Recover the charge lost by a pointwise derivative

The field of a point charge is \(q\hat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). Its radial divergence formula gives zero at every \(r>0\), whereas a sphere of any positive radius has flux \(q/\epsilon_0\). To make sense of both observations, replace the point by a smooth model potential of width \(a>0\),

\[V_a(r)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{r^2+a^2}},\]
\[E_a(r)=\frac{qr}{4\pi\epsilon_0(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}.\]

Differentiate \(r^2 E_a\) before dividing by \(r^2\). The two derivative terms combine to leave a sharply concentrated charge density:

\[\rho_a(r)=\frac{3qa^2}{4\pi(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}},\]
\[Q_a(R)=q\frac{R^3}{(R^2+a^2)^{3/2}}.\]

For each fixed \(R>0\), \(Q_a(R)\to q\) as \(a\to0\). For each fixed \(r>0\), \(\rho_a(r)\to0\). A probe with a smooth spatial response \(f\) measures \(\int f\rho_a\,d^3r\), which tends to \(qf(0)\). The object defined by this last operation is the three-dimensional delta distribution. Poisson’s equation for Coulomb’s potential therefore reads \(\nabla^2(1/r)=-4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf r)\). The singular part supplies exactly the missing flux.

Worked measurement with two collecting spheres

For \(a=2.0\) mm, a spherical meter at \(R=a\) encloses \(q/2^{3/2}=0.354q\). At \(R=5a\) it encloses \(125q/26^{3/2}=0.943q\). A mesh representing the smooth source needs cells considerably narrower than \(a\) to reproduce its peak. An integrated flux can already be close to the point-charge answer well outside that core. Reporting a peak and reporting an enclosed charge are distinct resolution tests.

Treat an interface as a source with a measured sign

An infinite uniform charge sheet at \(z=0\) generates \(E_z=\sigma\,\operatorname{sgn}(z)/(2\epsilon_0)\) when reflection symmetry makes both sides equivalent. Integrating its derivative over a thin interval gives the jump \(\sigma/\epsilon_0\) irrespective of the interval's thickness. Thus \(\partial_z E_z=(\sigma/\epsilon_0)\delta(z)\). The surface density has units C m\(^{-2}\) and the one-dimensional delta contributes m\(^{-1}\), as a volume density requires.

A finite-volume calculation can use that integral directly. For a cell of area \(A\) straddling the sheet the face-flux difference is \(A(E_z^+-E_z^-)=A\sigma/\epsilon_0\). As the cell becomes thinner its density estimate grows, while its charge stays fixed. If fields of \(+600\) and \(-200\) V/m are recorded just above and below a sheet, its density is

\[\epsilon_0(800\ {\rm V/m})=7.08\ {\rm nC/m^2}.\]

Their mean, \(200\) V/m, is an independently applied background compatible with the same jump.

Ask what lies inside the spanning surface

Consider \(\mathbf F=C\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}/s\) outside a cylindrical excluded region, with \(s\) the distance from its axis. Its local curl vanishes. A circular traversal still has circulation \(2\pi C\). A disk spanning that circle cuts the excluded region, where the assumed smooth field is unavailable. Every sufficiently small contractible loop has zero circulation. A single-valued global scalar potential on the entire punctured region would require zero circulation on the large loop as well. This is why the topology of the domain is part of a potential formulation.

Measure the flux that a finite sphere captures

A point source would put all its charge inside every positive collector radius. For a 1 mm smooth core, a 2 mm collector encloses 71.6%. Increase the radius and infer which assumption an experimental flux measurement can resolve.

Recover a core width from one flux fraction

A calibrated spherical probe of radius \(R\) encloses \(q/2\) for the smooth source above. Determine \(a/R\) and predict the fraction enclosed at radius \(2R\).

Work through the solution

Raise the measured fraction to the power \(2/3\). It becomes \(R^2/(R^2+a^2)=2^{-2/3}\), so

\[a/R=\sqrt{2^{2/3}-1}=0.766.\]

The second meter collects \([1+(0.766/2)^2]^{-3/2}=0.814\) of the total, to three significant figures. Disagreement with both readings suggests an incorrect source shape or an off-centre probe.

Diagnose a circulation measurement

A loop encloses a very long changing flux tube. Outside it the magnetic field is negligible. Can the induced electric field have zero local curl at every point of the loop and a nonzero integral around it?

Work through the solution

Yes. Outside the tube \(-\partial_t\mathbf B=0\), and a local curl probe records zero. A spanning disk reaches inside the tube. Its integrated flux derivative gives

\[\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-d\Phi_B/dt.\]

Doubling the loop radius leaves this integral fixed for the same enclosed flux; cylindrical symmetry then halves the azimuthal field on the larger loop.
