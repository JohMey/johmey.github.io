From first principles

Count the charge within a spherical surface

A potential probe gives a number at a point. To predict that reading from charge density, begin with two physical inputs in vacuum. Gauss’s law relates the electric flux through a closed surface to its enclosed charge. In a static field the work per unit charge between two points is independent of path. For a spherically symmetric density \(\rho(r)\), contributions in tangential directions cancel. The radial field has the same magnitude everywhere on a sphere of radius \(r\). A shell of radius \(s\) and thickness \(ds\) contains charge \(4\pi s^2\rho(s)\,ds\). Hence

\[Q(r)=4\pi\int_0^r \rho(s)s^2\,ds,\]
\[4\pi r^2 E_r(r)=Q(r)/\epsilon_0 .\]

For a sphere of radius \(R\) carrying uniform density, the enclosed charge grows as \(r^3\) inside and stops growing outside. The surface through which the flux passes continues to grow as \(r^2\). Dividing gives two different field laws:

\[E_r(r)=\frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0}\ (r\le R),\]
\[E_r(r)=\frac{\rho R^3}{3\epsilon_0 r^2}\ (r\ge R).\]

Choose zero potential at infinity and integrate the work inward. For an interior probe split the path at the surface so that the appropriate field is used on each segment:

\[V(r)=\int_R^\infty E_{\rm out}(s)\,ds+\int_r^R E_{\rm in}(s)\,ds=\frac{\rho}{6\epsilon_0}(3R^2-r^2).\]

The centre has a finite voltage even though its field vanishes. The probe measures the accumulated work along a path, while the field is the local slope of that voltage.

Derivation and applications

How an interior voltage reveals a radial charge gradient

An outside probe cannot distinguish spherically symmetric charge distributions with the same total charge. To see what an inside probe adds, suppose an insulating sphere of radius \(R\) holds an immobile charge density \(\rho(r)=\rho_0(1-r/R)\) in vacuum. Ignore polarisation of the material and set potential zero at infinity. A spherical shell of thickness \(dr\) contributes its density times \(4\pi r^2dr\). The resulting enclosed charge is

\[q(r)=4\pi\rho_0\left(\frac{r^3}{3}-\frac{r^4}{4R}\right),\]
\[Q=q(R)=\frac{\pi\rho_0R^3}{3}.\]

Rotational symmetry fixes the direction of the field and makes its magnitude constant over the Gaussian sphere. Substituting the enclosed charge, then eliminating the density amplitude using the measured total charge, gives

\[E_r(r)=\frac{q(r)}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}=\frac{kQ}{R^2}(4x-3x^2),\]
\[x=\frac rR,\]
\[k=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}.\]

At the surface this expression joins the exterior Coulomb field. Positive charge concentrated towards the centre makes the field peak before reaching the surface. Potential instead accumulates the contributions of the field along the whole radial path. Starting from the known surface potential fixes the integration constant even when the voltmeter only accesses the interior.

\[V(r)=\frac{kQ}{R}+\int_r^R E_r(s)\,ds=\frac{kQ}{R}(2-2x^2+x^3).\]

Worked example

Identify an unknown charge profile

A specimen of radius 60.0 mm has a surface voltage of 30.0 V relative to a distant reference. The inferred charge is \(Q=VR/k=0.200\) nC. The gradient model predicts a centre voltage of \(2(30.0)=60.0\) V, so a differential probe between centre and surface should read 30.0 V. A uniformly charged sphere with that same outside voltage gives 45.0 V at the centre. A sufficiently small probe could distinguish these source profiles with a single additional reading.

Second application

Find where a field probe reads its maximum

For another sphere, \(R=90.0\) mm and \(Q=0.900\) nC. Differentiate \(4x-3x^2\) with respect to \(x\). Its derivative vanishes at \(x=2/3\), with negative second derivative. The peak is therefore 60.0 mm from the centre and has magnitude \((4/3)kQ/R^2=1.33\) kV/m. The surface value is only 0.999 kV/m. The enclosed charge still grows beyond the peak, but increasingly dilute outer shells provide too little additional flux to keep pace with the increasing area.

Problem sheet 6 · Can impedance identify a dielectric by itself?

Read the model setup and preparatory problems

Can an exterior field tell you where the charge sits?

A spherical cloud and a thin shell each carry charge \(Q\) and have the same outer radius \(R\). Before moving either slider, sketch the two fields along a radius. Which part of the sketch could distinguish the sources in a measurement?

A Gaussian sphere of radius \(r\) encloses \(Q(r/R)^3\) in the solid ball for \(r<R\). Dividing by its area gives \(E=Qr/(4\pi\epsilon_0R^3)\). The shell encloses zero until its surface. Outside, both enclose the same \(Q\). The shell curve shows the exterior value at the discontinuity.

Keep \(Q\) fixed and double \(R\). The maximum field falls by a factor of four, while the interior slope falls by a factor of eight. Explain why those are distinct measurements.

Why a charge in an ionic solution has a finite electrostatic range

Put a weakly charged small bead in a large bath of two mobile ion species carrying charges \(+e\) and \(-e\). Each species has equilibrium number density \(n_0\) far away. The solvent is a continuum with uniform permittivity \(\epsilon\), and a heat bath fixes temperature \(T\). In a vacuum the measured potential of a spherical charge falls as \(1/r\), even very far from it. In this liquid the electric field redistributes material, and the material changes the field. We can determine the decay law and the cloud that produces it using the same Gauss law. Fix the potential reference at infinity, where the liquid is electrically neutral.

Let thermal motion compete with electrical energy

An ion's local electrostatic energy is \(+eV\) or \(-eV\). In dilute thermal equilibrium its local density acquires a Boltzmann factor, \(n_\pm=n_0\exp[\mp eV/(k_BT)]\), because the far-away solution acts as a reservoir of particles. A positive bead repels positive ions and attracts negative ions. The mobile charge density is therefore \(\rho_m=e(n_+-n_-)=-2en_0\sinh[eV/(k_BT)]\). This is a source in Poisson's equation, not a new constitutive law for the solvent. We have separately included the solvent's polarisation in \(\epsilon\).

If \(|eV|\ll k_BT\), expand to first order. The symmetric mixture cancels even powers. This gives \(\rho_m=-(2e^2n_0/k_BT)V\), and an externally prescribed bead charge density \(\rho_b\) now satisfies

\[\nabla^2 V-\kappa^2 V=-\rho_b/\epsilon,\]
\[\kappa^2=\frac{2e^2n_0}{\epsilon k_BT}.\]

The inverse length \(\kappa\) came from comparing the field-curvature coefficient with the charge response. Increasing either mobile species' density shortens the distance over which a disturbance survives. Increasing temperature weakens the concentration change caused by a given voltage. Within these assumptions that length grows as \(\sqrt T\) at fixed permittivity and density.

Solve the exterior and test its near-source assumption

In the liquid outside a spherical bead of radius \(a\), put \(v=rV\). The radial Laplacian becomes \(v''/r\), so \(v''-\kappa^2v=0\). Discard the exponentially increasing solution at infinity. For a bead with fixed free charge \(q\) and no mobile ions inside, take a spherical Gaussian surface just outside the surface. Spherical symmetry and the boundary condition give \(-4\pi a^2\epsilon V'(a^+)=q\). Solving for the remaining coefficient yields

\[V(r)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon(1+\kappa a)}\frac{e^{-\kappa(r-a)}}r,\]
\[E_r(r)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon(1+\kappa a)}e^{-\kappa(r-a)}\left(\frac1{r^2}+\frac{\kappa}r\right),\]
\[r\ge a .\]

The maximum potential magnitude in the liquid occurs at the bead surface. Its value \(q/[4\pi\epsilon a(1+\kappa a)]\) therefore tests the linearisation everywhere outside. Shrinking a bead at fixed \(q\) ultimately violates this local condition even if the distant tail remains small. An ideal point-source expression can guide a far-field fit without faithfully describing the molecular neighbourhood of the source.

Account for the apparent disappearance of charge

A collector of radius \(r\) measures free enclosed charge \(4\pi\epsilon r^2E_r\), counting the bead and the mobile ions. It therefore encloses the fraction

\[\frac{Q_{\rm enc}(r)}q=\frac{1+\kappa r}{1+\kappa a}e^{-\kappa(r-a)} .\]

At the bead it reads one. At infinity it tends to zero. The missing charge is precisely the countercharge in the surrounding liquid. It can be verified independently without differentiating the field: integrate \(4\pi r^2\rho_m=-4\pi\epsilon\kappa^2r^2V\) from \(a\) to infinity. The radial integral of \(r e^{-\kappa(r-a)}\) is \(a/\kappa+1/\kappa^2\), cancelling the factor \(1+\kappa a\). The resulting total mobile charge is \(-q\). A screening length describes a decay scale; charge is not abruptly cancelled on one particular shell.

For \(\kappa a=1\), at \(r=a+\kappa^{-1}=2a\) the voltage has fallen to \(e^{-1}/2=18.4\%\) of its surface value. Yet the collector still encloses \(3/(2e)=55.2\%\) of the bead charge. Voltage and enclosed charge are different operations on the solution. A simulation or probe returning the voltage ratio cannot be read directly as the fraction of ions that have neutralised the bead.

Predict what changes when the ion valence changes

For several ionic species with charge \(z_i e\), the Boltzmann expansion gives \(\rho_m=\sum_i z_i e n_{i0}-(e^2/k_BT)\sum_i z_i^2 n_{i0}V+\cdots\). Bulk electroneutrality cancels the first sum. The screening coefficient instead contains squared valences, \(\kappa^2=e^2\sum_i z_i^2n_{i0}/(\epsilon k_BT)\). The sign of a species determines which way it moves in a given field, while its contribution to linear screening is positive for either sign. A divalent ion both feels double the electrical energy change and carries double the charge when it rearranges.

Compare two salts at the same formula-unit number density \(n\). A fully dissociated 1:1 salt contributes \(2n\) to the squared-valence sum. A 2:1 salt with one divalent cation and two singly charged anions contributes \(4n+2n=6n\). The predicted length is smaller by \(1/\sqrt3\), even though both baths are electrically neutral. The comparison assumes dilute ideal ions with the same solvent response. At higher concentrations, ion-size and correlation effects can remove this simple dependence.

Recover a screening length from a pair of readings

A bead has radius 8.0 nm. Relative to infinity two probes read 3.0 mV at 12 nm and 0.90 mV at 20 nm. Infer the screening length and surface voltage within the model. How could a third probe falsify the interpretation?

Derive the result and check it independently

The ratio of \(rV\), not \(V\) alone, isolates the exponential. Here \(r_2V_2/(r_1V_1)=18/36=1/2\), so \(\kappa^{-1}=8.0/\ln2=11.54\) nm. Extrapolating to \(a\) gives \(V(a)=V(12)(12/8)e^{4/11.54}=6.36\) mV. At room temperature \(k_BT/e\) is approximately 25.7 mV. The largest ratio is 0.247 and the cubic correction in \(\sinh x/x\) there is about 1%, making linearisation a plausible first approximation.

Plot \(\ln[rV(r)]\) against \(r\) for a third and further radii. A single straight line with slope \(-\kappa\) must describe all of them, without any external calibration of \(q\). Curvature or dependence on the chosen pair of radii would challenge the isolated spherical linear model. Before interpreting a fitted slope as an ionic strength, independently measure or specify \(\epsilon,T\), species charges and background electrostatic conditions.

Enclose the source before integrating the field

Imagine an insulating sphere charged uniformly throughout its volume. A probe outside it measures the field of a point charge, yet that representation fails at its centre. Our first objective is to see how a local charge distribution determines which measurements can distinguish the two sources. Use electrostatics in vacuum, with a sphere of radius \(R\) and total free charge \(Q\). A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium would distribute its excess charge differently.

Gauss’s law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is the enclosed charge divided by \(\epsilon_0\). Flux is the integral of the component normal to the surface. Pulling the field magnitude outside that integral requires symmetry in addition to the law itself. For this sphere rotational symmetry forces the field to be radial and of equal magnitude everywhere on a concentric spherical surface. The test surface can pass through the charged material, since it is a geometrical construction and does not insert a conductor.

Match the interior and exterior solutions

The uniform density is \(\rho=3Q/(4\pi R^3)\). At a radius \(r<R\) the enclosed charge is \(Q(r/R)^3\). At \(r>R\) the surface encloses all of \(Q\). Substitution in \(4\pi r^2 E_r=Q_{\rm encl}/\epsilon_0\) gives an interior field \(E_r=Qr/(4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3)\) and an exterior field \(E_r=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). The field vanishes at the centre and is continuous at the surface. A finite change in volume density changes the slope there. A true surface sheet of charge could instead create a finite jump in the normal field.

Since a static field has zero curl, a scalar potential can be chosen such that \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\). Take \(V(\infty)=0\) and integrate the radial field inward. The exterior potential equals \(Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)\). To reach an interior point, continue the integral from the surface with the interior field. This gives

\[V(r)=\frac{Q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left(3-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right),\qquad 0\le r\le R.\]

Matching at the surface supplies the additive constant which an interior integration alone cannot fix. A zero electric field at the centre means that the slope of the potential vanishes there. Its value need not vanish.

Assemble the sphere and account for the work

Build the charge by adding successive spherical shells while holding the final density fixed. When the assembly reaches radius \(r\), its charge is \(q(r)=Q(r/R)^3\). Bringing a small additional shell \(dq=3Qr^2dr/R^3\) from infinity costs \(dW=q(r)dq/(4\pi\epsilon_0 r)\). Integration gives \(W=3Q^2/(20\pi\epsilon_0 R)\). This positive work is stored in the electrostatic configuration. The shell’s interaction with itself is higher order in the infinitesimal added charge.

A complementary description uses the energy density \(\epsilon_0 E^2/2\). Integration over the exterior alone gives \(Q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0R)\), which is five sixths of the total. The remainder resides inside the charge distribution. A thin spherical conducting shell of equal radius and charge produces the same outside field, yet it has no interior field in its empty cavity. Its total energy is therefore lower.

Interpret three probe readings

For \(R=0.10\) m and \(Q=2.0\) nC, the surface field is \(1.80\times10^3\) V/m. At half the radius the field halves to about 899 V/m. At twice the radius it is roughly 449 V/m. The surface potential is 180 V, while the centre is near 270 V. Their difference predicts the work per unit charge required to carry a small positive test charge from surface to centre. A sufficiently large test charge would disturb the source distribution and violate the assumed measurement model.

The assembly energy is only \(2.16\times10^{-7}\) J. Doubling \(Q\) multiplies the field and potential by two but multiplies the stored energy by four. A comparison of fields outside two spherical samples probes their total charge. A measurement of assembly work at fixed radius can reveal how that charge was distributed inside them.

If the charge is reduced gradually while its spatial shape is preserved, the surface voltage is proportional to total charge. The incremental work needed to assemble the next element therefore grows with charge already present. Integrating that growth explains the familiar factor of one half in electrostatic self-energy expressions.

Insert a dielectric into a spherical capacitor

An enclosed-charge construction also works when different radial regions store different electric energies. Put a conducting sphere of radius \(a\) inside a concentric thin conducting shell of radius \(b\). Give the inner sphere free charge \(+Q\) and the outer shell \(-Q\). Fill \(a<r<c\) with a linear dielectric of permittivity \(\epsilon_1\) and \(c<r<b\) with \(\epsilon_2\). There is no free charge at the interface. Assume the shells and both materials share a common centre so that a spherical Gaussian surface has uniform radial displacement.

Gauss's law for free charge gives \(D_r=Q/(4\pi r^2)\) throughout the gap. The electric field in material \(i\) is \(D_r/\epsilon_i\). Thus the normal field can jump at \(c\) even though normal displacement is continuous. To predict the measured voltage, integrate the radial field outward from \(a\) to \(b\); the positive quantity obtained is the inner voltage minus the outer voltage. This yields

\[\frac1C=\frac{V_a-V_b}{Q}=\frac1{4\pi}\left[\frac{1/a-1/c}{\epsilon_1}+\frac{1/c-1/b}{\epsilon_2}\right].\]

The sum is naturally a sum of inverse capacitances since the same flux passes through both layers and their voltage drops add. Replacing the two dielectric constants by a simple average would weight every radial thickness equally, despite the field being stronger near the small inner sphere. Setting the two permittivities equal cancels the arbitrary interface location, a useful check on the integration.

Decide where a small amount of dielectric matters most

Choose \(a=10.0\) mm, \(c=20.0\) mm, \(b=40.0\) mm, \(\epsilon_1=4\epsilon_0\) and \(\epsilon_2=\epsilon_0\). The radial contributions after factoring out \(1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\) are \((100-50)/4=12.5\) m⁻¹ and \(50-25=25.0\) m⁻¹. Hence \(C=4\pi\epsilon_0/(37.5\,{\rm m}^{-1})=2.97\) pF. A charge of \(0.300\) nC produces about 101 V, one third across the inner material and two thirds across the outer one. An all-vacuum device of the same radii has \(C=1.48\) pF.

For a further check integrate local energy density \(u=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D/2\) over each layer. The integration element \(4\pi r^2dr\) reduces the energy integral to \(Q^2(1/r_{\rm in}-1/r_{\rm out})/(8\pi\epsilon_i)\). The two contributions are again in the ratio one to two, summing to \(Q^2/(2C)=15.2\) nJ. Moving dielectric towards the inner conductor usually has a larger effect per unit volume because the undisturbed energy density is higher there. Near metal contact surfaces roughness or air pockets can make precisely this region differ from its intended permittivity.

At the interface voltage is continuous but radial field just inside the outer material is four times its value just inside the inner material for these permittivities. A finite jump in voltage would imply an infinitely strong field over zero thickness, absent any imposed dipole sheet. Inserting these interfacial fields into the constitutive law gives identical normal displacement from either side. This is a more local test than comparing the terminal capacitance: a calculation can accidentally reproduce a similar integrated voltage while placing the field discontinuity on the wrong side of the boundary.
