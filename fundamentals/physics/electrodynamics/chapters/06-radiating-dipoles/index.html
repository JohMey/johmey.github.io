Radiation from a small electric dipole

From first principles

From a time-dependent current to dipole radiation

Take a localized, slowly moving source much smaller than the wavelength, in vacuum. At an observation distance \(r\) large compared with both source size and wavelength, the part of the retarded vector potential that survives at order \(1/r\) samples the whole small source at approximately one retarded time \(t_r=t-r/c\). Define its dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho\,dV'\). Charge conservation with vanishing current at the outer boundary gives, by integration by parts,

\[\dot{\mathbf p}=-\int\mathbf r'\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J\,dV'=\int\mathbf J\,dV',\]
\[\mathbf A\simeq\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\dot{\mathbf p}(t_r).\]

When taking a curl far from the source, differentiating the retarded time supplies a factor \(1/c\) and retains \(1/r\). Differentiating the geometrical prefactor gives a smaller \(1/r^2\) term. With \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) pointing to the detector, the leading fields are

\[\mathbf B_{\rm rad}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi cr}\,\ddot{\mathbf p}(t_r)\times\hat{\mathbf n},\]
\[\mathbf E_{\rm rad}=c\,\mathbf B_{\rm rad}\times\hat{\mathbf n}.\]

The first cross product removes the longitudinal dipole acceleration. Thus a sinusoidal dipole has a dark radiation axis and maximum intensity broadside. The flux decreases as \(1/r^2\), just cancelling the area growth of a distant sphere. A constant dipole or one increasing at a constant rate has no such dipole radiation term.

Derivation and applications

Calculate how much dipole radiation a collector intercepts

An angular scan gives intensity along one line of sight. Turning it into collected power requires integrating the radiation pattern over the angles accepted by the optics. Assume a pointlike linearly oscillating electric dipole in vacuum, an observer many wavelengths away and a lossless collector. Its axisymmetric far-field power per unit solid angle is \(K\sin^2\theta\), where \(K\) sets the strength and \(\theta\) is measured from the oscillation axis. The solid angle of a narrow polar ring includes a factor of \(\sin\theta\) arising from its circumference.

\[dP=2\pi K\sin^3\theta\,d\theta,\]
\[P_{\rm tot}=2\pi K\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=\frac{8\pi K}{3}.\]

Consider a collector accepting the full azimuth from the positive axis out to half-angle \(\alpha\). Set \(u=\cos\theta\) in the integral. The term \(1-u^2\) is the angular weight and the limits in \(u\) run from \(\cos\alpha\) to one.

\[f_{\rm cap}=\frac{P(0\leq\theta\leq\alpha)}{P_{\rm tot}}=\frac34\int_{\cos\alpha}^1(1-u^2)\,du=\frac{2-3\cos\alpha+\cos^3\alpha}{4}.\]

For a collector surrounding the equator, take the band between \(\pi/2-\delta\) and \(\pi/2+\delta\). Its omitted power resides in two identical polar caps. Subtracting their contributions gives

\[f_{\rm band}=\frac{3\sin\delta-\sin^3\delta}{2}.\]

A hemisphere admits exactly one half of the power by symmetry. Near the axis the small-cap result begins as \(3\alpha^4/16\) for \(\alpha\) in radians, because both intensity and available solid angle shrink there.

Worked example

Collect fluorescence through a polar cone

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

A fixed dipole emits 8.00 nW and an objective in vacuum accepts a polar cone with \(\alpha=60.0^\circ\). Substituting \(\cos\alpha=0.500\) gives

\[f_{\rm cap}=(2-1.50+0.125)/4=0.15625.\]

The incident optical power is 1.25 nW. If a downstream filter transmits 80.0 percent throughout this cone, the detector receives 1.00 nW. Randomly rotating emitters would require averaging the orientation before applying a collection factor.

Second application

Infer total power from an equatorial band

Change the inputs and investigate this derivation.

An annular monitor accepts all azimuths from 60.0° to 120.0°. Here \(\delta=30.0^\circ\) and \(f_{\rm band}=(1.50-0.125)/2=0.6875\). A calibrated reading of 5.50 µW implies total emitted power \(5.50/0.6875=8.00\) µW. The band covers only half of the sphere's solid angle, so treating this dipole as isotropic would overestimate the total by 3.00 µW.

How much radiation fits inside a cone?

An electrically short dipole radiates a far-zone intensity proportional to \(\sin^2\theta\). A detector subtends a circular cap of half-angle \(\alpha\) about the dipole axis. Guess whether a 45-degree cap intercepts one quarter of the radiated power.

A ring on the viewing sphere has area \(2\pi r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\). The extra sine comes from area, even for an isotropic source. Integrating \(\sin^3\theta\) from 0 to \(\alpha\) and dividing by its integral from 0 to \(\pi\) gives \((2-3\cos\alpha+\cos^3\alpha)/4\).

A dipole pulse has different clocks for displacement, radiation and colour

A small neutral source changes its electric dipole moment along a fixed axis. Unlike a harmonic antenna operating indefinitely, this source begins and ends with negligible dipole moment, current and acceleration. Let its history in the source frame be \(p(t)=p_0\exp[-t^2/(2T^2)]\) along \(z\). Assume that the source size is much smaller than \(cT\), that the detector is far outside the source and its near zone over the main spectral band, and that the environment is vacuum. The aim is to infer both the time profile and total energy of the radiated pulse from this smooth change in charge distribution. At no point do we create or destroy net charge.

Translate the mechanical history into a detector trace

For a localized conserved source at leading electric-dipole order, the far electric field at fixed polar angle has magnitude proportional to \(\ddot p(t-r/c)\sin\theta/r\). The sign of a polar component depends on basis convention, but relative lobes in a single fixed detector keep their signs. Introduce reduced retarded time \(u=(t-r/c)/T\). Two derivatives give

\[\dot p=-\frac{p_0}{T}u e^{-u^2/2},\]
\[\ddot p=\frac{p_0}{T^2}(u^2-1)e^{-u^2/2}.\]

The source moment is largest at \(u=0\), its first derivative vanishes there, and its second derivative is negative with maximal magnitude. A detector sensitive to signed electric field observes a central lobe bracketed by oppositely signed weaker lobes. Its zero crossings are \(u=\pm1\); the side extrema are at \(u=\pm\sqrt3\), where their magnitude relative to the central peak is

\[2e^{-3/2}=0.446.\]

A power detector squares the waveform and loses its signs. Inferring the original \(p(t)\) from a power-only trace therefore needs additional phase or source information.

Integrate the radiated energy in the time domain

The instantaneous angular power per emission time in the nonrelativistic electric-dipole approximation is

\[dP/d\Omega=\ddot p^{\,2}\sin^2\theta/(16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3).\]

Integrating over directions gives

\[P=\ddot p^{\,2}/(6\pi\epsilon_0c^3).\]

Use \(dt=T\,du\), and expand \((u^2-1)^2=u^4-2u^2+1\). Gaussian moments are \(\int e^{-u^2}du=\sqrt\pi\), \(\int u^2e^{-u^2}du=\sqrt\pi/2\), and \(\int u^4e^{-u^2}du=3\sqrt\pi/4\) over the whole line. The time integral becomes

\[W=\frac{p_0^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0 c^3T^3}\,\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}.\]

Stretch the event to twice its duration at fixed maximum moment. The radiative field peak decreases by four, its peak power decreases by sixteen, but the pulse lasts twice as long, giving one eighth the total radiated energy. This strong time-scale dependence is a useful diagnostic: a trace with unchanged integrated energy following such a stretch would challenge the assumed fixed dipole amplitude or the dipole-only description.

Check the total with a frequency-domain measurement

Define \(\widetilde p(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(t)e^{i\omega t}dt=p_0T\sqrt{2\pi}\exp[-(\omega T)^2/2]\). The transform of \(\ddot p\) is \(-\omega^2\widetilde p\), with no endpoint terms. Parseval's identity with this convention gives a one-sided energy spectrum

\[\frac{dW}{d\omega}=\frac{\omega^4|\widetilde p(\omega)|^2}{6\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3},\qquad \omega>0 .\]

The factor of two for negative frequencies has already been included. Although \(|\widetilde p|\) peaks at zero frequency, radiation has no zero-frequency component for this pulse. Maximising \(\omega^4e^{-(\omega T)^2}\) gives

\[\omega_{\max}=\sqrt2/T.\]

Integrating the spectrum over \(\omega>0\) reproduces the time-domain result because \(\int_0^\infty v^4e^{-v^2}dv=3\sqrt\pi/8\). This is an independent check of both the dipole differentiation and the Fourier convention. A spectrum plotted per unit wavelength would need the Jacobian \(|d\omega/d\lambda|\) and generally has a different peak.

For \(T=0.50\) ns, the two signed field zero crossings are 1.00 ns apart while maximum spectral energy per angular-frequency interval occurs near \(450\) MHz when converted to ordinary frequency. If the detector is placed 0.60 m farther along the same far-field ray, every feature arrives 2.00 ns later. After multiplying the signed fields by their respective radii and compensating this flight time, the traces should match. Failure of the scaling can reveal a near-field contribution or a frequency-dependent transfer function in the receiving system.

Two integral tests for the sign of a measured waveform

For a single far-field detector at fixed angle outside a radiation node the signed voltage, after calibrating its response, is proportional to \(\ddot p(t-r/c)\). The area of that trace is zero when \(\dot p\) has the same value before and after the event. Integrate the first time moment by parts:

\[\int t\ddot p\,dt=[t\dot p]_{-\infty}^\infty-[p]_{-\infty}^\infty.\]

It too vanishes for the specified Gaussian pulse. These identities constrain both the relative signs and the wing amplitudes. A clipped recording can violate them even when the main central peak is accurately sampled.

The second time moment survives. Two integrations by parts give \(\int t^2\ddot p\,dt=2\int p\,dt\), equal to \(2\sqrt{2\pi}p_0T\) for the model. A common delay shifts \(t^2\) into a sum of quadratic, linear and constant terms. The lower two moments vanish, so the second moment is unchanged by that delay. This supplies an independent way to compare two radius-corrected detector traces without first finding the centre of a noisy lobe. Its usefulness depends on retaining the wings long enough to control the quadratic time weighting.

Recover a pulse timescale without identifying a carrier

An equatorial detector records a signed pulse whose central lobe crosses zero at 6.2 and 7.8 ns. A spectrometer, calibrated per unit ordinary frequency, finds the strongest signal near 280 MHz. Decide whether the observations fit the Gaussian dipole model. What should a detector on the dipole axis measure?

Derive the result and check it independently

The two time zeros bracket \(2T\), giving \(T=0.80\) ns and the source-centred retarded event at detector time 7.0 ns. The predicted spectral peak is

\[f_{\max}=\sqrt2/(2\pi T)=281\,\mathrm{MHz}.\]

Converting from angular to ordinary frequency changes the spectral density by a constant \(2\pi\), so the peak is at the frequency just calculated. Both measurements agree within the stated precision. They still do not fix the absolute pulse energy without amplitude calibration and distance.

Along the dipole axis the transverse radiation field vanishes in this approximation. A finite nearby axial detector can respond to electrostatic or induction fields and a finite optical aperture can capture off-axis radiation. Changing distance and angular aperture separately can distinguish these effects. A signed time integral of the far electric trace should also vanish because it is proportional to the difference between final and initial \(\dot p\).

From potentials to a distant detector

An alternating current in a short conductor moves positive and negative charge relative to one another. A current probe at the source and a field probe well outside it measure different aspects of the same charge motion. Consider a localised neutral source of size \(d\), oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\) in vacuum. Assume \(d\ll\lambda=2\pi c/\omega\) so that phase changes across the source are small. The leading electric multipole is its dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho(\mathbf r',t)d^3r'\).

A signal emitted at source time \(t_r=t-r/c\) reaches an observation point a distance \(r\) away at time \(t\). Keeping the leading distant term of the retarded vector potential gives

\[\mathbf A\simeq\mu_0\int\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t_r)d^3r'/(4\pi r).\]

By charge continuity the current integral equals \(\dot{\mathbf p}\). One sees this by differentiating the integral defining a component of \(\mathbf p\), substituting \(-\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J\) for \(\partial_t\rho\) and integrating by parts. No surface term remains for a localised source whose current vanishes at the enclosing boundary.

Extract the transverse radiation field

Curling the potential differentiates both \(1/r\) and the retarded time. The former produces a term falling as \(1/r^2\). Differentiating \(t_r\) produces a term proportional to \(\ddot{\mathbf p}/(cr)\), which dominates sufficiently far away. The resulting magnetic field is transverse to the direction of observation. The radiative electric field can be written as the transverse vector \(\hat{\mathbf r}\times(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}(t_r))/(4\pi\epsilon_0 c^2r)\). The magnetic field is \(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf E/c\).

Take \(\mathbf p=p_0\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf z}\). The electric amplitude is proportional to \(\omega^2p_0\sin\theta/r\), where \(\theta\) is the angle to the dipole axis. Along the axis no transverse component survives. In the equatorial plane the radiation is strongest. These conclusions concern the energy-carrying far field. The electrostatic dipole field and the induction field decay with higher inverse powers of radius and may dominate a probe whose distance is small on the wavelength scale.

Integrate over a sphere

Use the plane-wave expression for the cycle-averaged Poynting flux locally in the far zone. Multiplying by \(r^2\) gives the power per unit solid angle. Integration uses \(d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\) and \(\int \sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3\). For a peak dipole moment the result is

\[\frac{d\langle P\rangle}{d\Omega}=\frac{\omega^4 p_0^2}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}\sin^2\theta,\]
\[\langle P\rangle=\frac{\omega^4p_0^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.\]

The sphere has more area at larger radius while the intensity falls as \(r^{-2}\), so total power is independent of the chosen far-field radius. At fixed dipole moment, doubling frequency increases the radiated power sixteenfold. If the experiment instead fixes the amplitude of current through a short element, the resulting dipole moment falls with increasing frequency because current is the time derivative of charge. The change in what is controlled matters.

Check an angular scan

A calibrated small dipole radiates 3.0 mW in total. At \(r=10\) m in its far zone, the equatorial mean intensity is \(3\langle P\rangle/(8\pi r^2)=3.58\) µW/m². At 30° from the axis it is one quarter of that value, 0.895 µW/m². Treating the source as isotropic would conceal both the axial null and the enhanced equatorial intensity. A dipole probe receiving the field must also be correctly polarised to measure the predicted amplitude.

Move a correctly oriented equatorial probe from 10 to 20 m. Its measured electric amplitude should halve and its measured power per area should fall by four. Failure of this scaling could indicate reflections, inadequate distance from the source or a directional response in the probe. Combining this distance test with an angular scan distinguishes a radiation measurement from a mapping of the near electrostatic field.

An oscillating electric dipole with fixed orientation emits linearly polarized radiation at a generic far-field point. The electric field points along the projection of the dipole axis onto the plane transverse to the line of sight. Turning a linear receiving antenna through a right angle can therefore remove its signal even at a location of maximum emitted intensity. To distinguish antenna misalignment from a source-pattern null, repeat the scan with two orthogonal receiving orientations and add the received powers.

Radiation from a driven bound charge

A dipole need not be fed by a wire. An incident optical field can drive a bound charge, which reradiates part of the incident power. Model a charge of magnitude \(q\) and effective mass \(m\) attached to a harmonic restoring force \(-m\omega_0^2x\). Expose it to a spatially uniform sinusoidal electric field of peak amplitude \(E_0\) at angular frequency \(\omega\). Uniformity here requires the oscillator size to be small compared with the wavelength. Include linear damping rate \(\gamma\) through the term \(m\gamma\dot x\). Steady harmonic response gives a dipole amplitude

\[|p|=\frac{q^2E_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}} .\]

The radiated power of a sinusoidal electric dipole with peak moment \(p\) is \(\omega^4|p|^2/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)\). Divide by the incident plane-wave intensity \(\epsilon_0c E_0^2/2\) to obtain a scattering cross section. Well below resonance, where the restoring term dominates, define the static polarizability \(\alpha_0=q^2/(m\omega_0^2)\) in SI units. Then

\[\sigma_{\rm sc}\simeq \frac{k^4}{6\pi}\left|\frac{\alpha_0}{\epsilon_0}\right|^2,\]
\[k=\omega/c.\]

The strong fourth-power dependence originates in the dipole's radiating acceleration. The amplitude of the induced moment varies little in the low-frequency regime, but twice differentiating it in time produces a field amplitude proportional to \(\omega^2\). Squaring that field gives the fourth power. Near an internal resonance the polarizability itself varies rapidly and this simple colour dependence no longer applies.

Predict the ratio and magnitude of scattered light

Suppose a small isotropic object has \(\alpha_0/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=1.00\times10^{-30}\) m³, and both 450 nm and 650 nm light are far from its absorption bands. At 450 nm, \(k=1.40\times10^7\) m⁻¹. Substitution gives \(\sigma_{\rm sc}= (8\pi/3)k^4(10^{-30}\,{\rm m^3})^2\simeq3.18\times10^{-31}\) m². At 650 nm the cross section is smaller by \((650/450)^4=4.35\), about \(7.32\times10^{-32}\) m². Equal incident irradiances therefore give that same ratio of total scattered powers.

The angular pattern remains dipolar, so a small detector also needs a known collection angle and polarization geometry. At a direction parallel to the incident electric polarization the ideal driven dipole has a null, irrespective of wavelength. A distribution of particle sizes with some dimensions comparable to wavelength can fill such a null and alter the colour ratio. Comparing angular scans and wavelength scans separates these two experimental predictions rather than attributing every departure to a changed number of scatterers.

An ideal absorption-free dielectric particle still removes some power from the original forward beam by redirecting radiation. For a dilute ensemble one might infer scattering cross section from the weak reduction in collimated transmitted power, provided light scattered into the detector aperture is excluded. If the material also absorbs, the reduction measures extinction and is larger than scattering alone. Measuring total scattered power and forward-beam attenuation separately then distinguishes reradiated energy from internal dissipation. Comparing measurements at several concentrations checks whether neighbouring objects remain independent scatterers or instead interfere appreciably across the ensemble.

The polarizability here is in SI units. Tabulated polarizability volumes must first be multiplied by \(4\pi\epsilon_0\) before substitution in the SI cross-section expression.
