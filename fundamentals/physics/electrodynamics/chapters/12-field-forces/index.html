From first principles

Recover electrostatic pressure from virtual work

Place two large parallel, perfectly conducting plates of area \(A\) a distance \(d\) apart in vacuum. Ignore edge fields and disconnect the voltage source so that charge \(Q\) stays fixed. A pillbox at the metal surface gives

\[E=Q/(\epsilon_0 A).\]

To assemble the charge slowly, bring each increment \(dq\) across the voltage \(V(q)=qd/(\epsilon_0 A)\) already established:

\[U(Q,d)=\int_0^Q V(q)\,dq=\frac{Q^2d}{2\epsilon_0 A}.\]

Take a small displacement increasing \(d\). With the leads disconnected there is no electrical work input. The work done by the field is minus the change in stored energy. Thus the signed force tending to increase the gap and the magnitude of its pressure are

\[F_d=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial d}\right)_Q=-\frac{Q^2}{2\epsilon_0 A},\]
\[p=\frac{|F_d|}{A}=\frac{\epsilon_0 E^2}{2}.\]

The minus sign gives attraction. The factor one half also has a direct force interpretation. At one plate, only the field of the other sheet acts on its net charge. That sheet produces half the total gap field. If a voltage source remains connected, charge moves during the displacement and the source’s work must enter the same energy account.

Derivation and applications

Find the force drawing a dielectric into a capacitor

A partially inserted dielectric slab feels a lateral force even though the strong field between broad plates points across the gap. Use a plate width \(w\), overlap length \(\ell\), gap \(d\) and a slab filling the gap for an inserted distance \(x\). The slab has constant relative permittivity \(\kappa\). Assume quasistatic motion, uniform plate separation and \(d\) much smaller than both occupied lengths and the width. The two regions share a voltage and their charges add, so the leading capacitance and its position derivative are

\[C(x)=\frac{\epsilon_0 w}{d}[\ell+(\kappa-1)x],\]
\[C'=\frac{\epsilon_0w}{d}(\kappa-1).\]

First disconnect the supply, leaving total free charge \(Q\) fixed. A small inward displacement lowers the field energy. Energy conservation assigns that decrease to mechanical work, giving the inward electric force

\[F_Q=-\left.\frac{d}{dx}\frac{Q^2}{2C(x)}\right|_Q=\frac{Q^2}{2C^2}C' .\]

With a battery attached instead, moving the slab draws additional charge \(dQ=V\,dC\). The battery does work \(VdQ\), while the field energy increases by half that amount. The difference supplies the mechanical work. This accounts for the same instantaneous force in either experiment when the initial voltage is the same.

\[F_V\,dx=V\,dQ-d\!\left(\frac12CV^2\right)=\frac12V^2\,dC,\]
\[F_V=\frac12V^2C' .\]

The microscopic lateral force arises where the field bends near the dielectric boundary. The capacitance method recovers the integrated force without needing that local field profile. It loses accuracy near the positions where the slab first enters or completely fills the capacitor.

Worked example

Predict the pull at constant voltage

Let \(w=50.0\) mm, \(d=0.500\) mm, \(\kappa=3.00\) and \(V=100\) V. Then \(C'=1.77\) nF/m and the force is \(8.85\) µN. During 10.0 mm of insertion, capacitance increases by 17.7 pF. The battery supplies \(V^2\Delta C=177\) nJ, of which 88.5 nJ becomes mechanical work and the remainder increases the stored electric energy.

Second application

Follow the force after disconnecting the supply

A different isolated apparatus starts at 80.0 pF and 60.0 V. Inserting the slab by 20.0 mm raises its capacitance to 100 pF, giving \(C'=1.00\) nF/m. The conserved charge is 4.80 nC and final voltage is 48.0 V. Initial and final forces are 1.80 and 1.15 µN. The work delivered is the drop in field energy, \((Q^2/2)(1/C_i-1/C_f)=28.8\) nJ. Its average force of 1.44 µN lies between the endpoint values.

Does separating capacitor plates weaken the force?

Set an air-filled parallel-plate capacitor to the reference voltage at a 1 mm gap. In one run leave the source attached and in the other disconnect it. Neglect edge fields. Both start with the same charge and pressure.

Write \(E=V/d\) with a voltage supply and \(E=\sigma/\epsilon_0\) for fixed free charge per unit area. The electrical pressure \(p=\epsilon_0 E^2/2\) then gives different distance dependences. On the fixed-voltage path the source exchanges energy with the field as the capacitance changes.

Find the distance at which the fixed-voltage pressure falls to one quarter of its initial value. Double the reference voltage and explain why the crossing distance of the two curves stays fixed.

Why the two halves of a charged sphere pull apart while its net force is zero

A thin spherical conducting shell of radius \(a\) carries total free charge \(Q\) in vacuum. Its two hemispheres are joined by a mechanically thin seam near the equator. Everything is initially at rest; any slit in the surface is negligibly narrow compared with \(a\). The whole isolated shell has no preferred direction and experiences zero net electromagnetic force. A force gauge in the seam can nevertheless register a finite load. We will calculate the force on the upper hemisphere by two different choices of surface and then determine what changes if the charge fills the volume instead of sitting on the metal skin.

Start with the force on a charged surface element

Spherical symmetry gives \(\sigma=Q/(4\pi a^2)\), \(\mathbf E_{\rm out}=\sigma\hat{\mathbf r}/\epsilon_0\) immediately outside and \(\mathbf E_{\rm in}=0\). Multiplying a sheet's charge directly by its outside field would include the force the tiny element's own field would exert on itself. Near a smooth element approximate its local patch as planar. Its own fields on its two sides are opposite, \(\pm\sigma\hat{\mathbf r}/(2\epsilon_0)\); the field of the rest of the assembly is continuous over that infinitesimal separation. Averaging fields on the two sides therefore extracts the field acting on the element. The force density per area is \(+\sigma^2\hat{\mathbf r}/(2\epsilon_0)\), an outward pressure \(p\).

Each patch acts along its own radial normal. Integrating only the vertical component \(p\cos\theta\) over \(0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2\) gives

\[2\pi a^2p\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta=\pi a^2p.\]

The result is pressure times projected circular area, not pressure times the whole curved hemisphere area. Thus

\[F_{\rm upper}=\frac{Q^2}{32\pi\epsilon_0a^2},\]
\[F_{\rm lower}=-F_{\rm upper}\]

where the signs refer to the \(z\) axis. Reversing the charge signs reverses the electric fields, but the mechanical load stays outward. The whole object's zero net force states only that these internal electromagnetic forces have an equal and opposite partner.

Move the integration surface away from the charges

Instead surround the entire upper half-space by an infinite hemisphere closed by the equatorial plane. In electrostatics the total force on enclosed matter is the outward flux of the Maxwell stress tensor. On the closure at infinity the stress falls as \(1/r^4\), so area growth cannot leave a finite contribution. On the equatorial plane the outward surface normal for the upper region is \(-\hat{\mathbf z}\). The electric field lies parallel to this plane outside the metal and vanishes inside. Thus \(T_{zz}=-\epsilon_0E^2/2\) for radial distance \(s>a\), yielding upward traction on the upper half-space. Its integral is

\[F_z=\int_a^\infty\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}\right)^2 2\pi s\,ds=\frac{Q^2}{32\pi\epsilon_0a^2}.\]

This surface passes through an empty region with no electric charge and gives the same finite force. It resolves an apparent puzzle: the equatorial disk inside the conductor has zero field, but the entire cut plane includes an exterior annulus where the field transmits stress. A numerical integration on a small flat disk confined to the hollow interior would report zero because it omitted nearly all of the relevant control surface.

Estimate the load and the error of a finite stress integral

At \(a=30.0\) mm and \(Q=5.0\) nC, the pressure is \(0.0110\) Pa and the upward force is \(31.2\) μN. Consider checking this value in a field calculation restricted to equatorial radii \(a<s<R\). Since the stress integrand is proportional to \(s^{-3}ds\), that annulus captures fraction \(1-a^2/R^2\) of the analytic plane integral. Extending to \(5a\) captures only 96%. A finite closed control surface would supply the missing contribution on its outer cap. An open truncated plane can instead be extrapolated using the measured \(1/R^2\) tail. Both checks are independent of the surface-pressure argument.

A third consistency check uses uniform expansion of the whole conducting shell with fixed charge, for which \(U=Q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0a)\). Then \(-dU/da=Q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0a^2)=4\pi a^2 p\), the outward pressure integrated over all area during a common radial displacement. It is larger than the hemispherical seam load by a factor of four because these are different virtual displacements. Differentiating a known energy requires matching the change of geometry to the actual force measurement.

Translate the electromagnetic force into a seam measurement

For a narrow continuous join on the equator, let the tensile load transmitted across it be expressed as force per unit seam length. Rotational symmetry makes it uniform, so \(t_{\rm seam}=F_z/(2\pi a)=Q^2/(64\pi^2\epsilon_0a^3)\). In the numerical example this is \(1.66\times10^{-4}\) N/m. Four equally loaded supports in an ideal symmetric mount would each carry about \(7.8\) μN if they carry the entire vertical separating load. This mechanical conversion uses the projection integral for the actual displacement; the radial pressure at a single point is not itself the measured force.

At fixed \(Q\) doubling \(a\) cuts the total seam force by four. Connect the shell instead to a voltage supply holding \(V=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0a)\) fixed as \(a\) changes and the charges supplied by the source change proportionally with \(a\). Substitution in the same static force formula now gives \(F_z=\pi\epsilon_0V^2/2\), independent of radius. The differing trends arise from comparing different prepared charges. A local field-stress calculation remains valid for either experimental protocol at its specified instantaneous state.

Repeat the force balance for a solid insulating sphere

Fill a sphere of the same radius with uniform fixed volume charge \(\rho=3Q/(4\pi a^3)\). Determine the force on all charge in the upper half and explain how the equatorial stress surface supplies the difference from the hollow metal case.

Derive the result and check it independently

Inside the uniformly charged sphere \(\mathbf E=\rho\mathbf r/(3\epsilon_0)\). Its vertical force density is \(\rho^2 z/(3\epsilon_0)\). The geometric moment of the upper half is

\[\int z\,dV=\int_0^a z\pi(a^2-z^2)\,dz=\pi a^4/4.\]

Integration gives \(F_z=\pi\rho^2a^4/(12\epsilon_0)=3Q^2/(64\pi\epsilon_0a^2)\), one and a half times the shell result. Self-interaction of the charge in the upper half contributes zero net force by pair symmetry, allowing the volume integral over the total smooth field.

The exterior annulus still yields exactly the original shell force, since the exterior field depends only on total charge. Now the equatorial disk inside the insulating sphere also contains a radial field \(\rho s/(3\epsilon_0)\). Its stress adds \(\int_0^a[\rho^2 s^2/(18\epsilon_0)]2\pi s\,ds=\pi\rho^2a^4/(36\epsilon_0)\). Combining disk and exterior reproduces the direct volume integral. An exterior voltage scan alone cannot distinguish these two charge distributions, but a force measurement on the separating halves can.

Momentum at a boundary

A capacitor can pull its plates together across vacuum. The ordinary mechanical contact stress of a solid is absent in the gap, yet a surrounding surface still records a force. In vacuum, the electric field contributes the electrostatic stress tensor \(T_{ij}=\epsilon_0(E_iE_j-\delta_{ij}E^2/2)\). Integrating the traction \(\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n\) around a body gives the electric force on enclosed material in a static configuration. An enclosing surface wholly in vacuum avoids having to specify internal material stresses.

To see the origin of this expression, use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\). The derivative of \(E_i E_j\) contains a field-gradient term \(E_j\partial_jE_i\). Electrostatic symmetry of derivatives makes this \(\partial_i(E^2/2)\), which cancels the isotropic term in the divergence. The remaining term is \(\rho E_i\), the local electric force density. The divergence theorem converts the volume force integral into the stated surface integral.

Pressure on a conducting plate

For a planar metal surface the field immediately outside is normal. The magnitude of traction is then

\[p=\epsilon_0E^2/2.\]

Another derivation splits the surface-sheet field symmetrically between its two sides. The charge feels the average of the limiting fields, excluding the sheet’s own force. With zero interior field the result is \(\sigma E/2\), identical since \(\sigma=\epsilon_0 E\). Using the full outside field as the field acting on every surface charge would double the force.

For plate area \(A\) and separation \(d\), neglect fringing and put \(C=\epsilon_0A/d\). If the plates are isolated with charge magnitude \(Q\), stored energy \(U=Q^2/(2C)\) increases linearly with separation. The signed force in the direction of increasing \(d\) is \(-\partial U/\partial d=-Q^2/(2\epsilon_0A)\). The force tends to close the gap, consistent with the traction calculation.

Include the supply in a fixed-voltage experiment

When a voltage source remains connected, \(Q\) varies as \(d\) changes. The source supplies work \(V\,dQ\). The relevant mechanical energy at fixed \(V\) is \(U-VQ=-CV^2/2\). Differentiating this expression gives

\[F_d=(V^2/2)dC/dd=-\epsilon_0AV^2/(2d^2).\]

The electrical stress formula gives the same instantaneous force for either boundary condition. What changes during motion is the relationship between field and separation.

Take \(A=0.0100\) m², \(d=1.00\) mm and \(V=500\) V. The field magnitude is \(5.00\times10^5\) V/m, the pressure is \(1.11\) Pa and attraction is \(0.0111\) N. Doubling the gap at fixed voltage quarters the force. Disconnecting first holds \(Q\) fixed and leaves force unchanged in the ideal wide-plate limit. A direct classroom test is to measure reaction force while controlling either voltage or charge. Departures as separation approaches plate width reveal fringing fields, which redistribute both stress and capacitance.

The planar calculation can be checked by dimensions before using any geometry. Electric-field energy density has units of joules per cubic metre, identical to newtons per square metre. It therefore has the units of pressure. This equality of dimensions alone cannot fix the direction of a force in a nonuniform field. Around the edge of a plate, for example, field lines bend and the tensor traction must be projected onto the direction being measured before integration.

Pressure from an incident and a reflected wave

For time-dependent fields the force on matter combines a flux of electromagnetic momentum through its boundary with the change of field momentum inside. A useful steady special case is a normally incident plane wave on a macroscopic stationary surface in vacuum. Its cycle-averaged energy flux is \(I\), and transport of energy \(dU\) carries momentum \(dU/c\) in the propagation direction. Let the surface reflect fraction \(\mathcal R\), transmit fraction \(\mathcal T\) into vacuum on the far side, and absorb fraction \(\mathcal A=1-\mathcal R-\mathcal T\). Assume all beams are collinear and neglect thermal reradiation in the mechanical measurement.

For an area \(A\) over time \(dt\), the incoming axial momentum is \(IA\,dt/c\). The reflected beam leaves with negative momentum and the transmitted beam with positive momentum. The difference delivered to the surface gives

\[p_{\rm rad}=\frac Ic(1+\mathcal R-\mathcal T)=\frac Ic(\mathcal A+2\mathcal R).\]

A fully absorbing sheet receives \(I/c\) even though there is no exiting optical wave. A perfect mirror receives \(2I/c\) because it reverses photon momentum. A perfectly transmitting transparent sheet with no reflection takes no average axial momentum in this stationary, vacuum-to-vacuum accounting. Interfaces bounding different media require a joint treatment of material and electromagnetic momentum; the simple outgoing-vacuum relation has intentionally fixed that complication.

The same mirror result follows from the stress at a conducting boundary. At normal incidence the tangential electric field cancels while magnetic field amplitudes add. Using the cycle-averaged magnetic pressure at the boundary then gives \(2I/c\). This provides an independent check on the momentum-flux interpretation without assigning a mechanical collision trajectory to a wave.

Predict a torsion-balance signal

Direct a continuous 2.00 W beam entirely onto a small mirror at normal incidence. Measured fractions are \(\mathcal R=0.960\), \(\mathcal T=0.0200\), leaving \(\mathcal A=0.0200\). Integrated axial force is

\[(2.00/c)(0.0200+1.920)=1.29\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}.\]

Mount the mirror so its force acts 40.0 mm from a torsion fibre axis with a perpendicular lever arm. Torque is \(5.18\times10^{-10}\) N m. A torsion constant \(2.00\times10^{-7}\) N m/rad gives a static twist of \(0.00259\) rad or \(0.148^\circ\).

The absorbed power is \(0.0400\) W. Heating can drive air currents or slow distortions that also deflect the balance, particularly outside vacuum. Reversing the mechanical lever arm while leaving absorbed power constant reverses the optical torque. Modulating the beam and measuring both delay and amplitude provides an additional way to distinguish the rapid radiation force from a slower thermal response.

For a plane mirror at oblique incidence the reflected momentum reverses only its component normal to the surface. A beam of known total power fully intercepted by the mirror imparts normal force \(2P\cos\alpha/c\) when it is perfectly reflected at angle \(\alpha\) to the normal. If the controlled quantity is irradiance per area perpendicular to the beam and the physical mirror area is fixed, intercepted power adds another projection factor. The pressure per unit mirror area is then \(2I\cos^2\alpha/c\). Specifying whether power or surface footprint is held fixed is essential before comparing an angular torque scan with either formula.
