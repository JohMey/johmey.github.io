Momentum at a boundary

A capacitor can pull its plates together across vacuum. The ordinary mechanical contact stress of a solid is absent in the gap, yet a surrounding surface still records a force. In vacuum, the electric field contributes the electrostatic stress tensor \(T_{ij}=\epsilon_0(E_iE_j-\delta_{ij}E^2/2)\). Integrating the traction \(\mathbf T\cdot\mathbf n\) around a body gives the electric force on enclosed material in a static configuration. An enclosing surface wholly in vacuum avoids having to specify internal material stresses.

To see the origin of this expression, use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\). The derivative of \(E_i E_j\) contains a field-gradient term \(E_j\partial_jE_i\). Electrostatic symmetry of derivatives makes this \(\partial_i(E^2/2)\), which cancels the isotropic term in the divergence. The remaining term is \(\rho E_i\), the local electric force density. The divergence theorem converts the volume force integral into the stated surface integral.

Pressure on a conducting plate

For a planar metal surface the field immediately outside is normal. The magnitude of traction is then \(p=\epsilon_0E^2/2\). Another derivation splits the surface-sheet field symmetrically between its two sides. The charge feels the average of the limiting fields, excluding the sheet’s own force. With zero interior field the result is \(\sigma E/2\), identical since \(\sigma=\epsilon_0 E\). Using the full outside field as the field acting on every surface charge would double the force.

For plate area \(A\) and separation \(d\), neglect fringing and put \(C=\epsilon_0A/d\). If the plates are isolated with charge magnitude \(Q\), stored energy \(U=Q^2/(2C)\) increases linearly with separation. The signed force in the direction of increasing \(d\) is \(-\partial U/\partial d=-Q^2/(2\epsilon_0A)\). The force tends to close the gap, consistent with the traction calculation.

Include the supply in a fixed-voltage experiment

When a voltage source remains connected, \(Q\) varies as \(d\) changes. The source supplies work \(V\,dQ\). The relevant mechanical energy at fixed \(V\) is \(U-VQ=-CV^2/2\). Differentiating this expression gives \(F_d=(V^2/2)dC/dd=-\epsilon_0AV^2/(2d^2)\). The electrical stress formula gives the same instantaneous force for either boundary condition. What changes during motion is the relationship between field and separation.

Take \(A=0.0100\) m², \(d=1.00\) mm and \(V=500\) V. The field magnitude is \(5.00\times10^5\) V/m, the pressure is \(1.11\) Pa and attraction is \(0.0111\) N. Doubling the gap at fixed voltage quarters the force. Disconnecting first holds \(Q\) fixed and leaves force unchanged in the ideal wide-plate limit. A direct classroom test is to measure reaction force while controlling either voltage or charge. Departures as separation approaches plate width reveal fringing fields, which redistribute both stress and capacitance.

The planar calculation can be checked by dimensions before using any geometry. Electric-field energy density has units of joules per cubic metre, identical to newtons per square metre. It therefore has the units of pressure. This equality of dimensions alone cannot fix the direction of a force in a nonuniform field. Around the edge of a plate, for example, field lines bend and the tensor traction must be projected onto the direction being measured before integration.

Pressure from an incident and a reflected wave

For time-dependent fields the force on matter combines a flux of electromagnetic momentum through its boundary with the change of field momentum inside. A useful steady special case is a normally incident plane wave on a macroscopic stationary surface in vacuum. Its cycle-averaged energy flux is \(I\), and transport of energy \(dU\) carries momentum \(dU/c\) in the propagation direction. Let the surface reflect fraction \(\mathcal R\), transmit fraction \(\mathcal T\) into vacuum on the far side, and absorb fraction \(\mathcal A=1-\mathcal R-\mathcal T\). Assume all beams are collinear and neglect thermal reradiation in the mechanical measurement.

For an area \(A\) over time \(dt\), the incoming axial momentum is \(IA\,dt/c\). The reflected beam leaves with negative momentum and the transmitted beam with positive momentum. The difference delivered to the surface gives

\[p_{\rm rad}=\frac Ic(1+\mathcal R-\mathcal T)=\frac Ic(\mathcal A+2\mathcal R).\]

A fully absorbing sheet receives \(I/c\) even though there is no exiting optical wave. A perfect mirror receives \(2I/c\) because it reverses photon momentum. A perfectly transmitting transparent sheet with no reflection takes no average axial momentum in this stationary, vacuum-to-vacuum accounting. Interfaces bounding different media require a joint treatment of material and electromagnetic momentum; the simple outgoing-vacuum relation has intentionally fixed that complication.

The same mirror result follows from the stress at a conducting boundary. At normal incidence the tangential electric field cancels while magnetic field amplitudes add. Using the cycle-averaged magnetic pressure at the boundary then gives \(2I/c\). This provides an independent check on the momentum-flux interpretation without assigning a mechanical collision trajectory to a wave.

Predict a torsion-balance signal

Direct a continuous 2.00 W beam entirely onto a small mirror at normal incidence. Measured fractions are \(\mathcal R=0.960\), \(\mathcal T=0.0200\), leaving \(\mathcal A=0.0200\). Integrated axial force is \((2.00/c)(0.0200+1.920)=1.29\times10^{-8}\) N. Mount the mirror so its force acts 40.0 mm from a torsion fibre axis with a perpendicular lever arm. Torque is \(5.18\times10^{-10}\) N m. A torsion constant \(2.00\times10^{-7}\) N m/rad gives a static twist of \(0.00259\) rad or \(0.148^\circ\).

The absorbed power is \(0.0400\) W. Heating can drive air currents or slow distortions that also deflect the balance, particularly outside vacuum. Reversing the mechanical lever arm while leaving absorbed power constant reverses the optical torque. Modulating the beam and measuring both delay and amplitude provides an additional way to distinguish the rapid radiation force from a slower thermal response.

For a plane mirror at oblique incidence the reflected momentum reverses only its component normal to the surface. A beam of known total power fully intercepted by the mirror imparts normal force \(2P\cos\alpha/c\) when it is perfectly reflected at angle \(\alpha\) to the normal. If the controlled quantity is irradiance per area perpendicular to the beam and the physical mirror area is fixed, intercepted power adds another projection factor. The pressure per unit mirror area is then \(2I\cos^2\alpha/c\). Specifying whether power or surface footprint is held fixed is essential before comparing an angular torque scan with either formula.
