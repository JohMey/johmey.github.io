Diffraction from an aperture of finite width

Geometrical optics predicts a sharp edge and an infinitely narrow ideal focus. A scalar diffraction integral describes how contributions with different phase interfere. A slit gives an exact far-field reference against which to test an oscillatory numerical integral.

Accumulate phase across an aperture

Let coherent monochromatic light pass through a slit of full width \(a\) in the plane \(z=0\). The slit is long in its other direction and illuminated by a normally incident uniform plane wave. Work with a scalar component in free space. Suppose observation distance \(z\) is large compared with \(a,|x|\) and the wavelength \(\lambda\). Contributions from aperture point \(\xi\) to detector position \(x\) carry a phase \(kR\), \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). The path expansion is

\[R=\sqrt{z^2+(x-\xi)^2}=z+\frac{(x-\xi)^2}{2z}-\frac{(x-\xi)^4}{8z^3}+\cdots .\]

Approximating slow amplitude and obliquity factors by their axial values while retaining the quadratic phase gives the Fresnel integral. Check the dropped phase \(k|x-\xi|^4/(8z^3)\ll1\) over both the aperture and detector range. Its smallness is more restrictive than simply calling the angle small in a high-frequency problem. The scalar aperture model also assumes that edge polarisation and finite screen thickness are negligible.

Reduce the integral to two dimensionless variables

Set \(s=\xi/a\), \(u=ax/(\lambda z)\) and \(N=a^2/(\lambda z)\). Expanding the square splits off a common phase depending only on \(x,z\). Normalise away that phase and the common geometrical amplitude, leaving the shape integral

\[F(u,N)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\exp[i\pi N s^2-2\pi ius]\,ds,\]
\[I_{\rm rel}=|F|^2 .\]

Here \(a\) denotes the full slit width. Definitions using the half-width produce Fresnel numbers four times smaller. The maximum phase of the \(s^2\) term is \(\pi N/4\). This sets a quantitative criterion for dropping it. In the far-field limit \(N\to0\), direct integration of the remaining exponential gives \(F(u,0)=\sin(\pi u)/(\pi u)\) and \(F(0,0)=1\) by continuity. The first zeros occur at \(u=\pm1\). Each slit half has an opposite-phase partner at this observation angle.

Discretise complex amplitude before forming intensity

Divide the slit into \(M\) equal cells. For their midpoint coordinates \(s_j=(j+1/2)/M-1/2\), evaluate \(\phi_j=\pi N s_j^2-2\pi us_j\) and accumulate the real and imaginary parts:

\[F_M=\frac1M\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}(\cos\phi_j+i\sin\phi_j),\]
\[I_M=(\operatorname{Re}F_M)^2+(\operatorname{Im}F_M)^2 .\]

Summing \(|e^{i\phi_j}|^2\) first gives a flat result and loses all interference. An estimate for the largest phase step is \(\pi(N+2|u|)/M\). Reduce it while checking convergence at specified points. For a smooth integrand, composite midpoint quadrature has error of order \(M^{-2}\) in complex amplitude at fixed \(u,N\). Relative error in intensity near a zero can be arbitrarily large. There compare absolute intensities or complex amplitudes. Agreement between a coarse grid and the far-field formula at large \(N\) is not a valid convergence test.

Design a detector scan and identify a false minimum

Choose a distance that resolves the first minimum

A slit has full width \(a=100\) μm in light of \(\lambda=500\) nm. At \(z=1.0\) m the Fresnel number is 0.020 and the dropped aperture phase is at most 0.0157 rad. The first far-field minimum lies at \(x=\lambda z/a=5.0\) mm. At \(z=20\) mm the same slit gives \(N=1\). Dropping 0.785 rad at the aperture edge can materially change the measured distribution. Moving the detector closer also changes the physical width associated with one unit of \(u\).

How much light fills the nominal dark point?

At \(u=1\) the Fraunhofer amplitude vanishes. For small \(N\), expand the chirp to first order. The remaining integral is \(i\pi N\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}s^2e^{-2\pi is}ds\). The imaginary odd integrand vanishes; two integrations by parts give the real integral \(-1/(2\pi^2)\). Hence \(F(1,N)\simeq-iN/(2\pi)\) and \(I_{\rm rel}(1,N)\simeq N^2/(4\pi^2)\). At \(N=0.10\) the predicted residual intensity is \(2.53\times10^{-4}\). A correctly converged computation can have a nonzero value at a nominal far-field dark point.

Integrate the slit and refine the aperture mesh

Here the slit width is the entire opening. At Fresnel number 0.10 the first far-field dark point has residual intensity about 0.000253 on the converged scale. Change the mesh and the Fresnel number independently to distinguish numerical error from a physical filling of the minimum.

Problem sheet · reconstruction and convergence

Use two measured minima

For wavelength 630 nm and distance 1.20 m, the separation of the first minima is 9.0 mm. Recover the slit width and check the implied far-field assumption.

Work through the solution

The distance from the centre to either first zero is 4.5 mm. Thus \(a=\lambda z/x=168\) μm. Then \(N=a^2/(\lambda z)=0.0373\), and the maximum neglected quadratic phase is 0.0293 rad. With the stated precision the use of the far-field formula is plausible. The relevant length in the inverse calculation was half the measured separation.

Choose and validate a quadrature

For \(|u|\leq3\) and \(N=4\), require the estimated phase change between adjacent source nodes to remain below 0.25 rad. What grid size is suggested? How would you estimate the remaining error?

Work through the solution

The bound gives \(M>\pi(4+6)/0.25\simeq126\), so 128 cells meet the criterion. Compare 128 and 256 cells at the centre and near the first fringes. In the second-order convergence regime the fine-grid amplitude error is approximately one third of the coarse-to-fine difference. Verify the trend with 512 cells if high accuracy is needed. Refining the detector sampling alone cannot fix inadequate sampling of the slit.
