The same metal sphere connected to ground or electrically isolated

A grounded sphere can draw charge through a wire. An isolated one can only rearrange what is already present. Both are equipotentials, but the second problem needs a different image system and gives a different force at long range.

Find the image by matching distances on a sphere

A positive test charge \(q\) sits at \(z=d>a\) outside a conducting sphere of radius \(a\) centred at the origin. In the exterior vacuum region the potential has the known Coulomb singularity at \(q\) and is otherwise harmonic. Look for an auxiliary point \(s\hat{\mathbf z}\) inside the metal whose distance from each surface point is proportional to the distance from the real charge. The squared distances at polar angle \(\theta\) are \(a^2+s^2-2as\cos\theta\) and \(a^2+d^2-2ad\cos\theta\). Matching their constant and cosine terms yields \(s=a^2/d\) and \(R_s=(a/d)R_q\).

A charge \(q'=-qa/d\) at the auxiliary point then cancels the real potential on the sphere. The superposition vanishes at infinity. Uniqueness for prescribed potentials makes this the actual exterior solution. The mathematical source is outside the physical domain of the solution. A surface enclosing just the conductor can be evaluated using the equivalent exterior field, so the total induced charge is \(q'\). It arrived through the grounding connection.

Change the constraint by adding one image

For an isolated sphere of prescribed total charge \(Q_c\), its surface potential is a constant that must be determined. Add a central charge \(q_0=Q_c-q'\). Its surface contribution \(q_0/(4\pi\epsilon_0 a)\) is uniform, preserving equipotentiality, while the total equivalent enclosed charge becomes \(Q_c\). The resulting sphere potential is \(V_s=Q_c/(4\pi\epsilon_0a)+q/(4\pi\epsilon_0d)\). The grounded and neutral isolated solutions agree only when the geometry or external charge makes their net-charge constraints coincide.

Use Gauss's boundary condition \(\sigma=-\epsilon_0\partial_r V|_{a^+}\). The grounded result and its modification are

\[\sigma_g(\theta)=-\frac{q}{4\pi a}\frac{d^2-a^2}{(d^2+a^2-2ad\cos\theta)^{3/2}},\]
\[\sigma_c(\theta)=\sigma_g(\theta)+\frac{q_0}{4\pi a^2}.\]

For a neutral sphere the charge layer facing the source is negative, while a sufficiently distant part of the surface becomes positive. Integrating over the sphere involves the area element \(2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\). A uniform angular average without that weight miscounts the sharply concentrated polar charge.

Take a force at the real charge, then take a large-distance limit

Evaluate the electric field of the image charges only at the real charge. The grounded force \(F_z\) and its neutral-isolated counterpart are

\[F_g=-\frac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{ad}{(d^2-a^2)^2},\]
\[F_{\rm neutral}=F_g+\frac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{a}{d^3}.\]

At \(d\gg a\), the grounded force begins at order \(d^{-3}\) because the grounded sphere acquires charge proportional to \(1/d\). In the neutral case that leading term cancels, leaving \(-2q^2 a^3/(4\pi\epsilon_0 d^5)\). The conductor responds mainly as an induced dipole. This cancellation offers a demanding numerical check at large separation. For the grounded case the energy relative to infinite separation follows from integrating \(-F_g\) with respect to \(d\), yielding \(U=-q^2a/[8\pi\epsilon_0(d^2-a^2)]\). It also equals \(qV_{\rm image}(d)/2\), with the half arising as the conductor polarises continuously during assembly.

Compare two preparations with identical geometry

Unplug a sphere before approaching it

Set \(a=20\) mm, \(d=40\) mm and \(q=1.0\) nC. The auxiliary image lies at 10 mm and has charge \(-0.50\) nC. Grounding gives \(F_g=-4.99\) μN. For a neutral isolated sphere the added central \(+0.50\) nC exerts \(+2.81\) μN and the total is \(-2.18\) μN. Its surface potential is \(q/(4\pi\epsilon_0d)\simeq225\) V. In both experiments the metal still has no interior electric field in static equilibrium.

Disconnect after the charge is already present

If the wire is removed at \(d=2a\), the sphere keeps its acquired \(-q/2\). With the real charge still at \(2a\) the exterior solution is unchanged. Move it to \(4a\). The new off-centre image is \(-q/4\) and the central image is also \(-q/4\). The net conductor charge remains \(-q/2\), and its potential becomes negative. A calculation that continually sets the sphere voltage to zero after disconnection would model an ongoing exchange of charge with an external reservoir.

Measure the charge density of two spheres with different connections

With the source at two sphere radii, the grounded surface draws a total charge −q/2; the neutral isolated sphere retains zero total charge. Change the distance and the trapped initial conductor charge. Inspect the source-facing pole and the far side before deciding whether a net force can be repulsive.

Problem sheet · charge balance and a near-surface limit

Recover a plane image from a curved surface

Let \(d=a+h\) with \(h\ll a\). Show that the near-surface force on the charge approaches the grounded plane result and determine when the central neutralising image matters.

Work through the solution

Use \(d^2-a^2=2ah+h^2\). The leading grounded force is \(-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0h^2)\), identical to attraction to an image \(-q\) a distance \(2h\) away. The central neutralising field is of order \(q/(4\pi\epsilon_0a^2)\), smaller by order \((h/a)^2\) in the force budget.

Where does a neutral sphere change surface charge sign?

For \(d=2a\) and \(Q_c=0\), solve \(\sigma_c=0\) for the polar angle. How can a sphere still attract the external charge with zero net charge?

Work through the solution

In units \(q/(4\pi a^2)\) the equation is \(-3/(5-4\cos\theta)^{3/2}+1/2=0\). Therefore \(\cos\theta=(5-6^{2/3})/4\) and the sign change occurs near \(65^\circ\). Negative surface charge is closer to the positive real charge; the inverse-square field weights its attraction more strongly than repulsion by distant positive charge.
