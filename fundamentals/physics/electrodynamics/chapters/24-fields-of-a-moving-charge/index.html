A moving charge, its retarded position and its radiation

A moving source moves while its field travels. Finding its position on the past light cone supplies an extra factor in its potential and explains why an accelerated charge can carry energy to arbitrarily large distances.

Locate the emission event

Let the charge follow the prescribed path \(\mathbf z(s)\). At time \(t\) and detector position \(\mathbf x\), define \(R(s)=|\mathbf x-\mathbf z(s)|\) and \(\mathbf n=(\mathbf x-\mathbf z)/R\). The emission time \(s\) solves \(h(s)=s+R(s)/c-t=0\). Differentiate to obtain

\[h'=1-\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta=\kappa,\]

with \(\boldsymbol\beta=\dot{\mathbf z}/c\). For a subluminal charge \(\kappa>0\), so the root is unique when it exists. Two emissions a time \(ds\) apart arrive only \(\kappa ds\) apart. Looking forwards along a fast charge's path shortens this interval.

Insert \(\rho=q\delta^3(\mathbf x'-\mathbf z(s))\) into the spacetime integral for the retarded potential. Integrate over \(\mathbf x'\) first. The remaining integral is proportional to \(\int ds\,\delta(h(s))/R(s)\). Changing variable from \(s\) to \(h\) introduces \(1/\kappa\). With the current density \(q\dot{\mathbf z}\delta^3(\mathbf x'-\mathbf z)\), the same argument yields the vector potential:

\[\phi=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\kappa R}\right]_{\rm ret},\]
\[\mathbf A=\left[\frac{\mathbf v}{c^2}\right]_{\rm ret}\phi .\]

Simply replacing the position of a static charge with its retarded position misses the Jacobian. Both the geometry and the current vary when taking derivatives of these potentials.

Distinguish persistent and radiative parts

Implicit differentiation of \(h=0\) gives

\[\partial_t s=1/\kappa,\]
\[\nabla s=-\mathbf n/(c\kappa).\]

Apply these identities in \(-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\). Derivatives of the velocity supply an acceleration term, while the remainder can be expressed using the retarded velocity and geometry:

\[\mathbf E=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{(1-\beta^2)(\mathbf n-\boldsymbol\beta)}{\kappa^3R^2}+\frac{\mathbf n\times[(\mathbf n-\boldsymbol\beta)\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}]}{c\kappa^3R}\right]_{\rm ret},\]
\[\mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf n\times\mathbf E}{c}.\]

A detector subtends a fixed solid angle with area proportional to \(R^2\). The square of the first field falls as \(R^{-4}\) and its flux through the detector vanishes at large radius. The second field is transverse and its square falls as \(R^{-2}\), so finite power travels to infinity. Uniform motion leaves only the first term.

Recover the nonrelativistic radiation law

For \(\beta\ll1\), \(\kappa\simeq1\). If \(\theta\) is measured from the acceleration, the radiative electric-field magnitude is \(q a\sin\theta/(4\pi\epsilon_0c^2R)\) in absolute value. Its magnetic partner has magnitude \(E/c\). Therefore the outward Poynting flux is \(\epsilon_0cE^2\), and integration over a distant sphere gives

\[\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^2a^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}\sin^2\theta,\]
\[P=\frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.\]

The angular factor integrates to \(2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=8\pi/3\). At finite speed with acceleration parallel to velocity, the vector triple product in the numerator has magnitude \((a/c)\sin\theta\). The flux per unit arrival time contains \(\kappa^{-6}\). Converting to power per unit emission time multiplies by \(dt/ds=\kappa\), leaving the angular dependence \(\sin^2\theta/(1-\beta\cos\theta)^5\). The distinction matters whenever emissions bunch up at the detector.

Reading charge motion from a signal

A moving source changes its pulse spacing

A charge moving at \(0.60c\) directly toward a distant observer emits two brief disturbances \(2.0\) ns apart in the lab frame. It travels \(0.36\) m between emissions. The second propagation time is shorter by \(1.2\) ns, leaving an arrival interval of \(0.80\) ns. Viewed from behind, the interval is \(3.2\) ns. This calculation concerns arrival times. An observer exactly along a parallel acceleration sees zero radiation in that direction.

How much power misses the polar caps?

For a slow charge, collect radiation at angles \(60^\circ\leq\theta\leq120^\circ\). Put \(u=\cos\theta\). The collected fraction is

\[\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}(1-u^2)\,du/(4/3)=11/16.\]

This equatorial belt spans half the solid angle but receives 68.75% of the power. Integrating with respect to \(\theta\) alone would overcount the polar regions.

Track the brightest direction of an accelerating charge

The two angular curves are each normalised to their own maximum. For parallel acceleration and β = 0.60, the maximum moves from 90° to about 31.8°. Compare peak location and the separately reported unnormalised peak height.

Problem sheet · inversion and beaming

Solve a retarded time

A charge moves along \(z=0.4cs\). At \(t=0\) a detector at \(z=3.0\) m receives its field. Find the emission time and the distance travelled by the signal.

Work through the solution

Before arrival the charge is behind the origin. Here \(R=3.0-0.4cs\) in metres, so \(0=0.6s+3.0/c\). The emission time is \(-16.7\) ns, at \(z=-2.0\) m. The propagation distance is 5.0 m. Using the current separation would predict only \(10.0\) ns of travel.

Locate the angular maximum

For parallel acceleration differentiate \(F(u)=(1-u^2)/(1-\beta u)^5\), \(u=\cos\theta\), and predict the maximum for \(\beta=0.6\).

Work through the solution

The numerator of \(F'\), after dropping positive factors, is \(5\beta-2u-3\beta u^2\). The physical root is \(u=5\beta/(1+\sqrt{1+15\beta^2})\). At 0.6 it equals 0.850, giving \(\theta\simeq31.8^\circ\). The other root lies outside the physical interval. At zero speed the limiting angle is \(90^\circ\), matching the dipole pattern.

Practicum · distinguish a large near field from outgoing power

A neutral dipole of size much less than the wavelength oscillates with complex moment \(p\,\hat{\mathbf z}\) at angular frequency \(\omega\). Two probes lie in its equatorial plane. An experimentalist proposes to estimate outgoing power as \(\epsilon_0c|E_\theta|^2/2\) times detector area at each radius. Is this plane-wave calibration valid close to the source? Compare field amplitudes, phases and a conserved flux before forming an opinion.

Recover the two equatorial fields from potentials

Use \(e^{-i\omega t}\), \(k=\omega/c\), \(x=kr\), and the Lorenz-gauge dipole potentials \(\phi=p\cos\theta\,(1-ikr)e^{ikr}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\),

\[\mathbf A=-i\mu_0\omega p\,e^{ikr}\hat{\mathbf z}/(4\pi r).\]

Recover \(E_\theta\) and \(B_\phi\) at \(\theta=\pi/2\).

Follow the calculation and check the result

At the equator \(\hat{\mathbf z}=-\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\). Applying \(-r^{-1}\partial_\theta\) to the scalar potential gives \(p(1-ix)e^{ix}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^3)\). The vector part \(i\omega A_\theta\) adds \(-p x^2 e^{ix}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^3)\). Define the common complex scale \(K=p e^{ix}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^3)\). Then

\[E_\theta=K(1-ix-x^2),\]
\[c B_\phi=K(-ix-x^2).\]

For the magnetic field use \(\nabla\times[f(r)\hat{\mathbf z}]=f'(r)\hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf z}\) with \(f=-i\mu_0\omega p\,e^{ikr}/(4\pi r)\). Both fields contain induction and radiation terms. The extra electrostatic term appears only in \(E_\theta\). It prevents the local field impedance from equalling the plane-wave value.

Find what two phase-sensitive probes would report

Multiply \(E_\theta(cB_\phi)^*\), retaining both quadratures. Determine the mean outward energy flux. How quickly does the out-of-phase component disappear relative to it?

Follow the calculation and check the result

The complex product is \(|K|^2(1-ix-x^2)(ix-x^2)=|K|^2(x^4+ix)\). Radiation measurements average real instantaneous products, so the mean radial flux is \(\epsilon_0c|K|^2x^4/2\), proportional to \(r^{-2}\) at every radius outside the ideal source. The imaginary component of the complex Poynting vector measures quadrature exchange; its ratio to the real part is \(x^{-3}\) in this convention. It does not add a second time-averaged outward power.

At \(kr=0.50\) the quadrature component is eight times the real part. At \(kr=2.00\) it is one eighth. A spherical integral restores the angular factor \(\sin^2\theta\) and area \(r^2\), yielding \(\omega^4|p|^2/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)\), independent of radius. This checks the result against the dipole radiation formula while retaining the full near-zone amplitudes.

Quantify the bias of an electric-only reading

Compute the ratio of the proposed plane-wave estimate to the true mean radial flux for the two probes. Design a measurement that can separate reactive and radiative fields.

Follow the calculation and check the result

The ratio is

\[|1-ix-x^2|^2/x^4=1-x^{-2}+x^{-4}.\]

It is 13 at \(x=0.5\): a large electric field can coexist with comparatively little outward energy transport. At \(x=2\) the ratio is 0.8125; the same calibration now underestimates the flux by 18.75%. A monotonic intuition based on discarding all inverse powers except \(1/r\) can even miss the sign of the error.

For a phase-sensitive measurement, record \(E_\theta\) and \(B_\phi\) against the same clock, form the real part of their cross-product and compare \(r^2\langle S_r\rangle\) at several radii. Simultaneously examine the imaginary-to-real ratio and check its \(r^{-3}\) trend. For a finite-sized source keep the closest probe outside the source and test the dipole approximation by changing the observation angle. Other multipoles or a reflected wave break the simple three-term model.
