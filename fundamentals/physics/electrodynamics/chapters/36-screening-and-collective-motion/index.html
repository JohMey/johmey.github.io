Static screening and collective charge oscillations

In a neutral ion–electron gas a small positive probe draws an excess of electrons. A displaced electron fluid also experiences a restoring electric field and can oscillate. The equilibrium screening length and the collective frequency involve the same density but describe different experiments.

Count the electrons in a weak electrostatic potential

Assume fixed singly charged positive ions of uniform density \(n_0\), and classical electrons in local thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T_e\). Electron potential energy in an electric potential \(\phi\) is \(-e\phi\), where \(e>0\). The Boltzmann distribution relative to distant neutral plasma is \(n_e=n_0\exp[e\phi/(k_BT_e)]\). The net plasma charge density \(e(n_0-n_e)\) is negative near a small positive potential. Expand the exponential when \(|e\phi|\ll k_BT_e\). Including a prescribed weak external charge density gives

\[(\nabla^2-\lambda_D^{-2})\phi=-\rho_{\rm ext}/\epsilon_0,\]
\[\lambda_D^2=\frac{\epsilon_0 k_BT_e}{n_0e^2}.\]

For a point source in an unbounded uniform plasma the source-free spherical equation at \(r>0\) is \((r\phi)''=(r\phi)/\lambda_D^2\). Discard the growing exponential at infinity. The flux through a sufficiently small sphere fixes the coefficient of the \(1/r\) singularity, yielding \(\phi=Qe^{-r/\lambda_D}/(4\pi\epsilon_0 r)\) as the ideal linear Green function. A literal point charge makes \(\phi\) large arbitrarily close to it. Applying this linear expression as a microscopic prediction there would violate the assumption that produced it.

At \(r=\lambda_D\) the potential is \(e^{-1}\) times the bare Coulomb value at that same radius. The electric field is its derivative, so its ratio to the unscreened field is \((1+r/\lambda_D)e^{-r/\lambda_D}\). At one Debye length this is \(2/e=0.736\). Equating the field ratio to the potential ratio would incorrectly remove a derivative term. If several mobile thermal species can equilibrate, the linear charge responses add, giving inverse squared screening lengths that add.

Displace the electron fluid before the ions move

Now consider a rapid, small longitudinal displacement \(\xi(x,t)\) of a cold electron fluid against the uniform positive background. Particle conservation gives density perturbation \(\delta n=-n_0\partial_x\xi\). The resulting charge perturbation is \(e n_0\partial_x\xi\). For a nonzero spatial Fourier mode Gauss's law gives

\[E=en_0\xi/\epsilon_0,\]

with any uniform externally imposed field excluded. Newton's law \(m_e\ddot\xi=-eE\) then gives

\[\ddot\xi+\omega_p^2\xi=0,\]
\[\omega_p^2=\frac{n_0e^2}{m_e\epsilon_0},\]
\[\omega_p\lambda_D=\sqrt{\frac{k_BT_e}{m_e}}.\]

This frequency is in radians per second. The oscillation cycles at \(f_p=\omega_p/(2\pi)\). The length–frequency product equals a one-dimensional thermal-speed scale. The derivations used different closures: the static density had time to approach thermal equilibrium, while the oscillation neglected pressure gradients, collisions and ion motion. At appreciable \(k\lambda_D\), pressure and kinetic effects change the longitudinal frequency and damping. A fully kinetic model is required to predict Landau damping.

Infer density and temperature from two measurements

At \(n_0=10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(k_BT_e=2\,\mathrm{eV}\), the expressions give \(\lambda_D=105\,\mu\mathrm m\) and \(f_p=898\,\mathrm{MHz}\). Doubling temperature alone increases screening length by \(\sqrt2\) and leaves this cold oscillation frequency fixed. Quadrupling density at fixed temperature halves the length and doubles the frequency. With a measured frequency \(1.80\,\mathrm{GHz}\), density would be about four times the above value. An independently measured \(\lambda_D\) then determines \(k_BT_e=m_e(2\pi f_p\lambda_D)^2\).

Set density and temperature in the graph and compare screened potential and field ratios at a selected physical distance. Use the readout to turn an oscillation period into a density estimate. Check the continuum hypothesis by estimating \(N_D=4\pi n_0\lambda_D^3/3\), the number of particles in a Debye sphere. It is approximately \(4.9\times10^4\) at the worked point, making a collective description plausible. A small \(N_D\), degeneracy or strong coupling would undermine the thermal mean-field treatment.

Tutorial on independent spatial and temporal tests

A probe measures \(f_p=0.90\,\mathrm{GHz}\) and \(\lambda_D=210\,\mu\mathrm m\). Infer approximate density and thermal energy relative to the worked example.

Work through the solution

The frequency is essentially unchanged, so density is approximately \(10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). The doubled screening length at fixed density requires four times the thermal energy, about 8 eV. The independent product check gives a thermal speed of roughly \(1.19\times10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\).

How far from a weak source does the screened potential fall to 1 percent of the bare value? Calculate the field ratio at that point.

Work through the solution

The distance is

\[r=\lambda_D\ln100=4.605\lambda_D.\]

The field ratio is

\[(1+4.605)/100=0.0561.\]

These are ratios to the appropriate unscreened quantities at the same physical position.

If both electrons and singly ionised positive ions with equal temperature and density can rearrange in the static experiment, compare the new screening length with the electron-only value. Does this change the electron-frequency formula on a time scale where ions are stationary?

Work through the solution

Both species respond to weak potential with charge-density changes proportional to \(-q_s^2 n_s\phi/(k_BT_s)\). The two identical inverse squared lengths add, yielding \(\lambda_D/\sqrt2\). On the much shorter oscillation time scale the ions remain the fixed background in the cold electron derivation.
