From first principles

Derive the local account of field energy

A material charge moving with velocity \(\mathbf v\) gains power \(q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E\). Summing gives the rate of work per volume \(\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\). To find the energy reservoir supplying this work, take the scalar product of the Ampère–Maxwell law with \(\mathbf E\) and of Faraday’s law with \(\mathbf H\). Assume linear, time-independent, nondispersive material with scalar \(\epsilon,\mu\).

\[\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H)=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J+\epsilon\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf E,\]
\[\mathbf H\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\mu\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf H.\]

Subtract the first identity from the second. The left side is the divergence of the cross product \(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H\). On the right each field times its own derivative is half the derivative of its square:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\epsilon E^2+\mu H^2}{2}\right)+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E .\]

Integrate over a volume. Positive outward flux and positive work on matter both decrease the field energy remaining inside. The cross product has units W/m² and gives the direction of energy travel. This provides a way to calculate power crossing the insulation of a cable even when no conduction current crosses that surface.

Derivation and applications

Follow the energy into a charging capacitor

A capacitor can store energy in an insulating gap while its supply leads carry conduction current. Consider circular vacuum plates of radius \(a\) separated by \(d\ll a\). Use cylindrical coordinates, with a positive, nearly uniform electric field \(E\) along \(+z\). Charge slowly enough to neglect radiation and propagation delays. The edge of the uniform-field model represents a surface just inside the rim. During charging, the increasing electric displacement contributes to Ampère's circulation law around any concentric circle of radius \(r\leq a\).

\[2\pi r H_\phi=\frac{d}{dt}\int\epsilon_0 E\,dA=\epsilon_0\pi r^2\dot E,\]
\[H_\phi=\frac{\epsilon_0 r}{2}\dot E .\]

The magnetic field grows linearly with radius because the enclosed displacement current scales with area while the circumference scales with radius. When \(E\) and \(\dot E\) are positive, the cross product of axial electric field and azimuthal magnetic field points radially inward. Integrating over the side of the chosen cylinder gives the incoming electromagnetic power.

\[P_{\rm in}=-2\pi rd\,S_r=2\pi rd\,E H_\phi=\epsilon_0\pi r^2d\,E\dot E .\]

Express the electric energy in this same cylindrical part of the gap through its volume. Its time derivative agrees with the surface result at every radius. At the rim, the voltage and total charging current give a third way of measuring this rate.

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\epsilon_0 E^2}{2}\pi r^2d\right)=P_{\rm in},\]
\[P_{\rm in}(a)=VI,\]
\[V=Ed,\]
\[I=\epsilon_0\pi a^2\dot E .\]

Worked example

Trace a voltage ramp

For \(a=40.0\) mm and \(d=1.00\) mm, the capacitance is \(\epsilon_0\pi a^2/d=44.5\) pF. A voltage ramp of 2.00 V/µs draws 89.0 µA. At 10.0 V, electrical input is 0.890 mW. The inward flux density at the rim is then \(0.000890/(2\pi\,0.0400\,0.00100)=3.54\) W/m². A cylinder of half the radius receives one quarter of the power because it encloses one quarter of the changing field energy.

Second application

Check a discharge with a local magnetic measurement

For a separate capacitor, \(d=2.00\) mm and \(E=3.00\) kV/m. A probe at \(r=20.0\) mm reads \(H_\phi=-2.00\) mA/m. The negative sign places the Poynting vector outwards. Its magnitude is \(3000(0.00200)=6.00\) W/m², so the energy leaves that cylinder at \(2\pi(0.0200)(0.00200)(6.00)=1.51\) mW. The axial voltage is still positive. The outward flux therefore identifies a decreasing field magnitude rather than a reversal of plate polarity.

Problem sheet 6 · Can impedance identify a dielectric by itself?

Read the model setup and preparatory problems

Can a standing field contain energy without transporting it?

Let a normally incident plane wave meet a reflector. The electric-amplitude reflection coefficient \(r\) is real and may be negative. At perfect reflection, first predict the electric energy and the net power at the boundary. Vary the observation phase through a cycle.

A right-going wave obeys \(Z H_i=E_i\). A left-going wave obeys \(Z H_r=-E_r\). In units of \(\epsilon E_0^2/2\), the electric and magnetic energy densities are therefore the squares of the sum and difference of the two scalar waveforms. In their product the cross terms cancel, giving cycle-averaged flux proportional to \(1-r^2\).

Try \(r=-1\) and zero phase. Locate a magnetic maximum where the electric field is zero. Advance by a quarter cycle and trace where the field energy has gone.

Follow energy through the bright and dark fringes of two crossing waves

Two coherent monochromatic plane waves of equal frequency and equal electric amplitude \(E_0\) travel in vacuum. Their wavevectors make angles \(+\theta,-\theta\) with the \(z\) axis in the \(xz\) plane. Both electric fields point along \(y\), and the waves are in phase at the origin. An intensity camera on the plane normal to \(z\) sees alternating bright and dark bands. Is a dark band devoid of electromagnetic energy? Inspecting the electric field alone would suggest yes. The magnetic field, Poynting flux and the observer's integration area give three complementary tests of that answer.

Construct electric and magnetic fields from the same phases

Write \(K=k\sin\theta\), \(k_z=k\cos\theta\), \(k=\omega/c\), and use complex amplitudes multiplying \(e^{-i\omega t}\). Then the two phases are \(e^{ik_z z}e^{\pm iKx}\). Their electric fields add to \(E_y=2E_0e^{ik_z z}\cos Kx\). For each wave separately \(c\mathbf B=\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E\). Its \(x\) components add with the same sign, while the \(z\) components have opposite signs. The total fields are therefore

\[cB_x=-2E_0\cos\theta\, e^{ik_z z}\cos Kx,\]
\[cB_z=2iE_0\sin\theta\, e^{ik_z z}\sin Kx.\]

At an electric node \(\cos Kx=0\) the magnetic field still oscillates in the longitudinal direction. Near the nodes it cannot be reconstructed by replacing the whole superposition with one plane wave propagating in \(+z\). Even though both constituents have \(E=cB\), that equality of pointwise magnitudes is not inherited by their sum. The phase \(i\) in the longitudinal magnetic term matters when calculating time-averaged transport.

Compute what the camera and a local energy measurement see

For complex peak-amplitude phasors, time-averaged flux is \(\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B^*)/(2\mu_0)\), and mean energy density is \(\langle u\rangle=\epsilon_0(|E|^2+c^2|B|^2)/4\). Substitute the fields. The \(x\) flux contains a purely imaginary product between \(E_y\) and \(B_z^*\), so its cycle average vanishes. The remaining observables are

\[\langle S_z\rangle=2\epsilon_0 c E_0^2\cos\theta\,\cos^2Kx,\]
\[\langle u\rangle=\epsilon_0 E_0^2[(1+\cos^2\theta)\cos^2Kx+\sin^2\theta\sin^2Kx].\]

At a dark band \(\langle S_z\rangle=0\) but \(\langle u\rangle=\epsilon_0 E_0^2\sin^2\theta\). The oscillatory magnetic field there stores positive energy. At a bright band the ratio of mean flux to mean energy density reaches \(2c\cos\theta/(1+\cos^2\theta)\leq c\). Between them the ratio varies with \(x\); neither its bright-band value nor its spatial average is the velocity of an individual light ray in free space. Local instantaneous transverse energy exchange can occur even though the mean \(x\)-flux is zero.

Integrate over a complete fringe and compare with incoming power

The bright-to-bright period along the camera is \(\pi/K=\lambda/(2\sin\theta)\), obtained from the squared electric field. Over a whole period both squared trigonometric terms have mean \(1/2\). The area-averaged axial flux and energy density become \(\epsilon_0 cE_0^2\cos\theta\) and \(\epsilon_0E_0^2\). Independently, each incident wave carries mean intensity \(I_0=\epsilon_0c E_0^2/2\) along its own direction, whose projection normal to the camera is \(I_0\cos\theta\). Their sum exactly reproduces the integrated fringe measurement. Interference redistributes energy crossing the screen without changing this integrated power.

Take \(\theta=30^\circ\), \(\lambda=600\) nm and incident \(E_0=100\) V/m peak per wave. The fringe spacing is 600 nm. A bright band receives mean normal power density \(46.0\) W/m², while integrating over one complete period gives \(23.0\) W/m². A dark band contains mean energy density \(2.21\times10^{-8}\) J/m³. A sufficiently small electric-dipole detector can be blind at the node even though a magnetic probe responds there. An instrument that averages over many fringe periods cannot reconstruct this local distinction from the total collected power.

Distinguish an imperfect dark band from lost energy

Change one incident electric amplitude to \(E_1\) and the other to \(E_2\), leaving directions and polarisation as before. Each forward field still gives a magnetic \(x\)-component proportional to its electric field. Consequently \(\langle S_z\rangle=(\epsilon_0 c\cos\theta/2)[E_1^2+E_2^2+2E_1E_2\cos(2Kx)]\). The intensity extrema now scale as \((E_1+E_2)^2\) and \((E_1-E_2)^2\). Even a perfectly resolving coherent detector will report shallow minima if the beam powers are unbalanced.

If one electric amplitude is twice the other, the peak-to-trough power ratio is nine, and the visibility is \(2E_1E_2/(E_1^2+E_2^2)=0.80\). Independently block each beam in turn and measure its mean power. Their ratio is four, not two, because the sensor measures power. From those two blocked-beam readings predict the coherent fringe visibility before attributing any discrepancy to an unstable relative phase. Unequal amplitudes also give a nonzero area-averaged lateral Poynting flux proportional to \((E_1^2-E_2^2)\sin\theta\), consistent with the unequal transverse components of incident power.

Distinguish visibility from coherence and from spatial resolution

Replace the ideal point detector with a rectangular pixel of width \(w\), centred at \(x_c\), uniformly sensitive to normally incident energy flux. Derive the contrast reduction, and find the largest pixel width giving at least 90% of ideal visibility in the first lobe.

Derive the result and check it independently

Write \(\cos^2Kx=[1+\cos(2Kx)]/2\). The pixel average of the oscillating term is \(\cos(2Kx_c)\sin(Kw)/(Kw)\). Thus measured visibility \((S_{\max}-S_{\min})/(S_{\max}+S_{\min})=|\sin(Kw)/(Kw)|\). In the first lobe the equation \(\sin y/y=0.90\) gives \(y\simeq0.787\), so \(w\leq0.250\) fringe periods, approximately 150 nm for these waves. A pixel covering exactly one fringe period has zero measured visibility, despite perfect mutual coherence.

As an independent test, move a narrow aperture across the pixel or change the crossing angle. The predicted visibility depends on \(w\sin\theta/\lambda\). Poor coherence and unequal beam intensities can also suppress visibility, but those effects need not follow this spatial averaging law. Conservation of power and the fringe-contrast measurement test different properties of the field.

Charge conservation requires a changing field term

Ampère’s magnetostatic law relates magnetic circulation to current. A surface passing through a wire detects a conduction current, whereas a second spanning surface between the plates of a charging capacitor may intercept none. The evolving electric field supplies the missing current. In vacuum, \(\nabla\times\mathbf B/\mu_0=\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E\). Taking the divergence of both sides and using Gauss’s electric law gives \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho=0\), the continuity equation for charge.

Together with Faraday’s law and the two divergence laws, this term permits waves even in a region containing no material charges. In source-free vacuum take the curl of \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B\). Use \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E\) and substitute the Maxwell–Ampère law. The result is \(\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_{tt}\mathbf E\). The coefficient sets \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}\).

Read a propagating field

Take a sinusoidal electric field \(\mathbf E=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf x}\). The wave equation gives \(\omega=ck\), and Faraday’s law fixes \(\mathbf B=(E_0/c)\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf y}\). The divergence equations require these plane-wave fields to be transverse. Their relative orientation selects the positive direction of travel. Flipping the sign of the magnetic field alone with the same electric waveform would reverse the local energy flow but would violate Faraday’s law for this chosen propagation phase.

Wave speed and field amplitude answer different questions. A stronger sinusoidal source increases the energy in a given volume without changing its speed through linear vacuum. Changing to a lossless homogeneous nondispersive material changes the propagation speed to \(1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\) and the impedance relating electric and magnetic fields to \(\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}\). Near a source, the simple wave relation between amplitudes need not hold.

Identify work and transport locally

Dot the Maxwell–Ampère equation with \(\mathbf E\) and Faraday’s law with \(\mathbf B/\mu_0\). Use the identity \(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\) to combine them. Rearranging gives Poynting’s theorem

\[\partial_t u+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E,\qquad u=\frac{\epsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0},\quad \mathbf S=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{\mu_0}.\]

Integrated over a volume, the equation says that net incoming electromagnetic power increases stored field energy or performs work on matter. In a vacuum plane wave the electric and magnetic energy densities are equal and \(\mathbf S=cu\,\hat{\mathbf z}\). In a standing wave counterpropagating energy flows can cancel on average while local fields and stored energy remain large. A detector of heating and a point probe of electric amplitude need not then respond in the same way.

Convert intensity into field and pulse energy

A broad, normally incident vacuum wave has mean intensity 2.0 W/m². For a sinusoid the cycle mean of \(\cos^2\) is one half. Therefore \(\langle S\rangle=E_0^2/(2Z_{\rm vac})\) with \(Z_{\rm vac}=\mu_0c\simeq377\) Ω. The electric peak is 38.8 V/m and the magnetic peak is \(1.30\times10^{-7}\) T. Using the RMS electric field instead gives \(\langle S\rangle=E_{\rm rms}^2/Z_{\rm vac}\). Mixing a peak-valued field with that RMS formula would overestimate the power by a factor of two.

An absorbing disk of area 0.010 m² intercepts a 3.0 ms pulse with this mean intensity throughout its flat top. Neglect edge effects and switching intervals. The energy received is \(2.0(0.010)(0.0030)=60\) µJ. In the lossless TEM line, the same flux principle becomes the circuit power \(VI\). The coaxial-line experiment reports \(Z_0=47.45\) Ω for the baseline cross-section. A 1 V RMS forward sinusoid there carries about 21.1 mW. A 1 V peak sinusoid carries half that mean power, which gives a direct check on the convention used by a voltage probe.

For a travelling plane-wave pulse, integrate the instantaneous Poynting flux over a plane normal to propagation and over the time during which the pulse crosses. The result is the total transported energy. Equivalently integrate field energy density throughout the pulse volume at one instant. The length of the pulse is its speed multiplied by its duration, so the two constructions agree. This provides a check when a detector reports time-averaged irradiance but an independent instrument reports energy per pulse; the averaging interval and illuminated area enter explicitly.

Which observer can see a purely magnetic field?

A charged particle responds to \(q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\) in one inertial frame. A frame moving alongside the particle initially sees no magnetic part of the force on it, so the assignment of a field as electric or magnetic cannot be frame independent. For a frame moving at velocity \(\mathbf u\) relative to the laboratory, let parallel and perpendicular denote components relative to \(\mathbf u\). The parallel fields remain unchanged. Transforming the electromagnetic field tensor gives the perpendicular components in SI units,

\[\mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E_\perp+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B),\qquad \mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf B_\perp-\mathbf u\times\mathbf E/c^2),\quad \gamma=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.\]

For crossed fields \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0\), choose \(\mathbf u=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2\). The vector triple product then gives \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B=-\mathbf E\). If \(E<cB\), this is an admissible subluminal boost and the moving observer sees \(\mathbf E'=0\). The remaining magnetic field has magnitude \(\sqrt{B^2-E^2/c^2}\). A charge initially moving at \(\mathbf u\) in the laboratory has zero Lorentz force, in agreement with a stationary charge in the new frame.

Let \(\mathbf E=6.00\) MV/m along \(x\) and \(\mathbf B=0.0500\) T along \(z\). The required velocity is \(1.20\times10^8\) m/s along negative \(y\), about \(0.400c\). The moving frame measures \(B'=0.0458\) T. At \(E=cB\) the required speed would be \(c\) and no inertial rest frame eliminates either field. This is the relation for a vacuum plane wave. Its electromagnetic field cannot be removed by trying to travel beside the wave.

Locate the energy entering a resistor

A steady resistor has constant fields and still receives a continuous supply of energy. Model a long straight cylindrical wire of radius \(a\), conductivity \(\sigma\) and resistive length \(\ell\). A DC current \(I\) flows in the positive axial direction. Assume a uniform current density, negligible end effects and a coaxial return sufficiently far away that the magnetic field immediately outside the wire has cylindrical symmetry. The internal axial electric field is \(E_z=I/(\sigma\pi a^2)\). Continuity of tangential electric field gives the same axial value immediately outside its lateral surface.

Ampère's law fixes the azimuthal magnetic field there at \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi a)\). Since \(\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_\phi=-\mathbf e_r\), the Poynting flux points radially inward. Multiplying its magnitude by the lateral area gives

\[P_{\rm in}=E_zH_\phi(2\pi a\ell)=\frac{I^2\ell}{\sigma\pi a^2}=I^2R .\]

The result is the familiar circuit power but obtained from fields on the surrounding surface. It accords with the local energy theorem: field energy within the chosen volume is steady while the inward flux supplies the material work \(\int\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\,dV\). A radial electrostatic field can also be present because of surface charge establishing a voltage relative to the return. It produces longitudinal Poynting transport in the gap. Its direction and magnitude depend on the wider circuit, whereas the inward component above accounts for the resistive loss in the selected segment.

Audit the flux at different radii

Use \(\ell=1.00\) m, \(a=0.500\) mm, \(\sigma=1.00\times10^6\) S/m and \(I=1.00\) A. The cross-sectional area is \(7.85\times10^{-7}\) m² and the resistance is \(1.273\) Ω. The axial electric field is \(1.273\) V/m and the surface magnetic field is \(318\) A/m. Their product gives an inward energy flux density of \(405\) W/m². Integrating over \(2\pi a\ell=0.00314\) m² yields \(1.27\) W, matching the measured electrical input.

Now evaluate the field on an imaginary cylinder of radius \(r=a/2\) inside the wire. Uniform current density encloses only \(I/4\). Its magnetic field is half the surface value, and its area is also halved. The integrated inward power is therefore one quarter, \(0.318\) W. It supplies precisely the heat produced in one quarter of the wire volume. As \(r\) tends to zero the local inward flux vanishes with \(r\) while volumetric heating density remains finite. Taking the divergence in cylindrical coordinates reconciles these limits. A skin-effect current profile at high frequency would invalidate the uniform-field calculation and redistribute both heating and energy input toward the surface.

Measured current and terminal voltage determine the inward energy rate without measuring either field directly, but they cannot locate the path of the incoming energy elsewhere in the circuit. To calculate that path one can choose a coaxial geometry with known conductor and return potentials. The radial field in the dielectric then combines with the azimuthal magnetic field to give longitudinal flux toward the load. Approaching the resistive segment, part of this flux turns inward as demonstrated above. Choosing a different return path may change the exterior pattern substantially while preserving the same resistance and the same total power budget.

Carry energy from source to load through a coaxial gap

The same energy theorem closes the circuit picture if the wire is the inner conductor of a coaxial delivery line. In the uniform, low-loss part take inner radius \(a\), return-conductor inner radius \(b\), potential difference \(V\) and equal opposing currents of magnitude \(I\). A cylindrical Gaussian surface gives radial field \(E_r=V/[r\ln(b/a)]\) in the dielectric. Ampère's law gives \(H_\phi=I/(2\pi r)\). The axial Poynting flux density is therefore proportional to \(1/r^2\). Integrate over the dielectric annulus,

\[P_z=\int_a^b E_rH_\phi\,2\pi r\,dr=\frac{VI}{\ln(b/a)}\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r}=VI.\]

The familiar terminal formula has emerged by integrating power moving through the dielectric. It remains compatible with power crossing the lateral surface into a resistor at the line's end. The outer return conductor also matters: it sets the radial electric field geometry and provides the return current defining where the external magnetic field cancels in the coaxial model.

Take \(V=10.0\) V, \(I=0.200\) A, \(a=1.00\) mm and \(b=4.00\) mm. Delivered power is 2.00 W. The fraction flowing between \(a\) and any intermediate radius \(r\) is \(\ln(r/a)/\ln(b/a)\). At \(r=2.00\) mm this is one half. The outer part of the gap has greater cross-sectional area yet carries the other half because flux density falls outward. If the inner conductor develops finite resistance per length \(R'\) and leakage is negligible, a small segment loses \(I^2R'\,dz\) of longitudinal power. The longitudinal difference equals its radial inward flux, providing a local field interpretation of the ordinary circuit voltage drop.
