Chapter 1 · Equations of state and response functions

Use compressibility and thermal expansion to predict what a pressure gauge will record when a material is heated in a closed vessel.

From first principles

Derive ideal-gas pressure from impacts on a wall

Pressure is the normal force on a wall per unit area. Consider \(N\) classical molecules of mass \(m\) in a cubic box of side \(L\). Neglect intermolecular forces and molecular volume. For an elastic collision at a wall perpendicular to the \(x\) axis, a molecule transfers normal momentum \(2m|v_x|\). It visits the same wall once in a round trip of duration \(2L/|v_x|\). Its time-averaged force there is

\[F_{x,1}=\frac{2m|v_x|}{2L/|v_x|}=\frac{mv_x^2}{L}.\]

Sum all contributions and divide by the wall area \(L^2\). With \(V=L^3\), equilibrium isotropy gives \(\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle/3\). Classical thermal equilibrium assigns mean energy \(k_BT/2\) to each quadratic velocity component. These inputs give

\[p=\frac{Nm}{V}\langle v_x^2\rangle=\frac{Nm}{3V}\langle v^2\rangle,\]
\[pV=Nk_BT .\]

This equation of state connects three directly measurable quantities at fixed particle number. The kinetic calculation also identifies where a real gas can differ: intermolecular forces transfer momentum, and dense molecules occupy an appreciable fraction of the volume.

Derivation and applications

Connect a sound-speed measurement to two compressibilities

A slowly compressed sample has time to exchange heat with its surroundings. A small sound pulse usually compresses a fluid element faster than heat can diffuse across a wavelength. The elastic response relevant to sound is then nearly adiabatic. Consider a homogeneous single-phase fluid at equilibrium and a small-amplitude pulse. Assume negligible viscosity and thermal diffusion over one oscillation, so the specific entropy of a moving element remains constant.

Let \(v\) be volume per unit mass, \(c_p\) the specific heat at constant pressure, \(\alpha=v^{-1}(\partial v/\partial T)_p\), and \(\kappa_T=-v^{-1}(\partial v/\partial p)_T\). From the specific Gibbs differential \(dg=-s\,dT+v\,dp\), equality of mixed derivatives gives \((\partial s/\partial p)_T=-(\partial v/\partial T)_p\). The entropy differential determines a fluid element’s temperature change under isentropic compression,

\[ds=\frac{c_p}{T}dT-v\alpha\,dp=0,\]
\[\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_s=\frac{Tv\alpha}{c_p}.\]

Insert this derivative into the volume response \(dv/v=\alpha\,dT-\kappa_T\,dp\). The resulting isentropic compressibility is

\[\kappa_s=-\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}\right)_s=\kappa_T-\frac{Tv\alpha^2}{c_p}=\kappa_T\frac{c_v}{c_p}.\]

The last step uses the heat-capacity difference for a simple compressible material. For \(\rho=1/v\), a density perturbation gives \(p'=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\rho'\). Linear momentum balance and continuity then combine into a wave equation with speed \(c\),

\[\partial_t^2\rho'=c^2\nabla^2\rho',\]
\[c^2=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_s=\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_s}.\]

Adiabatic heating adds a pressure response on compression for an ordinary expanding fluid. The two compressibilities coincide where thermal expansion vanishes.

Worked example

Predict the speed in an ideal gas

For dry model air at \(p=1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\), take \(\rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(c_p/c_v=1.40\). Since \(\kappa_T=1/p\), sound propagates at \(\sqrt{1.40p/\rho}=342\,\mathrm{m/s}\). An isothermal model gives \(289\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Comparing the two speeds measures the square root of the heat-capacity ratio under these gas assumptions.

Second application

Infer a heat-capacity ratio for a liquid

An ultrasonic pulse travels at \(1480\,\mathrm{m/s}\) through a liquid of density \(998\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). It gives \(\kappa_s=1/(998\times1480^2)=4.57\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}\). A slow isothermal experiment gives \(4.65\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}\). Their ratio yields \(c_p/c_v=1.017\), which explains why the choice of compressibility produces a much smaller acoustic correction for this liquid.

Decide which variables specify a state

An equilibrium sample of one simple phase and fixed composition can be described by pressure, volume and temperature related by an equation of state. A piston experiment can prescribe pressure while changing volume; a sealed rigid vessel instead fixes volume. The distinction matters because the same temperature change can lead to expansion in one apparatus and a large pressure increase in the other. Assume quasistatic measurements so that a single pressure and temperature describe the sample.

An equation of state is a constitutive statement. The ideal-gas relation \(pV=nRT\) treats particles as dilute enough for their mutual interactions to have negligible effect on the measured pressure. An incompressible-liquid approximation often gives an adequate volume balance at a chosen pressure but can make a constrained heating problem ill posed if applied literally. Both small thermal expansion and small but finite compressibility may be needed to compute the pressure response.

Describe a neighbourhood of equilibrium

Treat volume as a differentiable function \(V(T,p)\) near a reference state. Its total differential contains the two independent slopes \((\partial V/\partial T)_p\) and \((\partial V/\partial p)_T\). Define the volume expansion coefficient \(\alpha=(1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_p\) and the isothermal compressibility \(\kappa_T=-(1/V)(\partial V/\partial p)_T\). For ordinary mechanically stable material the latter is positive. The local relation is

\[\frac{dV}{V}=\alpha\,dT-\kappa_T\,dp.\]

Both terms are dimensionless. Expansion has units of inverse kelvin, while compressibility has units of inverse pascal. For fixed volume the pressure slope is \((\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha/\kappa_T\). This relation is a consequence of using the same volume differential. It does not assume a particular molecular model and it remains informative for a liquid for which the ideal-gas equation would give a poor answer.

For an ideal gas at fixed amount, differentiating \(V=nRT/p\) gives \(\alpha=1/T\) and \(\kappa_T=1/p\). Their ratio is \(p/T\), as follows independently from \(p=nRT/V\). At 300 K and 100 kPa, warming a sealed ideal gas by 3.0 K raises the pressure by 1.0 kPa. At fixed pressure instead the relative volume increase is one percent.

Separate intensive, extensive and measured responses

Doubling the amount of material at the same temperature and pressure doubles volume but leaves the two normalised coefficients unchanged. Such intensive coefficients can be compared across samples. A vessel’s own volume, however, can respond to both pressure and temperature. If its expansion coefficients are appreciable, the measured pressure slope describes the coupled sample and vessel. One cannot simply substitute the unconstrained expansion of the sample into a rigid-vessel formula.

To see this, let the vessel’s fractional volume response be \(\alpha_v\,dT+C_v^{\rm vessel}dp\), where its mechanical compliance is positive for an internal pressure increase. Equal fractional changes of vessel and sample give \((\alpha-\alpha_v)dT=(\kappa_T+C_v^{\rm vessel})dp\). A compliant wall reduces the pressure rise, and a vessel that expands just as much as the sample can nearly cancel the temperature signal. Here the symbol for vessel compliance denotes a mechanical quantity and has different units from heat capacity.

Compare liquid and container data

Take a representative liquid with \(\alpha=2.5\times10^{-4}\) K⁻¹ and \(\kappa_T=5.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹ near its operating point. In an ideally rigid container, a 2.0 K increase gives \(\Delta p\simeq1.0\) MPa. The unconstrained volume change at fixed pressure would be only 0.050%. This combination explains how a small fractional expansion can produce a significant pressure response when the fluid is only weakly compressible.

If the container has \(\alpha_v=5.0\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and \(C_v^{\rm vessel}=3.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹, the same warming gives about 0.50 MPa. Inverting a measured slope without the vessel data could thus give a misleading estimate of compressibility. These are local linear predictions. Over a broad pressure or temperature range the coefficients must be evaluated along the path, and crossing a phase boundary can invalidate the single-phase differential altogether.

Convert an isobaric calorimeter reading into an isochoric one

A sample can absorb different amounts of heat per kelvin depending on which variable is held fixed. For a fixed amount of a simple, single-phase material in equilibrium, \(C_V=T(\partial S/\partial T)_V\) and \(C_p=T(\partial S/\partial T)_p\). Apply the chain rule to \(S(T,V)\) along the constant-pressure path. Subtracting the two derivatives leaves the effect of expansion. From \(dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV\) equality of mixed derivatives gives \((\partial S/\partial V)_T=(\partial p/\partial T)_V\). Therefore

\[C_p-C_V=T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p=\frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}.\]

Here \(\alpha\) is the volume expansion coefficient and \(\kappa_T\) the isothermal compressibility. The last equality uses \(dV/V=\alpha dT-\kappa_Tdp\). For a mechanically stable material \(\kappa_T>0\), so the difference is nonnegative even for a material that contracts when warmed. For one mole of ideal gas, inserting \(\alpha=1/T\), \(\kappa_T=1/p\) and \(pV=RT\) gives \(C_p-C_V=R\).

At 300 K a solid has molar volume \(1.20\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol, volumetric expansion coefficient \(6.00\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and bulk modulus \(B_T=1/\kappa_T=100\) GPa. The molar difference is \(TV_m B_T\alpha^2=1.30\) J/(mol K). A constant-pressure measurement of 25.0 J/(mol K) thus implies \(C_{V,m}=23.7\) J/(mol K). For a cubic solid the linear expansion coefficient is approximately one third the volume coefficient. Using it directly in this formula would underestimate the correction ninefold. This conversion matters when comparing a tabulated calorimetric result with a vibrational model calculated at fixed volume.

Predict the temperature change of a throttled gas

Some pressure changes take place in flowing rather than closed matter. Consider a well-insulated porous plug that sustains a pressure difference between two steady streams of the same single-phase gas. There is no shaft work and inlet and outlet speeds and heights have negligible differences in kinetic and gravitational energy. Flow work at each end is already included in the molar enthalpy \(h=u+pv\). The steady energy balance then gives \(h_{\rm out}=h_{\rm in}\). A pressure drop through the restriction can nevertheless change temperature if the gas is nonideal.

From \(dh=T\,ds+v\,dp\) and the Maxwell relation \((\partial s/\partial p)_T=-(\partial v/\partial T)_p\), write the enthalpy differential in terms of \(T,p\). The coefficient of \(dT\) at fixed pressure is the molar heat capacity \(c_p\). Setting \(dh=0\) gives the Joule–Thomson coefficient

\[\mu_{\rm JT}=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_h=\frac{T(\partial v/\partial T)_p-v}{c_p}=\frac{v(T\alpha-1)}{c_p}.\]

For an ideal gas \(\alpha=1/T\), so an isenthalpic throttle produces no temperature change. Isentropic expansion in a turbine is a different process. A throttle's pressure drop is irreversible; constant enthalpy follows here from the steady energy balance and does not imply constant entropy.

Estimate a small temperature drop from gas data

Near a chosen operating point let the low-pressure molar volume be \(v=RT/p+B(T)\), retaining a second virial coefficient \(B=b-a/(RT)\). Here \(a\) and \(b\) characterize an approximate real-gas equation of state. The ideal-volume term cancels in \(T(\partial v/\partial T)_p-v\). The remaining numerator is \(TB'-B=2a/(RT)-b\). At fixed local heat capacity, attractions and excluded volume therefore compete in determining the sign of the temperature response.

Take \(a=0.140\) Pa m⁶/mol², \(b=4.00\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol, \(T=300\) K and \(c_p=29.0\) J/(mol K). Then \(2a/(RT)=1.12\times10^{-4}\) m³/mol and \(\mu_{\rm JT}=2.49\times10^{-6}\) K/Pa, or 0.249 K/bar. A pressure change of \(-2.0\) bar predicts temperature change \(-0.50\) K if the properties remain close to these values. The leading model changes sign at \(T=2a/(Rb)=842\) K. This inversion estimate should not be extrapolated into a dense or multiphase region. A proper finite pressure drop integrates the measured local coefficient along an isenthalp. Recording both enthalpy balance and inlet-to-outlet entropy increase offers an independent test of whether unwanted heat transfer through the apparatus affected the result.

The entropy change of the throttled stream can be checked without a calorimeter. Along an isenthalp the Gibbs relation gives \(ds=-v\,dp/T\). Since molar volume and temperature are positive, any pressure decrease raises the molar entropy. Even when an ideal gas shows zero temperature change, its pressure decrease leaves a positive entropy difference. This reveals precisely why absence of observed cooling is insufficient evidence for thermodynamic reversibility. A pressure reading, inlet temperature and appropriate volume data together supply a sign test independently of the small temperature slope used to estimate the Joule–Thomson coefficient.

An independent heat-leak estimate is particularly important near inversion, where a small parasitic heat flow can dominate the intrinsic temperature change.

Chapter 2 · Heat, work and the limits of a cycle

Track changes of internal energy around a gas cycle and derive the temperature bound on the efficiency of a reversible engine.

From first principles

Derive the reversible engine efficiency from two balances

A working substance in a cyclic engine returns to its initial state. Its internal energy is a state function, so the energy gained from a hot reservoir must equal the useful work plus the heat delivered to a cold reservoir. Let \(Q_h,Q_c\) be positive heat magnitudes and \(W\) positive work delivered by the engine. Energy conservation gives

\[W=Q_h-Q_c,\]
\[\eta=\frac{W}{Q_h}=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}.\]

To determine the smallest possible heat rejection, apply the second law. Reservoirs at constant absolute temperatures \(T_h>T_c\) lose entropy \(Q_h/T_h\) and gain entropy \(Q_c/T_c\) respectively. The engine itself returns to its original entropy. The total entropy production is therefore

\[S_{\rm gen}=\frac{Q_c}{T_c}-\frac{Q_h}{T_h}\ge0,\]
\[\eta\le1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.\]

A reversible cycle attains equality. Friction or heat transfer across finite temperature differences produces entropy and reduces efficiency at fixed reservoir temperatures. The ratio uses absolute temperatures because reservoir entropy changes are defined by reversible heat divided by thermodynamic temperature.

Derivation and applications

Derive the efficiency of an idealized four-stroke gas cycle

A compression ratio predicts an efficiency for a model cycle only after the heat-transfer paths are specified. Consider a fixed amount of ideal gas with constant molar heat capacities and \(\gamma=C_p/C_V>1\). In the Otto model the gas undergoes reversible adiabatic compression from state 1 to 2, heat addition at fixed volume from 2 to 3, reversible adiabatic expansion from 3 to 4, then heat rejection at fixed volume back to 1. Use positive magnitudes \(Q_h,Q_c\) for the absorbed and rejected heat.

For either adiabat the first law gives \(nC_VdT=-p\,dV=-nRT\,dV/V\). Separation and integration give \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}\). Introducing \(r=V_1/V_2=V_4/V_3>1\) sets both adiabatic temperature ratios,

\[\frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{T_3}{T_4}=r^{\gamma-1}.\]

The two fixed-volume steps do no boundary work. Their respective heats are internal energy changes, and cyclic energy conservation converts the heat ratio to efficiency,

\[Q_h=nC_V(T_3-T_2),\]
\[Q_c=nC_V(T_4-T_1),\]
\[\eta=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}.\]

Substitute \(T_3=r^{\gamma-1}T_4\) and \(T_2=r^{\gamma-1}T_1\). Both temperature differences contain the same factor \(T_4-T_1\), which cancels and leaves

\[\eta=1-r^{1-\gamma}.\]

The gas absorbs and rejects heat over intervals of temperature. Its efficiency therefore depends on the chosen paths even with reversible adiabatic strokes. At \(r=1\) the pressure-volume loop collapses and produces no net work. Increasing the compression ratio also raises the temperature reached before heat addition, which can invalidate a constant-\(\gamma\) model.

Worked example

Close a cycle with measured endpoint temperatures

For one mole with \(r=8.00\), \(\gamma=1.40\), \(T_1=300\,\mathrm K\) and \(T_3=1600\,\mathrm K\), the adiabatic factor is 2.297. Hence \(T_2=689\,\mathrm K\), \(T_4=696\,\mathrm K\), and \(C_V=R/0.40=20.8\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}\). The gas absorbs \(18.9\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and rejects \(8.24\,\mathrm{kJ}\), yielding \(10.7\,\mathrm{kJ}\) of work and efficiency \(0.565\).

Second application

Infer a compression ratio for a different working gas

A model monatomic gas has \(\gamma=5/3\). Achieving efficiency \(0.600\) requires \(r=(1-0.600)^{-1/(\gamma-1)}=3.95\). Starting at \(280\,\mathrm K\), its temperature at the end of compression is \(280/(1-0.600)=700\,\mathrm K\). A cycle whose hottest temperature were \(650\,\mathrm K\) could not realize the specified heat-addition stroke with this initial state.

OneSimulation laboratory · Separate the equilibrium temperature from the relaxation time

Put signs on a physical boundary

Enclose a gas inside a movable cylinder. When the gas expands, it can raise an external weight. When heated at fixed volume, it can increase its temperature while doing no boundary work. To fix signs, take heat \(Q\) into the gas as positive and work \(W\) done by the gas as positive. The first law is \(\Delta U=Q-W\). Internal energy is a state property; the other two quantities describe transfer during a process. A cyclic device returns to its initial state, so its net work equals its net heat input.

For a quasistatic expansion, a piston moving distance \(dx\) under external pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\) receives work \(p_{\rm ext}A\,dx=p_{\rm ext}dV\). In mechanical equilibrium \(p_{\rm ext}\) approaches the gas pressure, giving the usual area under a pressure–volume curve. A suddenly expanding gas is not generally uniform or in mechanical equilibrium. Extending the equilibrium pressure formula to an unconstrained free expansion would predict work when no piston or other boundary force actually receives it.

Follow an ideal gas along two paths

For a calorically ideal gas, \(dU=nC_{V,m}dT\) with constant molar heat capacity. Reversible isothermal expansion from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) therefore gives zero internal energy change and \(Q=W=nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\). Revise the process so that the gas is thermally insulated but still expands reversibly. Then \(nC_{V,m}dT=-p\,dV=-nRT\,dV/V\). Integrating and introducing \(\gamma=C_{p,m}/C_{V,m}=1+R/C_{V,m}\) gives \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant}\). Temperature now falls because the gas supplies work from its energy store.

The two paths can pass through a common starting state but they generally end at different pressures and temperatures for a given final volume. If one wants to compare work between identical endpoints one must specify a connecting step as well. This prevents a common misreading of a pressure–volume plot: an isotherm and an adiabat through the same point have different slopes and cannot serve as alternate paths to the same later point without additional processes.

Construct a reversible two-reservoir cycle

Let the gas expand isothermally at \(T_h\), then expand adiabatically down to \(T_c\), compress isothermally there and return adiabatically to the original state. If the first isotherm changes volume by a factor \(r\), the two adiabatic relations force the cold compression to change volume by the reciprocal factor. The hot heat input is \(Q_h=nRT_h\ln r\). The magnitude of heat expelled during cold compression is \(Q_c=nRT_c\ln r\). Thus

\[W=Q_h-Q_c,\qquad \eta=\frac W{Q_h}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.\]

The working gas provides one explicit construction. More generally a reversible cycle exchanges entropy \(Q_h/T_h\) with the hot reservoir and returns exactly that entropy to the cold one. For an irreversible cyclic engine between the same two reservoirs, the reservoir entropy change \(Q_c/T_c-Q_h/T_h\) is nonnegative. This gives the same expression as an upper bound on efficiency. The temperatures must be absolute and must refer to the reservoirs actually exchanging heat.

Test a proposed engine

An engine receives 1.50 kJ per cycle from a 450 K reservoir and rejects heat to a 300 K sink. Reversibility permits at most 0.500 kJ of useful work. A measured cycle delivering 0.360 kJ must reject 1.140 kJ by the first law. The working fluid’s entropy returns to its original value, while the two reservoirs together gain \(1140/300-1500/450=0.467\) J/K per cycle.

The energy and entropy balances answer different design questions. Energy accounting gives the amount of rejected heat. Entropy generation explains why some of the available work has been lost relative to the reversible limit. Raising the running frequency can increase power even at lower efficiency, since power depends on work per unit time. The reversible limit describes a thermodynamic bound, and achieving a useful power output additionally depends on finite rates of heat transfer and mechanical motion.

Extract work from two finite heat reservoirs

The usual Carnot limit assumes two fixed reservoir temperatures. Two finite bodies instead change temperature as a reversible engine transfers energy between them. Consider insulated hot and cold blocks with constant heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\). A cyclic engine operating between them can deliver work to an external mechanical store. The block temperatures vary slowly relative to internal equilibration, and the reversible engine draws and rejects each small amount of heat at its corresponding instantaneous block temperature.

If an infinitesimal amount of entropy passes through the engine reversibly, the hot block loses exactly the amount gained by the cold block. Integrating until both reach a common final temperature \(T_f\) yields

\[C_h\ln(T_f/T_{h,0})+C_c\ln(T_f/T_{c,0})=0,\qquad T_f=T_{h,0}^{C_h/(C_h+C_c)}T_{c,0}^{C_c/(C_h+C_c)}.\]

The logarithms contain temperature ratios, so the product expression is dimensionally a temperature. Once temperatures coincide a cyclic engine has no thermal difference left from which to extract work. Conservation of energy gives \(W_{\max}=C_h(T_{h,0}-T_f)+C_c(T_{c,0}-T_f)\). Any irreversible entropy production raises the attainable common temperature relative to the reversible result at the same initial conditions, leaving more energy in the blocks and less exported as work.

Compare a reversible engine with passive contact

Let \(C_h=C_c=1.00\) kJ/K, \(T_{h,0}=400\) K and \(T_{c,0}=300\) K. The reversible common temperature is the geometric mean \(\sqrt{400\times300}=346.41\) K. The engine receives 53.59 kJ from the hot block and transfers 46.41 kJ to the cold block; their difference is 7.18 kJ of work. The overall work fraction of extracted hot-side heat is 0.134. At the beginning the instantaneous reversible efficiency is \(1-300/400=0.250\), falling to zero at the end. Applying that initial efficiency throughout would overestimate the work.

Now put the same blocks directly in thermal contact without an engine. Energy conservation gives the arithmetic mean of 350 K and zero work. The positive entropy produced is \(1000\ln[350^2/(400\times300)]=20.6\) J/K. The final temperature exceeds the reversible result by 3.59 K for each block, precisely accounting for 7.18 kJ retained as thermal energy. In the limit of a very small initial temperature difference the geometric and arithmetic means coincide to first order. Recoverable work thus starts at second order in the initial difference even though the heat passed between the blocks is first order, explaining why small temperature contrasts are poor resources for mechanical work.

The arithmetic and geometric means also reveal how sensitive the possible work is to initial temperature calibration. For equal heat capacities, write initial temperatures as \(T_m+\delta\) and \(T_m-\delta\). The reversible common value is \(\sqrt{T_m^2-\delta^2}\simeq T_m-\delta^2/(2T_m)\). Multiplying the difference from the arithmetic mean by the combined heat capacity gives \(W_{\max}\simeq C\delta^2/T_m\). Thus a small absolute error in a temperature difference can have a doubled relative effect on the predicted maximum work. Measuring both blocks with matched instruments matters more here than obtaining many digits for the mean temperature alone.

Problem sheet 1 · State variables and energy conversion

Apply the local material response of Lecture 1 and the path-dependent energy balances of Lecture 2.

Correct a pressure thermometer

A sealed cell warms by 4.0 K and the pressure increases by 0.80 MPa. The vessel has expansion coefficient \(4.0\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and pressure compliance \(2.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹. The liquid has expansion coefficient \(2.0\times10^{-4}\) K⁻¹. Infer its compressibility and predict the pressure rise in a truly rigid cell.

Require the two volumes to coincide

The observed slope is \(2.0\times10^5\) Pa/K. Equality of sample and vessel volume changes gives \(\kappa_T=(\alpha-\alpha_v)/(dp/dT)-C_v^{\rm vessel}=6.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹. In a rigid cell the rise is \(4\alpha/\kappa_T=1.33\) MPa. Treating the original vessel as rigid would have given an apparent compressibility \(1.0\times10^{-9}\) Pa⁻¹, overstating the intrinsic response.

Compare isothermal and adiabatic expansion

One mole of monatomic ideal gas at 400 K expands reversibly to twice its initial volume. Find the final temperatures, work and heat for an isothermal path and a thermally insulated path. For which path is the final pressure lower?

Track work and internal energy

Isothermally \(T_f=400\) K and \(Q=W=RT\ln2=2.31\) kJ. Adiabatically \(\gamma=5/3\) gives \(T_f=400\times2^{-2/3}=252\) K. The work is the drop in internal energy, \(W=(3R/2)(400-252)=1.85\) kJ, with \(Q=0\). Both paths end at the same volume, so the colder adiabatic path also gives a lower final pressure. They are distinct final states, despite the same expansion ratio.

Diagnose a refrigerator claim

A cyclic refrigerator takes 600 J from a reservoir at 270 K and gives heat to a room at 300 K. Find the minimum work. A prototype claims to use 50 J per cycle. Test the claim by computing total reservoir entropy change.

Apply both laws to one full cycle

At reversible operation the equality \(Q_h/300=600/270\) gives \(Q_h=666.7\) J and minimum work \(Q_h-Q_c=66.7\) J. The claimed input gives \(Q_h=650\) J by energy conservation. Reservoir entropy would change by \(650/300-600/270=-0.0556\) J/K per cycle, incompatible with the two-reservoir cyclic description. A hidden heat source or an incorrect energy measurement would need to be included.

Test a calorimeter conversion near zero expansion

Chapter 3 · Thermodynamic potentials and phase boundaries

Select the appropriate potential for a controlled experiment and infer the slope of a coexistence line from latent heat and volume change.

From first principles

Derive a coexistence slope from equality of chemical potentials

At fixed temperature and pressure a closed single-component system lowers its Gibbs energy when matter moves into a phase with lower molar chemical potential. Transfer an infinitesimal amount \(dn\) from phase 1 to phase 2. Along the coexistence line, either direction is possible in equilibrium, so

\[dG=(\mu_2-\mu_1)\,dn=0,\]
\[\mu_1(T,p)=\mu_2(T,p).\]

Moving along the line must preserve that equality. The Gibbs differential for one mole gives \(d\mu=-s_m\,dT+v_m\,dp\), with molar entropy \(s_m\) and volume \(v_m\). Equating the differentials of the two phases leaves

\[(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})\,dp=(s_{m,2}-s_{m,1})\,dT .\]

The latent heat per mole for reversible conversion \(1\to2\) is \(L_m=T(s_{m,2}-s_{m,1})\). Therefore the slope is \(dp/dT=L_m/[T(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})]\) for a nonzero volume jump. Its sign depends on both latent heat and volume change. This relation requires measured or modeled properties of the two phases at the same coexistence point.

Derivation and applications

Calculate how a curved interface changes the equilibrium vapour pressure

A small liquid droplet in its own vapour reaches equilibrium at a different vapour pressure from a flat liquid surface. Consider a spherical droplet of radius \(r\) at uniform temperature \(T\), with surface tension \(\gamma\) independent of size. Let \(p_v\) and \(p_l\) be the exterior vapour and interior liquid pressures. A virtual change of radius changes area and volume by \(dA=8\pi r\,dr\) and \(dV=4\pi r^2dr\). Balancing the pressure work against the surface free energy gives

\[(p_l-p_v)dV=\gamma\,dA,\]
\[p_l=p_v+\frac{2\gamma}{r}.\]

Use a flat interface at the same temperature as a chemical-potential reference. Both phases there have pressure \(p_\infty\). The difference of their chemical potentials is zero for the flat reference and for the equilibrium droplet. Integrating \(d\mu=v_m\,dp\) along an isotherm for each phase then gives, for ideal vapour and nearly incompressible liquid of molar volume \(v_l\),

\[RT\ln(p_v/p_\infty)=v_l(p_l-p_\infty)=v_l(p_v-p_\infty)+\frac{2\gamma v_l}{r}.\]

For a dilute vapour the molar volume greatly exceeds the liquid value. When \(p_v-p_\infty\) is also small compared with the curvature-induced pressure jump, its contribution on the right is negligible. The Kelvin relation follows,

\[\ln(p_v/p_\infty)\simeq\frac{2\gamma v_l}{rRT}.\]

A convex liquid surface raises the required equilibrium vapour pressure. The result tends continuously to flat coexistence at large radius. At molecular radii the continuum description, including the assumption of a bulk surface tension, requires independent justification.

Worked example

Estimate the supersaturation over a droplet

For water-like material at \(298\,\mathrm K\) take \(\gamma=0.0720\,\mathrm{N/m}\), \(v_l=18.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3/mol}\), and \(r=20.0\,\mathrm{nm}\). The exponent is \(0.0523\), giving \(p_v/p_\infty=1.054\). The mechanical pressure jump is \(7.20\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Even with \(p_\infty=3.17\,\mathrm{kPa}\), the exterior pressure increase is only about \(170\,\mathrm{Pa}\), verifying the term dropped in obtaining the Kelvin relation.

Second application

A different liquid has \(\gamma=0.0250\,\mathrm{N/m}\) and \(v_l=100\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3/mol}\) at \(300\,\mathrm K\). A chamber stabilized at \(p_v/p_\infty=1.020\) matches radius \(r=2\gamma v_l/[RT\ln(1.020)]=101\,\mathrm{nm}\). A smaller droplet has a higher required vapour pressure and tends to evaporate in that chamber. A larger one tends to grow, so matching the pressure selects an unstable balance if the vapour pressure is held fixed.

Change which variables are controlled

Consider a closed simple compressible system that can exchange heat with a reservoir and work by moving a boundary. In equilibrium its fundamental energy differential for fixed amount is \(dU=T\,dS-p\,dV\). This expression makes entropy and volume natural independent variables, yet laboratory experiments more frequently control temperature and pressure. A change of thermodynamic potential exchanges one independent variable for its conjugate without losing any equilibrium information.

For the Helmholtz free energy \(F=U-TS\), differentiation gives \(dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV\). Its slope with respect to volume at fixed temperature is minus the pressure. For the Gibbs free energy \(G=U+pV-TS\), the differential is \(dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp\). At fixed temperature and pressure a spontaneous change in a closed sample with only pressure–volume work tends to lower its total \(G\). The external reservoir can exchange heat during that change, so a fall in sample energy by itself would not be the correct selection criterion.

Extract a measurable cross derivative

The entropy of a gas is harder to measure directly than its thermal expansion. Since \(G(T,p)\) is a state function with commuting mixed derivatives inside a smooth single phase, \(-(\partial S/\partial p)_T=(\partial V/\partial T)_p\). This is one Maxwell relation. It converts an entropy response to a derivative accessible from volume measurements. The equality applies to the same material and the same choice of fixed variables on both sides.

For one mole of ideal gas \((\partial V/\partial T)_p=R/p\). Compressing isothermally therefore changes its entropy by \(\Delta S=-R\ln(p_2/p_1)\). Doubling pressure gives \(-5.76\) J/(mol·K), in agreement with an independent reversible-heat calculation. The Maxwell relation obtains the state difference without requiring the actual compression process to be reversible. Only an equilibrium path used to evaluate it has to connect well-defined states.

Derive a coexistence-line slope

For one pure substance the molar Gibbs energy is the chemical potential. At two-phase equilibrium both phases must have the same chemical potential; otherwise transferring matter to the phase of lower value decreases total \(G\). Label the phases 1 and 2. Along their coexistence curve a small change in \(T\) and \(p\) changes their chemical potentials equally. Substituting \(d\mu=-s_m dT+v_m dp\) for each yields

\[\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{s_{m,2}-s_{m,1}}{v_{m,2}-v_{m,1}}=\frac{L_m}{T(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})}.\]

Here \(L_m\) is the molar latent heat for the transition from phase 1 to 2. The slope can be negative for melting when the liquid has smaller molar volume than the solid. For vaporisation the volume difference is usually positive and large at modest pressure. A pressure reading and a thermometer together can therefore constrain a latent heat through measurements of the slope and molar volumes.

Estimate a boiling temperature near atmospheric pressure

For vaporising water near 373 K use \(L_m=40.7\) kJ/mol, a vapour volume of 0.0306 m³/mol and a much smaller liquid volume. The coexistence slope is approximately \(40700/[373(0.0306)]=3.57\) kPa/K. An increase of pressure by 10 kPa therefore raises the boiling temperature by roughly 2.8 K over this limited interval. The dimensional check is J/(m³·K), equivalent to Pa/K.

Over a larger interval, approximate the vapour as ideal, neglect the liquid volume and treat \(L_m\) as constant. The slope becomes \(d\ln p/dT=L_m/(RT^2)\), giving \(\ln(p_2/p_1)=-(L_m/R)(1/T_2-1/T_1)\). This form clarifies why a straight line may appear on a logarithmic-pressure versus reciprocal-temperature plot. Curvature in measured data can carry information about temperature-dependent latent heat and departures from ideal-gas behaviour. Close to a critical point the assumptions behind the simple integrated expression cease to be reliable.

For a substance whose solid is less dense than its liquid, melting can have a negative coexistence slope even though the latent heat of melting is positive. In the Clapeyron relation the sign then comes from the volume difference. Increasing pressure at fixed temperature favours the phase taking less volume. This sign check connects the derivative calculation to an independently measurable density contrast.

Choose the equilibrium pressure inside a two-phase region

A nonmonotone model isotherm can predict several volumes at one pressure below the critical temperature. The middle segment with \((\partial p/\partial v)_T>0\) is mechanically unstable. Removing that segment alone does not identify the two coexisting phases. At the coexistence pressure \(p_*\) both endpoints have equal molar chemical potential. Write molar Gibbs energy as \(\mu=f+p_*v\), where \(f\) is molar Helmholtz energy on this isotherm. Since \(df=-p(v)\,dv\) at fixed temperature, equality of endpoint values implies

\[0=\mu_g-\mu_l=-\int_{v_l}^{v_g}p(v)\,dv+p_*(v_g-v_l).\]

Geometrically, the areas above and below the horizontal line \(p_*\) cancel. The same condition makes that line's negative slope the common tangent of \(f(v)\) at \(v_l\) and \(v_g\). Between these endpoint volumes the equilibrium material changes the relative amounts of liquid and gas at fixed pressure.

As a local model take \(p=p_0+A(x-x^3)\) with \(x=(v-v_0)/b\), \(A>0\), \(v_0=2.00\times10^{-4}\) and \(b=3.00\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol. At \(p=p_0\), the outer roots \(x=\pm1\) have negative slopes, and the excess pressure is odd in \(x\), verifying equal areas exactly. The stable molar volumes are \(1.70\times10^{-4}\) and \(2.30\times10^{-4}\) m³/mol. At average volume \(2.12\times10^{-4}\) m³/mol, conservation of volume gives a gas mole fraction \((2.12-1.70)/(2.30-1.70)=0.700\). The spinodals, where the original slope vanishes, instead occur at \(x=\pm1/\sqrt3\). They delimit local stability. They are not the coexistence volumes. This cubic is a local representation of the loop and should not be extrapolated to very large or negative volumes.

Why a phase can persist beyond the coexistence line

Equality of bulk chemical potentials determines coexistence, yet a sample can remain liquid below its equilibrium freezing temperature. Forming a small solid nucleus introduces an interface whose free-energy cost can initially outweigh the bulk driving force. Consider a spherical nucleus of radius \(r\) in a pure, uniformly undercooled liquid. Assume an isotropic solid–liquid interfacial energy \(\gamma>0\) and a positive bulk free-energy advantage per volume \(\Delta g_v\) for the solid. Ignore elastic accommodation, walls, impurities and curvature dependence of \(\gamma\).

The gain in stable bulk material is proportional to the volume while the cost is proportional to area. Relative to uniform liquid the work is

\[\Delta G(r)=4\pi r^2\gamma-\frac{4\pi}3r^3\Delta g_v .\]

Differentiating gives the nonzero stationary size \(r_*=2\gamma/\Delta g_v\). The second derivative there is negative, so the critical nucleus lies at a barrier rather than at a stable droplet size. Smaller nuclei reduce their free energy by shrinking, and larger ones reduce it by growing. Substituting the radius into the original expression gives \(\Delta G_*=16\pi\gamma^3/(3\Delta g_v^2)\). Near coexistence, the difference of the two bulk free energies changes as the entropy-of-fusion difference times undercooling. With latent heat per unit volume \(L_v\), use \(\Delta g_v\simeq L_v(T_m-T)/T_m\) for a small fractional undercooling.

Determine how an undercooling changes the barrier

For an illustrative melt take \(T_m=400\) K, \(T=380\) K, \(L_v=3.00\times10^8\) J/m³ and \(\gamma=0.0300\) J/m². The bulk drive is \(1.50\times10^7\) J/m³. Critical radius is \(4.00\) nm and the barrier is approximately \(2.01\times10^{-18}\) J. Since \(k_BT=5.25\times10^{-21}\) J at 380 K, this is roughly 383 thermal energy units, a formidable homogeneous nucleation barrier. This comparison alone is insufficient for an exact waiting time, which also requires a kinetic prefactor and the sample volume.

Doubling undercooling to 40 K doubles the approximate bulk drive, halves the critical radius to 2.00 nm and reduces the energy barrier by four. The nucleation probability per attempt can thus vary by many orders of magnitude while the equilibrium transition temperature remains fixed. As undercooling tends to zero, both critical radius and barrier diverge in this ideal capillarity model. A container wall or an impurity can reduce the interface area needed and initiate crystallization much earlier. This distinction between the coexistence curve and observed onset explains why reducing the cooling rate or changing surface preparation can change a freezing record.

The spherical calculation exposes a distinction between a critical nucleus and a nucleus for which the total formation work has already become negative. Set the formation work itself, rather than its derivative, to zero. Apart from zero radius this gives \(r=3\gamma/\Delta g_v\), larger than the critical radius by a factor of one and a half. A cluster just above the critical radius can still have positive total formation work relative to the uniform liquid, yet growth reduces its free energy. Using the zero-work radius as a kinetic threshold would therefore overestimate the size needed for downhill growth.

Chapter 4 · Counting states and defining temperature

Use a model of discrete energy sharing to derive thermodynamic temperature and distinguish a most probable configuration from one microscopic state.

From first principles

Derive thermal equilibrium by counting possible energy divisions

For an isolated system with a fixed energy range, assume all accessible microscopic states in that range have equal probability. Its multiplicity \(\Omega(E)\) counts those states. Entropy is \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\); the logarithm makes entropy additive when independent state counts multiply. Put systems A and B into weak thermal contact so they share a fixed total energy without exchanging particles or doing work. The number of states compatible with an energy \(E_A\) is

\[\Omega_{\rm total}(E_A)=\Omega_A(E_A)\Omega_B(E_{\rm total}-E_A).\]

The most probable energy division maximizes this product. For large systems with smooth entropy curves, take its logarithm and differentiate. A gain of A is a loss of B, so at an interior maximum

\[0=\frac{d(S_A+S_B)}{dE_A}=\left(\frac{\partial S_A}{\partial E_A}\right)_{V,N}-\left(\frac{\partial S_B}{\partial E_B}\right)_{V,N}.\]

The common quantity is the inverse thermodynamic temperature, \(1/T=(\partial S/\partial E)_{V,N}\). This definition relates a counting problem to the observed absence of net energy exchange at equilibrium. Positive heat capacities give a locally stable maximum and set the width of the energy-sharing fluctuations.

Derivation and applications

Find the width of the most probable energy division

An isolated pair of subsystems can have sharply defined total energy while the energy in each part fluctuates. Let \(E_A+E_B=E_{\rm tot}\), and assume weak coupling, rapid internal equilibration and equal statistical weights of accessible microstates. For two macroscopic subsystems with smooth entropies, the probability density of a particular division of energy is proportional to its state count,

\[P(E_A)\propto\exp\!\left(\frac{S_A(E_A)+S_B(E_{\rm tot}-E_A)}{k_B}\right).\]

At its interior maximum \(d(S_A+S_B)/dE_A=1/T_A-1/T_B=0\). The temperatures there equal \(T\). To examine nearby divisions, differentiate again. Each subsystem at fixed volume and particle number has \(d(1/T)/dE=-1/(T^2C)\), with heat capacity \(C\). Squaring the chain derivative of \(E_B\) makes the two curvature contributions add,

\[\frac{d^2(S_A+S_B)}{dE_A^2}=-\frac{1}{T^2}\left(\frac1{C_A}+\frac1{C_B}\right).\]

For positive heat capacities the maximum is stable. Write \(\delta E=E_A-E_A^*\) relative to the most probable energy. A quadratic expansion of the entropy about the maximum yields a Gaussian with variance

\[P(\delta E)\propto e^{-\delta E^2/(2\sigma_E^2)},\]
\[\sigma_E^2=k_BT^2\frac{C_A C_B}{C_A+C_B}.\]

The finite partner reduces the width because it must undergo exactly the opposite energy fluctuation. For \(C_B\gg C_A\) the canonical variance \(k_BT^2C_A\) is recovered. A temperature inferred from the subsystem energy has fluctuation scale \(\sigma_E/C_A\) when its heat capacity is locally constant. A macroscopic state's relative fluctuations shrink because heat capacity is extensive.

Worked example

Estimate fluctuations of a small calorimeter

At \(T=300\,\mathrm K\), let \(C_A=1.00\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J/K}\) and \(C_B=3.00\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J/K}\). The effective capacity in the variance is \(0.750\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J/K}\). With \(k_B=1.381\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}\), the root-mean-square energy fluctuation is \(9.65\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm J\), equivalent to \(0.965\,\mathrm{mK}\) of inferred temperature in A.

Second application

Determine whether the partner acts as a reservoir

Another apparatus measures an energy standard deviation for A of \(0.800\) times the value predicted for an infinite bath. The variance ratio is \(0.640=C_B/(C_A+C_B)\). Thus \(C_B/C_A=0.640/0.360=1.78\). For \(C_A=2.00\,\mathrm{nJ/K}\) this implies \(C_B=3.56\,\mathrm{nJ/K}\). Using the standard-deviation ratio directly in the variance formula would substantially overestimate the partner capacity.

OneSimulation laboratory · Separate the equilibrium temperature from the relaxation time

State exactly what is counted

Consider \(N\) independent distinguishable vibrational modes, all of angular frequency \(\omega\), with a common energy quantum \(\varepsilon=\hbar\omega\). Suppose they contain \(q\) excitation quanta in total. A microstate is an ordered list of occupation numbers \((q_1,\ldots,q_N)\) with nonnegative integers adding to \(q\). Giving two quanta to mode 1 differs from giving the same two quanta to mode 2, while exchanging two identical quanta within one mode creates no new microstate. An additive ground-state energy plays no role in the counting below.

Represent a distribution by writing \(q\) dots and separating the modes with \(N-1\) dividers. The locations of the dividers among all \(q+N-1\) symbols determine the occupations, including a zero when two dividers coincide in succession. The multiplicity is therefore \(\Omega(N,q)=\binom{q+N-1}{q}\). It counts the microscopic realisations of a specified macroscopic energy, not different possible values of that energy.

Allow subsystems to exchange energy

Put two such solids into weak thermal contact but isolate the pair from the environment. For a fixed division \(q_A\) and \(q_B=q-q_A\), weak coupling makes the joint multiplicity the product \(\Omega_A(q_A)\Omega_B(q_B)\). Under the microcanonical hypothesis of equal probabilities for accessible microstates of the pair, this product sets the probability weight for an observed division. The most probable division maximises the product, though for finite systems other divisions retain nonzero probability.

As a concrete small system take \(N_A=N_B=2\) and \(q=4\). Each solid has multiplicity \(q_i+1\). The weights for \(q_A=0,1,2,3,4\) are \(5,8,9,8,5\) with sum 35. Equal sharing is the most probable single division, yet it occurs only \(9/35=0.257\) of the time. Any one specific occupation list still has probability \(1/35\). The distinction between a microstate and a macrostate resolves the apparent tension between equal probabilities and a preferred energy division.

Obtain temperature from an entropy slope

Introduce \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\) so that independent multiplicities produce additive entropy. For macroscopic \(N\) and \(q\), use Stirling’s approximation \(\ln n!\simeq n\ln n-n\) and drop corrections small relative to \(N\). Writing \(U=q\varepsilon\) and holding \(N\) fixed gives

\[S/k_B\simeq(q+N)\ln(q+N)-q\ln q-N\ln N,\qquad \frac1T=\frac{k_B}{\varepsilon}\ln(1+N/q).\]

This derivative defines an equilibrium temperature from how quickly the number of ways to store energy grows. The entropy maximum of two systems in contact then requires equal inverse temperatures, even if the systems have different numbers of modes. Heat flows towards the subsystem gaining more entropy per added energy. The equilibrium criterion concerns equality of slopes; the two subsystems need not contain the same total energy.

Solving the derivative for energy per mode yields \(U/N=\varepsilon/[\exp(\varepsilon/k_BT)-1]\). At high temperature the exponential in the denominator expands to first order, giving energy near \(k_BT\) for one harmonic mode. At low temperature only a small fraction of modes carry excitations. The energy and heat capacity above their ground values are then strongly suppressed.

Connect the count with a thermometer

For a set of modes with \(\varepsilon/k_B=240\) K, an observed mean occupation \(q/N=0.50\) gives \(T=240/\ln 3=218\) K. Packing the same excitations into twice as many identical modes changes \(q/N\) to 0.25 and gives \(T=240/\ln5=149\) K. Equal quantities of excitation energy therefore need not indicate equal temperatures. The reservoir of available modes matters.

These estimates assume the modes have a common frequency and have explored accessible distributions sufficiently for the statistical description to apply. A newly driven single mode could contain nearly all the energy while a thermometer responds differently from the occupation-based estimate. As the sample size increases the spread of typical energy per mode narrows, but finite-time preparation and weak internal relaxation still determine whether equilibrium counting is an appropriate description of a measurement.

The entropy curvature also determines whether sharing energy is stable. Near the maximum, expand the combined entropy to second order in a small transfer between subsystems. A negative summed curvature makes the probability fall on either side of the most likely energy. For ordinary positive heat capacities, each entropy curvature is negative, so the two bodies return statistically towards equal temperature after a small redistribution.

A bounded energy spectrum reverses the entropy slope

An assembly of independent localized two-state units supplies a finite system in which the sign of temperature can be read directly from multiplicity. Take \(N\) units with low energy zero and high energy \(\varepsilon\). If \(n\) are excited, total energy is \(n\varepsilon\) and the multiplicity of that sharp-energy macrostate is \(\binom Nn\). It grows up to half occupancy and decreases when nearly all units are forced into the high state. This assumes the ensemble is internally equilibrated over the available states near its specified energy.

Use the entropy of a narrow energy shell \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\). Adding one excitation changes its entropy by \(k_B\ln[(N-n)/(n+1)]\). Dividing by the associated energy increment defines a finite-difference inverse temperature. For large \(N\) with neither level nearly empty, replacing \(n+1\) by \(n\) gives

\[\frac1T\simeq\frac{k_B}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{N-n}{n},\qquad \frac nN=\frac{1}{1+e^{\varepsilon/(k_BT)}} .\]

Ordinary positive temperatures have more units in the lower state. Approaching equal populations from below sends inverse temperature toward zero. Above half occupancy the entropy falls as energy increases, giving a negative shell temperature. Such an internally equilibrated population inversion requires a bounded spectrum and isolation from unbounded energy reservoirs on the observation timescale. It cannot describe an unrestricted equilibrium ideal gas with arbitrarily high translational energies.

Predict the direction of exchange

Consider \(N=10,000\) units with \(\varepsilon/k_B=120\) K. A preparation with 2500 excited units has \(T\simeq120/\ln3=109\) K. A preparation with 7500 excited units has \(T\simeq120/\ln(1/3)=-109\) K. Join the inverted system through weak energy exchange to a large positive-temperature reservoir at 300 K. If the system loses positive energy \(\delta E\) to the reservoir, the combined entropy change is \(\delta E(1/300-1/(-109))>0\). Net energy therefore leaves the inverted sample even though comparing the signed temperature numbers as ordinary magnitudes would suggest otherwise.

For \(\delta E=1.00\times10^{-20}\) J, the first-order change is about \(1.25\times10^{-22}\) J/K, or nine Boltzmann constants. This uses an exchange small relative to the original ensemble energy so that the temperature slopes stay nearly constant. At exactly half occupancy the inverse temperature is zero in the large-system limit. Pumping a system into an inverted population and allowing it to equilibrate internally are separate experimental steps. A measured majority of excited units alone is evidence for inversion; additional checks on internal energy exchange are needed before assigning a thermodynamic temperature to that preparation.

The preparation also shows why inverse temperature is often more convenient than temperature itself. Moving the upper-state fraction continuously from slightly less than one half to slightly more than one half sends temperature through positive and negative infinity, while inverse temperature crosses zero smoothly. A weak probe with the same small level spacing can test the slope through the relative probabilities of up and down transitions when equilibrated with the sample. A probe with unrestricted kinetic energy, in contrast, can drain the inversion before it becomes a useful thermometer. The compatibility of thermometer and sample therefore matters near a bounded-spectrum population inversion.

Chapter 5 · Partition functions and thermal fluctuations

Calculate the occupation, heat capacity and energy fluctuations of a two-level system from a single partition function.

From first principles

Obtain Boltzmann weights by counting the bath’s available states

Put a small system in weak thermal contact with a much larger bath. Treat the combined pair as isolated with energy \(E_{\rm tot}\). If the small system occupies a particular microstate \(i\) of energy \(E_i\), the bath must have energy \(E_{\rm tot}-E_i\). The probability of that microstate is proportional to the number of bath states compatible with the remaining energy,

\[P_i\propto\Omega_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_i)=\exp[S_{\rm bath}(E_{\rm tot}-E_i)/k_B].\]

A large bath changes temperature negligibly over the small system’s range of exchanged energies. Expand its entropy to first order, using \(\partial S_{\rm bath}/\partial E=1/T\); an arbitrary energy reference contributes only a common factor. Normalizing the resulting probabilities gives

\[P_i=\frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z},\]
\[Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j},\]
\[\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.\]

The sum called the partition function counts every microstate, so equal-energy states contribute separately. Differentiating it yields the mean energy \(U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z\) when the energy levels are temperature independent. A constant shift of all levels changes \(Z\) and \(U\) but leaves the normalized probabilities unchanged.

Derivation and applications

Measure a static response from spontaneous fluctuations

Consider a coordinate \(x\) that can exchange energy with a heat bath at fixed temperature \(T\). A weak constant force \(f\) changes its energy from \(H_0\) to \(H_f=H_0-fx\). This sign gives lower energy to positive displacement under a positive force. Assume that the accessible states themselves do not depend on the force and that the instrument allows enough time for the system to sample equilibrium. Write \(d\Gamma\) for a sum or integral over states and \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). Differentiation of the Boltzmann weight gives

\[Z(f)=\int e^{-\beta(H_0-fx)}d\Gamma,\]
\[\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial f}=\beta\langle x\rangle_f .\]

A second derivative acts on both the weighted numerator for the mean and its normalization. The quotient rule subtracts the square of the mean. Hence the static compliance, measured with temperature held fixed, obeys

\[\left(\frac{\partial\langle x\rangle_f}{\partial f}\right)_T=\beta\left(\langle x^2\rangle_f-\langle x\rangle_f^2\right).\]

For a harmonic confining potential \(H_0=kx^2/2\), with spring constant \(k>0\), completing the square makes the interpretation especially direct. The position distribution remains Gaussian and its centre shifts with the load,

\[\tfrac12kx^2-fx=\tfrac12 k(x-f/k)^2-\frac{f^2}{2k},\]
\[\langle x\rangle_f=\frac{f}{k},\]
\[\operatorname{var}x=\frac{k_BT}{k}.\]

The same stiffness controls response and equilibrium noise. For an anharmonic potential the general variance identity still gives the local response at the current force, although the compliance can change at larger loads. A finite exposure time can average away rapid motion, so measured positions must resolve the fluctuating coordinate.

Worked example

Calibrate a bead trap at thermal equilibrium

A bead suspended in a harmonic optical trap at \(295\,\mathrm K\) has a measured one-coordinate standard deviation of \(40.0\,\mathrm{nm}\). Its stiffness is \(k=k_BT/(40.0\times10^{-9})^2=2.55\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N/m}\). A steady force of \(0.100\,\mathrm{pN}\) should shift the mean by \(39.3\,\mathrm{nm}\). Resolving such a shift requires averaging over many statistically independent positions.

Second application

Predict noise from a measured mechanical compliance

A second device near a fixed bias force responds to an extra \(0.500\,\mathrm{pN}\) with a mean displacement of \(10.0\,\mathrm{nm}\). Its local compliance is \(2.00\times10^4\,\mathrm{m/N}\). At \(310\,\mathrm K\) the expected displacement standard deviation is \(\sqrt{k_BT(2.00\times10^4)}=9.25\,\mathrm{nm}\). This inference uses only local linear response and thermal equilibrium, so it remains applicable even if the device is nonlinear over a larger displacement range.

Put a small system in a heat bath

A spectroscopic probe may resolve two energy levels even when a bulk thermometer gives only one temperature. Consider many independent units with ground energy zero and excitation energy \(\Delta>0\). Each unit has two nondegenerate states and is weakly coupled to a much larger bath at temperature \(T\). Weak coupling lets us assign energies to the unit while still permitting energy exchange over the measurement time.

Under the canonical distribution the probability of a microstate \(j\) is \(p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). The factor \(Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\) normalises probabilities. This form comes from expanding the bath entropy about its energy before the exchange: the number of bath states compatible with system energy \(E_j\) is proportional to \(\exp[-E_j/(k_BT)]\). A bath with appreciable temperature change after one exchange would call for a finite-reservoir correction.

Differentiate the normalisation

The mean energy is \(\sum_j p_j E_j\). Differentiating \(\ln Z\) with respect to \(\beta\) inserts a factor \(-E_j\) in the weighted sum, giving \(\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\ln Z\). Differentiating a second time produces \(\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2\), which is the energy variance. Changing variables from \(\beta\) to \(T\) produces the fluctuation relation

\[C=\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T}=\frac{\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2}{k_BT^2}.\]

Hold fixed the external parameters that set the energy levels while taking this derivative. A change of magnetic field or volume can shift the levels and introduce an additional energy response. For independent identically prepared units, variances add. The standard deviation of total energy grows as the square root of the number of units, while its mean grows in proportion to that number. Relative fluctuations consequently shrink as a macroscopic sample is assembled.

Locate the heat-capacity peak

For one two-level unit, \(Z=1+e^{-x}\) with \(x=\Delta/(k_BT)\). Its excited-state probability is \(p=1/(1+e^x)\). Hence \(\langle E\rangle=\Delta p\), the variance is \(\Delta^2p(1-p)\) and the heat capacity is \(C/k_B=x^2e^x/(1+e^x)^2\). At low temperature the excited population is exponentially small and little energy can be absorbed by a small temperature increase. At high temperature the two populations approach one half, so changing temperature redistributes them only weakly. A maximum must occur between these limits.

Setting the derivative of \(\ln C\) with respect to \(x\) to zero gives \(2/x=\tanh(x/2)\). Its positive solution is about 2.40, placing the peak at \(T\simeq0.417\Delta/k_B\). This broadened heat-capacity feature is the Schottky anomaly of a two-level population. No collective phase transition is required to produce it. Degeneracy of either level would change the population ratio and modify the peak position.

Infer an energy scale and observe a fluctuation

Take \(\Delta=12.0\) meV, so \(\Delta/k_B\simeq139\) K. A peak near 58 K is expected. At \(T=139\) K one has \(x\simeq1\), \(p=0.269\), mean energy 3.23 meV and \(C\simeq0.197k_B\) per unit. For a single unit the standard deviation of the energy is \(\Delta\sqrt{p(1-p)}=5.32\) meV. The standard deviation exceeding the mean is possible because most single observations return the ground energy and a minority return the full excitation energy.

For a million independent units at the same temperature, the mean total energy scales up by a million and the standard deviation by a thousand. A calorimeter measuring the bulk value will therefore see a much smoother response than a single-unit spectroscopic probe. If measured fluctuations scale faster than this independent-unit prediction, correlations, common temperature fluctuations or instrumental noise deserve investigation before assigning a microscopic heat capacity.

For a noninteracting collection the total partition function factorizes into one factor per unit. Taking its logarithm converts that product into a sum, so mean energy and heat capacity become extensive. The variance of total energy also grows in proportion to the number of units when correlations vanish. The standard deviation grows only as the square root of that number, explaining how a large specimen can display a reproducible macroscopic energy while its microscopic constituents exchange energy continuously with the bath.

Predict particle-number fluctuations in an open subsystem

The canonical partition function sums over energies for a fixed particle number. A small region that exchanges both particles and energy with a large bath requires another weight. If the region gains energy \(E\) and \(N\) particles at fixed volume, the bath entropy changes to first order by \((-E+\mu N)/T\), from \(dS=(dU-\mu\,dN)/T\). With \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) and fixed reservoir chemical potential \(\mu\), sum over the states of each particle-number sector,

\[\Xi(T,V,\mu)=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}e^{\beta\mu N} Z_N(T,V),\qquad \langle N\rangle=\partial_{\beta\mu}\ln\Xi,\qquad \operatorname{var}N=\partial_{\beta\mu}^2\ln\Xi .\]

These derivatives are taken at fixed temperature and volume. Each derivative brings down a factor of \(N\) from the exponential, and differentiating the normalization supplies the subtraction of \(\langle N\rangle^2\). The grand potential is \(-k_BT\ln\Xi\). Number fluctuations therefore measure a thermodynamic response, \(\operatorname{var}N=k_BT(\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu)_{T,V}\).

Suppose 200 independent adsorption sites are exposed to a large particle reservoir. Each site can be empty with zero energy or contain one particle of energy \(\epsilon\). The single-site grand sum is \(1+e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}\) and the sample sum is its 200th power. Choose \(\mu-\epsilon=-k_BT\ln3\): occupancy probability is \(1/4\), mean population is 50, variance is \(200(1/4)(3/4)=37.5\) and standard deviation is 6.12 particles. Doubling the number of sites doubles mean and variance, while relative fluctuations fall by \(\sqrt2\). If the entire sample were sealed with exactly 50 particles, its total-number variance would instead be zero. A small observation window inside it could still fluctuate.

Count rotational states of a diatomic molecule

A partition sum can also show how a continuous-looking thermal degree of freedom switches on. Model a heteronuclear diatomic molecule as a rigid rotor of fixed bond length and moment of inertia \(I\) about an axis perpendicular to the bond. Ignore rotation about the bond, vibrational excitation, electronic excitation and intermolecular interactions. Its quantum levels are \(E_J=\hbar^2J(J+1)/(2I)=k_B\Theta_rJ(J+1)\), with degeneracy \(2J+1\) from the independent projections of angular momentum. Thus

\[Z_{\rm rot}=\sum_{J=0}^\infty(2J+1)e^{-\Theta_rJ(J+1)/T},\qquad \Theta_r=\frac{\hbar^2}{2Ik_B}.\]

At temperatures large compared with \(\Theta_r\), many \(J\) values contribute and the sum is well approximated by an integral. Substitute \(u=J(J+1)\), which supplies \(du=(2J+1)dJ\). The integral is then \(T/\Theta_r\). Differentiating \(\ln Z\) gives \(U_{\rm rot}\simeq k_BT\) and \(C_{\rm rot}\simeq k_B\) per molecule. The two classical quadratic rotational degrees of freedom each supply one half of this energy.

At very low temperature the first excited level lies \(2k_B\Theta_r\) above the ground state and has degeneracy three. Keeping just \(J=0,1\) gives \(Z\simeq1+3e^{-2\Theta_r/T}\). Its excitation probability is the second term divided by \(Z\). The mean rotational energy and heat capacity become exponentially small. If nuclei are identical, exchange symmetry can change allowed rotational populations through nuclear-spin weights; the deliberately heteronuclear model avoids that extra counting condition.

Infer when the first rotational line becomes populated

Use an illustrative rotor with \(\Theta_r=25.0\) K. At \(T=10.0\) K the weight of \(J=1\) is \(3e^{-5}=0.0202\) and that of \(J=2\) is \(5e^{-15}=1.53\times10^{-6}\). Neglecting the latter gives excited fraction \(0.0198\). The gap is \(50.0k_B\) K, giving mean energy per molecule approximately \(0.990k_B\) K. The two-level variance formula gives \(C_{\rm rot}/k_B=(50/10)^2p(1-p)=0.486\). The heat capacity is a derivative and can already be appreciable even when the excited-state fraction is only two percent.

At \(T=250\) K many rotational states contribute and the leading high-temperature sum gives \(Z\simeq10\) and molar rotational heat capacity near \(R\). The line positions also constrain structure. Inverting \(\Theta_r=\hbar^2/(2Ik_B)\) gives \(I=1.61\times10^{-47}\) kg m². With independently known reduced nuclear mass \(\mu=1.50\times10^{-27}\) kg, the effective bond length \(\sqrt{I/\mu}=0.104\) nm. Calorimetry and rotational spectroscopy can thus test the same moment of inertia through distinct observables.

Absolute line intensities provide an additional check on the rotor model. A line originating from the first excited rotational manifold samples its occupation, but a measured signal also contains transition matrix elements and instrument response. To compare with the 1.98-percent prediction, either calibrate those factors or compare the same line across temperatures. The gap can then be estimated from the slope of a logarithmic population ratio against inverse temperature, while the ratio's intercept contains the degeneracy. This separates a genuine error in moment of inertia from an omitted factor counting the allowed projection states.

At higher temperatures vibrational excitation can add to rotational calorimetry. An energy-resolved line measurement helps identify which internal degree of freedom has actually become populated.

Chapter 6 · Indistinguishable particles and quantum gases

Use single-state occupancy to derive Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions and estimate when classical gas statistics cease to apply.

From first principles

Derive the mean population of one quantum state

Consider a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\) exchanging particles and energy with a large reservoir at temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\). In a noninteracting gas, occupation \(n\) of this state changes \(E-\mu N\) by \(n(\epsilon-\mu)\). Its statistical weight is \(q^n\), where \(q=e^{-(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}\). For one specified state including its spin label, fermions allow occupations 0 and 1. Bosons allow every nonnegative integer,

\[Z_F=1+q,\]
\[Z_B=1+q+q^2+\cdots=\frac{1}{1-q}.\]

The Bose sum requires \(q<1\). Differentiating a weight with respect to \(q\) brings down its occupancy, so the normalized average is \(q\,\partial_q\ln Z\). Applying this to the two sums gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1},\]
\[\bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

For \(q\ll1\), both means approach \(q\); visits by additional particles to the same state are then rare. The difference between the distributions follows entirely from which occupation numbers the quantum statistics allow.

Derivation and applications

Derive the first exchange correction to ideal-gas pressure

Before quantum degeneracy becomes strong, exchange statistics already affect the relation between pressure and density. Consider noninteracting particles of mass \(m\) in a large three-dimensional box. All \(g\) internal states have the same energy and chemical potential. Introduce fugacity \(z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\), thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\), and a sign \(s=+1\) for bosons or \(-1\) for fermions. At small fugacity the occupation of a one-particle energy \(\epsilon\) is a geometric expansion,

\[\bar n(\epsilon)=z e^{-\beta\epsilon}+s z^2e^{-2\beta\epsilon}+O(z^3),\]
\[\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}.\]

Integrating the first term over momentum gives \(g/\lambda_T^3\) times \(z\). In the second term, doubling the inverse temperature narrows each Cartesian Gaussian by \(\sqrt2\). The density integral therefore gains a factor \(2^{-3/2}\). At fixed temperature the Gibbs–Duhem relation \(dp=n\,d\mu\) gives \(d[p/(k_BT)]=n\,dz/z\). Integration from the empty-gas limit gives the pressure series,

\[\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{3/2}}+O(z^3),\]
\[\frac{p\lambda_T^3}{g k_BT}=z+\frac{s z^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(z^3).\]

Experiments often fix density. Put \(y=n\lambda_T^3/g\) and invert the density series once to get \(z=y-s y^2/2^{3/2}+O(y^3)\). Substituting into the pressure eliminates the unmeasured chemical potential,

\[\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1-\frac{s}{2^{5/2}}\frac{n\lambda_T^3}{g}+O(y^2).\]

At fixed density exchange raises the pressure of fermions and lowers that of bosons. The magnitude grows on cooling because the thermal wavelength increases. Real interactions can also alter the pressure, and the truncated expansion requires small \(y\).

Worked example

Estimate a pressure excess for a dilute Fermi gas

A spin-balanced ideal Fermi gas has \(g=2\), \(n=2.00\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(\lambda_T=400\,\mathrm{nm}\). It has \(y=0.0640\) and \(p/(nk_BT)\simeq1.0113\). A measured classical pressure scale \(nk_BT=20.0\,\mathrm{pPa}\) would therefore acquire an exchange correction of about \(0.226\,\mathrm{pPa}\).

Second application

Recover density from a bosonic pressure deficit

A single-state Bose gas at known \(\lambda_T=500\,\mathrm{nm}\) gives \(p/(nk_BT)=0.9950\). To leading order the deficit fixes \(y=0.0050\times2^{5/2}=0.0283\). The inferred density is \(y/\lambda_T^3=2.26\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). At unchanged temperature halving this density halves the fractional pressure deficit, whereas the absolute pressure correction falls by a factor of four.

Decide which exchanges leave the state unchanged

An ideal classical gas can often be treated with Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. Cooling the gas or increasing its density eventually makes wave packets overlap appreciably. For indistinguishable quantum particles, merely assigning names to exchanged particles must not create extra states. In an ideal gas one can instead specify the occupation of each single-particle state. Identical fermions occupy a given quantum state at most once, including its spin label. Identical bosons can accumulate in the same state.

Use a reservoir that exchanges both energy and particles with the system. Its temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\) are fixed. A microstate with energy \(E\) and particle number \(N\) has grand-canonical weight proportional to \(\exp[-\beta(E-\mu N)]\). This follows by applying the thermodynamic entropy differential to a reservoir that loses both \(E\) and \(N\). The chemical potential sets the mean particle number through these statistical weights.

Sum the allowed occupancies of one state

For a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\), define \(z_\epsilon=\exp[-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)]\). An occupancy of \(n\) contributes weight \(z_\epsilon^n\). For fermions the single-state grand partition sum is \(1+z_\epsilon\), so the mean occupancy is \(z_\epsilon/(1+z_\epsilon)\). For bosons the sum runs over all nonnegative integers and equals \(1/(1-z_\epsilon)\), provided \(z_\epsilon<1\). Differentiating the logarithm of each sum with respect to \(\ln z_\epsilon\) gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad \bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.\]

These expressions describe one state, not the total population within an energy interval. To get the latter, multiply by the density of states and integrate. If the exponential is large then both occupancies reduce to \(e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\). The upper sign suppresses double occupancy while the lower sign enhances occupation near the lowest available energy.

Count the occupied sphere of a cold electron gas

In a large cubic volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries, allowed wave vectors fill reciprocal space with one state per volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) for each spin. At zero temperature the noninteracting electron gas fills all states inside a sphere of radius \(k_F\) and leaves higher ones empty. Including two spin states per wave vector gives \(N=2V(4\pi k_F^3/3)/(2\pi)^3\), so \(k_F=(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}\). For electrons of mass \(m_e\), the Fermi energy is \(\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m_e)\).

With density \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(k_F=1.33\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\simeq6.77\) eV. The associated temperature \(T_F=E_F/k_B\) is approximately \(7.9\times10^4\) K. At room temperature \(k_BT\) is small compared with this energy, so only a narrow region around the Fermi surface changes occupation noticeably. A classical equipartition estimate of the electron contribution to heat capacity would ignore this restriction.

Test the dilute limit with a cold atomic gas

A useful scale for translational wave packets is the thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\). For a single internal state the dimensionless product \(n\lambda_T^3\) compares the volume per particle with the thermal wave volume. Small values support the dilute classical approximation. Near unity the occupancy of low-energy states can become appreciable and the statistics of indistinguishability matter.

For particles of mass \(6.6\times10^{-27}\) kg at 1.0 K the thermal wavelength is approximately 0.88 nm. At density \(10^{25}\) m⁻³, \(n\lambda_T^3\simeq0.0068\). Reducing temperature to 0.040 K at unchanged density multiplies that product by \(25^{3/2}=125\), giving about 0.85. This is a degeneracy estimate, not a prediction of the phase of a real interacting gas. Once quantum statistics matter, particle interactions, dimensionality and confinement can also affect which phase the experiment actually finds.

Internal degeneracy enters these comparisons through the density per available internal state. Suppose the same total particle density is spread equally across two spin states with identical spatial dispersion. Each can fill the same momentum levels independently. In a cold ideal Fermi gas the Fermi wavevector at fixed total density is then smaller than in a fully spin-polarized gas. A measured pressure change after altering spin populations can therefore contain a statistical contribution even if the mass, volume and total particle count remain fixed.

Determine the capacity of excited Bose states

Ideal bosons can accumulate macroscopically in one spatial state. Consider a uniform three-dimensional noninteracting gas of identical spinless particles with fixed particle number in a large box. Set the lowest energy to zero. The excited-state occupation at energy \(\epsilon\) is \(1/[\exp((\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT))-1]\) and requires \(\mu\le0\). In the thermodynamic limit, counting momentum states in a sphere gives an excited-state density per volume proportional to \(\sqrt\epsilon\). Consequently, as the density rises, taking \(\mu\) toward zero from below can increase the excited population only up to a finite value.

Explicitly integrate \(d^3p/(2\pi\hbar)^3\) over positive energies \(\epsilon=p^2/(2m)\). At \(\mu=0\), setting \(x=\epsilon/(k_BT)\) reduces the integral to \(\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}/(e^x-1)dx=\Gamma(3/2)\zeta(3/2)\). The result is

\[n_{\rm ex,max}=\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\lambda_T^3},\qquad \lambda_T=\frac h{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}},\qquad \zeta(3/2)=2.6124\ldots .\]

Below the temperature where this capacity equals the total density, additional particles occupy the lowest state. Solving gives \(T_c=(2\pi\hbar^2/mk_B)[n/\zeta(3/2)]^{2/3}\) and \(N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^{3/2}\). The state count explains why a high phase-space density matters. Reaching this regime does not require individual particles to lose their identity at a sharply defined minimum separation.

Estimate the onset temperature in a box

Take \(n=1.00\times10^{20}\) m⁻³ and particle mass \(1.44\times10^{-25}\) kg. At the ideal transition the thermal wavelength is \((2.6124/n)^{1/3}=297\) nm. Inverting its definition gives \(T_c=h^2/(2\pi m k_B\lambda_T^2)=0.398\) μK. At \(T=0.250\) μK, the excited fraction is \((0.250/0.398)^{3/2}=0.498\) and the condensate fraction is approximately 0.502. Doubling the density raises \(T_c\) by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\) at fixed particle mass.

The predicted fraction tends to zero continuously as \(T\) approaches \(T_c\) from below, and tends to one as temperature approaches zero for the ideal gas. A harmonic trapping potential has a different density of states and hence a different temperature exponent from a uniform box. Finite size smooths the mathematical onset; interactions and inaccurate thermometry can shift its inferred location. Comparing the number in the narrow lowest-momentum component with the thermal-cloud population requires that the analysis resolve these two contributions rather than simply count all particles near the centre of a spatial image.

The dimensionality of the gas is as essential as Bose exchange itself. In a uniform two-dimensional ideal box the single-particle density of states tends to a constant at low energy. At chemical potential approaching zero, Bose occupation varies as \(k_BT/\epsilon\) there. Its energy integral consequently diverges logarithmically rather than approaching a finite excited-state capacity. The three-dimensional derivation of a nonzero transition temperature cannot then be carried across unchanged. A finite box, a confining trap or interparticle interactions can all alter the observed low-energy behaviour and must be identified when comparing different experimental geometries.

Repeat the counting in a harmonic trap

A harmonic trap changes the number of available excited states even for particles with the same mass and statistics. For three oscillator frequencies \(\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z\), measure energy above the lowest level and suppose \(k_BT\) greatly exceeds each \(\hbar\omega_i\). The quantum labels \(n_i\) are nonnegative integers with energy \(\sum_i\hbar\omega_i n_i\). The count below energy \(E\) is approximately the volume of the positive-coordinate tetrahedron bounded by this plane, namely \(E^3/(6\hbar^3\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)\). Differentiating gives an energy density of states proportional to \(E^2\).

At the onset of condensation integrate this density against \(1/(e^{E/k_BT}-1)\). The identity \(\int_0^\infty x^2/(e^x-1)\,dx=2\zeta(3)\) then gives

\[N_{\rm ex,max}=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar\omega}\right)^3,\qquad \bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}.\]

For \(N=1.00\times10^5\) particles in an isotropic 100 Hz trap, \(\hbar\bar\omega/k_B=4.80\) nK and \([N/\zeta(3)]^{1/3}=43.7\), so the predicted transition temperature is \(0.210\) μK. At \(0.150\) μK the condensate fraction is \(1-(0.150/0.210)^3=0.636\). An analysis using the uniform-box exponent at the same temperature ratio would instead give 0.396. The difference comes entirely from the confinement-dependent state count.

Reducing all three trap frequencies at fixed particle number lowers the onset temperature in direct proportion. This differs from enlarging a uniform box at fixed mean density, which leaves its bulk critical temperature unchanged. Specifying how particle number, volume and external confinement are controlled is therefore essential when interpreting a measured condensate fraction.

Problem sheet 3 · Populations and quantum statistics

A level with two ways to be excited

A unit has a nondegenerate ground state and two excited states of identical energy \(\Delta\). At some temperature the combined excited population is one half. Infer \(\Delta/(k_BT)\), the energy variance and the heat capacity per unit at that temperature.

Distinguish a level’s degeneracy from one state’s probability

The partition function is \(1+2e^{-x}\) with \(x=\Delta/(k_BT)\). Half occupation of the excited level group requires \(2e^{-x}=1\), so \(x=\ln2\). Individual excited states each have probability one quarter. Energy has two possible values with equal probabilities, hence variance \(\Delta^2/4\). The fluctuation relation gives \(C/k_B=(\ln2)^2/4=0.120\). The degeneracy matters even though the two excited states carry the same energy.

Decide whether a dilute formula suffices

For a particular state, \((\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)=\ln 5\). Compute its Bose, Fermi and dilute-limit occupancies. Repeat for \(\ln 101\) and decide whether the dilute estimate is accurate to 2% relative to both exact values.

Sum the single-state weights

For the first state, Bose occupancy is \(1/(5-1)=0.25\), Fermi occupancy \(1/(5+1)=0.1667\) and the dilute approximation \(1/5=0.20\). For the second the corresponding numbers are \(1/100\), \(1/102\) and \(1/101\). The dilute estimate differs by \(1/101=0.99\%\) relative to either exact result. Statistics matters when actual occupation per state is appreciable, not merely because particles are cold by an absolute temperature scale.

Compress an ideal electron gas

At zero temperature a spin-½ electron gas has Fermi energy 5.0 eV. Compress it to half its volume at fixed particle number. Find the changes in \(k_F\), Fermi speed and Fermi energy. If the spin degeneracy could instead be reduced from two to one at the original volume, which of those scalings would repeat?

Recount the available momentum states

State counting gives \(N/V=gk_F^3/(6\pi^2)\) for degeneracy \(g\). Doubling density scales \(k_F\) by \(2^{1/3}=1.260\). Since \(v_F=\hbar k_F/m\), it gains the same factor. The energy grows by \(2^{2/3}=1.587\) to 7.94 eV. Removing one spin species at fixed density gives the same factors in this ideal counting exercise. Infer a chemical potential difference from a fluctuating surface

A surface has 500 independent single-occupancy sites at 300 K. Its mean population is 400. Infer \(\mu-\epsilon\) and the predicted standard deviation. How would the variance change if the reservoir were adjusted to half occupation?

Invert the single-site probability

With \(p=0.800\), the odds \(p/(1-p)=4=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}\). Thus \(\mu-\epsilon=k_BT\ln4=35.8\) meV. The population variance is \(500(0.8)(0.2)=80\), giving standard deviation 8.94 particles. At half occupancy the variance rises to 125 despite a smaller mean. Correlated occupations or a finite reservoir can change the relation between mean and variance.

Chapter 7 · Pressure and effusion from molecular motion

Use velocity distributions to predict gas pressure and determine why particles passing through a pinhole have a different average energy.

From first principles

Count the particles that can reach a small opening

A small hole in a vessel of dilute equilibrium gas samples molecules moving toward its plane. Let \(n\) be number density, let \(v_x>0\) point outward, and let \(f_x(v_x)\) be the normalized distribution of this velocity component. In a short time \(dt\), only molecules starting within a layer of thickness \(v_xdt\) can cross. Its volume is hole area \(A\) times that thickness. Summing over positive velocities gives the one-way number flux,

\[J=\frac{dN}{A\,dt}=n\int_0^\infty v_x f_x(v_x)\,dv_x .\]

At classical thermal equilibrium the component distribution is Gaussian with variance \(k_BT/m\). Substitution and integration using the derivative of the exponential give

\[J=n\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}}\int_0^\infty v_x e^{-mv_x^2/(2k_BT)}\,dv_x=n\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m}} .\]

This is the outgoing flux for an opening small compared with a mean free path and thin enough to avoid significant internal passage effects. Any gas outside can generate a reverse flux. The velocity factor in the integral explains why faster molecules are overrepresented among those leaving.

Derivation and applications

Predict how an effusing gas mixture changes composition

A particle's contribution to the number flux through a small hole increases with its velocity normal to the opening. In a mixture at a common temperature this biases the escaping gas toward lighter species. Consider a rigid vessel of volume \(V\), with hole area \(A\) much smaller than the wall area and hole diameter smaller than the molecular mean free path. Take the exterior pressure to be negligible. The walls maintain temperature \(T\), and the reservoir re-equilibrates much faster than it empties. Thus the remaining species \(i\) has spatially uniform density \(N_i/V\) and a Maxwellian velocity distribution.

\[-\dot N_i=A\frac{N_i}{V}\int_0^\infty v_x\sqrt{\frac{m_i}{2\pi k_BT}}e^{-m_iv_x^2/(2k_BT)}dv_x=\frac{A N_i}{V}\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m_i}} .\]

The minus sign assigns positive outgoing flux to a decreasing inventory. Each species therefore has a constant decay rate in time under the isothermal assumption. Its time constant and its abundance ratio relative to another species are

\[\tau_i=\frac{V}{A}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi m_i}{k_BT}},\]
\[\frac{N_i(t)}{N_j(t)}=\frac{N_i(0)}{N_j(0)}e^{-t(1/\tau_i-1/\tau_j)} .\]

At any instant the outgoing number ratio has a further velocity weighting compared with the gas left in the vessel,

\[\frac{|\dot N_i|}{|\dot N_j|}=\frac{N_i}{N_j}\sqrt{\frac{m_j}{m_i}}.\]

The ratio of molar masses may be used in this square root since the conversion to mass per particle cancels. A sample collected over a long time mixes successively different outlet compositions. A thick tube or a hole large enough for collective flow requires a different flow model.

Worked example

Compare the outlet and reservoir compositions

Start with equal numbers of helium and neon atoms, taking molar masses 4.00 and \(20.0\,\mathrm{g/mol}\). Initially the outlet helium-to-neon number ratio is \(\sqrt5=2.24\), giving a helium fraction \(2.24/(1+2.24)=0.691\). The initial reservoir helium fraction is \(0.500\). A detector reporting mass flow instead of particle count would multiply each contribution by its corresponding particle mass.

Second application

Recover a heavy-gas residence time from a light tracer

In a separate chamber a helium tracer signal halves in \(120\,\mathrm s\). For argon of molar mass \(40.0\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) the predicted half-life is \(120\sqrt{40.0/4.00}=379\,\mathrm s\). After \(240\,\mathrm s\), helium retains \(0.250\) of its initial number and argon retains \(2^{-240/379}=0.645\). With equal starting numbers the residual helium fraction is \(0.250/(0.250+0.645)=0.279\), showing the preferential depletion accumulated over time.

Pressure counts momentum flux

Consider a dilute gas at equilibrium with number density \(n\), molecular mass \(m\) and no mean flow. Molecular diameters are small compared with intermolecular spacing. At a stationary wall an elastic specular collision reverses the normal component of velocity, transferring \(2mv_x\) to the wall when \(v_x>0\). In time \(dt\) only molecules within a slab of thickness \(v_xdt\) can reach it. Thus fast molecules strike the wall more frequently even at the same concentration.

With normalized velocity density \(f(\mathbf v)\) the pressure is \(2nm\int_{v_x>0}v_x^2 f\,d^3v\). Reflection symmetry converts this to \(nm\langle v_x^2\rangle\). Isotropy makes each Cartesian variance a third of total mean square speed, giving \(p=nm\langle v^2\rangle/3\). Combining with the ideal gas equation gives \(\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle=3k_BT/2\). Internal rotations and vibrations can store energy but do not directly carry translational momentum to the wall in this model.

Count particles crossing a hole

A small opening in a thin wall allows the crossing molecules to escape. Its diameter must be small compared with the mean free path, and the gas on the receiving side must be sufficiently dilute to neglect backflow. The outgoing flux per unit area is \(J=n\int_{v_x>0}v_x f\,d^3v\). The positive-half integral of the Maxwellian normal component is \(\sqrt{k_BT/(2\pi m)}\), equivalently

\[J=n\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m}}=\frac{n\overline v}{4},\qquad \overline v=\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}} .\]

The factor of one quarter combines the half of velocity directions facing the hole with their angular projection. The emerging speed distribution is weighted by one extra power of speed relative to the equilibrium speed distribution, since faster particles cross the hole more often. For a Maxwell gas it is proportional to \(v^3e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}\). Dividing its energy-weighted integral by its normalization gives mean translational energy \(2k_BT\) per escaping molecule. A thermally isolated remaining gas can consequently cool as it leaks.

Estimate a molecular leak

Nitrogen of molecular mass \(4.65\times10^{-26}\) kg is at \(300\) K and pressure \(1.00\) Pa. The number density is \(p/(k_BT)=2.41\times10^{20}\) m⁻³ and mean speed is \(476\) m/s. A circular hole of diameter \(20.0\) μm has area \(3.14\times10^{-10}\) m². The emission rate \(JA\) is approximately \(9.0\times10^{12}\) molecules/s. This large count corresponds to only \(4.2\times10^{-13}\) kg/s. The small-hole condition must be checked independently using the collision mean free path.

At equal pressures and temperatures two pure gases have the same number density. Their effusion-rate ratio therefore depends only on inverse square root of molecular mass. If back pressure develops, apply the flux equation separately to both sides and subtract opposing fluxes. A pressure difference across a wide tube requires a flowing-gas model instead.

A pressure gauge attached to the receiving chamber reports a quantity affected by the pumping speed as well as by the leak. Weighing a collected condensate, measuring the source pressure decay in a known constant-temperature volume, or calibrating the pump independently can provide an absolute flux determination. Each method tests a different piece of the chain from measured pressure to inferred molecular rate.

A thermally isolated effusing gas cools as it loses particles

The speed distribution in an escaping beam differs from the distribution in its source. This selective loss can feed back on the source temperature. Consider a fixed, insulated vessel of volume \(V\) containing a dilute monatomic gas. A small aperture of area \(A\) lets molecules escape into vacuum. Assume collisions in the vessel establish a Maxwellian temperature much faster than particles leave, the aperture remains in the free-molecular regime, and thermal exchange with the vessel walls is negligible. These assumptions separate rapid internal equilibration from the slow evolution of temperature and particle number.

The gas stores \(U=3Nk_BT/2\), while the mean energy of each escaping molecule is \(2k_BT\). During a small change \(dN<0\), the internal energy change is therefore \(dU=2k_BT\,dN\). Expanding the left side and collecting terms gives \(dT/T=dN/(3N)\). The resulting temperature law is \(T/T_0=(N/N_0)^{1/3}\). It predicts cooling because the outgoing beam carries more energy per particle than the source average, despite the absence of externally extracted mechanical work.

The effusive rate is \(dN/dt=-(A/V)N\sqrt{k_BT/(2\pi m)}\). Define \(\tau_0=V/[A\sqrt{k_BT_0/(2\pi m)}]\). Substitution of the temperature law and integration give

\[\frac{N}{N_0}=\left(1+\frac{t}{6\tau_0}\right)^{-6},\qquad \frac{T}{T_0}=\left(1+\frac{t}{6\tau_0}\right)^{-2}.\]

A fixed-temperature reservoir would instead exhibit a simple exponential decay in particle number. The initial logarithmic loss slopes agree, but cooling changes the later curve.

Predict pressure at half the initial gas inventory

Take argon of mass \(6.63\times10^{-26}\) kg initially at 300 K in a 1.00 litre vessel. A small hole of area \(1.00\times10^{-8}\) m² gives \(\sqrt{k_BT_0/(2\pi m)}=99.7\) m/s and \(\tau_0=1003\) s. To reach \(N/N_0=1/2\) requires \(t=6\tau_0(2^{1/6}-1)=737\) s. The gas temperature is then \(300\times2^{-1/3}=238\) K. Its pressure relative to the starting value is \((N/N_0)(T/T_0)=0.397\) at fixed volume.

If a wall heater maintained 300 K instead, pressure at half inventory would be one half of its original value, reached after \(\tau_0\ln2=695\) s. Monitoring both pressure and temperature can distinguish these cases. In practice chamber walls can have far greater heat capacity than a rarefied gas and reheat it rapidly. The isolated-gas prediction is therefore a limiting case that an experiment must establish, rather than something guaranteed merely by insulating the outer vessel.

The cooling law can be tested even if the absolute aperture area is uncertain. Plot \(\ln(T/T_0)\) against \(\ln(N/N_0)\) using simultaneous temperature and inventory records. The predicted slope is one third and does not contain the hole area, particle mass or vessel volume. The initial time derivative, by contrast, depends on all three. A correct logarithmic slope but an incorrect time constant therefore points toward an aperture calibration or transmission probability issue. A changing slope suggests thermal leakage, additional molecular energy stores or loss of internal equilibration during the measurement.

A seal or valve letting room-temperature gas enter the chamber also violates the assumed one-way balance and changes both logarithmic slopes.

Chapter 8 · Diffusion from a random walk

Derive a transport coefficient from a microscopic model and separate a diffusion length from a relaxation time in a finite container.

From first principles

Recover the diffusion equation by counting random steps

Consider independent particles on a line with sites spaced by \(\ell\). Every time interval \(\tau\), each particle takes one step left or right with equal probability. With no creation, loss or imposed drift, the expected concentration \(c\) at a site after a step equals the average of the earlier concentrations at its two neighbours,

\[c(x,t+\tau)=\tfrac12 c(x-\ell,t)+\tfrac12 c(x+\ell,t).\]

For a concentration changing slowly over one site and one step, expand both sides in Taylor series. The zeroth-order terms cancel and the two first spatial derivatives have opposite signs. The leading surviving change gives

\[\tau\,\partial_t c\simeq\frac{\ell^2}{2}\partial_x^2c,\]
\[D=\frac{\ell^2}{2\tau}.\]

The resulting diffusion equation is \(\partial_tc=D\,\partial_x^2c\). Its coefficient has units of length squared per time. A constant concentration has zero curvature, so the mean concentration stays fixed even while individual particles keep moving. Unequal step probabilities would leave a first-derivative drift term; the model assumes the microscopic motion has no preferred direction.

Derivation and applications

Derive a penetration depth for periodically driven diffusion

A periodically varying concentration at a surface penetrates a diffusive medium with a measurable delay and amplitude loss. Treat the medium as a uniform half-space \(x>0\) with constant diffusivity \(D\), no advection and no reaction. At \(x=0\) impose \(c=c_0+C\cos(\omega t)\) with \(C<c_0\). Far inside the sample the perturbation vanishes. Wait until the start-up transient has decayed at the observation positions. The diffusion equation is linear, so one can look for the real part of a single complex harmonic with a spatial attenuation factor,

\[c(x,t)-c_0=\operatorname{Re}\!\left[C e^{i\omega t-qx}\right],\]
\[i\omega=Dq^2 .\]

There are two complex square roots. The condition at large \(x\) selects the root with positive real part. Its real and imaginary parts have the same magnitude,

\[q=(1+i)\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{2D}}=\frac{1+i}{\delta},\]
\[\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2D}{\omega}} .\]

Taking the real part gives the decaying oscillation and directly connects two independent measurements to the same transport coefficient,

\[c-c_0=C e^{-x/\delta}\cos(\omega t-x/\delta),\]
\[\ln\!\frac{C(0)}{C(x)}=\omega\Delta t=\frac{x}{\delta}.\]

Here \(\Delta t\) is the delay of the corresponding peak, with the phase unwrapped across depth. Diffusion causes strong attenuation over a full phase cycle, so the apparent travel of a peak does not imply rigid translation of a material parcel. A reflecting far boundary would change this result once the sample thickness becomes comparable to \(\delta\).

Worked example

Determine diffusivity from an amplitude measurement

A surface concentration oscillates with period \(100\,\mathrm s\). At \(200\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) depth its amplitude is \(0.368\) times the boundary amplitude. This gives \(\delta\simeq200\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), hence \(D=\pi\delta^2/(100\,\mathrm s)=1.26\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). The model also predicts a lag of one radian, corresponding to \(100/(2\pi)=15.9\,\mathrm s\).

Second application

Check a transport coefficient with a phase measurement

For a second tracer the forcing period is \(80.0\,\mathrm s\). A peak at \(150\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) depth occurs \(10.0\,\mathrm s\) after the corresponding surface maximum. Its phase lag is \(\pi/4\), giving \(\delta=191\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) and \(D=1.43\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). The independent amplitude prediction is \(e^{-\pi/4}=0.456\) of the surface signal. A calibrated amplitude far from this fraction would challenge the assumed diffusion-only dynamics.

A spreading cloud without a mean velocity

Release a narrow band of neutral tracer particles into a stationary uniform medium. Repeated collisions erase velocity memory. At times long compared with the collision time, labelled particles can spread while the mean tracer velocity remains zero. Start with an ideal symmetric random walk on a line. During a time step \(\tau\) every particle moves a distance \(\ell\) left or right with equal probabilities, independently of previous steps.

The concentration in cell \(x\) after a step is the mean of the two neighbouring concentrations before that step. Expanding the left side in \(\tau\) and the right side in \(\ell\) cancels all odd spatial derivatives, leaving

\[\partial_t c=D\,\partial_x^2c,\qquad D=\ell^2/(2\tau).\]

After \(N=t/\tau\) independent steps the variance is \(N\ell^2=2Dt\), confirming the same coefficient without a continuum expansion. The exact prefactor depends on which microscopic step model is used. In an isotropic gas the directional velocity correlations and distribution of free-flight lengths matter; an order-of-magnitude estimate is \(D\sim\overline v\ell_{\rm mfp}/3\). Substituting a fixed one-dimensional step length into this three-dimensional estimate would mix incompatible models.

Why a gradient drives current

Consider the cell boundary between \(x\) and \(x+\ell\). The two crossing populations are proportional to the concentrations on their respective sides. Outgoing minus incoming probability current is therefore proportional to \(c(x)-c(x+\ell)\). Taylor expansion gives Fick's law \(j=-D\partial_xc\). Local conservation \(\partial_tc=-\partial_xj\) recovers diffusion for constant \(D\) and remains useful when the transport coefficient varies in space. The continuum model assumes concentration changes little over one microscopic step.

In an unbounded domain the response to a narrow initial band is Gaussian at late times with variance increasing by \(2Dt\). In a finite closed channel the final concentration is instead uniform. Reflecting ends impose zero gradient, so a prepared concentration modulation \(c(x,0)=c_0+a\cos(\pi x/L)\) relaxes through a single amplitude \(a(t)\). Substitution into diffusion gives \(a(t)=a(0)e^{-D\pi^2t/L^2}\). Spatial wavelength matters quadratically because a broad concentration feature supplies only a weak gradient for the mass that must move.

Measure a coefficient two ways

Suppose a dyed band in water broadens from variance \(0.250\) mm² to \(0.610\) mm² in 300 s, with negligible flow. Taking the difference removes the initial width and gives \(D=0.360/(600)=0.000600\) mm²/s, or \(6.00\times10^{-10}\) m²/s. In a 2.00 mm sealed channel, the longest nonuniform cosine mode then decays by \(1/e\) in \(L^2/(\pi^2D)=675\) s. The prediction connects an open-domain variance measurement to a finite-domain contrast decay.

Bulk drift shifts the measured band centroid and can be removed by centring each profile. Shear, by contrast, can spread the band even after centring. Independent checks of the variance law and of the finite-channel decay help distinguish molecular diffusion from unresolved fluid motion.

Over times short compared with a collision time, individual tracer trajectories are approximately straight and the displacement variance initially grows as the square of time. A fit to a diffusion coefficient should exclude this ballistic region. At long times finite walls can likewise limit the spread.

Add a weak force and recover the Einstein relation

A tracer concentration in a fluid can relax even when an external force produces a steady drift. Consider noninteracting overdamped particles at uniform temperature in one dimension. Define mobility \(b\) by the drift speed \(v_d=bF\) under a constant, sufficiently weak mechanical force \(F\). If concentration changes gently in space, the particle current combines a systematic drift and Fick transport: \(j=bcF-D\,\partial_xc\). The two contributions can cancel at equilibrium even though individual particles remain in motion.

For a conservative force \(F=-\partial_xU\), equilibrium concentration must have the Boltzmann form \(c_{\rm eq}\propto e^{-U/(k_BT)}\). Differentiating it gives \(\partial_x c_{\rm eq}=c_{\rm eq}F/(k_BT)\). Requiring zero current for this equilibrium distribution for arbitrary weak potentials gives

\[D=bk_BT .\]

This is the Einstein relation for a dilute thermal tracer. A fluctuation quantity measured without an imposed force, its diffusion coefficient, determines a response quantity measured by applying a force. It presumes local thermal equilibrium, linear mobility and the same degrees of freedom in both measurements. Self-propelled particles, crowded media or substantial hydrodynamic coupling may invalidate this simple one-particle relation.

Use a sedimentation profile to weigh a colloid

Set the upward coordinate to \(z\) and let \(m_b\) be the buoyant particle mass after subtracting displaced liquid mass. Its downward force is \(-m_bg\). Equilibrium then predicts \(c(z)=c(0)e^{-z/\ell_g}\) with gravitational length \(\ell_g=k_BT/(m_bg)\). Suppose an optical count at 298 K falls by a factor of four over 30.0 μm. The measured length is \(\ell_g=30.0/\ln4=21.6\) μm. Thus \(m_b=k_BT/(g\ell_g)=1.94\times10^{-17}\) kg. This is a mass contrast with the solvent, so an independently known density difference is needed to infer particle volume.

An independent tracking experiment gives \(D=0.400\) μm²/s. The mobility is \(D/(k_BT)=9.72\times10^7\) m/(N s). The downward drift speed is \(b m_bg=D/\ell_g=0.0185\) μm/s. Without boundaries, a mean drift of one gravitational length would therefore take about 1170 s, while random displacement over that interval remains substantial for an individual particle. A large ensemble is needed to resolve the mean against diffusion. In the density-matched limit \(m_b\to0\), the sedimentation length diverges and the vertical profile becomes uniform even though measurements of diffusion and of mobility under a different externally applied force still yield finite values.

The mean-square displacement used in mobility inference should be computed after any steady drift has been removed. Over an interval \(\Delta t\), a downward drift gives mean displacement \(-v_d\Delta t\), while one-dimensional thermal variance is \(2D\Delta t\). The raw mean-square displacement about zero contains the extra term \(v_d^2\Delta t^2\). Fitting that quantity to a linear relation at long times can overestimate diffusion and consequently distort the mobility comparison. Independent sedimentation and tracking measurements become especially valuable when the drift is too small to resolve in a single trajectory but emerges reliably in an ensemble average.

The statistical precision of the variance estimate also depends on the number of independent trajectories.

Problem sheet 4 · Molecular leaks and diffusing tracers

Use microscopic counting to distinguish a mean speed, an escape rate and a diffusive spreading rate.

Compare gases in a molecular leak

Helium and argon are separately admitted at equal number density and temperature to the same tiny hole in a thin wall. With atomic masses 4.00 and 40.0 u, find their number-flux and mass-flux ratios. Determine which species is enriched in the emerging beam from an equimolar mixture.

Show the flux weighting

The outgoing number flux is \(J=n\sqrt{k_BT/(2\pi m)}\), so \(J_{\rm He}/J_{\rm Ar}=\sqrt{40/4}=3.16\). Multiplying by particle mass reverses the trend, giving helium-to-argon mass flux ratio \(0.316\). In an equimolar mixture the predicted helium number fraction among emerging atoms is \(3.16/(1+3.16)=0.760\). This conclusion uses the collisionless aperture regime. A bulk hydrodynamic flow would entrain both components differently.

Variance grows from \(0.0900\) to \(0.250\) mm², giving \(D=(0.250-0.0900)/(2\times200)=4.00\times10^{-4}\) mm²/s. The mean drift is \(0.400/200=0.00200\) mm/s. After another 200 s the variance is \(0.410\) mm², or standard deviation \(0.640\) mm. The centroid is displaced by a total \(0.800\) mm from its starting point. A second moment about a fixed origin would also contain the squared centroid displacement and overestimate diffusion.

Choose a faster concentration test

A closed 3.00 mm channel supports initial concentration modulations proportional to \(\cos(\pi x/L)\) and \(\cos(3\pi x/L)\). For \(D=5.0\times10^{-10}\) m²/s find both half-decay times. Explain why an apparently similar measurement using a sinusoid could violate the boundary condition.

Show the mode selection

No-flux ends require \(\partial_xc=0\) at \(0,L\), satisfied by the cosines. Substitution into diffusion gives \(t_{1/2}=L^2\ln2/(D n^2\pi^2)\). Chapter 9 · Thermal radiation in a cavity

Count electromagnetic states, derive the temperature scaling of radiation energy and connect a cavity measurement to emitted power.

From first principles

Derive the cavity spectrum by counting electromagnetic modes

Consider a large empty cavity at uniform temperature \(T\). With periodic boundaries each independent wavevector occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\), and each nonzero wavevector admits two transverse polarizations. Counting a spherical shell of radius \(k\) and thickness \(dk\), then using \(\omega=ck\), gives the number of modes per unit cavity volume and angular-frequency interval,

\[\frac{g(\omega)}{V}\,d\omega=\frac{2(4\pi k^2dk)}{(2\pi)^3}=\frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}\,d\omega .\]

The cavity walls can create and absorb photons, so equilibrium photon number is unrestricted and its chemical potential is zero. A mode has mean occupation \(1/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]\). Multiplying by its photon energy \(\hbar\omega\) and the density of modes gives the thermal spectrum,

\[u_\omega(T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]} .\]

This quantity is energy density per unit angular frequency. Integrating over frequency and changing variable to \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) gives a total proportional to \(T^4\). The continuum mode count assumes a cavity large compared with the wavelengths contributing most of that energy.

Derivation and applications

Locate the peak that a spectrometer actually measures

A cavity at temperature \(T\) emits thermal radiation through a small aperture. An instrument can report energy in equal intervals of frequency or equal intervals of wavelength. The energy in any given physical band agrees, but the positions of the spectral-density maxima depend on the bin coordinate. Assume an ideal blackbody and a calibrated detector reporting energy, with response corrections applied. The spectral energy density per ordinary frequency \(\nu\) has the Planck form

\[u_\nu=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

The energy in corresponding bins must be equal. Substitute \(\nu=c/\lambda\) and include the magnitude of its derivative to obtain the density per unit wavelength,

\[u_\lambda=u_\nu(c/\lambda)\left|\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\right|=\frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.\]

For frequency bins set \(x=h\nu/(k_BT)\); for wavelength bins set \(x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)\). Temperature-dependent prefactors can be ignored in locating the maximum at fixed \(T\). Both maximizations reduce to differentiating \(x^m/(e^x-1)\), with different powers \(m\). Its logarithmic derivative vanishes when

\[\frac{m}{x}-\frac{1}{1-e^{-x}}=0,\]
\[x=m(1-e^{-x}).\]

The positive interior roots are \(x_\nu=2.82144\) for \(m=3\) and \(x_\lambda=4.96511\) for \(m=5\). The stationary points are maxima because both tails fall below the interior peak. Bin widths and what the detector counts are part of the observable. A photon counter without energy weighting or a spectrometer recording per logarithmic bandwidth would require its own density transformation.

Worked example

Compare two instruments viewing the same furnace

For \(T=1200\,\mathrm K\), the wavelength instrument peaks at \(hc/(4.96511k_BT)=2.41\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The frequency instrument peaks at \(2.82144k_BT/h=70.5\,\mathrm{THz}\), corresponding to \(4.25\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). Both spectra describe the same beam. A wavelength interval of a given width spans more frequency bandwidth at shorter wavelengths, which shifts its maximum toward higher photon energies.

Second application

Infer temperature and forecast another peak

A calibrated equal-frequency instrument finds a maximum at \(35.0\,\mathrm{THz}\) from a second cavity. This gives \(T=h\nu/(2.82144k_B)=595\,\mathrm K\). An equal-wavelength instrument should then peak at \(2.898\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}/595\,\mathrm K=4.87\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The wavelength \(c/\nu=8.57\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) still identifies the same photons as the frequency maximum and can be used to match a narrow band between instruments.

Photons in thermal equilibrium

An empty cavity with walls at temperature \(T\) contains electromagnetic radiation. The walls absorb and emit photons, so equilibrium does not constrain their number. The chemical potential of equilibrium photons is zero. Each mode of angular frequency \(\omega\) carries a thermal mean energy \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) above its temperature-independent vacuum energy. The spectrum follows by multiplying this occupation by the number of modes in a frequency interval.

Take a large box of volume \(V\) and periodic boundary conditions for counting. One state per polarization occupies a reciprocal-space cell \((2\pi)^3/V\). A thin spherical shell contributes \(4\pi k^2dk\) times two independent transverse polarizations. Using \(\omega=ck\) gives the state density per volume \(\omega^2/(\pi^2c^3)\). Hence

\[u_\omega(T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}.\]

At low frequency the mean mode energy approaches \(k_BT\) as in classical equipartition. Extending that classical formula to arbitrarily high frequencies would produce an infinite integral. Quantum occupation suppresses these modes exponentially when a single photon costs much more than the thermal energy.

Energy, pressure and flux

Integrate over positive \(\omega\), putting \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\). The integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) gives energy density \(u=aT^4\) with \(a=\pi^2k_B^4/(15\hbar^3c^3)\). Pressure follows from radiation momentum: a photon carries momentum \(E/c\), while crossing a plane weights directions by their projected speed. Evaluating the normal momentum flux over isotropic directions introduces \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\), hence \(p=u/3\).

A small hole in a black cavity admits only outgoing directions. The half-sphere average of the normal speed gives net emitted flux \(cu/4\), which is the Stefan–Boltzmann law \(\sigma T^4\) with \(\sigma=ac/4\). The different factors of three and four describe different angular averages and observables. A wall facing another large black surface also receives thermal radiation, so its net radiative heat loss depends on the fourth powers of both temperatures.

Observe cooling power

A 1.00 cm² aperture faces a large black surrounding enclosure at 300 K. The cavity temperature is 600 K. The outgoing power is \(\sigma A T^4=0.735\) W while incoming power is \(0.0459\) W. The difference, \(0.689\) W, must be replaced to maintain temperature if all other thermal exchanges are negligible. The interior radiation energy density is only \(9.81\times10^{-5}\) J/m³; repeated replenishment at the walls sustains the outgoing flux.

A wavelength spectrometer and a frequency spectrometer plot different spectral densities. To convert use \(u_\lambda=u_\nu|d\nu/d\lambda|\), since both must assign the same energy to corresponding intervals. Merely substituting \(\nu=c/\lambda\) into a density per hertz shifts the plotted maximum incorrectly. The integrated energy and the predicted net aperture power are independent of which spectral coordinate is chosen.

The photon equation of state also predicts a reversible adiabatic temperature change. Write \(U=aVT^4\) and \(p=aT^4/3\), then set \(dU=-p\,dV\). Collecting terms gives \(dT/T=-dV/(3V)\), or \(TV^{1/3}=\mathrm{constant}\). Halving the linear dimension of a cubical equilibrium radiation cavity would double its temperature. This scaling agrees with the shift of every electromagnetic mode frequency under uniform contraction.

Determine the entropy carried by equilibrium radiation

A beam emitted through a small black-cavity opening carries entropy as well as energy. For large equilibrium photon systems, extensivity gives the Euler relation \(U=TS-pV+\mu N\). The photon chemical potential is zero and \(p=u/3\), where \(u=U/V=aT^4\). Solving for entropy density gives \(s=(u+p)/T=4aT^3/3\). The same result follows independently by integrating \(dS=C_VdT/T\) at fixed volume, since \(C_V=4aVT^3\).

Inside the cavity the radiation is isotropic. Photons leaving through a small aperture sample each outgoing direction in proportion to its projected velocity, giving \(c/4\) times the interior density for either energy or entropy. Thus the emitted energy flux is \(\sigma T^4\) and entropy flux is \((4/3)\sigma T^3\). The latter exceeds energy flux divided by \(T\) by a factor of \(4/3\). Opening the cavity to a colder environment is an irreversible emission process, not the reversible transfer of an equal amount of heat through a material boundary at the source temperature.

Consider a device intercepting ideal black radiation from a hot reservoir at \(T_h\) while exchanging a counterpropagating equilibrium field with an environment at \(T_0\). The net incoming energy flux per area is \(\sigma(T_h^4-T_0^4)\) and the corresponding net entropy flux is \((4/3)\sigma(T_h^3-T_0^3)\). A reversible converter must return this entropy to the ambient reservoir, requiring at least \(T_0\) times the net entropy flux as rejected heat. The remaining energy is the maximum possible work for these specified radiation streams,

\[\frac{\dot W_{\max}}A=\sigma T_h^4\left[1-\frac43\frac{T_0}{T_h}+\frac13\left(\frac{T_0}{T_h}\right)^4\right].\]

Evaluate an optical energy and entropy budget

Take a 1.00 cm² aperture, \(T_h=600\) K and \(T_0=300\) K. Outgoing energy is \(0.735\) W and incoming ambient radiation \(0.0459\) W. Net energy input is therefore \(0.689\) W. The net entropy supply is \((4/3)\sigma A(600^3-300^3)=1.43\times10^{-3}\) W/K. Returning this to 300 K requires at least \(0.429\) W, leaving approximately \(0.260\) W available as work. The result is about 35.4 percent of the hot aperture's outgoing radiation power.

As \(T_h\) tends to \(T_0\), net energy and net entropy transport vanish and the maximum work vanishes as well. A grey, directional or spectrally filtered source may have different relationships between intensity and entropy from these two ideal black streams. Using only a bolometer reading and a colour temperature to infer available work is therefore justified only if the assumed angular and spectral distributions are also credible.

A check on the limiting expression is to compare it with an ideal material heat engine fed at \(T_h\). Such an engine can in principle extract a fraction \(1-T_0/T_h\) of a reversible heat input. In the optical problem the incoming stream already has a specified entropy-to-energy ratio. Delivering it to a converter cannot undo the entropy generated by producing that stream in the first place. This accounts for the smaller work fraction at the same two nominal temperatures and makes clear which boundary is being used when assigning a maximum work to emitted radiation.

Chapter 10 · Vibrations and the Debye heat capacity

Count vibrational modes in a solid and explain why a crystal’s low-temperature heat capacity follows a cube law.

From first principles

Derive the low-temperature heat capacity from acoustic modes

In a large three-dimensional crystal, long-wavelength acoustic vibrations have approximately linear dispersion \(\omega=vk\). Count allowed wavevectors inside a sphere of radius \(k\). Its volume grows as \(k^3\), so the number of modes below \(\omega\) grows as \(\omega^3\). The frequency density is its derivative. In a single-speed model normalized to the \(3N\) displacements of \(N\) atoms,

\[g(\omega)=\frac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3},\]
\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N .\]

Each mode stores mean thermal energy \(\hbar\omega/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]\). Multiply by the mode count and substitute \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) to expose the temperature dependence,

\[U_{\rm th}=\frac{9N(k_BT)^4}{\hbar^3\omega_D^3}\int_0^{\hbar\omega_D/(k_BT)}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx .\]

When \(T\ll\hbar\omega_D/k_B\), the omitted tail above the upper limit is exponentially small. The integral approaches a constant, giving \(U_{\rm th}\propto T^4\) and \(C_V=(\partial U/\partial T)_V\propto T^3\). The cube law relies on both three-dimensional mode counting and linear low-frequency dispersion.

Derivation and applications

Recover the Debye velocity from distinct sound branches

A three-dimensional solid usually carries one longitudinal and two transverse acoustic polarizations. Their speeds can differ substantially. Consider a large elastically isotropic crystal of volume \(V\), with linear dispersion \(\omega=v_s k\) for each polarization \(s\) in the thermally populated range. Impose periodic boundaries; one wavevector state occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For each polarization count all points in a sphere of radius \(\omega/v_s\), which includes opposite propagation directions. Differentiating gives the total density of modes,

\[N_s(<\omega)=\frac{V\omega^3}{6\pi^2v_s^3},\]
\[g(\omega)=\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi^2}\left(v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}\right).\]

A slower sound branch has more low-frequency states because the same frequency reaches a larger wavevector sphere. Define one effective velocity \(v_D\) to preserve this acoustic density of states. Requiring an isotropic monatomic Debye model to contain \(3N\) vibrations, with number density \(n=N/V\), fixes its frequency cutoff and temperature scale,

\[\frac{3}{v_D^3}=\frac{1}{v_L^3}+\frac{2}{v_T^3},\]
\[\omega_D=v_D(6\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\]
\[\Theta_D=\frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B}.\]

At low temperature the cutoff can be moved to infinity in the thermal energy integral. Changing variables to \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\) uses \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\). A temperature derivative yields the directly measurable coefficient

\[\frac{C_V}{VT^3}=\frac{2\pi^2 k_B^4}{15\hbar^3}\left(v_L^{-3}+2v_T^{-3}\right).\]

The inverse cubes explain why replacing the three velocities by their arithmetic mean can noticeably underestimate the heat capacity. Optical or electronic contributions must be identified separately in specimens where they are thermally active.

Worked example

Predict the acoustic temperature scale

For \(v_L=6000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(v_T=3000\,\mathrm{m/s}\), the longitudinal branch contributes only \(1/17\) of the low-temperature coefficient. The effective velocity is \(3000(24/17)^{1/3}=3365\,\mathrm{m/s}\). With \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), the cutoff wavevector is \(1.44\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), giving \(\Theta_D\simeq369\,\mathrm K\). The long-wave estimates apply well below that scale.

Second application

Extract the transverse speed from calorimetry

Suppose a separate specimen gives \(v_D=2500\,\mathrm{m/s}\) from its low-temperature heat capacity and \(v_L=5000\,\mathrm{m/s}\) from a longitudinal pulse. Solving for the missing speed gives \(v_T=[2/(3/v_D^3-1/v_L^3)]^{1/3}=2.22\times10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}\). A shear-wave transit across \(10.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) should consequently take \(4.51\,\mathrm{\mu s}\). This timing test uses a distinct mechanical measurement of the same states counted by calorimetry.

What a solid can store

Consider an electrically insulating three-dimensional crystal of \(N\) atoms, volume \(V\), sufficiently cold that its structure remains fixed and normal-mode frequencies vary weakly with temperature. In a harmonic approximation displacements about equilibrium separate into \(3N\) independent oscillators. Their excitations are called phonons. Phonon number is not conserved when the solid exchanges heat, so each mode has a Bose mean occupation with zero chemical potential. The modes exist because the crystal can deform, including at very long wavelengths.

A model assigning the same frequency to every oscillator predicts an exponentially small low-temperature heat capacity, since exciting any mode would require a fixed nonzero energy. Acoustic vibrations instead approach zero frequency as their wavenumber approaches zero. Even in a cold macroscopic crystal, long-wavelength modes can remain thermally accessible. The key quantity is how many have energies of order \(k_BT\) or less.

Test the single-frequency model before introducing a spectrum

The premise of the Einstein solid is deliberately restrictive. All \(3N\) vibrational coordinates of a crystal are independent harmonic oscillators with one frequency \(\omega_E\). For one coordinate the levels are \(E_n=\hbar\omega_E(n+1/2)\). Write \(x=\hbar\omega_E/(k_BT)=\Theta_E/T\). Summing the geometric series gives \(Z_1=e^{-x/2}/(1-e^{-x})\). The partition function of the crystal is \(Z_1^{3N}\), because each normal coordinate can be occupied independently and phonon number is free to change.

Apply \(-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\), to obtain \(U=3N\hbar\omega_E[1/2+1/(e^x-1)]\). The zero-point energy contributes no heat capacity at fixed volume. Differentiating the occupation term and using \(dx/dT=-x/T\) gives

\[C_{V,E}=3Nk_B\,\frac{x^2e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}.\]

At high temperature \(1-e^{-x}\simeq x\), so the heat capacity per mole of atoms approaches \(3R\). At low temperature the denominator tends to one, and the surviving factor \(x^2e^{-x}\) measures the cost of the lowest available excitation. For \(\Theta_E=240\) K the same mole would have \(C_V=18.06\) J/(mol K) at 120 K, \(7.585\) at 60 K and \(0.536\) at 30 K. Halving temperature does not give a fixed ratio. The curve crosses from a classical plateau to thermally rare excitations.

A real three-dimensional macroscopic crystal supports collective vibrations whose frequencies approach zero with wavelength increasing. Replacing the single frequency by a distribution puts modes below any fixed nonzero thermal scale. The next calculation finds the density of those modes, and therefore a low-temperature power law. High-temperature agreement alone cannot distinguish the two models since both count the same total number of coordinates.

Count to a cutoff

Use a continuum approximation with three acoustic polarizations and a common sound speed \(v\). The modes in a shell of wavevectors grow as \(k^2dk\), as for photons. Real crystals generally have distinct longitudinal and transverse speeds. An effective Debye speed averages their inverse cubes. Converting via \(\omega=vk\) gives density \(g(\omega)=3V\omega^2/(2\pi^2v^3)\). The continuum would supply infinitely many modes, whereas a crystal with \(N\) atoms has exactly \(3N\). Choose an upper cutoff \(\omega_D\) to match that count,

\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N,\qquad g(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3 .\]

This cutoff is an approximate representation of the lattice at high wavenumber. The low-temperature prediction depends on the long-wave acoustic spectrum and is insensitive to precise details near the cutoff. In a multi-atom cell additional optical branches change the intermediate-temperature behaviour, which cannot generally be reconstructed with a single effective speed.

Two limits and a measurement

The thermal energy is the integral of \(g(\omega)\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) over the band. Define \(\Theta_D=\hbar\omega_D/k_B\). When \(T\ll\Theta_D\), extend the upper limit of the dimensionless integral to infinity. The resulting energy scales as \(T^4\) and differentiation yields

\[C_V=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3.\]

At high temperature each mode instead approaches \(k_BT\) of thermal energy and the heat capacity tends to \(3Nk_B\). The acoustic model has the correct count by construction. The constant zero-point energy never enters either heat capacity because it disappears upon differentiation.

For one mole of atoms with \(\Theta_D=300\) K, the low-temperature prediction at 10.0 K is \(0.0720\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below the classical \(3R=24.9\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 20.0 K the cubic asymptote predicts \(0.576\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Measuring several temperatures and plotting \(C/T^3\) tests the constant coefficient directly. In a conducting solid an electronic term proportional to \(T\) can dominate at low temperatures. Separating that term through a plot of \(C/T\) against \(T^2\) allows the intercept and slope to reveal the two physically distinct energy stores.

Finite size eventually supplies a lower frequency cutoff related to the crystal dimensions, so the continuum counting argument presupposes a specimen large enough to have many wavelengths populated throughout the temperature interval.

Connect vibrations to thermal expansion

A purely harmonic crystal with force constants independent of volume can store vibrational energy yet has no vibrational mechanism for thermal expansion. Real vibrational frequencies usually vary as the lattice spacing changes. The quasiharmonic approximation treats each mode as harmonic at each volume while allowing its angular frequency \(\omega_j(V)\) to change smoothly. Define the mode Grüneisen parameter \(\gamma_j=-\partial\ln\omega_j/\partial\ln V\). A positive value means expansion softens that mode.

For one oscillator the Helmholtz free energy is \(F_j=\hbar\omega_j/2+k_BT\ln[1-e^{-\hbar\omega_j/(k_BT)}]\). Differentiation at fixed \(T\) gives \(\partial F_j/\partial\omega_j=U_j/\omega_j\), where \(U_j\) includes the zero-point energy and the mean thermal excitation. Since vibrational pressure is \(-\partial F/\partial V\), the chain rule gives \(p_{\rm vib}=V^{-1}\sum_j\gamma_j U_j\). A temperature derivative at fixed volume suppresses the constant zero-point contribution. The mechanical relation \((\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha_V B_T\) finally yields

\[\alpha_V=\frac{\sum_j\gamma_j C_{V,j}}{B_TV} .\]

Here \(B_T\) is the isothermal bulk modulus and \(\alpha_V\) the volume expansion coefficient. The mode sum weights each Grüneisen parameter by its thermally active heat capacity. A low-frequency mode that softens under compression can contribute negatively and can dominate in a cold crystal while high-frequency modes remain frozen. A single positive average parameter is therefore a material model rather than a thermodynamic necessity.

Infer a mode average from dilatometry

For a cubic crystal take measured molar heat capacity \(C_{V,m}=12.0\) J/(mol K), molar volume \(V_m=1.20\times10^{-5}\) m³/mol and bulk modulus \(B_T=100\) GPa. A measured linear expansion coefficient \(6.00\times10^{-6}\) K⁻¹ corresponds to \(\alpha_V\simeq3\alpha_L=1.80\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹. The effective heat-capacity-weighted parameter is then \(\bar\gamma=\alpha_VB_TV_m/C_{V,m}=1.80\). The factor of three comes from differentiating the cube of a length, not from counting acoustic polarizations again.

If the same positive average and bulk modulus remain approximately constant down to a regime where phonons give \(C_V\propto T^3\), the volume expansion coefficient should also fall as \(T^3\). Doubling temperature there increases the coefficient by about eight, although the accumulated length change requires integrating it over temperature. A comparison of \(\alpha_V/C_V\) at several temperatures can test whether the active modes really share a common volume sensitivity. Molar heat capacity and molar volume must be taken on the same chemical counting basis before inferring any dimensionless Grüneisen parameter.

Hydrostatic pressure spectroscopy gives an independent way to measure a mode parameter. By the chain rule, the definition yields \(\gamma_j=B_T(\partial\ln\omega_j/\partial p)_T\) within the quasiharmonic description. For the numerical bulk modulus, a mode with parameter 1.80 would increase its frequency by approximately 1.8 percent under a 1.00 GPa hydrostatic pressure increment. Agreement between this slope and the value inferred from expansion requires that the probed mode be representative of the thermally populated spectrum. A strong optical mode visible to a spectrometer need not dominate low-temperature heat capacity or expansion.

Quoted pressure derivatives must therefore specify the mode, the temperature and whether hydrostatic or directional stress was applied in the measurement.

The missing calorimeter coefficient

An idealized large sheet supports one acoustic branch with linear dispersion \(\omega=vk\) in two dimensions. Derive the low-temperature power of the thermal energy and heat capacity. Explain why a freely suspended membrane with a flexural branch may behave differently.

Chapter 11 · Chemical equilibrium and reaction extent

Minimize Gibbs energy along a permitted reaction and predict a composition change when the system is compressed.

From first principles

Build the reaction quotient from chemical potentials

At fixed temperature and pressure, a closed reacting mixture equilibrates by minimizing Gibbs energy subject to conservation of its constituent elements. Let \(\nu_i\) be the balanced stoichiometric coefficient of species \(i\), positive for a product and negative for a reactant. Advancing the reaction by a small extent \(d\xi\) changes each amount by \(dn_i=\nu_i d\xi\). The free-energy change per mole of reaction is then

\[\frac{dG}{d\xi}=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=\Delta_rG .\]

For an interior equilibrium this derivative vanishes. Chemical potentials can be written \(\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln a_i\) using dimensionless activities relative to specified standard states. Collecting all the logarithms gives

\[\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q,\]
\[Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i},\]
\[K=e^{-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)}.\]

The equilibrium composition satisfies \(Q=K\). For an ideal gas, \(a_i=p_i/p^\circ\); nonideal mixtures require activities measured or modeled for the mixture. If \(Q<K\), a small forward reaction lowers \(G\). This thermodynamic preference determines a direction near the present state while leaving the reaction timescale to kinetics.

Derivation and applications

Propagate an equilibrium constant when reaction heat capacity changes the enthalpy

An equilibrium constant quoted at one temperature does not determine the equilibrium composition at another unless the energy change of the reaction is also known. Consider a reversible reaction with a dimensionless standard equilibrium constant \(K(T)\) and unchanged standard-state choices. All reaction quantities below are per mole of reaction as written. At fixed standard pressure, the Gibbs differential \(dG=-S\,dT\), together with \(G=H-TS\), gives \(d(G/T)/dT=-H/T^2\). Apply this identity to the stoichiometric differences of standard molar quantities,

\[\ln K=-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT},\]
\[\frac{d\ln K}{dT}=\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ(T)}{RT^2}.\]

If heat capacities of products and reactants differ, the enthalpy changes with temperature. Over an interval where their stoichiometric difference \(\Delta_r C_p^\circ\) can be treated as constant, integration of \(dH^\circ=C_p^\circ dT\) gives

\[\Delta_rH^\circ(T)=\Delta_rH^\circ(T_1)+\Delta_r C_p^\circ(T-T_1).\]

Insert this linear enthalpy into the derivative for \(\ln K\). The constant part integrates as \(1/T^2\); the term proportional to temperature also produces a logarithm. The two-temperature relation is

\[\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1}=\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ(T_1)}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)+\frac{\Delta_r C_p^\circ}{R}\left[\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+\frac{T_1}{T_2}-1\right].\]

Both terms vanish when the two temperatures coincide. For a small temperature interval the heat-capacity correction begins at second order, consistent with the local slope depending only on the current reaction enthalpy. Phase changes would interrupt the assumed smooth enthalpy law.

Worked example

Estimate a change in an association equilibrium

Take \(K_1=4.00\) at \(300\,\mathrm K\), \(\Delta_rH^\circ(T_1)=-20.0\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\), and \(\Delta_rC_p^\circ=100\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}\). At \(360\,\mathrm K\) the enthalpy term in \(\ln(K_2/K_1)\) is \(-1.336\) and the heat-capacity term is \(+0.188\). Thus \(K_2=4.00e^{-1.148}=1.27\). The association remains exothermic throughout the interval, although its magnitude declines on heating.

Second application

Infer enthalpy from equilibrium measurements

For a different reaction \(K\) rises from \(0.500\) at \(290\,\mathrm K\) to \(0.900\) at \(310\,\mathrm K\). If the reaction heat-capacity difference is negligible here, inversion gives \(\Delta_rH^\circ=R\ln(0.900/0.500)/(1/290-1/310)=22.0\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\). The positive sign is consistent with the increased equilibrium constant on heating. At \(300\,\mathrm K\) the same enthalpy predicts a local logarithmic slope of \(0.0293\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), testable with a more closely spaced measurement.

A reaction has one constrained direction

A closed vessel can change particle species while conserving the total number of atoms of each element. At fixed temperature and pressure the equilibrium state minimizes Gibbs energy. Introduce a reaction extent \(\xi\) in moles and stoichiometric coefficients \(\nu_i\), negative for reactants and positive for products. A small reaction advance changes amounts by \(dn_i=\nu_i\,d\xi\). The Gibbs differential at fixed \(T,p\) therefore gives

\[dG=\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i=\Delta_r G\,d\xi,\qquad \Delta_rG=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i .\]

An interior minimum satisfies \(\Delta_rG=0\). A negative value means that a small positive advance lowers \(G\). Reversibility of the microscopic chemistry is assumed for equilibrium to be accessible. Thermodynamic preference alone does not provide the time needed to reach it.

Activities and the equilibrium constant

For ideal gases \(\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ(T)+RT\ln(p_i/p^\circ)\). The activity is the dimensionless ratio \(a_i=p_i/p^\circ\). Inserting this expression into the reaction Gibbs energy gives \(\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q\), with \(Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}\). The equilibrium value of \(Q\) is \(K=\exp[-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)]\). Standard pressure supplies the reference that makes the logarithm and the equilibrium constant dimensionless even when the total molecular count changes.

For the dimerization \(2A\rightleftharpoons A_2\), begin with one mole of monomer units and define \(\alpha\) as the fraction of units bound into dimers. Then \(n_A=1-\alpha\), \(n_{A_2}=\alpha/2\) and total gas amount is \(1-\alpha/2\) moles on this basis. Partial pressures follow from mole fractions times total pressure. Substitution into the reaction quotient gives

\[Q=\frac{\alpha(2-\alpha)}{4(1-\alpha)^2}\frac{p^\circ}{p}.\]

The ratio of activities is determined by both composition and total pressure. Compression at the original equilibrium composition immediately lowers \(Q\), making the forward reaction favourable. The subsequent association reduces the number of gas particles while conserving monomer units.

A pressure change at fixed temperature

Suppose \(K=0.750\) at the chosen temperature. Let \(y=1-\alpha\). Because \(\alpha(2-\alpha)=1-y^2\), solving \(Q=K\) gives \(y=[1+4Kp/p^\circ]^{-1/2}\). At \(p=p^\circ\) this gives \(\alpha=0.500\), with one half of all units bound and only one third of gas particles being dimers. At \(p=4p^\circ\) the bound-unit fraction is \(1-1/\sqrt{13}=0.723\). The distinction between fractions of conserved units and fractions of gas molecules matters for interpreting a spectroscopic composition measurement.

A temperature change can also move equilibrium. Differentiating \(\ln K=-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)\) and using the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation gives \(d\ln K/dT=\Delta_rH^\circ/(RT^2)\). For an exothermic association increasing temperature decreases \(K\) locally. Pressure changes at fixed temperature leave \(K\) itself fixed under the same standard-state convention. It is the composition-dependent quotient that adjusts to the imposed mechanical conditions.

The distinction between a chemical potential per mole and one per particle is essential in this derivation. Here partial pressures were combined with \(R\) because the extent was measured in moles. Molecular statistical mechanics instead uses \(k_B\) with energies per molecule, but both conventions give the same dimensionless ratio of equilibrium activities. A useful numerical check is to scale every initial amount and the container volume by the same factor at fixed temperature. Mole fractions and partial pressures stay fixed, so the equilibrium fraction is unchanged even though twice as many molecules may react.

Use a membrane to turn chemical equilibrium into pressure

Equality of chemical potential can constrain a species even when other species cannot move. Separate pure solvent at pressure \(p\) from a dilute ideal solution at pressure \(p+\Pi\) by a membrane permeable only to solvent. Hold temperature fixed and assume a nearly incompressible solvent with approximately constant partial molar volume \(\bar v_w\). The solvent can exchange across the membrane; solute cannot. Its equilibrium condition is therefore equality of solvent chemical potentials, even though total pressures and solute concentrations differ.

For an ideal solution the solvent chemical potential relative to pure solvent at the same pressure changes by \(RT\ln x_w\), where \(x_w\) is the solvent mole fraction. Increasing pressure by \(\Pi\) raises the solvent chemical potential by \(\int_p^{p+\Pi}\bar v_w\,dp'\simeq\bar v_w\Pi\). Equating the two sides gives

\[\Pi=-\frac{RT}{\bar v_w}\ln x_w\simeq \frac{RT}{\bar v_w}x_s\simeq RT c_s .\]

The last approximation assumes a small solute mole fraction \(x_s\) and solution volume close to the solvent volume, with \(c_s\) in moles of independently dissolved particles per cubic metre. The pressure difference is positive because mixing lowers the chemical potential of solvent. If pressure is released, solvent flows into the solution, modifying its concentration until a new balance is attained.

Estimate a hydrostatic head and a molar mass

At 298 K dissolve 2.00 g/L of a nonvolatile solute with molar mass 0.200 kg/mol. The mass concentration is 2.00 kg/m³, so \(c_s=10.0\) mol/m³. Predicted osmotic pressure is \(8.314\times298\times10.0=24.8\) kPa. With solution density approximated by \(1000\) kg/m³, an open-column experiment would need height difference \(\Pi/(\rho g)=2.53\) m to balance it. The final concentration must be measured after the column rises, since transferred solvent can appreciably dilute a small sample.

Conversely, for the same 2.00 g/L preparation a pressure reading of 12.4 kPa would imply an apparent molar mass \(RT(2.00)/12400=0.400\) kg/mol. Association of two formula units into one particle is one possible explanation if the chemical analysis gives 0.200 kg/mol. Nonideal activities, leakage and concentration calibration can also change the inferred particle count. Repeating at lower concentrations tests whether \(\Pi/c_s\) approaches the ideal-gas constant times temperature. In the pure-solvent limit \(x_w\to1\) the chemical-potential difference vanishes so no osmotic pressure is required, despite the membrane still being present.

Concentration units can be a major source of error in this example. One mole per litre is a thousand moles per cubic metre, so the gas constant in joules per mole kelvin gives a pressure in pascals only after this conversion. For fully dissociated dilute electrolytes there may also be more than one independently mobile particle per formula unit. Conversely strong ion pairing or aggregation can reduce the count. Measuring a nonvolatile neutral reference solute of known molar mass in the same apparatus provides a way to test both the pressure scale and the effectiveness of the membrane.

Chapter 12 · Entropy production and relaxation

Derive a measurable rate of entropy production during contact between finite bodies and test it using temperature histories.

From first principles

Derive entropy production during heat exchange

Connect two bodies in a thermally isolated enclosure. Assume each body reaches internal equilibrium quickly enough to have a single temperature, and no external work is done. Let \(J>0\) denote energy flowing per time from the hot body at \(T_h\) to the cooler one at \(T_c\). Their internal energies change at equal and opposite rates. Using the equilibrium state relation \(dS_i=dU_i/T_i\) for each body at fixed volume and composition gives

\[\dot S_{\rm total}=-\frac{J}{T_h}+\frac{J}{T_c}=J\frac{T_h-T_c}{T_hT_c}.\]

This entropy production is positive while heat goes from hot to cold. To predict a relaxation time requires an empirical heat-flow relationship. If the contact has constant conductance \(G\), so \(J=G(T_h-T_c)\), and the heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\) stay constant, energy balance gives

\[\frac{d(T_h-T_c)}{dt}=-G\left(\frac1{C_h}+\frac1{C_c}\right)(T_h-T_c).\]

The temperature difference decays exponentially. The sign of the entropy change follows from thermodynamics, while the measured conductance controls how quickly equilibrium is reached.

Derivation and applications

Connect the recovery of a two-state population to entropy production

A system can return to equilibrium exponentially even when its entropy production has a more complicated time dependence. Consider many independent units with two internal configurations A and B in contact with one heat bath at fixed temperature \(T\). Each changes from A to B with rate \(k_+\) and back with rate \(k_-\), both positive and constant. Write \(p\) for the fraction in B. Conservation of population gives the net forward rate per unit and its stable fixed point,

\[\dot p=k_+(1-p)-k_-p=-(k_++k_-)(p-p_*),\]
\[p_*=\frac{k_+}{k_++k_-}.\]

The solution is \(p(t)=p_*+[p(0)-p_*]\exp[-(k_++k_-)t]\). To relate that motion to thermodynamics, suppose the rates obey detailed balance with the bath. For an independently mixed ensemble the excess free energy per unit relative to its equilibrium population is

\[F(p)-F(p_*)=k_BT\left[p\ln\frac{p}{p_*}+(1-p)\ln\frac{1-p}{1-p_*}\right].\]

This expression combines the change in the two-state energy average with the entropy of distributing the units between A and B. Differentiate with respect to \(p\), multiply by \(\dot p\), and use \(p_*/(1-p_*)=k_+/k_-\). Defining forward and reverse event rates \(j_+=k_+(1-p)\) and \(j_-=k_-p\) gives the entropy production per unit,

\[\dot S_{\rm total}=-\frac{\dot F}{T}=k_B(j_+-j_-)\ln(j_+/j_-)\geq0 .\]

The inequality follows because the larger positive flux also has the larger logarithm. At equilibrium the two one-way event rates can both be finite even though their difference and total entropy production vanish. Near equilibrium the thermodynamic driving force and net rate are both linear in \(p-p_*\), so their product decays twice as rapidly in its exponential time dependence.

Worked example

Compute the initial dissipation during recovery

Set \(k_+=3.00\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(k_-=1.00\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and prepare \(p=0.250\). The forward and reverse rates are \(2.25\) and \(0.250\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Initial entropy production is \(2.00\,k_B\ln9\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}=4.39\,k_B/\mathrm s\) per unit. After \(0.250\,\mathrm s\), one relaxation time, the population has risen to \(0.750-0.500/e=0.566\).

Second application

Infer microscopic rates from a relaxation trace

A different ensemble reaches equilibrium fraction \(p_*=0.200\). The magnitude of a small population offset halves every \(0.500\,\mathrm s\). The sum of the rates is \(\ln2/0.500=1.386\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\); hence \(k_+=0.277\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(k_-=1.109\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). At equilibrium each one-way flux is \(0.222\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) per unit. Event counting can test this prediction even when the mean population looks stationary.

OneSimulation laboratory · Separate the equilibrium temperature from the relaxation time

Two bodies with no external heat exchange

Connect two bodies through a weak thermal contact inside a perfectly insulated enclosure. Assume each equilibrates internally much faster than they exchange heat, so each has a single instantaneous temperature \(T_h\) or \(T_c\). Take their heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\) as positive constants over the range, with no work or phase change. Let \(J\) denote heat flowing per second from the initially hot body into the initially cold one. Energy conservation gives \(C_h\dot T_h=-J\) and \(C_c\dot T_c=J\). This immediately fixes the conserved weighted mean temperature.

A body's entropy change for this model depends only on its successive internal equilibrium states, so \(dS_i=C_i\,dT_i/T_i\). The bridge can carry irreversible heat while that state relation remains valid inside each nearly uniform body. Adding both entropy rates gives

\[\dot S_{\rm total}=J(1/T_c-1/T_h).\]

Positive heat flow down the temperature difference produces positive entropy. Equilibrium sets the thermodynamic force \(1/T_c-1/T_h\) to zero. Thermodynamics constrains the sign of \(J\), yet a constitutive heat-transfer law is still needed to predict how quickly temperatures change.

Identify a rate law experimentally

Suppose \(J=G(T_h-T_c)\) with temperature-independent thermal conductance \(G\). Subtracting the two temperature balances shows that \(\Delta=T_h-T_c\) satisfies \(\dot\Delta=-G(1/C_h+1/C_c)\Delta\). The decaying difference and conserved weighted mean determine both temperature histories. The entropy-production rate becomes \(G\Delta^2/(T_hT_c)\). Near final temperature \(T_f\), the temperature difference decays exponentially while the entropy-production rate decays approximately twice as fast because it depends on the square.

The total entropy increase can also be obtained without integrating this rate. Integrating each state differential from its initial temperature to the common final value gives

\[\Delta S=C_h\ln(T_f/T_{h,0})+C_c\ln(T_f/T_{c,0}).\]

This expression is independent of conductance so long as the enclosure is isolated and the final state is the same. Larger conductance produces entropy faster at early times but shortens the duration of the process. Replacing a sliding-contact resistance by a smaller one changes the temperature record even when the equilibrium entropy gain is identical.

Predict a quantity beyond the equilibrium temperature

Take \(C_h=500\) J/K, \(C_c=1500\) J/K, \(T_{h,0}=360\) K, \(T_{c,0}=300\) K and \(G=2.00\) W/K. Conservation gives \(T_f=315\) K. The initial heat current is 120 W, and \(\dot S(0)=120(1/300-1/360)=0.0667\) W/K. The relaxation time is 187.5 s. The net entropy gain is \(500\ln(315/360)+1500\ln(315/300)=6.42\) J/K to two decimal places.

Use the two-body temperature histories to estimate the initial entropy-production rate and sum changes in \(C_i\ln T_i\) over the record. Doubling the conductance should double the initial rate and halve the measured relaxation time. Extending both runs to the same final tolerance should recover the same total entropy gain. A discrepancy that decreases as the sampling interval shrinks points to time resolution in the estimated initial slope.

At a finite recorded time the entropy increase is smaller than its asymptotic endpoint value. Evaluate the state-function expression at the last recorded temperatures before attributing a shortfall to the numerical algorithm. The remaining difference measures entropy still to be produced during subsequent equilibration.

Couple heat transport to electrical current

A thermal bridge can also carry electric charge. In a simple thermoelectric generator a temperature difference produces an electromotive force through the Seebeck effect, while current carries Peltier heat at each end. Take an idealized device with constant effective Seebeck coefficient \(S\), internal resistance \(R\), open-circuit thermal conductance \(K\) and no additional contact losses. Let \(T_h>T_c\) be externally fixed endpoint temperatures and choose positive current \(I\) as useful generator output. Connect a passive load \(R_L\) to the device.

The open-circuit voltage is \(S(T_h-T_c)\). Current through the load is \(I=S\Delta T/(R+R_L)\), giving useful power \(P=I^2R_L=IS\Delta T-I^2R\). For a uniform element with constant properties, half its internal Joule heat flows to each end. The reversible heat carried per unit charge is \(ST\) by the Kelvin relation. Combining these contributions with Fourier conduction gives hot-side input and cold-side output,

\[\dot Q_h=ST_hI+K\Delta T-\tfrac12I^2R,\qquad \dot Q_c=ST_cI+K\Delta T+\tfrac12I^2R .\]

Their difference is exactly the electrical output. With the sign conventions fixed, the total reservoir entropy production is \(\dot Q_c/T_c-\dot Q_h/T_h\). The Peltier contributions cancel, leaving a positive term from conduction and another from Joule dissipation. This explicitly separates a reversible cross effect from two irreversible processes in the same device.

Find a matched-load operating point

Use \(T_h=350\) K, \(T_c=300\) K, \(S=200\) μV/K, \(R=0.100\) Ω and \(K=0.00200\) W/K. The open-circuit voltage is \(0.0100\) V. With matched load \(R_L=0.100\) Ω, current is \(0.0500\) A and useful power \(0.250\) mW. Conductive heat leakage alone is \(0.100\) W. At the hot end the Peltier contribution is 3.50 mW and half the internal Joule heating is 0.125 mW. Thus \(\dot Q_h=103.375\) mW and \(\dot Q_c=103.125\) mW. Their measured difference is much smaller than either heat current.

The operating efficiency \(P/\dot Q_h=0.00242\) is far below the reversible limit \(1-300/350=0.143\). Maximizing \(P=(S\Delta T)^2R_L/(R+R_L)^2\) at fixed endpoint temperatures indeed gives \(R_L=R\), but this choice need not maximize efficiency because the heat input also depends on current. With the circuit open the load receives zero power while \(K\Delta T\) still crosses the element and produces entropy. A reliable laboratory test therefore records electrical power and both thermal flows separately instead of interpreting an open-circuit voltage as evidence of appreciable useful energy conversion.

The numerical heat currents allow a direct entropy audit. The reservoirs together gain \(0.103125/300-0.103375/350=4.84\times10^{-5}\) W/K. Most of this is already explained by bare heat conduction, since \(0.100(1/300-1/350)=4.76\times10^{-5}\) W/K. The small remainder is the Joule contribution deposited at the two ends. This calculation uses reservoir rates with signs for heat leaving the hot bath and entering the cold bath. A sign error can suggest negative entropy production when the parameters instead describe an ordinary inefficient generator.

Recording the cold-end heat flow with its sign explicitly marked avoids the common confusion between heat entering the cold reservoir and heat entering the device.

Change the gas volume

At fixed temperature an ideal dimerization \(2A\rightleftharpoons A_2\) has \(K=2.00\). At total pressure \(p^\circ\) find the bound-unit fraction and dimer mole fraction. Immediately expand isothermally until all original partial pressures have halved. Find the instantaneous reaction quotient and the preferred direction.

Show the composition and direction

With bound-unit fraction \(\alpha\), equilibrium gives \(1-\alpha=(1+4Kp/p^\circ)^{-1/2}=1/3\). Hence \(\alpha=2/3\) and the dimer mole fraction \(\alpha/(2-\alpha)=1/2\). Because \(Q=a_{A_2}/a_A^2\), halving both partial pressures doubles \(Q\) from 2 to 4 before chemical readjustment. Thus \(\Delta_rG=RT\ln(Q/K)>0\), favouring dissociation. Continuing mechanical readjustment depends on whether volume or pressure is subsequently held fixed.

Two equal heat capacities

Two insulated blocks each have \(C=200\) J/K and start at 320 and 280 K. Find the common temperature, total entropy gain and initial entropy-production rate for conductance 1.50 W/K. What is their temperature difference after 50.0 s?

Show the balance and rate

The final temperature is 300 K. Entropy gain is \(200\ln[300^2/(320\times280)]=0.891\) J/K. Initial current is \(1.50(40)=60.0\) W and initial entropy production is \(60(1/280-1/320)=0.0268\) W/K. With equal capacities \(\tau=C/(2G)=66.7\) s. The difference after 50.0 s is \(40e^{-0.75}=18.9\) K. The final entropy gain depends only on endpoints, while initial rate and time history depend on conductance.

Determine whether a drift is heat leakage

A trace from two strongly insulated bodies has equal capacities of 150 J/K. Initially the measured temperatures are 330 and 290 K. After the difference has vanished both read 309 K. Estimate the apparent net heat exchange with the surroundings. Can increasing internal contact conductance explain it? Give an independent instrumental diagnosis.

Show the energy inventory

For equal capacities the initial arithmetic mean is 310 K and must stay fixed under pure internal heat exchange. Final readings imply \(\Delta U=150(309-330)+150(309-290)=-300\) J. Finite contact conductance can change the approach rate, but its internal current cancels in the total energy balance. A common thermometer drift of \(-1\) K between beginning and end would create the same apparent loss. Calibration against an independent temperature reference, or an enclosure heat-flux measurement, distinguishes these possibilities.

Extract work from a finite hot body

A body of constant heat capacity 1.00 kJ/K cools from 500 K to 300 K. A large reservoir stays at 300 K. Find the maximum work recoverable, the heat delivered to the reservoir and the entropy generated by direct thermal contact. Then predict how much of the available work remains if cooling stops at 400 K.

Worked solution

For a reversible sequence of small engines the instantaneous efficiency is 1 − T₀/T, where T is the body's current temperature. The body supplies −C dT on cooling, so Wₘₐₓ = C[(Tᵢ − T₀) − T₀ ln(Tᵢ/T₀)] = 46.75 kJ. Applying the initial Carnot efficiency to the entire 200 kJ would overestimate the work because the hot-side temperature falls.

The remaining 153.25 kJ enters the reservoir. Its entropy gain is 153.25/300 = 0.5108 kJ/K, equal to the magnitude of C ln(300/500) lost by the body. With direct contact, the reservoir instead receives all 200 kJ and total entropy production is 0.6667 − 0.5108 = 0.1558 kJ/K. Multiplication by 300 K recovers the 46.75 kJ of lost work.

At 400 K the body still has C[100 − 300 ln(4/3)] = 13.70 kJ available relative to the reservoir. The reversible work already extracted is therefore 33.05 kJ. Additivity of these two stages provides a check on both the logarithm and the choice of reference temperature.

Applied study

Connect heat exchange and thermal equilibrium to energy conservation, entropy and microstates.

Which temperature will two bodies reach?

Touch two metal blocks together inside an insulated container. Their final temperature is constrained by conservation of energy. That law alone also permits the hot block to heat up at the expense of the cold one. A second idea, entropy, selects the direction we actually observe. A third, thermal conductance, sets the time it takes.

Assume that each block is internally close to equilibrium and has a constant total heat capacity \(C_i\) in J/K. The blocks exchange heat but do no external work. A transfer \(dQ\) from block 1 to block 2 produces \(C_1dT_1=-dQ\) and \(C_2dT_2=dQ\). Adding and integrating gives

\[C_1T_1+C_2T_2=\text{constant},\qquad T_*=\frac{C_1T_{1,0}+C_2T_{2,0}}{C_1+C_2}.\]

The second expression assumes the bodies have reached a common temperature. A larger heat capacity puts more weight on that block’s starting temperature.

Count the ways to share energy

At fixed total energy \(U\), suppose block 1 has energy \(U_1\). Weakly coupled blocks have \(\Omega_1(U_1)\Omega_2(U-U_1)\) accessible combinations of microscopic states. For an isolated equilibrium system with equally likely microstates, this multiplicity determines the probability of an energy partition. Taking \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\) turns the product into a sum. At its maximum

\[\frac{dS_{\rm total}}{dU_1}=\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}=0,\qquad \frac{1}{T_i}=\frac{\partial S_i}{\partial U_i}.\]

Positive heat capacities make the curvature negative, since \(\partial^2S_i/\partial U_i^2=-1/(C_iT_i^2)\). Equal temperature thus maximises total entropy. Before equilibrium a small transfer from hot to cold increases entropy by \(dQ(1/T_{\rm cold}-1/T_{\rm hot})>0\). Temperatures here must be absolute.

From an isolated pair to a canonical ensemble

When a small system contacts a much larger reservoir, its temperature is effectively set by the reservoir. The chance of observing a particular microstate of energy \(E_j\) is proportional to the number of reservoir states at energy \(U-E_j\). Expand the reservoir entropy about \(U\): \(S_R(U-E_j)\simeq S_R(U)-E_j/T\). Since multiplicity is exponential in entropy, the probability becomes \(p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) and \(Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\).

For one unit with levels \(0\) and \(\Delta\), \(Z=1+e^{-\beta\Delta}\). If \(\Delta=k_BT\ln 3\), the excited state has probability \(1/4\) and mean energy is \(\Delta/4\). Raising temperature at fixed gap drives the two probabilities toward one half. This microscopic account relates heat capacity to the response of mean energy to temperature.

Same change of state, different heat and work

For a closed system the first law reads \(dU=\delta Q-\delta W\), with work positive when the system does it. Internal energy and entropy are state functions. Heat and work depend on the path. To see this, compare two ways for one mole of ideal gas initially at 300 K to expand to twice its volume.

During reversible isothermal expansion, \(p=RT/V\) and \(W=\int p\,dV=RT\ln2=1.73\) kJ. The ideal gas’s internal energy depends only on temperature, so \(Q=W\). Free expansion into an evacuated chamber inside an insulated shell instead gives \(W=Q=0\). The final temperature is again 300 K. Both paths give the same gas entropy increase \(R\ln2=5.76\) J/K. In the reversible case the reservoir loses this much entropy, while in free expansion the insulated gas produces it internally. In the two-block problem the entropy production likewise measures irreversibility inside the chosen boundary.

Give the contact a conductance

Model a short thermal bridge by a constant conductance \(G\) in W/K with negligible heat storage. Its heat flow is \(\dot Q=G(T_1-T_2)\). Subtract the two heat balances to obtain \(d(T_1-T_2)/dt=-G(1/C_1+1/C_2)(T_1-T_2)\). The difference decays exponentially with \(\tau=C_1C_2/[G(C_1+C_2)]\). Meanwhile the entropy production rate is \(G(T_1-T_2)^2/(T_1T_2)\), which falls to zero at equilibrium.

The equilibrium temperature is independent of the bridge conductance, provided heat transfer can continue. The same energy balance for a body in contact with a reservoir gives a different time constant because the reservoir temperature stays fixed. Cooling a body in a room.

A hot block and a larger cold block

Take \(C_1=500\) J/K at 360 K, \(C_2=1500\) J/K at 300 K and \(G=2.0\) W/K. The common equilibrium temperature is \(315\) K. Block 1 eventually releases \(500(360-315)=22.5\) kJ and block 2 absorbs \(1500(315-300)=22.5\) kJ. The initial heat current is 120 W and \(\tau=187.5\) s.

To find the entropy change between the initial and final states, integrate \(dS=C\,dT/T\) for each block along a reversible reference path. The sum is \(500\ln(315/360)+1500\ln(315/300)=6.42\) J/K. Energy transfer cancels between the two bodies. Their entropy changes have different magnitudes because they receive or lose heat at different temperatures.
