Finite-size criticality and the evidence for a phase transition

A finite magnet in zero field can reverse between two equally magnetised directions. Its exact mean magnetisation is then zero even below the temperature where a bulk material orders. Learn how to extract order, correlation length and critical exponents from finite measurements, and which apparent crossings or peaks would survive the thermodynamic limit.

What a finite magnet can and cannot spontaneously break

Consider a ferromagnetic Ising model with spins \(s_i=\pm1\), \(N=L^d\) sites, short-range coupling \(J>0\), and uniform field \(h\) in energy units. Its Hamiltonian is \(-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j-h\sum_i s_i\). A finite partition sum contains finitely many positive analytic terms at positive temperature. For finite \(N\) it cannot develop a true nonanalyticity on the real positive-temperature axis. Yet derivatives can become large and measured distributions strongly bimodal as size grows.

Define total magnetisation \(M=\sum_i s_i\) and intensive order \(m=M/N\). At zero field, spin inversion pairs configurations of \(M\) and \(-M\), making the exact finite equilibrium mean zero. To define spontaneous order in the positive direction, take the thermodynamic limit at positive \(h\), then let \(h\to0^+\). Reversing that order gives zero. A finite record that stays on one side because reversals are rare has not performed the full finite-system equilibrium average. It can approximate a symmetry-selected state only with that interpretation stated.

The absolute magnetisation or its root-mean-square value remains positive in finite zero-field calculations. Even independent disordered spins produce \(\langle|m|\rangle\) of order \(N^{-1/2}\). Therefore a nonzero magnitude in one simulation cannot establish bulk order. Repeat over sizes and inspect its scaling. A weak applied field, surfaces or slow field drift can also select one sign in a laboratory, modifying the finite-size comparison.

Derive a response from connected fluctuations

Differentiate the finite partition function at fixed temperature. The susceptibility per site to energy field \(h\) is

\[\chi_L=\frac{\partial\langle m\rangle}{\partial h}=\frac{\beta}{N}(\langle M^2\rangle-\langle M\rangle^2)=\beta\sum_{\mathbf r}G_c(\mathbf r),\]

where the last equality assumes a translationally invariant system and \(G_c(\mathbf r)=\langle s_0s_{\mathbf r}\rangle-\langle s_0\rangle\langle s_{\mathbf r}\rangle\). Long correlations amplify the response because many sites contribute together when one responds to a field. For an experiment using magnetic induction instead of the energy field, include magnetic moments and unit-system factors.

In a symmetric finite zero-field sample below the ordering temperature, transitions between the two bulk-like peaks can give a large contribution proportional to \(N\) to the full fluctuation susceptibility. To estimate within-phase fluctuations one sometimes forms \(\beta N(\langle m^2\rangle-\langle|m|\rangle^2)\). This is a different observable and not exactly the derivative of the finite symmetric mean with respect to \(h\). Report which definition is plotted. Mixing those definitions in a size collapse can change both amplitudes and the apparent location of extrema.

A correlation length may be estimated from the large-distance connected correlation decay away from criticality, or from the ratio of small-wavevector structure factors. In lattice units, an often used periodic second-moment length is

\[\xi_L=\frac{1}{2\sin(\pi/L)}\sqrt{\frac{S(0)}{S(k_{\min})}-1},\]
\[k_{\min}=2\pi/L .\]

This relation uses the small-\(k\) expansion of the correlation structure factor and a lattice wavevector convention. Subtract means consistently, especially if estimating connected rather than symmetric correlations. If a small noisy \(S(0)/S(k_{\min})-1\) is negative, replacing it by zero without describing the noise can bias a length. Larger samples or more suitable observables may be preferable.

Derive the finite-size variable from competing lengths

Write reduced temperature \(t=(T-T_c)/T_c\). Suppose the bulk correlation length diverges as \(\xi_\infty=\xi_0|t|^{-\nu}\). A finite periodic sample cannot support correlations extending arbitrarily beyond \(L\). The competition is governed by \(L/\xi_\infty\), equivalently \(tL^{1/\nu}\) with its sign and a metric factor. If the bulk magnetisation, susceptibility and heat capacity have exponents \(\beta_m,\gamma,\alpha\), a standard finite-size ansatz below the appropriate upper critical dimension and without extra dangerous variables is

\[\langle|m|\rangle=L^{-\beta_m/\nu}F_m(tL^{1/\nu}),\]
\[\chi_L=L^{\gamma/\nu}F_\chi(tL^{1/\nu})+\chi_{\rm reg}.\]

Here \(\beta_m\) is an order-parameter exponent, distinct from reciprocal thermal energy \(\beta\). The functional forms match the bulk powers when the scaled argument becomes large at fixed nonzero \(t\). A rounded finite-size peak of \(\chi\) occurs at an approximately fixed scaled argument, so its temperature shift and width scale roughly as \(L^{-1/\nu}\), allowing boundary and subleading corrections. Its height has an additional smooth background that can be significant for modest sizes.

For a two-dimensional short-range Ising universality class the known values \(\nu=1\), \(\gamma=7/4\), \(\beta_m=1/8\) give useful benchmarks. Doubling \(L\) at criticality should raise leading susceptibility by \(2^{7/4}=3.364\) and reduce order magnitude by only \(2^{-1/8}=0.917\). The very slow decrease of order is easy to mistake for nonzero bulk order at the critical temperature. The heat-capacity singularity in this example is logarithmic rather than a simple positive power; fitting a few peak heights to a power law can give a misleading small apparent exponent.

A dimensionless crossing and its systematic drift

A convenient shape diagnostic is \(U_L=1-\langle m^4\rangle/[3\langle m^2\rangle^2]\). A zero-mean Gaussian distribution has fourth moment three times variance squared and gives zero. Two infinitely narrow equal-weight peaks at \(\pm m_0\) give \(2/3\). At criticality, rescaling \(m\) by \(L^{-\beta_m/\nu}\) cancels between numerator and denominator, so curves for different sizes can approach an approximately common crossing. The limiting value depends on geometry, boundary conditions and convention, despite the exponent universality.

Include a leading correction \(U_L=U_*+a\,tL^{1/\nu}+bL^{-\omega}+\cdots\). Equating the expansions for \(L\) and \(sL\) shows that the pair crossing drifts as \(t_\times\propto L^{-(1/\nu+\omega)}\) when the coefficients are nonzero. An exactly common crossing is therefore not guaranteed in finite data. Sample temperature resolution, uncertainty of higher moments and correlations between neighbouring reweighted temperatures can also shift the visual crossing.

For a data set with sizes 16, 32 and 64, suppose adjacent pair crossings occur at reduced-temperature-like coordinates 2.31 and 2.28 when plotted as \(k_BT/J\). Without a calibrated correction exponent, taking their simple mean gives no justified bulk critical temperature. If a model independently predicts a crossing displacement decreasing by four per doubling, solve \(T_{16,32}=T_c+A\), \(T_{32,64}=T_c+A/4\), giving \(T_c=2.27 J/k_B\). Vary the correction assumption and use an additional larger size to test it. Extrapolated digits considerably beyond the scatter of these alternatives are not warranted.

Separate thermodynamic rounding from sampling error

Near criticality long spatial correlations often coincide with long relaxation times. A sequential Monte Carlo update can require many sweeps to produce independent configurations. If an observable has normalised autocorrelation \(\rho(k)\), its effective independent sample count for a long record is approximately \(N_{\rm samp}/[1+2\sum_{k>0}\rho(k)]\). Blocking time records and recomputing Binder ratios from blocks is more reliable than treating individual sweeps as independent.

If a susceptibility peak is represented by ten nearby temperatures generated from the same energy histogram, their errors are correlated. Temperature reweighting uses factors \(e^{-(\beta'-\beta)E}\) and is dependable only where sampled energy distributions overlap. A smooth reweighted curve extending outside sampled energies can look precise while depending on a few rare configurations. Use independent anchor simulations or directly measured temperatures to verify both tails.

Inspect distributions as well as means. A first-order transition can also show a large susceptibility peak and bimodality, with leading peak scaling proportional to volume in many finite systems. A continuous transition's order distribution may itself be broad or even possess two lobes. Distinguish using latent heat or its size dependence, interface free-energy barriers, scaling of peak width and appropriate thermal observables. One finite-size double peak in magnetisation is not a universal transition-order test.

An exact finite chain tests what a rapidly growing length means

A one-dimensional nearest-neighbour Ising chain in zero field has no positive-temperature bulk ordering transition, but at low temperature its correlation length grows extremely rapidly. This makes it a valuable counterexample when interpreting a finite magnet. The transfer matrix has entries \(e^{Kss'}\) with \(K=J/(k_BT)\). Its two eigenvalues are \(\lambda_+=2\cosh K\) and \(\lambda_-=2\sinh K\). For a periodic chain of \(L\) spins, \(Z_L=\lambda_+^L+\lambda_-^L\). Keeping only the larger eigenvalue is permissible in the thermodynamic free-energy density at fixed positive temperature but need not accurately represent a short cold chain.

In the infinite chain the zero-field correlation is \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle=(\tanh K)^r\), yielding \(\xi=-1/\log(\tanh K)\) in lattice spacings. At low temperature \(\xi\simeq e^{2K}/2\). A chain of length 100 can therefore be strongly correlated across its entire length around \(K\simeq2.65\), while the infinite chain still has zero spontaneous order at every finite \(K\). Setting \(\xi\sim L\) gives an apparent ordering scale \(k_BT_L/J\simeq2/\log(2L)\), which moves slowly toward zero as the sample is enlarged.

For a periodic finite chain there are two paths connecting site zero and site \(r\). The exact correlation is \([q^r+q^{L-r}]/[1+q^L]\), \(q=\tanh K\). Fitting it to a single exponential out to separations near half the box would overestimate the length because the second periodic image contributes. Use the periodic expression explicitly or restrict fits to a window that is both larger than microscopic distances and smaller than half the sample. A high-quality apparent fit over a narrow middle interval can still mask the wrong boundary geometry.

Numerically simulate chains of lengths 32, 64 and 128 at a sequence of \(K\). Compare the measured absolute magnetisation, full connected correlation and transfer-matrix predictions. If a simulation remains stuck in one sign sector its short-distance correlation can agree with theory while the time average of signed magnetisation does not. Increasing the number of Monte Carlo sweeps and increasing the physical chain length are separate limits. Following only one of them cannot establish a nonzero thermodynamic order parameter.

A response derivative offers an exponent without partition-function normalisation

Let \(U_L\) be a dimensionless shape ratio such as the Binder cumulant. If \(U_L=\mathcal U(tL^{1/\nu})+\cdots\), its slope with respect to reduced temperature at the critical point scales as \(L^{1/\nu}\). A logarithmic slope ratio between two sizes can estimate the correlation-length exponent independently of the magnetisation or susceptibility exponents. Corrections to scaling still matter, especially if the sampled point is not near a fixed critical argument for both sizes.

An equilibrium derivative can also be computed without a finite temperature difference. For any state observable \(A\) with no explicit temperature dependence, differentiating its canonical mean gives \(\partial_\beta\langle A\rangle=-{\rm cov}(A,E)\). Apply this to the second and fourth moments entering a Binder ratio, then use the chain rule. The resulting slope uses energy–magnetisation correlations from one run. Taking two numerically close independent temperatures instead can amplify noise by division by their small separation. A covariance estimator avoids that particular subtraction but requires accurate sampling of joint tails.

Suppose measured absolute crossing slopes on sizes 24, 48 and 96 are 5.7, 10.9 and 21.5 per unit reduced temperature. Adjacent estimates are \(1/\nu=\log(10.9/5.7)/\log2=0.935\) and \(0.980\). They approach the expected value one in this benchmark. A single fit through all sizes would give a smaller formal statistical uncertainty but might obscure a systematic drift. Report adjacent effective exponents and test sensitivity to omitting the smallest size.

The numerical derivative must use the same temperature variable for each size. A slope against inverse temperature differs by \(d\beta/dT\), a known factor near a common critical temperature; plotting against independently fitted centre temperatures can introduce an additional calibration dependence. A nonlinear temperature sensor or a slowly drifting coupling also changes the physical conversion from laboratory temperature to the simulated dimensionless control. Use an independently determined parameter scale if the goal is to compare experiment with an absolute transition temperature rather than just a scaling exponent.

Estimate an interface cost from a probability trough

At a first-order transition, a finite periodic sample may switch between two phases separated by an interfacial configuration. A histogram of an appropriate extensive observable can show peaks corresponding to nearly pure phases and a trough representing intermediate states. At equal bulk phase weight, a probability ratio gives a free-energy difference \(\Delta F_L=-k_BT\log(P_{\rm trough}/P_{\rm peak})\), provided the same bin measure or a properly defined density is used. An exponentially small trough amplifies sampling errors, so the measured minimum is often only an upper bound on its probability.

In a slab geometry under periodic boundaries, an intermediate configuration may contain two approximately flat interfaces. If each interface costs \(\gamma L^{d-1}\), the leading barrier is \(2\gamma L^{d-1}\), with subleading capillary fluctuations, geometry corrections and possible droplet-to-slab crossovers. A cube with open boundaries can instead permit interfaces of different number or shape. Counting two interfaces automatically for every order-parameter histogram would misestimate the inferred tension.

For an illustrative two-dimensional periodic square, suppose the measured barrier grows from \(8k_BT\) at \(L=40a\) to \(15k_BT\) at \(L=80a\), where \(a\) is lattice spacing. From the difference of the two barriers and the increase in total interface length \(2(40a)\), an effective line tension is \(0.0875\,k_BT/a\). Using the absolute smaller-size barrier divided by \(2L\) gives \(0.100\,k_BT/a\), revealing a finite-size offset. More sizes, an observed slab geometry and adequate trough sampling are needed before assigning a bulk interface tension.

This analysis should not be transplanted unchanged to a continuous critical distribution with two shoulders in finite volume. A critical finite-size order distribution can be non-Gaussian without an interfacial barrier growing as \(L^{d-1}\) at its critical point. Plot the barrier's size dependence and inspect actual spatial configurations. A peak-height ratio alone contains no direct information about how many interfaces or what type of correlations generated the intermediate states.

Estimate a ratio of moments without losing its covariance

The second and fourth magnetisation moments in a Binder cumulant usually come from the same record. Treating their errors as independent can grossly misestimate the uncertainty because the fourth moment tends to be large precisely in configurations contributing a large squared moment. Divide the record into blocks longer than its integrated autocorrelation time, recompute the ratio after deleting each block and estimate uncertainty using a block jackknife or use independent replicate simulations. Recomputing the nonlinear statistic inside each resample retains numerator–denominator covariance.

Histogram reweighting across nearby temperatures should likewise be recomputed within each block when extracting a crossing. Fitting smooth curves to the full sample and then adding independent random errors to individual temperature points ignores strong correlations induced by the shared configuration weights. Repeat the root-finding step for each independent or block-resampled pair of systems to obtain an uncertainty on the crossing temperature itself.

Check block-size convergence. If estimated errors continue growing as block duration is increased, slow modes may not be resolved or the record may have drifted. A huge number of recorded spin configurations cannot compensate for fewer than a handful of independent reversals when the statistic depends on those reversals. This issue is measurable: compare direct covariance estimates, block estimates and independent starts, and extend the run where those diagnostics disagree.

A block-average variance exposes a correlation length without fitting a tail

Divide a large zero-field spin image into square blocks of side \(b\) and record the average spin in each block. Its variance is the sum of all connected correlations within the block divided by the squared number of sites. For independent spins of variance one, it is exactly \(1/b^2\) in two dimensions. If neighbouring spins are correlated over a length \(\xi\) much smaller than the block, the variance still falls approximately with inverse block area, but with a larger prefactor proportional to the integrated correlation. Below block sizes comparable to \(\xi\), this averaging becomes much less effective.

At a critical point the connected correlation decays algebraically rather than exponentially over the accessible range. If in dimension \(d\) it behaves as \(G(r)\propto r^{-(d-2+\eta)}\), integrating pair separations to the block scale gives \({\rm var}(m_b)\propto b^{-(d-2+\eta)}\) in the scaling regime. For the two-dimensional short-range Ising benchmark with \(\eta=1/4\), doubling the block side reduces the variance by only \(2^{-1/4}=0.841\), compared with 0.25 for independent spins. This is an observable distinction even when a full bulk thermodynamic limit is unavailable.

Block analysis of one image does not generate independent thermodynamic systems. Neighbouring blocks share correlated surroundings, overlapping blocks are statistically dependent, and every block inherits global constraints or drift in the original preparation. Increasing block size also reduces the available count sharply. Repeat across independent configurations, compare several whole-image sizes and study block-size windows well above a lattice spacing and well below the full image. An apparently accurate critical exponent from the final two largest blocks of one image is particularly unreliable.

A real magnetic image may measure a blurred field projection rather than the local spin itself. Spatial point spread creates detector-induced correlations at small separations. Determine it with a reference pattern and omit or model blocks comparable to the blur. Conversely spatially varying illumination adds long-range covariance unrelated to the sample. Flat-field calibration and sample rotations can separate these artefacts from material correlations. A second-moment structure-factor estimate and a block-variance estimate respond differently to these measurement effects, providing a useful cross-check.

This method also links microscopic and macroscopic noise. The scaled block variance in the large-block regime approaches the same integrated connected correlation controlling uniform susceptibility, with boundary terms that shrink as block dimensions exceed \(\xi\). Comparing a weak-field response with block statistics therefore tests equilibrium fluctuation–response and spatial calibration simultaneously. If the discrepancy grows only as blocks approach the full system size, a fixed total magnetisation constraint or incomplete sampling of global sign reversals deserves examination.

For a concrete noise budget, consider a camera averaging adjacent pairs of independent sites before reporting them as displayed pixels. Those displayed pixels can have a positive nearest-neighbour correlation if their averaging footprints overlap, even when the original spins are completely uncorrelated. A measurement of the apparent short-range correlation length would then reflect the instrument kernel. Blocking on sizes much larger than the overlap can recover the correct large-scale inverse-area trend, while direct comparison of single-pixel variance with independent-spin theory fails. Calibrating only the detector's global gain cannot fix this spatial response. A measured point-spread map, an uncorrelated reference preparation and tests of blocks at several image resolutions can separate the resolution scale from the material's growing correlation length as temperature is varied.

Tutorial · Extract two exponent ratios from a size doubling

At a candidate critical temperature susceptibilities for sizes 32 and 64 are 110 and 360 in common units. Absolute magnetisations are 0.310 and 0.282. Estimate the corresponding exponent ratios and compare with the two-dimensional Ising benchmarks.

Use logarithmic ratios and inspect the small differences

The susceptibility ratio gives \(\gamma/\nu=\log(360/110)/\log2=1.71\). For magnetisation \(\beta_m/\nu=-\log(0.282/0.310)/\log2=0.137\). These are near 1.75 and 0.125, but no error bars or corrections have been supplied. An additive susceptibility background or a slight critical-temperature offset can bias both effective exponents. Use several sizes and a dimensionless crossing diagnostic before assigning a universality class.

Tutorial · Determine whether a finite sample really switched phases

A zero-field record contains 20,000 sweeps of positive magnetisation near \(+0.6\), followed by one reversal and 20,000 sweeps near \(-0.6\). It yields a near-zero mean and large variance. Why does the nominal record length not establish a precise symmetric susceptibility? What extra records or controls would help?

Identify the slow event determining the uncertainty

Within each sign sector there may be many locally decorrelated fluctuations, yet the relative weight of the two peaks is estimated from essentially one reversal. Treating 40,000 sweeps as independent underestimates the error of the peak weights and of statistics sensitive to global sign. Starting multiple independent runs with both signs can improve coverage, but deliberately balancing starting signs is not a measurement of true equilibrium weights if a weak residual field exists.

Record switching-time distributions over much longer runs or use an equilibrium-preserving algorithm that efficiently samples both sectors, while distinguishing computational updates from physical kinetics. Apply small positive and negative fields to test susceptibility and residual bias. For a within-phase susceptibility condition on a clearly defined phase and report the changed observable.

Tutorial · Explain a crossing that drifts with the boundary

Two groups study the same lattice Hamiltonian. One uses square periodic samples; the other uses twice-as-long rectangular samples with open ends. Their Binder curves cross near similar temperatures but at different cumulant values. Is this evidence against universal critical behaviour?

Separate universal exponents from finite-geometry functions

Changing aspect ratio and boundary conditions changes the finite-size scaling function and hence the crossing value of a dimensionless ratio. Universal bulk exponent comparisons do not require identical Binder values across different finite geometries. Match boundary conditions, shape, operator and cumulant convention before comparing purported universal amplitude ratios.

Each group can still extrapolate its own crossing temperatures, examine moment slopes proportional to \(L^{1/\nu}\) and test bulk exponent ratios with geometry fixed across its own size series. If one dimension is held fixed while the other grows, however, the family approaches a strip rather than a bulk two-dimensional limit. The thermodynamic sequence itself is an experimental variable, not just a plotting preference.
