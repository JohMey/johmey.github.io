Transport coefficients from equilibrium fluctuations

How can a fluid in equilibrium reveal the diffusion constant that would govern a spreading drop, or the viscosity that would resist an applied shear? Derive an exact connection between displacements and velocity correlations, generalise it to stress and heat currents and test how a finite record can bias each number. The assumptions behind convergence matter as much as the prefactor.

Integrate the velocity correlation before taking a long-time limit

Observe one Cartesian coordinate of a tagged particle in a homogeneous equilibrium fluid with zero mean drift. The displacement over time \(t\) is the integral of its velocity. Square that integral, average over the equilibrium ensemble and use stationarity to replace the two-time expectation by \(C_v(t_1-t_2)\). For a scalar component in a time-reversal-symmetric equilibrium state this correlation is even. Integrate over the two triangles of the time square:

\[\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^2\rangle=2\int_0^t(t-s)C_v(s)\,ds,\]
\[\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\langle\Delta x^2(t)\rangle=\int_0^t C_v(s)\,ds .\]

If the correlation is integrable and boundary effects remain absent, the long-time slope yields a diffusion coefficient \(D=\int_0^\infty C_v(s)ds\). In \(d\) isotropic dimensions one may use \(D=(1/d)\int\langle\mathbf v(s)\cdot\mathbf v(0)\rangle ds\) or the mean-square-displacement slope divided by \(2d\). Using the full vector dot product without division by dimension gives an overestimate. Directional diffusion in an anisotropic material requires tensor components rather than a forced scalar average.

The derivation is kinematic and does not assume a Markovian motion. A fluid with velocity memory or an oscillating solid still obeys the finite-time relation. Those systems need not exhibit the same long-time proportionality to time. A bound oscillator can have persistent or alternating velocity correlations and a bounded displacement. An observation window in which the mean square grows over a fraction of an oscillation is insufficient to claim diffusion.

A solvable benchmark from ballistic to diffusive motion

For one coordinate take the underdamped Langevin equation \(m\dot v=-\zeta v+\xi\), with zero-mean white noise satisfying \(\langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle=2k_BT\zeta\,\delta(t-t')\). In equilibrium \(\langle v^2\rangle=k_BT/m\). Condition on \(v(0)\) and integrate for positive time. The later random force has no correlation with the initial velocity, giving \(C_v=(k_BT/m)e^{-t/\tau_v}\), where \(\tau_v=m/\zeta\). Its area and the resulting displacement are

\[D=\frac{k_BT}{\zeta},\]
\[\langle\Delta x^2(t)\rangle=2D[t-\tau_v(1-e^{-t/\tau_v})].\]

At \(t\ll\tau_v\), the constant and linear terms inside the brackets cancel and displacement is ballistic:

\[\langle\Delta x^2\rangle\simeq(k_BT/m)t^2.\]

At \(t\gg\tau_v\) the slope approaches \(2D\). Fitting a line through the origin at times only a few relaxation times overestimates the influence of the negative intercept in this long-time expression and underestimates \(D\). Comparisons at multiple lags distinguish crossover curvature from a true anomalous power law.

For an inert sphere of radius 0.50 micrometres and density \(2000\) kg per cubic metre in water with \(\eta=1.00\) mPa s at 298 K, its bare mass is \(1.05\times10^{-15}\) kg and Stokes drag \(9.42\times10^{-9}\) kg/s. The estimate \(\tau_v=0.111\) microseconds is far faster than ordinary video tracking. \(D=0.437\) square micrometres per second and the one-coordinate rms displacement over 10 ms is 0.0935 micrometres. Hydrodynamic added mass and memory affect very short-time behaviour; the simple exponential is an analytical reference, not a claim that a water suspension remains Markovian down to arbitrary times.

Relate a weak imposed force to equilibrium motion

In the same soluble model a small constant force \(F\) turns the mean velocity into \(F(1-e^{-t/\tau_v})/\zeta\), so the mobility \(b=\lim\langle v\rangle/F=1/\zeta\). Together with equilibrium diffusion this gives the Einstein relation \(D=k_BTb\). A broader equilibrium argument considers a dilute suspension in a weak conservative potential \(U(x)\). Its current is

\[j=-D\,\partial_x n+bFn,\]

with \(F=-\partial_xU\). The Boltzmann distribution \(n\propto e^{-U/k_BT}\) must carry zero current; substitution gives the same relation without assuming exponential velocity relaxation.

Linear response connects the full weak-force impulse response to an equilibrium correlation. For a perturbation coupled as \(-F(t)x\), classical equilibrium response of velocity has causal kernel \(C_v(t)/(k_BT)\) for \(t>0\) under the usual microreversibility assumptions. Integrating the response kernel for a constant weak force gives

\[b=(1/k_BT)\int_0^\infty C_v(t)dt,\]

which recovers the kinematic diffusion relation. Strong forcing, an active bath or a nonequilibrium steady state need not obey this simple equilibrium identity.

A direct check compares independently measured weak sedimentation or calibrated drift with freely diffusing trajectories. For the previous bead a force 10 fN would produce a mean drift about 1.06 micrometres per second in the ideal Stokes regime. During a 10 ms frame its mean drift is about 0.011 micrometres, much smaller than rms Brownian motion, so many frames or many particles are needed. A single short jagged record can appear to move against the force. Use equal positive and negative applied forces to cancel constant stage drift in the estimated mobility.

Stress and heat current carry their own normalisations

In a homogeneous isotropic laboratory fluid, define \(P_{xy}\) as a spatially averaged off-diagonal microscopic stress, with units Pa. Its equilibrium mean is zero in an isotropic state without imposed shear. A weak velocity gradient couples to momentum transport. The Green–Kubo expression for dynamic shear viscosity is

\[\eta=\frac{V}{k_BT}\int_0^\infty\langle P_{xy}(t)P_{xy}(0)\rangle\,dt .\]

If a program outputs an extensive stress moment \(J_{xy}=V P_{xy}\), the prefactor is \(1/(Vk_BT)\). Multiplying an already extensive current by volume again is a common orders-of-magnitude error. Check dimensions: pressure squared times time times volume divided by energy gives pascal seconds. Kinetic and interaction contributions to microscopic stress must both be included according to the model; a calculation with ideal kinetic flux alone need not reproduce the viscosity of a dense fluid.

Similarly for a component of total heat current \(J_{Q,x}\) with units energy times length per time, the thermal conductivity in an isotropic equilibrium single-component system is

\[\kappa=\frac{1}{Vk_BT^2}\int_0^\infty\langle J_{Q,x}(t)J_{Q,x}(0)\rangle\,dt .\]

Here \(k_BT^2\) means \(k_B\) times \(T^2\). If a heat-flux density is used instead, the volume prefactor moves as for stress. Heat current must exclude energy carried by a net mass flow as appropriate to the chosen thermodynamic constraint. In mixtures several conserved species currents can couple, and the measured conductivity at zero mass flux can differ from one unprojected current coefficient. The meaning of the measured flux and the imposed condition must be specified before comparing coefficients.

For an illustrative volume \(10^{-24}\) cubic metres at 300 K, assume one stress autocorrelation \(C_P(t)=(2.0\times10^6\ {\rm Pa})^2 e^{-t/(2.0\,{\rm ps})}\). Its area is 8.0 square pascal seconds. Multiplication by \(V/(k_BT)\) gives viscosity \(1.93\times10^{-3}\) Pa s. Doubling the simulated volume of an otherwise similar homogeneous fluid generally reduces the variance of its volume-averaged stress, keeping the physical transport coefficient intensive. Holding the same numerical stress variance while merely changing the prefactor would describe a different equilibrium fluctuation model.

Find a correlation integral's useful time window

An estimate at lag \(s\) averages over pairs of samples separated by that lag. In a record of duration \(T_{\rm rec}\), the number of available pairs decreases with lag. Overlapping time origins strongly correlate the estimates, so plotting one error bar per raw pair exaggerates precision. Integrating an estimated correlation to the maximum possible lag frequently accumulates late random noise rather than useful signal. Conversely stopping too early omits a physical tail.

For a positive single-exponential reference, integrating to three correlation times retains \(1-e^{-3}=95.0\) percent of the area. Five times retain 99.3 percent. A correlation containing a small, much slower component can look essentially flat on the main decay scale while contributing appreciably to the area. Divide a long run into blocks, compare running integrals and check several window lengths. Report the variation between independent blocks and any explicit tail model used beyond the measured interval.

At short lags, finite exposure averages correlated positions or velocities. Uncorrelated localisation errors in position increase nonzero-lag displacement variance by a constant \(2s_x^2\) per coordinate. Numerically differentiating those same noisy positions creates large artificial velocity variance and negative correlations between adjacent increments. An apparent negative velocity lobe can therefore be physical backscattering or a measurement artefact. Comparing a raw displacement fit with the velocity integral after independently characterising detector noise is more informative than differentiating first and interpreting every feature as fluid memory.

A forced shear wave gives a second viscosity measurement

An equilibrium stress correlation estimates viscosity without imposing any flow. A separate driven measurement can test that coefficient against a directly observed penetration depth. Take a Newtonian fluid filling \(y>0\), driven by a laterally oscillating plate at \(y=0\) with speed \(U_0\cos\omega t\). Neglect boundaries at large \(y\), compressibility and nonlinear convective effects in this spatially uniform tangential shear. The momentum equation reduces to

\[\partial_tu=\nu\partial_y^2u,\]

with kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\eta/\rho\).

Seek \(u=\operatorname{Re}[U_0e^{-\lambda y-i\omega t}]\) and choose the square root with positive real part. Then \(\lambda=(1-i)/\delta\),

\[\delta=\sqrt{2\nu/\omega}.\]

Both amplitude decay and phase lag carry information: at distance one penetration depth the amplitude is \(e^{-1}\) of the wall speed and the lag magnitude is one radian. A single complex velocity map can determine viscosity from either slope and use their agreement to test the simple model.

For a fluid of density \(1000\) kg per cubic metre and measured penetration depth 0.126 mm at 20 Hz, \(\nu=\omega\delta^2/2\simeq1.00\times10^{-6}\) square metres per second and \(\eta\simeq1.00\) mPa s. A stress-fluctuation integral yielding a value twice as large would predict a penetration depth larger by \(\sqrt2\), approximately 0.178 mm. Such a comparison can discriminate a missing component or volume factor in the equilibrium calculation if density, temperature and fluid composition match.

At high enough frequency the fluid may have a complex frequency-dependent viscosity rather than the zero-frequency coefficient from a Green–Kubo area. In linear response that generalisation is the one-sided Fourier transform of the equilibrium stress correlation with appropriate prefactors. A single-exponential stress memory gives a frequency-dependent response with a characteristic roll-off and phase lag. Comparing a high-frequency penetration measurement directly to a zero-frequency area without checking the timescale can therefore produce a genuine difference, not a statistical failure.

Remove a centre-of-mass mode only when the observable requires it

In a finite periodic particle simulation total momentum may be exactly conserved. If the initial centre of mass has a random velocity and no thermostat removes it, every particle's measured laboratory coordinate includes the common drift \(V_{\rm CM}t\). Its mean-squared displacement gains a ballistic term \(\langle V_{\rm CM}^2\rangle t^2\). In a canonical ensemble the centre-of-mass velocity variance in one direction is \(k_BT/M_{\rm tot}\). Though it falls with system size, at sufficiently long times its quadratic contribution exceeds the linear diffusive part.

For 1000 equal particles of mass \(m\), an equilibrium centre-of-mass mode contributes one thousandth of the single-particle instantaneous velocity variance. In a long-time autocorrelation this small constant never integrates to a finite diffusion coefficient. Subtract centre-of-mass displacement or work in a zero-total-momentum ensemble before taking the long-time integral of an internal tracer's motion. In a real sample with externally imposed drift, decide whether the physical quantity sought is diffusion about that drift or absolute laboratory displacement; the subtraction has a physical meaning rather than merely a cosmetic improvement.

Conversely, indiscriminately subtracting the mean velocity of a tiny observation subregion separately at every time can remove genuine hydrodynamic long-wavelength motion. A strongly applied single-particle thermostat can alter current correlations and suppress transport even while maintaining the correct static temperature histogram. Check the thermostat strength and system size; compare an equilibrium-prepared weakly thermostatted trajectory with an energy-conserving production segment where appropriate. Maintaining the correct one-time distribution is not sufficient to preserve all dynamical correlation functions.

The distinction also matters when removing an estimated mean stress before integrating its fluctuations. The true shear mean may vanish by symmetry, but a short noisy record has a nonzero sample mean. Subtracting a slowly varying trend on the same timescale as a genuine stress tail can erase physical viscosity. Use independent samples, separately measured drifts and explicit sensitivity to the subtraction window. A persistent nonzero off-diagonal stress under nominal isotropic conditions may indicate incomplete equilibration, a deformed box or an unrelaxed solid rather than a fluid transport coefficient.

Heat and particle currents can share fluctuations

Consider two thermodynamic fluxes \(J_1,J_2\) driven weakly by conjugate forces \(X_1,X_2\), chosen so entropy production density is \(J_1X_1+J_2X_2\). Linear response writes \(J_i=\sum_jL_{ij}X_j\). Near time-reversal-symmetric equilibrium, with flux parities chosen appropriately and no magnetic-field reversal complications, reciprocal relations give

\[L_{12}=L_{21}.\]

The symmetric part must be positive semidefinite so that entropy production is nonnegative for every small force. Thus \(L_{11},L_{22}\ge0\) and \(L_{12}^2\le L_{11}L_{22}\).

Suppose a thermal measurement enforces \(J_1=0\) by allowing a compensating force \(X_1\) to develop, as when a concentration or electrochemical gradient cancels a coupled particle flux. Solving the first row gives

\[X_1=-L_{12}X_2/L_{11}.\]

Substitution into the second yields response coefficient \(L_{22}-L_{21}L_{12}/L_{11}\). The measured constrained heat response can therefore be smaller than the coefficient at fixed \(X_1=0\). The difference has a simple operational origin in the boundary condition enforced during the experiment.

Take consistent normalised coefficients \(L_{11}=4,L_{22}=3,L_{12}=L_{21}=2\). At zero first force the heat flux slope is three. At zero first flux it is \(3-4/4=2\). Both are positive and consistent with the same equilibrium correlation matrix. Comparing a simulated heat-current autocorrelation corresponding to the unconstrained coefficient against a zero-particle-flux experiment requires the mixed-current correlation too. Apparent disagreement by a factor of one and a half can arise entirely from this distinction.

Green–Kubo cross-correlations must use properly defined conjugate fluxes and units. Redefining heat current by adding a constant multiple of particle current changes the matrix entries but must leave a physically defined zero-particle-flux heat measurement invariant when the forces are transformed consistently. This reference invariance is analogous to subtracting a per-particle energy reference in grand-canonical covariance. It provides a powerful test of a multicomponent transport implementation without relying on any empirical transport value.

A lattice trajectory provides an exactly known correlation integral

For a one-dimensional nearest-neighbour walk with steps of length \(a\) every interval \(\Delta t\), suppose successive step directions have correlation \(\langle s_{j+k}s_j\rangle=r^k\) with \(|r|<1\), and zero mean. Squaring the sum of \(n\) steps gives

\[a^2[n+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)r^k].\]

In the large-\(n\) limit divide by \(2n\Delta t\) to obtain

\[D=a^2(1+r)/[2\Delta t(1-r)].\]

The uncorrelated random-walk value is recovered at \(r=0\).

With \(a=1\) micrometre and \(\Delta t=0.10\) s, uncorrelated diffusion is five square micrometres per second. Persistence \(r=0.5\) raises it to fifteen, while anticorrelation \(r=-0.5\) lowers it to \(5/3\). All three walks have the same step-size variance, so an estimate based only on one-frame displacement misses the entire correlation effect. The finite-lag autocorrelation sum and a long-time displacement fit should recover the same value on a long simulated record.

The discrete formula also clarifies the special zero-lag weight in numerical integration. An integral approximated from sampled symmetric correlations gives half weight to the zero lag on a one-sided trapezoidal rule. A careless double count of the instantaneous self term predicts twice the uncorrelated diffusion. Benchmark a numerical estimator with this exact walk before applying it to molecular data with unknown late tails and noisy velocity derivatives.

Measure an off-diagonal diffusion coefficient after rotating the sample

In an anisotropic sheet a tracer's two-dimensional displacement covariance need not align with the camera axes. Let its long-time principal diffusion coefficients be \(D_1,D_2\) and the first principal axis make angle \(\theta\) with laboratory \(x\). In matrix form the laboratory tensor is \(R\,{\rm diag}(D_1,D_2)R^{\mathsf T}\), with \(R\) the planar rotation. At sufficiently large lag in an effectively unbounded drift-free region, the displacement covariance matrix tends to \(2D t\). An off-diagonal covariance is a measurable correlation between displacement components even if the noise is independent in the material principal directions.

Take \(D_1=2.0\) and \(D_2=0.50\) square micrometres per second with \(\theta=30^\circ\). Laboratory components are \(D_{xx}=1.625\), \(D_{yy}=0.875\) and \(D_{xy}=0.650\), in the same units. At 0.10 s the cross covariance is 0.130 square micrometres. Rotating the physical sheet by ninety degrees interchanges the two diagonal values and reverses the sign of the off-diagonal one. An instrument-fixed camera readout artefact would not generally follow this material rotation.

To infer the principal axes from data, fit each covariance component versus lag using a common drift subtraction and independent position calibration. Diagonalise the symmetric fitted diffusion matrix. Positive semidefiniteness requires

\[D_{xy}^2\le D_{xx}D_{yy}.\]

A strong violation indicates incompatible estimates, unit conversion errors or statistical uncertainty, not a physically permissible strongly anisotropic diffusion tensor. Near-degenerate principal values make the orientation poorly determined even when the mean diffusion coefficient is precise. Report uncertainties on eigenvalues and orientation separately.

A finite field of view introduces a further bias. Fast particles along the high-diffusion direction leave the image sooner, and a tracking pipeline retaining only long trajectories can favour slow or fortuitously confined paths. Compare different fields of view and minimum track durations, or fit a measurement model incorporating censoring. A physical material anisotropy should persist after correcting these effects; apparent anisotropy aligned with the short direction of a rectangular observation window demands particular scrutiny.

A weak calibrated force along different axes supplies an independent check through the tensor Einstein relation under equilibrium conditions. If the diffusion tensor predicts off-axis mean drift, the mobility experiment should observe its sign and magnitude after reversing the force. A discrepancy could reveal active forcing, nonstationarity, an incorrect temperature or a mismatch between the mobility and displacement observables. It cannot be diagnosed from a scalar mean square alone.

Another rotational invariant is the trace of the diffusion tensor. In the example its value stays 2.50 square micrometres per second for every sample orientation, while the determinant stays one square micrometre to the fourth per second squared. A comparison of two camera orientations therefore provides numerical checks independent of the fitted angle. A drift mistakenly left in the displacement covariance can violate the expected linear dependence on lag, making the inferred effective eigenvalues grow with the selected fitting window. Subtract the ensemble mean displacement at each lag and record that mean separately as a flow measurement. Conversely do not subtract an individual best-fit line from every short trajectory without modelling the resulting loss of long-time fluctuations; that procedure can bias both diffusion coefficients downward.

Tutorial · Invert diffusion measurements at two lags

In a one-dimensional diffusive window a measured displacement variance is 0.024 square micrometres at 10 ms and 0.064 at 50 ms. Assume long-time Brownian displacements plus independent frame-localisation variance per position measurement. Estimate \(D\) and localisation precision.

Fit slope and intercept separately

The variance slope is \((0.064-0.024)/(0.050-0.010)=1.00\) square micrometre per second, so \(D=0.500\) square micrometres per second. The intercept is 0.014 square micrometres, equal to twice the single-frame localisation variance. The implied rms localisation error is \(\sqrt{0.007}=0.0837\) micrometres. Forcing the fit through the origin at the shortest lag would give \(D=1.20\), more than twice the inferred value.

Acquire more lags to test the linear model. A static drift contributes a term growing as \(t^2\) if its mean displacement is not removed, while finite exposure can lower the physically measured short-lag variance. Two points always define a line and cannot independently validate either noise model.

Tutorial · Estimate a hidden slow tail in viscosity

A stress correlation is modelled as \(A[e^{-t/\tau}+0.02e^{-t/(40\tau)}]\). What fraction of its true area comes from the low-amplitude tail? How much does a cutoff at \(5\tau\) capture?

Weight a correlation by its duration

The fast and slow areas are \(A\tau\) and \(0.80A\tau\). The slow contribution is 44.4 percent of total. Integration to five fast times captures \(A\tau[(1-e^{-5})+0.80(1-e^{-5/40})]\simeq1.087A\tau\), or about 60.4 percent. Visual disappearance of the dominant peak therefore does not establish convergence of the transport coefficient.

The slow contribution must be supported by independent long blocks or a physically motivated model. A fitted tiny constant baseline can generate arbitrarily large apparent area if extrapolated indefinitely. Subtracting a sample mean estimated from the same short record can conversely suppress a genuine slow mode. Compare records much longer than the proposed tail time before trusting the extrapolation.

Tutorial · Is an algebraic tail compatible with ordinary diffusion?

Suppose a measured long-time velocity correlation remains positive and scales as \(t^{-\alpha}\). Determine when its Green–Kubo area exists and infer the displacement growth for \(0<\alpha<1\) and for \(\alpha=1\). Why would a finite periodic simulation require care?

Integrate the time-dependent slope before naming a diffusion coefficient

For \(\alpha>1\) the tail integral converges. For \(0<\alpha<1\), the running area grows as \(t^{1-\alpha}\), so displacement variance grows as \(t^{2-\alpha}\) under the stated persistent positive tail. At \(\alpha=1\), the running area grows logarithmically and the displacement variance is of order \(t\log t\). Sign oscillations, finite-size termination or another late-time crossover can alter these conclusions.

A periodic box admits only discrete long-wavelength modes and may cut off a hydrodynamic tail. Repeating at larger sizes and comparing the range before signals traverse the box helps identify a bulk regime. Unwrap particle coordinates crossing periodic boundaries before calculating a displacement. A wrapped coordinate can falsely saturate and imitate a confined particle even in a freely diffusing periodic fluid.
