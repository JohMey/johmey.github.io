Eutectic mixtures, freezing-point depression and boiling-point elevation

Adding a nonvolatile solute can lower the temperature at which a solvent begins to freeze. In a binary eutectic both species can solidify. The two observations follow from the same equality of chemical potentials, with different phases available for equilibrium.

Derive the boundary between liquid and a pure solid

At fixed pressure take a liquid ideal solution and immiscible pure solids A and B. Let \(x_i\) be a liquid mole fraction. The liquid potential is \(\mu_i^{l,*}+RT\ln x_i\) where the star denotes the pure liquid at the same temperature. Equilibrium with pure solid \(i\) requires \(\mu_i^{l,*}+RT\ln x_i=\mu_i^{s,*}\). At its pure melting point \(T_{m,i}\) the standard potentials coincide.

Use the Gibbs–Helmholtz identity \(d(\mu/T)/dT=-h/T^2\) at fixed pressure on the pure-phase difference. Assuming the molar enthalpy of fusion stays constant over the temperature interval gives

\[\ln x_i=-\frac{\Delta h_{{\rm fus},i}}{R}\left(\frac1T-\frac1{T_{m,i}}\right).\]

Cooling a liquid enters a two-phase region when one species first satisfies this equality. As that pure solid forms, material balance forces the remaining liquid composition along its liquidus. Both pure solids can coexist with the liquid only where the two predicted liquid mole fractions sum to one. The isobaric phase rule gives \(F=C-P+1=0\) at this three-phase point.

Read amounts before and after reaching the eutectic

Use model components with \(T_{m,A}=900\) K, \(T_{m,B}=700\) K and fusion enthalpies 14.5 and 10.0 kJ/mol. The eutectic lies at 569.65 K and liquid \(x_B=0.67493\). Start at overall \(z_B=0.40\). Solid A forms first. Just above the eutectic all B resides in the liquid, so its mole-basis amount fraction is \(z_B/x_{B,l}=0.593\) and the remaining 0.407 is already solid A.

At constant pressure the remaining eutectic liquid then converts isothermally into two solids as latent heat leaves. Below the eutectic the final amounts of pure B and A are 0.40 and 0.60 of the total moles. A sufficiently fast quench can supercool the sample or prevent equilibrium phase compositions. A micrograph alone does not locate the equilibrium temperature.

Reproduce with OneSimulation

Change one fusion enthalpy and predict which liquidus moves. OneSimulation solves the monotone composition closure equation. Check the returned composition against each single-component equation before interpreting the figure.

Run the downloaded notebook with OneSimulation, NumPy and Matplotlib installed. The output below was recorded with the displayed calculation.

"""Ideal liquid in equilibrium with two immiscible pure solids."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from onesim.numerics.nonlinear.kernels.local_solvers.inversion.bracketed_inverse import bisect_increasing

gas_constant = 8.314462618
melting_a, melting_b = 900.0, 700.0
fusion_a, fusion_b = 14500.0, 10000.0  # J per mol

def liquid_fraction(t, melting, heat):
    return np.exp(-heat / gas_constant * (1/t - 1/melting))

def fraction_sum(t):
    return liquid_fraction(t, melting_a, fusion_a) + liquid_fraction(t, melting_b, fusion_b)

temperature = float(bisect_increasing(fraction_sum, 1.0, 250., melting_b))
x_b = liquid_fraction(temperature, melting_b, fusion_b)
print(f"Eutectic temperature = {temperature:.2f} K")
print(f"Eutectic liquid mole fraction of B = {x_b:.5f}")
print(f"Composition closure error = {abs(fraction_sum(temperature)-1):.2e}")
ta = np.linspace(temperature, melting_a)
tb = np.linspace(temperature, melting_b)
plt.plot(1-liquid_fraction(ta, melting_a, fusion_a), ta, label="Liquid + solid A boundary")
plt.plot(liquid_fraction(tb, melting_b, fusion_b), tb, label="Liquid + solid B boundary")
plt.axhline(temperature, linestyle=":", color="grey", label="Three-phase temperature")
plt.scatter([x_b], [temperature], color="black")
plt.xlabel("Mole fraction of B")
plt.ylabel("Temperature (K)")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("physics-eutectic.png", dpi=160)

Expand the solvent equilibrium about its pure transition temperatures

Take a small mole fraction \(x_s\) of nonvolatile solute. For freezing into a pure solvent solid use \(x_A=1-x_s\) and \(\ln(1-x_s)\simeq-x_s\). Expanding the reciprocal temperature around the pure freezing point \(T_f\) gives

\[T_f-T_{\rm freeze}\simeq\frac{R T_f^2}{\Delta h_{\rm fus}}x_s .\]

For boiling into pure solvent vapour at fixed external pressure the equality is \(\mu_g^*=\mu_l^*+RT\ln x_A\). The entropy difference between vapour and liquid at \(T_b\) is positive. Expanding \(\mu_g^*-\mu_l^*\simeq-(\Delta h_{\rm vap}/T_b)(T-T_b)\) gives the opposite temperature shift.

\[T_{\rm boil}-T_b\simeq\frac{R T_b^2}{\Delta h_{\rm vap}}x_s .\]

In the dilute limit \(x_s\simeq m_s M_A\) with solute molality \(m_s\) in mol/kg and solvent molar mass \(M_A\) in kg/mol. For water, \(M_A=0.0180\), \(T_f=273.15\) K, \(\Delta h_{\rm fus}=6.01\) kJ/mol. A 0.10 mol/kg nonelectrolyte gives a freezing-point depression near 0.186 K. Using \(T_b=373.15\) K and \(\Delta h_{\rm vap}=40.7\) kJ/mol gives a boiling-point elevation near 0.051 K.

Recall · Count dissolved particles and choose the stable phases

Dissociation changes particle count. Activities replace simple mole fractions when solutes interact. Solid solubility requires a separate chemical potential for solute in the solid. A shift caused by nucleation delay depends on cooling rate and can be distinguished by comparing heating and cooling scans.

Tutorial on a cooling curve

1 · Follow a B-rich sample

Work through the solution

For \(z_B=0.80\) solid B forms first. Immediately above the eutectic all A remains liquid. The liquid amount is \((1-0.80)/(1-0.67493)=0.615\). A lever rule requires specifying whether compositions are mole or mass fractions.

2 · Estimate the apparent molar mass

Work through the solution

Dissolving 1.00 g of an ideal nonelectrolyte in 0.100 kg of water depresses freezing by 0.186 K. With \(K_f=1.86\) K kg/mol the molality is 0.100 mol/kg, so 0.0100 mol weighs 1.00 g and the molar mass is 100 g/mol.

Work through the solution

A liquid containing only 40% B cannot first precipitate solid B in the model when its liquidus is on the A side. Solids seen after complete equilibrium solidification can contain both A and B even though the first nuclei were solely A.
