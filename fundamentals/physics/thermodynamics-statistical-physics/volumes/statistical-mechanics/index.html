Fundamentals / Physics / Thermodynamics and statistical physics

Statistical mechanics from microscopic counting to material response

Count states, construct ensembles and derive macroscopic predictions. Use fluctuations, time dependence and finite sample size to test how well a thermodynamic limit describes a measurement.

    Contents

    1. Counting, absolute entropy and the entropy of mixing

      An ideal gas expands into an evacuated bulb and its temperature can remain essentially unchanged. What changed if a thermometer reports the same value? Begin by specifying a microscopic count, recover the pressure and temperature of the gas, and test the meaning of distinguishability by removing a partition between two samples. The calculation also explains why the classical formula for absolute entropy needs a microscopic action scale.

    2. Ensembles, thermodynamic potentials and measurable curvature

      A sample sealed inside an insulated container, a sample weakly coupled to a temperature bath and a surface exchanging particles with a reservoir need different probability laws. Derive the three laws from one composite-system count. Then use the curvature of their generating functions to predict noise, response and parameter uncertainty without assuming that microscopic quantities take their mean values in every run.

    3. Pair correlations, the virial gas and a pressure measurement

      A dilute nonideal gas can display almost ideal pressure while its particles strongly avoid one another at short distance. Connect a two-particle interaction to pressure, internal energy and a measurable pair correlation. Then use the resulting integral equations to ask which features of a potential can actually be inferred from a limited set of pressure readings.

    4. Rotational and vibrational populations in a molecular gas

      A pressure thermometer and a calorimeter do not count molecular degrees of freedom in the same way. A dilute diatomic gas can retain nearly ideal pressure while its heat capacity changes appreciably as discrete internal levels become populated. Build the molecular partition function, derive the rotor and oscillator contributions, and infer temperatures from line populations without treating spectral intensity as a direct state count.

    5. Bose condensation, dimensionality and a finite trap

      Cooling a gas of identical bosons can populate a single orbital macroscopically. The temperature at which that occurs depends on the low-energy density of states, not simply on the energy needed to put one particle in the first excited level. Compare a uniform box with a harmonic trap, estimate a condensate fraction and design an imaging test that separates occupation of a mode from a narrow but entirely thermal cloud.

    6. Degenerate fermions, heat and magnetic response

      An ordinary metal at room temperature contains electrons with characteristic kinetic energies of several electronvolts, yet its electronic heat capacity is small compared with the classical estimate. Identify which electrons respond to heat, compression and a weak magnetic field. Then compare three independent ways to determine a density of states and investigate what a disagreement would mean.

    7. Master equations, detailed balance and probability currents

      A two-state detector can switch at random while displaying reproducible occupation fractions and relaxation times. A three-state detector can maintain exactly constant occupancies while carrying a persistent directed current. Derive the probability dynamics, infer transition rates from timed records and decide what a stationary histogram can and cannot reveal about equilibrium.

    8. From binary collisions to the Boltzmann equation

      An initially anisotropic velocity distribution can relax even though every individual collision conserves energy and momentum. Describe a dilute gas with a phase-space density, derive the structure of its collision term and prove which distribution can stop producing entropy. Then examine the assumptions behind that proof and the measurements most likely to reveal their failure.

    9. Transport coefficients from equilibrium fluctuations

      How can a fluid in equilibrium reveal the diffusion constant that would govern a spreading drop, or the viscosity that would resist an applied shear? Derive an exact connection between displacements and velocity correlations, generalise it to stress and heat currents and test how a finite record can bias each number. The assumptions behind convergence matter as much as the prefactor.

    10. Finite-size criticality and the evidence for a phase transition

      A finite magnet in zero field can reverse between two equally magnetised directions. Its exact mean magnetisation is then zero even below the temperature where a bulk material orders. Learn how to extract order, correlation length and critical exponents from finite measurements, and which apparent crossings or peaks would survive the thermodynamic limit.

    11. Phase nucleation, metastability and waiting-time statistics

      A cooled liquid can remain liquid below its equilibrium freezing temperature and then transform abruptly. How can a bulk phase be thermodynamically favoured while a small embryo of it tends to shrink? Balance interfacial cost against bulk gain, convert a barrier into a probabilistic waiting-time prediction and test which measurements can distinguish surface nucleation from a bulk mechanism.

    12. Work fluctuations and equilibrium free-energy differences

      Repeated rapid changes of a laboratory control can give a broad distribution of work even when every run follows the same prescribed protocol. Derive why a particular exponential average retrieves an equilibrium free-energy difference, why the average work is generally larger, and why a finite experiment can miss the rare trajectories that make the equality true.

    13. Put a thermometer on a bounded spin spectrum

      A collection of identical two-level degrees of freedom has both a lowest and a highest energy. Count the accessible states at each energy and determine from adjacent entropy differences which way energy flows when two systems are brought into weak thermal contact.

    14. Follow a refrigerant through four devices and a gas through a liquefier

      A thermometer on a valve tells us little about the cooling capacity of a refrigerator. We need to follow one kilogram through its entire cycle and distinguish flash evaporation inside the valve from heat absorbed from the cold load.

    15. Eutectic mixtures, freezing-point depression and boiling-point elevation

      Adding a nonvolatile solute can lower the temperature at which a solvent begins to freeze. In a binary eutectic both species can solidify. The two observations follow from the same equality of chemical potentials, with different phases available for equilibrium.

    16. From reaction affinity to a reversible cell voltage

      An electrochemical cell transfers reaction free energy into electrical work through a controlled charge transfer. A measured voltage can therefore tell us about a reaction quotient, provided the electrodes are in reversible equilibrium.
