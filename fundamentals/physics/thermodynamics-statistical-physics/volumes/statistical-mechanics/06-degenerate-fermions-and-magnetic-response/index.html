Degenerate fermions, heat and magnetic response

An ordinary metal at room temperature contains electrons with characteristic kinetic energies of several electronvolts, yet its electronic heat capacity is small compared with the classical estimate. Identify which electrons respond to heat, compression and a weak magnetic field. Then compare three independent ways to determine a density of states and investigate what a disagreement would mean.

Count states and fill them with fixed particle number

A spin-half noninteracting fermion orbital of specified spin can be empty or occupied once. Its grand partition factor is \(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\), giving mean occupation \(f=1/[e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1]\). At zero temperature the occupations form a step. In a uniform three-dimensional box, fill both spin states at each wavevector up to radius \(k_F\). Counting reciprocal-space cells gives

\[n=\frac{k_F^3}{3\pi^2},\]
\[E_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m},\]
\[v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m},\]
\[D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E .\]

Here \(D\) counts both spin projections per unit energy for the whole sample. It obeys \(D(E_F)=3N/(2E_F)\). Distinguish this density from a density per volume or per spin, since factors of volume and two matter in susceptibility and specific heat. A lattice band may have a quite different dispersion and density of states; simply substituting the vacuum electron mass is a model to be tested for a given solid.

At \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\) electrons per cubic metre and the free electron mass, \(E_F=7.05\) eV, \(T_F=81{,}800\) K and \(v_F=1.57\times10^6\) m/s. Room temperature is only 0.00367 of this Fermi temperature. The gas is highly degenerate despite not being near absolute zero in everyday terms. These speeds are random occupied-state group velocities, with equal positive and negative contributions to net current in equilibrium. A metal with no applied field does not transport its entire electron density in one direction at that speed.

Integrating \(E D(E)\) to the Fermi level gives

\[U_0=3NE_F/5.\]

Volume differentiation at fixed \(N\), using \(E_F\propto V^{-2/3}\), gives pressure \(p_0=2nE_F/5\). Thus a nonzero kinetic pressure remains at zero temperature. It describes the electron subsystem and is balanced by other forces in a neutral solid. It is not the pressure one could read by attaching an ordinary fluid pressure gauge to a solid metal specimen.

Derive the low-temperature expansion without losing particle conservation

For a smooth function \(H(E)\), compare \(\int H(E)f(E)\,dE\) with its zero-temperature integral up to \(\mu\). Write the difference as contributions above and below \(\mu\), substitute \(x=|E-\mu|/(k_BT)\) and use \(1-f(\mu-k_BTx)=f(\mu+k_BTx)\). The difference then samples \(H(\mu+k_BTx)-H(\mu-k_BTx)\), so only odd derivatives of the smooth function contribute. Integrating the leading term gives

\[\int_0^\infty H(E)f(E)\,dE=\int_0^\mu H(E)\,dE+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2H'(\mu)+O(T^4).\]

This expansion assumes the Fermi level is many thermal energies above the bottom and that \(H\) varies smoothly through the thermal shell. Near a band edge or a very narrow density-of-states peak the assumption can fail even when temperature looks modest relative to some larger energy. Direct numerical quadrature retaining the correct band limits and a particle-number solution for \(\mu\) is then preferable.

For fixed \(N\), use \(H=D\) and demand that the result remain its zero-temperature value. The chemical potential shift is \(\delta\mu=-(\pi^2/6)(k_BT)^2D'(E_F)/D(E_F)\). For \(D\propto\sqrt E\) it becomes

\[\mu\simeq E_F[1-(\pi^2/12)(T/T_F)^2].\]

Next use \(H=ED(E)\) in the energy integral. Its \(T^2\) contribution from the shifted upper limit cancels the part involving \(E_FD'\), leaving

\[U-U_0=\frac{\pi^2}{6}D(E_F)(k_BT)^2,\]
\[C_V=\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2T D(E_F).\]

The typical number of thermally active state slots scales as thermal energy times \(D(E_F)\). This produces a heat capacity linear in \(T\), not proportional to the entire electron count times a temperature-independent classical factor. At 300 K in the example the electronic heat capacity is \(0.0181Nk_B\). Its volume coefficient \(C_V/(VT)\) is about 70.8 J per cubic metre per kelvin squared. A much larger lattice contribution may hide it at room temperature. At low temperature fit \(C/T\) against \(T^2\) to separate an electronic intercept from a Debye \(T^3\) lattice term where these asymptotic laws are valid.

Apply a weak field to the spin populations

For a weak uniform magnetic induction \(B\) neglect orbital coupling temporarily. Spin moments of magnitude \(\mu_B\) acquire energy offsets \(\mp\mu_BB\) for their two alignments in the simple electron model. At fixed total density and zero temperature, one spin population gains states at its Fermi surface and the other loses an equal number to first order. The density of states per spin is \(D(E_F)/2\); the difference in population is thus \(D(E_F)\mu_BB\). Magnetisation is this difference times \(\mu_B/V\), giving the weak-field SI susceptibility

\[M=\frac{\mu_B^2D(E_F)}V B,\]
\[\chi_P=\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\simeq\mu_0\mu_B^2 D(E_F)/V .\]

We have used \(B\simeq\mu_0H\) in a weakly magnetic macroscopic state and ignored demagnetising corrections. The result is approximately temperature-independent for a strongly degenerate smooth band. Independent localised moments would give a Curie susceptibility proportional to inverse temperature in their high-temperature weak-field regime. A small low-temperature upturn can therefore come from a small concentration of local moments even when most carriers remain itinerant.

For the earlier density \(\chi_P\simeq1.22\times10^{-5}\). A field of 1.0 T splits spin energies by \(2\mu_BB=0.116\) meV, vastly smaller than 7.05 eV. The fractional spin imbalance is \(3\mu_BB/(2E_F)\simeq1.23\times10^{-5}\). Tiny spin imbalance can produce a reproducible measurable magnetisation because the sample contains a macroscopic number of electrons. Linear susceptibility should be tested by field reversal. A hysteretic or saturating impurity background cannot be removed reliably by fitting one positive-field point.

At finite temperature the response replaces sharp \(D(E_F)\) by \(\int D(E)[-\partial f/\partial E]dE\) at the corresponding chemical potential. The negative occupation derivative integrates to approximately one and has width several thermal energies. This Fermi-window average is another reason the susceptibility and heat capacity emphasise nearby states rather than the entire filled band. Their precise kernels differ, so a rapidly varying band structure or interactions can alter their ratio.

Infer the same states through a density response

At zero temperature density varies with chemical potential as \(\partial n/\partial\mu=D(E_F)/V\). Since \(dp=n\,d\mu\) at fixed temperature, the electronic inverse compressibility \(1/\kappa_T=n^2/(\partial n/\partial\mu)=2nE_F/3\). In the example this corresponds to about 64 GPa. A compressibility measurement of the full solid includes lattice elasticity and charge-neutrality effects, so it cannot generally be substituted directly for an isolated electron-fluid result.

A confined electronic layer contacted through a gate can instead provide a thermodynamic density response through its quantum capacitance. If an induced charge increment per area is \(e\,dn\), part of the applied electrochemical voltage changes chemical potential by \(d\mu/e\). The corresponding capacitance per area is \(C_Q/A=e^2(\partial n_{2D}/\partial\mu)\). In a simple series combination the geometric dielectric capacitance and quantum capacitance both reduce the measured gate capacitance. Comparing a measured total directly to \(e^2D\) without removing the geometrical part can create an artificial density-of-states suppression.

Three independent measurements of low-temperature \(C/T\), Pauli spin response and a properly interpreted compressibility all probe the density of states in this noninteracting model. Their ratio then removes \(D(E_F)\) from some combinations. Correlations can renormalise spin response differently from specific heat. Orbital diamagnetism, closed-shell ions and temperature-dependent impurity moments must also be separated from a measured total susceptibility. A disagreement is an opportunity to distinguish these separate ingredients, not immediate evidence that state counting itself has failed.

A nonlinear zero-temperature spin response beyond Pauli's slope

At low magnetic fields the shift of either spin population is linear and nearly invisible in total number. To see where the susceptibility approximation comes from, retain the exact zero-temperature band populations. For a parabolic three-dimensional band with field-independent mass, absorb the two equal spin degeneracies into a coefficient \(A\) such that \(n_\pm=A[\mu\pm\mu_BB]_+^{3/2}\). The positive part enforces an empty spin band if its shifted edge lies above the chemical potential. The total \(n_++n_-\) must remain fixed in an isolated neutral sample, while \(M=\mu_B(n_+-n_-)\).

Expand both powers at small field. Linear terms cancel in the total density, so the chemical potential changes only at quadratic order. At fixed original Fermi energy the solution is

\[\mu=E_F-(\mu_BB)^2/(4E_F)+\cdots.\]

Population imbalance retains the linear term \(3\mu_BB/(2E_F)\) as a fraction of total density. This derivation checks why simply leaving \(\mu\) fixed gives the correct weak-field magnetisation slope yet fails in higher-order density calculations.

Complete spin polarisation in this model occurs when \(\mu-\mu_BB\) reaches zero. All particles then occupy the lower spin band, with effective Fermi energy relative to that band's shifted bottom equal to \(2^{2/3}E_F\). It follows that \(\mu_BB_{\rm pol}=2^{-1/3}E_F\). For a 7.05 eV metal this corresponds to roughly \(9.7\times10^4\) tesla, far outside the regime of an ordinary static bench measurement and outside a naive description of unmodified material bands. The number is a scale comparison, not a proposed magnet design. A system with a much lower Fermi energy or an enhanced effective magnetic moment can show nonlinearity at far lower applied fields.

A practical diagnostic compares positive and negative fields after subtracting the holder signal. Pure weak Pauli response is odd and linear in field with little temperature dependence. Localised impurity spins may also be odd but exhibit curvature or saturation when their Zeeman energy competes with temperature. A carrier density changing with field can alter the inferred Pauli slope. Therefore pair a magnetic measurement with a density or transport measurement before attributing every nonlinearity to spin-band depletion.

Extract a two-dimensional density of states from a gate measurement

Take a uniform conducting layer gated by a planar metal through an insulating spacer. An increment in gate voltage partly changes electrostatic voltage across the insulator and partly shifts the electronic chemical potential. In a simple uniform single-sheet model the charge-density increment \(dq=e\,dn_{2D}\) obeys \(dV_g=dq/c_g+d\mu/e\), where lowercase \(c_g\) is geometric capacitance per area. Writing \(c_q=e^2\,dn_{2D}/d\mu\) gives

\[1/c_{\rm meas}=1/c_g+1/c_q.\]

This derivation assumes a stable uniform equilibrium state and a calibrated voltage reference with negligible leakage.

Suppose independent dielectric calibration gives \(c_g=5.00\,\mu{\rm F\,cm^{-2}}\), while a low-frequency gate measurement yields

\[c_{\rm meas}=3.00\,\mu{\rm F\,cm^{-2}}.\]

The inferred quantum capacitance is \(c_q=7.50\,\mu{\rm F\,cm^{-2}}=0.0750\) F per square metre. Dividing by elementary charge squared gives \(2.92\times10^{36}\) states per joule per square metre, or \(4.68\times10^{17}\) states per eV per square metre. Confusing total sample capacitance with capacitance per area would produce a quantity that cannot be compared across different device sizes.

Sensitivity of the inverse step depends strongly on the ratio of the two measured values. If \(c_q\gg c_g\), the measured value approaches the geometric one. Small calibration offsets then dominate their reciprocal difference. For example, a measured value only one percent below \(c_g\) implies \(c_q\simeq99c_g\); an absolute error of one percentage point can remove any useful upper bound. In that limit the data support a lower bound on density of states rather than a precise central value.

Vary gate frequency to test whether traps or charge-relaxation delays contribute. A legitimate equilibrium compressibility response should be approached in the low-frequency limit under conditions where the electronic layer remains coupled to its reservoir. Interface states with slower charging can add a dispersive contribution that looks like extra density of states at one chosen frequency. Compare in-phase capacitance and loss, control for parasitic capacitance and measure several layer areas. Area-proportional capacitance and perimeter-dependent fringe capacitance can often be separated with devices of differing aspect ratio.

A differential heat pulse resolves the small electronic component

At low temperature a metal frequently has \(C_{\rm sample}=\gamma T+\beta_D T^3\) over a limited range. A platform and thermometer add their own heat capacities. A measurement might apply a known energy pulse and fit a temperature relaxation through a calibrated weak thermal link. To infer instantaneous heat capacity from \(Q/\Delta T\), the pulse must be small enough that \(C\) is nearly constant across the excursion and losses over the pulse negligible, or the energy balance must explicitly include them.

For a 10 mmol sample with \(\gamma=0.80\) mJ per mole per kelvin squared and \(\beta_D=0.10\) mJ per mole per kelvin to the fourth, molar heat capacity at 2 K is \(1.60+0.80=2.40\) mJ per mole kelvin. The sample heat capacity is 24.0 microjoules per kelvin. A 1.2 microjoule pulse would raise it by about 0.050 K neglecting heat leakage and addenda. The electronic fraction here is two thirds. At 4 K the lattice contribution exceeds the electronic term, changing which temperatures most strongly constrain the intercept in a \(C/T\) fit.

An exact small-range energy calibration uses the integral \(Q=\gamma(T_f^2-T_i^2)/2+\beta_D(T_f^4-T_i^4)/4\) times the sample amount. This avoids confusing a finite pulse average with the capacity at the initial temperature. Measure the empty platform through the same range and apply the same thermal model. A mismatch in thermal contact may create a second relaxation time and imitate sample heat capacity. Two pulses of different sizes and a delayed thermometer provide an independent test of the assumed lumped-temperature approximation.

The density of states inferred from \(\gamma\) measures excitations that can equilibrate during the experiment. A superconducting transition, magnetic ordering, a low-lying impurity contribution or a gap around the Fermi level would change the temperature dependence qualitatively. Rather than forcing a linear plus cubic fit over such structure, identify a normal-state range or include a separately justified physical term. A visually straight line on a narrow interval can hide curvature large enough to alter the intercept materially.

How a Fermi window rounds a sharp density-of-states edge

A two-dimensional band with a constant density of states starting abruptly at energy zero provides an exactly soluble compressibility edge. Differentiating its number equation gives \(dn/d\mu=D_0/[1+e^{-\beta\mu}]\) if \(D_0\) is per area. At zero temperature the response jumps from zero to \(D_0\) as the chemical potential crosses the band edge; at finite temperature it rises as a logistic function. The width between ten and ninety percent equals \(2k_BT\log9\), approximately \(4.39k_BT\).

At 10 K that intrinsic energy width is about 3.79 meV. At 30 K it becomes 11.36 meV. A gate experiment converts voltage to energy using a lever arm that may depend on the same quantum capacitance being measured. Calibrate that conversion before inferring temperature from edge width. Static disorder, inhomogeneous local band edges or electrical noise broaden the edge even at low temperature. Plot width against temperature and compare the full convolution model rather than interpreting a nonzero intercept as an electronic thermal floor without independent evidence.

This edge example shows exactly where a low-temperature expansion in smooth density derivatives fails. Every derivative of the constant band interior is zero, yet if the chemical potential lies within a few thermal energies of its abrupt edge there is a strong measurable density response crossover. Numerical integration of the full Fermi function or the exact analytic expression automatically retains this boundary. A calculation based solely on the local derivative of a density of states sampled within the band cannot recover it.

Separate Pauli spin response from independent local moments with a concentration test

A common experimental model of weakly magnetic metallic matter combines an approximately constant itinerant-electron susceptibility with a dilute Curie term \(C/T\) from paramagnetic impurities. At modest fields the local moment of independent spin-half sites with magnetic moment magnitude \(\mu\) has average \(m=\mu\tanh(\mu B/k_BT)\). Its small-field derivative gives volume susceptibility \(\mu_0 n_i\mu^2/(k_BT)\). The conduction-electron spin response instead scales with the density of states at the Fermi level. Two subsystems exposed to the same bath can therefore have markedly different temperature dependences.

Suppose two volume-susceptibility readings in SI units are \(2.20\times10^{-5}\) at 10 K and \(1.45\times10^{-5}\) at 40 K. A fit to \(\chi_0+C/T\) gives \(C=1.00\times10^{-4}\) K and \(\chi_0=1.20\times10^{-5}\). For independent moment magnitude \(\mu_B\), the implied impurity number density is \(Ck_B/(\mu_0\mu_B^2)\simeq1.28\times10^{25}\) per cubic metre. Relative to a carrier density of \(8.5\times10^{28}\) this is only about 150 parts per million. A small population can dominate the temperature-dependent change without representing most of the material.

Two points cannot verify the Curie form, so repeat across temperatures and fields. At sufficiently high \(B/T\) the independent moments approach saturation while the ideal degenerate electron subsystem remains approximately linear. At fixed \(B/T\), plotting the temperature-dependent residual should approximately collapse for independent moments. Interacting or clustered impurities can display a shifted Curie–Weiss temperature, blocking or history dependence, and a distribution of moments can broaden the saturation shape. A successful two-point subtraction alone does not establish a unique background.

A related check uses calorimetry. A split two-level impurity contributes a field-dependent Schottky anomaly when its gap is several thermal energies. Changing field shifts this anomaly, whereas the leading electronic linear coefficient of a simple normal metal varies much less at modest fields. The integrated entropy of an isolated two-state population tends toward \(n_i k_B\log2\) when both orientations become equally populated. A count inferred from susceptibility can therefore be tested by a separately integrated excess heat capacity, once lattice and electronic backgrounds are constrained.

Take care with units and signs when comparing papers or instruments. Some report molar susceptibility, others volume susceptibility, and cgs and SI magnetic conventions differ by factors involving \(4\pi\) and volume conversion. Divide a volume susceptibility by number density only after identifying whether the number refers to all host atoms, carriers or the inferred localised impurities. Agreement to a few significant figures after an unverified unit conversion has little diagnostic value.

A final test estimates when local spins should start to saturate at a given field. At 5 tesla and 2 K a magnetic moment equal to one Bohr magneton has \(\mu_BB/(k_BT)\simeq1.68\). The independent-spin alignment is \(\tanh(1.68)=0.933\), already close to saturation. In the same field the example metal's relative itinerant spin imbalance remains only around \(6\times10^{-5}\). A common high-field measurement can therefore discriminate two very different microscopic populations even if both looked nearly linear at much smaller fields. Remanent magnetisation on returning to zero would be inconsistent with both ideal noninteracting equilibrium models. It would motivate a check for ferromagnetic contamination, anisotropic barriers or hysteresis in the measurement apparatus before refining a fit to either simple susceptibility law.

Tutorial · Recover a density of states from a calorimeter

A sample exhibits a low-temperature molar heat capacity \(C/T=0.80\,{\rm mJ\,mol^{-1}K^{-2}}+bT^2\). Use the intercept to estimate the total density of electronic states per mole per electronvolt. Compare with the prediction for one conduction electron per atom and \(E_F=7.0\) eV.

Extract the electronic coefficient and convert energy units

In joules the total molar density is \(D=3(0.00080)/(\pi^2k_B^2)=1.28\times10^{42}\) states per joule per mole. Multiplying by the number of joules per electronvolt gives \(2.05\times10^{23}\) states per eV per mole. For one electron per atom the ideal value is \(3N_A/(2E_F)=1.29\times10^{23}\) states per eV per mole. The fitted effective density is therefore about 1.59 times larger. A molar heat capacity must be paired with a molar density of states; using a sample volume here without a molar volume would create a dimensional mismatch.

To decide whether the enhancement reflects a nonfree band, interactions or a wrong temperature window, examine the linearity of \(C/T\) versus \(T^2\) over several restricted fit ranges. A low-lying impurity or additional vibrational mode can produce curvature that changes the intercept. Compare with independent band information and spin response after relevant backgrounds are subtracted.

Tutorial · Determine a weak-field magnetisation and its background

At 1.0 T the measured volume magnetisation is 7.0 A/m, independent of temperature over a low-temperature range. The sample's predicted Pauli susceptibility is \(1.22\times10^{-5}\). Estimate the residual susceptibility if the magnetisation is linear and explain why its sign alone does not identify an orbital model.

Compare quantities in the same magnetic convention

One tesla corresponds to \(H\simeq7.96\times10^5\) A/m in this weakly magnetic setting. The predicted Pauli magnetisation is 9.71 A/m. The residual is minus 2.71 A/m or \(\chi_{\rm residual}\simeq-3.4\times10^{-6}\). Orbital diamagnetism of an ideal bulk free-electron gas supplies a negative contribution, but core diamagnetism, band orbital effects and sample-holder background can also contribute. Measure an empty holder and both field signs, consider specimen shape and calibrate the sample volume. A nearly temperature-independent negative residual does not uniquely identify any one mechanism.

Tutorial · Why a constant two-dimensional density of states behaves differently

For an ideal two-dimensional parabolic band of constant total density \(D_0\) above energy zero, derive the finite-temperature chemical potential at fixed \(N=D_0E_F\). Compare its leading low-temperature change with the power-law shift in three dimensions.

Evaluate the number integral before expanding

The integral gives

\[N=D_0k_BT\log[1+e^{\mu/(k_BT)}].\]

Solving yields

\[\mu=k_BT\log[e^{E_F/(k_BT)}-1]=E_F+k_BT\log[1-e^{-E_F/(k_BT)}].\]

Its deviation from \(E_F\) at low temperature is exponentially small, consistent with \(D'(E_F)=0\). A program that always imposes the three-dimensional negative quadratic shift would violate particle conservation in this band. The low-temperature heat capacity remains linear because \(D_0\) itself is nonzero.

At high temperature the same exact expression approaches the classical logarithmic chemical potential. Evaluate the low-temperature numerical form as \(E_F+k_BT\log(1-e^{-E_F/k_BT})\) to avoid overflowing the large exponential. Here numerical stability and correct dimensional density of states are independent requirements; solving one does not fix the other.
