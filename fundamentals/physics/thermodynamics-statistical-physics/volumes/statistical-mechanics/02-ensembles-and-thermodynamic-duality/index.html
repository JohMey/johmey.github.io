Ensembles, thermodynamic potentials and measurable curvature

A sample sealed inside an insulated container, a sample weakly coupled to a temperature bath and a surface exchanging particles with a reservoir need different probability laws. Derive the three laws from one composite-system count. Then use the curvature of their generating functions to predict noise, response and parameter uncertainty without assuming that microscopic quantities take their mean values in every run.

Expand the reservoir count rather than postulating a probability

Let a small system occupy a microstate \(i\) of energy \(E_i\) while a weakly coupled reservoir holds the remainder of a fixed total energy. The composite system is isolated. Conditional on \(i\), the number of compatible reservoir states is \(\Omega_R(E_{\rm total}-E_i)\). This weight is the same for degenerate system microstates of equal energy. For a macroscopic reservoir, expand its entropy about an appropriate reference energy. The first derivative is \(1/T\), and its second derivative at fixed other extensive parameters is \(-1/(T^2 C_R)\).

\[S_R(E_R-\epsilon)=S_R(E_R)-\frac{\epsilon}{T}-\frac{\epsilon^2}{2T^2C_R}+\cdots .\]

Exponentiating and taking the limit where the quadratic and further corrections are negligible yields \(p_i=e^{-\beta E_i}/Z\), \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\), and \(Z=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}\). A constant shift in all system energies multiplies both numerator and partition sum by the same factor. Relative probabilities are therefore independent of energy-zero convention. If a calculation sums over energy values rather than microstates, insert their degeneracies explicitly. These two equivalent ways of counting should never be applied simultaneously.

The derivation assumes weak coupling so that assigning energies separately makes sense, and a reservoir whose temperature does not respond significantly to typical exchanges. A small heat capacity in the bath can suppress sample fluctuations even at the same mean temperature. The relevant test concerns excursions explored by the sample, not simply whether the bath contains more particles. Near a transition or in an engineered small reservoir a canonical distribution may need a measurable correction.

Differentiate the normalisation to obtain energy and its variance

Differentiate \(\log Z\) with respect to \(\beta\) at fixed energy levels. The first derivative yields minus mean energy. A second derivative gives the mean square minus the squared mean. Changing differentiation from \(\beta\) to temperature gives the equilibrium heat capacity:

\[\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\log Z,\]
\[\operatorname{var}(E)=\partial_\beta^2\log Z=k_BT^2 C_V .\]

This proves nonnegative canonical heat capacity for a temperature-independent Hamiltonian. If the model Hamiltonian contains parameters that themselves vary with temperature after other degrees of freedom have been eliminated, the derivative also acts on those parameters. Inserting measured effective levels into the simple identity without that dependence need not reproduce the calorimetric capacity.

A two-level system has energies zero and \(\Delta\), with upper probability \(p=1/(1+e^{\beta\Delta})\) for equal degeneracies. Its variance is \(\Delta^2p(1-p)\) and \(C/k_B=(\beta\Delta)^2p(1-p)\). At \(\Delta/k_BT=2\), \(p=0.1192\) and \(C=0.420k_B\). In one thousand independent copies the expected upper count is 119 and its standard deviation about 10.2. An actual count of 130 is unremarkable. An experiment resolving only one sample at one time cannot identify the variance from its energy alone.

Higher derivatives of \(\log Z\) generate cumulants. A Gaussian approximation discards cumulants beyond the second. For a small two-level population near low occupancy the histogram is skewed and the approximation can yield artificial negative occupancies. An exactly binomial count or a direct discrete partition sum handles this elementary case without assuming that the central-limit regime has arrived. Sample-to-sample correlations can also prevent variances from scaling as the sum of independent variances.

Why the Helmholtz free energy knows the same equilibrium as entropy

Group the canonical sum by energy. A shell with entropy \(S(E)\) has weight proportional to \(e^{S(E)/k_B-\beta E}\). For a macroscopic system with a dominant smooth interior maximum this exponent is stationary at \((\partial S/\partial E)_{V,N}=1/T\). The leading free energy \(-k_BT\log Z\) is then the minimum of \(E-TS(E)\); the width of the peak gives the energy fluctuations. The exact finite-system integral is a Laplace transform. Replacing it by its stationary contribution is an approximation, often called the Legendre-transform limit.

An equivalent variational argument works for finite discrete states without a saddle point. Choose arbitrary trial probabilities \(q_i\), their mean energy \(U[q]\) and Shannon entropy \(S[q]=-k_B\sum_iq_i\log q_i\). Subtract the canonical free energy and insert the canonical weights. Algebra gives

\[U[q]-TS[q]-F=k_BT\sum_i q_i\log(q_i/p_i)\ge0 .\]

The final inequality follows from convexity of the logarithmic divergence and becomes an equality only for the canonical distribution on the permitted support. For an isolated system one instead maximises entropy subject to its prepared energy constraint. Helmholtz free-energy minimisation at fixed temperature is thus a consequence of reservoir exchange and normalisation, not an additional force sending every microstate to the lowest energy. At finite temperature high-multiplicity excited states can dominate the equilibrium probability.

Now include volume work in the fundamental differential \(dU=TdS-pdV+\mu dN\). Product differentiation gives \(dF=-SdT-pdV+\mu dN\), \(dG=-SdT+Vdp+\mu dN\), and \(d\Omega=-SdT-pdV-Nd\mu\) for \(\Omega=F-\mu N\). A potential's natural variables specify which gradient directly returns an observable. From \(F(T,V,N)\) one obtains

\[p=-(\partial F/\partial V)_{T,N},\]
\[S=-(\partial F/\partial T)_{V,N};\]

their cross derivatives agree if the potential is smooth. At phase boundaries use phase branches and appropriate coexistence conditions instead of differentiating through a kink as if it were smooth.

Let the reservoir exchange particles as well as energy

With both exchanges allowed, expand reservoir entropy in \(E\) and \(N\). Its differential includes \(-\mu_R dN_R/T\). A system microstate with \(E,N\) therefore acquires weight \(e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)}\). Summing normalises the grand canonical distribution. At fixed temperature and volume, derivatives with respect to \(\beta\mu\) give mean particle number and number variance. In a homogeneous equilibrium region with suitable thermodynamic limit, \(\Omega=-pV\).

\[\Xi=\sum_Ne^{\beta\mu N}Z_N,\]
\[\langle N\rangle=\partial_{\beta\mu}\log\Xi,\]
\[\operatorname{var}(N)=k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V}.\]

A surface of \(M\) independent adsorption sites, each empty or holding one particle with binding energy \(-\epsilon\), has \(\Xi=[1+e^{\beta(\mu+\epsilon)}]^M\). Its occupancy fraction is logistic and its variance \(Mf(1-f)\). At \(M=400\) and half coverage, the mean occupancy is 200 with standard deviation 10. The slope with respect to chemical potential is largest there. Near saturation, a small number variance and an apparently quiet detector indicate poor sensitivity to further increases of \(\mu\), not necessarily high precision in inferring \(\mu\).

Compare the adsorption response slope with the independently predicted number variance

The grand formula links number fluctuations to isothermal compressibility in a bulk one-component fluid. Write \(n=\langle N\rangle/V\) and use \(dp=n\,d\mu\) at constant \(T\). Then \((\partial n/\partial\mu)_T=n^2\kappa_T\) and \(\operatorname{var}N/\langle N\rangle=n k_BT\kappa_T\). A dilute classical ideal gas gives unity. These fluctuations refer to a region exchanging particles. If an observer counts all particles of a strictly sealed closed box, the number variance is zero even though its compressibility is not. The boundary condition of the measurement is part of the formula.

Infer a temperature and explain when it is identifiable

The logarithm of a measured population ratio is linear in \(\beta\) for nondegenerate known levels. With enough independent samples one can estimate temperature either from this ratio or by matching measured energy to its canonical expectation. If all observed states have nearly the same energy, changing \(\beta\) barely changes their probabilities. The available temperature information then becomes small, regardless of detector precision.

For independent canonical draws, differentiating a single log probability gives

\[\partial_\beta\log p_i=-(E_i-\langle E\rangle).\]

Its squared expectation is energy variance, the Fisher information per observation about inverse temperature. At \(n_s\) independent draws information adds. Equality between response and noise reappears as a statistical bound on temperature estimation. A long record with correlation time comparable to its length may contain few independent draws despite a large sample count. Recording more frequently without extending the observation window need not improve a temperature estimate.

A pressure reservoir selects enthalpy-weighted volume fluctuations

An isothermal piston in contact with a pressure reservoir supplies a fourth ensemble closely related to the first three. A configuration at volume \(V\) costs external work \(p_{\rm ext}V\) in addition to its microscopic system energy. At fixed \(T,N,p_{\rm ext}\), integrate the canonical configurational partition over possible piston positions with weighting \(e^{-\beta p_{\rm ext}V}\). For an ideal gas in a cylinder of fixed cross-sectional area the piston coordinate is proportional to \(V\), so its geometric measure is a constant times \(dV\). Mechanical kinetic factors independent of \(V\) cancel from its normalised distribution.

For \(Z_N(V)\propto V^N\), the probability density for volume is proportional to \(V^N e^{-\beta p_{\rm ext}V}\) on the positive real axis. Normalisation uses \(\int_0^\infty V^Ne^{-aV}dV=N!/a^{N+1}\). The maximum, mean and variance are

\[V_{\rm mode}=\frac{N}{\beta p_{\rm ext}},\]
\[\langle V\rangle=\frac{N+1}{\beta p_{\rm ext}},\]
\[{\rm var}(V)=\frac{N+1}{(\beta p_{\rm ext})^2}.\]

The finite-\(N\) offset between mode and mean comes from the skewed piston-volume distribution. The most likely volume obeys the usual ideal gas law evaluated using \(N\), while the exact mean differs in this simple piston model. At \(N=99\) the difference is about one percent and relative volume fluctuations are ten percent. At thermodynamic sizes the fractional difference vanishes. When calibrating nanoscale pressure from a histogram, a mean, a peak and a mean of reciprocal volume should therefore not be treated as interchangeable.

Check the force equilibrium directly. The instantaneous gas pressure conditional on \(V\) is \(Nk_BT/V\). The gamma distribution has \(\langle1/V\rangle=\beta p_{\rm ext}/N\), so its mean gas pressure equals the imposed external pressure exactly. Substituting the mean volume into the nonlinear pressure formula instead gives \(N p_{\rm ext}/(N+1)\). This calculation illustrates a general statistical rule: mechanical balance concerns the mean actual force, not necessarily that force evaluated at the mean position.

If the piston lies horizontally and dry static friction is appreciable, configurations can remain stuck with nonzero force imbalance. A distribution fitted to the equilibrium piston formula would then return apparent biases in pressure or particle number. Reverse the pressure change and vary waiting times to expose hysteresis, or remove the mechanical sticking before interpreting volume fluctuations as an equilibrium susceptibility.

A matrix of fluctuations controls coupled responses

Many experiments permit exchange of more than one extensive quantity. Write a general ensemble with natural control parameters \(\theta_a\) and weights proportional to \(e^{\sum_a\theta_a X_a}\), with a fixed set of microscopic state values \(X_a\). The first derivatives of the log normalisation give their means and the second derivatives give the full covariance matrix. Off-diagonal entries describe coupled responses. For the grand canonical ensemble choose \(\theta_1=-\beta\), \(\theta_2=\beta\mu\), \(X_1=E\), \(X_2=N\). One must keep the other natural parameter fixed while taking a partial derivative in this form.

A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite because the variance of any linear combination of the \(X_a\) is nonnegative. Consequently \({\rm var}(E){\rm var}(N)\geq{\rm cov}(E,N)^2\). A measured matrix violating this inequality cannot describe these two observables on the same equilibrium sample space. Possible causes include mixing variances and covariances from different sample preparations, subtracting a poorly estimated instrumental background or an inconsistent energy reference when counting particles.

Suppose a volume exchanges particles each carrying a fixed energy \(e_0\) plus other thermal energy. If the system energy reference shifts by \(a\) per particle, new energy is

\[E'=E+aN.\]

Its covariance with \(N\) changes by \(a\,{\rm var}(N)\), although no physical heat changed. A useful combination for isolating fluctuations uncorrelated with particle transport is \(E-bN\), with \(b={\rm cov}(E,N)/{\rm var}(N)\). Minimising the variance with respect to \(b\) gives residual variance \({\rm var}(E)-{\rm cov}(E,N)^2/{\rm var}(N)\), invariant under this reference shift. In a general nonlinear conditional distribution that residual is a best linear prediction variance, not automatically the exact variance conditional on a fixed integer \(N\).

For a unit-normalised example, suppose energy variance is \(25\epsilon^2\), number variance is 4 and their covariance is \(8\epsilon\). The best linear energy increment per exchanged particle is \(2\epsilon\), and the residual energy variance is \(9\epsilon^2\). Replacing energy by \(E-3\epsilon N\) changes the covariance to \(-4\epsilon\), but recalculating the residual returns the same value. A data-analysis code can use this reference-invariance as a check on both numerical units and susceptibility derivatives.

The same covariance structure controls simultaneous inference. If two state observables are nearly proportional over all sampled configurations, the log-likelihood curvature has a nearly zero eigenvalue. The individual fields conjugate to them are then hard to determine even if one linear combination is tightly constrained. Preparing the system in a region with different accessible states or adding an independent observable can resolve the degeneracy. Increasing the sampling rate on exactly the same narrow manifold cannot create a missing independent direction.

When the maximum-probability replacement loses a phase

The passage from a Laplace transform to a Legendre transform assumes one dominant smooth contribution or handles multiple branches explicitly. Near a first-order transition two energy ranges can have comparable canonical weights. Replacing the energy distribution by a single Gaussian around its mean then produces a state that may be relatively improbable, lying in the trough between two peaks. The finite partition sum remains smooth, but its derivatives can vary rapidly over a narrowing range of temperature.

In a simple two-phase approximation take branch partition contributions proportional to \(e^{-\beta F_1(T)}\) and \(e^{-\beta F_2(T)}\). Their ratio is \(e^{-\beta(F_2-F_1)}\). At coexistence the leading bulk free-energy densities agree. If their entropy densities differ, then a small temperature change produces an extensive branch free-energy difference proportional to volume times that temperature shift. The equal-weight finite-size window therefore narrows inversely with volume in this simplest picture. Interfacial states, boundaries and phase degeneracy can shift detailed crossing locations.

Consider an energy histogram with equal phase probabilities and sharply peaked branch energies differing by total \(\Delta E\). The mixture variance includes \(p(1-p)(\Delta E)^2=(\Delta E)^2/4\) in addition to within-phase fluctuations. As \(\Delta E\) is extensive this gives an extensive heat-capacity peak per particle in a finite coexistence window, much larger than the smooth single-phase contribution. This does not imply an infinite heat capacity throughout an interval of bulk temperature. The peak becomes higher and narrower and encodes latent heat in the thermodynamic limit.

An experimental or simulated time series can undersample one branch when interfacial barriers make switching rare. Estimating the variance from one branch then yields a completely different apparent heat capacity from a record containing a few switches. Compare heating and cooling preparations, identify branch-specific energy distributions and establish a reproducible equilibrium weight ratio before treating the union as a canonical histogram. In a macroscopic sample, calorimetric latent heat often provides a more reliable bulk quantity than a noisy attempt to observe whole-sample switching.

Use one equilibrium histogram to predict a nearby temperature

If a canonical run at inverse temperature \(\beta_0\) samples energies \(E_i\), a nearby expectation at \(\beta_1\) can be expressed as a ratio of weighted averages with factors \(w_i=e^{-(\beta_1-\beta_0)E_i}\). Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same constant allows one to subtract a reference energy to maintain numerical precision. Mathematically the ratio is exact when the original distribution covers every important configuration. Finite data can render an exact identity practically useless outside its overlap window.

For an approximately Gaussian energy histogram of standard deviation \(\sigma_E\), reweighting shifts its influential energy region by roughly \(-(\beta_1-\beta_0)\sigma_E^2\). Expressed in original standard deviations the shift is \(|\beta_1-\beta_0|\sigma_E\). A value of 0.3 stays near the observed centre; a value of five asks for configurations five original widths away. At the latter value a smooth computed curve can be governed almost entirely by one observation at the extreme edge of the histogram.

Inspect the effective weight count \((\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_iw_i^2\) alongside the raw record length. This statistic does not account for time correlation or prove that missing configurations are irrelevant, but a collapse to a handful of weights is a clear failure of overlap. Simulate or measure directly at a second temperature displaced toward the target and compare its actual histogram with the reweighted prediction. This connects the formal equivalence of ensemble descriptions to a concrete question of measurement coverage and uncertainty.

A finite-spin thermodynamic cycle tests mixed derivatives

Consider independent two-state magnetic moments with energies \(\mp\mu B\) in a prescribed induction \(B\). Ignore interactions and orbital response. Per moment the canonical sum is \(2\cosh x\), \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\). Its Helmholtz free energy is \(-k_BT\log(2\cosh x)\) and the average magnetic moment is

\[m=-\partial F/\partial B=\mu\tanh x.\]

The entropy follows from \(-\partial F/\partial T\), yielding \(S/k_B=\log(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x\). These component equations define an exactly soluble controlled system in which thermal and magnetic responses are coupled.

Equality of the mixed free-energy derivatives predicts

\[(\partial S/\partial B)_T=(\partial m/\partial T)_B.\]

Both are negative for positive field in this model. Increasing field at fixed temperature aligns spins and reduces their entropy. If the spin system is instead isolated reversibly, its entropy is constant, which requires \(x\) constant away from singular limiting cases. Reducing field then reduces temperature in proportion to field. This is the ideal paramagnetic demagnetisation argument, derived here from the canonical weights rather than an empirical cooling rule.

For an idealised system prepared at 3.0 K and 1.5 T, reducing the field quasistatically to 0.50 T under thermally isolated conditions gives 1.0 K. Its mean alignment fraction stays constant even as the Zeeman gap shrinks. A real specimen includes lattice heat capacity, residual interactions and inevitable heat leakage. If the lattice remains equilibrated with the moments, the total entropy includes both subsystems and the simple proportional temperature drop is modified. If spin–lattice exchange is too slow, a single temperature may fail to describe both during the field ramp.

A numerical test can use a rectangular loop in the \(T,B\) plane. Evaluate the change in \(F\) along one path using the integral of \(-S\,dT-m\,dB\), then return along the other path. At successively finer quadrature the closed integral should approach zero. By contrast heat and magnetic work evaluated separately generally have nonzero loop integrals and cancel only in their appropriate state-function combination. A nonzero free-energy residual that does not shrink under refinement points toward a sign error, an inconsistent field-energy convention or different energy references on the two paths.

Finally use the weak-field limit to estimate experimental sensitivity. There \(m\simeq\mu^2B/(k_BT)\), so a thermometer inferred from magnetisation is sensitive to both the magnetic-field scale and the independent number of active moments. Saturation measurements can calibrate their total number, while a field reversal removes a constant magnetometer offset. A mixed-derivative test based on independently measured calorimetry and magnetisation is more discriminating than fitting a temperature parameter to a single magnetic trace that already assumed the predicted population law.

The spin example also gives a direct thermal-noise measurement of susceptibility. For one moment the variance of its projection is \(\mu^2[1-\tanh^2x]\), and differentiating mean moment with respect to induction gives exactly this variance divided by thermal energy. At \(x=1\), magnetisation is 0.762 of saturation and variance is \(0.420\mu^2\). For a thousand independent moments the total rms fluctuation is about \(20.5\mu\), even though their mean total moment is about \(762\mu\). An instrument averaging over many reversals may see the mean accurately and suppress the variance; replaying the same averaging operation on a simulated equilibrium time series makes the comparison meaningful. A direct susceptibility inferred from a very large field modulation would instead sample the curved magnetisation over a finite interval. Use a small symmetric modulation and verify its amplitude scaling to compare with the local fluctuation slope.

Tutorial · Can a finite bath explain unusually quiet energy data?

A sample has canonical heat capacity \(60k_B\) at the mean temperature. Contact with a small reservoir reduces the measured equilibrium energy variance to three quarters of its canonical value. Approximate both entropies quadratically near their common equilibrium and infer the bath heat capacity. What other measurement could mimic the reduction?

Add the two inverse susceptibilities and check the observation

The joint log probability has curvature \(-[1/C_s+1/C_R]/(k_BT^2)\). Hence the effective variance is \(k_BT^2 C_sC_R/(C_s+C_R)\). Dividing by \(k_BT^2C_s\) and setting the ratio to \(3/4\) gives

\[C_R=3C_s=180k_B.\]

The temperature at maximum probability still agrees for bath and sample at leading order. The suppressed fluctuations reflect their shared total-energy constraint.

Finite temporal averaging of a fast energy signal can also suppress measured variance. Vary detector bandwidth and check convergence of the measured area, then repeat with a different independently estimated reservoir capacity. A corrected variance scaling with the harmonic combination supports the finite-bath interpretation. A single reduced number cannot distinguish thermodynamic curvature from the detector response.

Tutorial · Determine a volume response from temperature data

For a fixed amount of a smooth single-phase material near one operating point, the thermal expansion coefficient is \(\alpha\), the isothermal compressibility is \(\kappa_T\) and the volume is \(V\). Derive \(C_p-C_V\) from a Maxwell relation. Evaluate it for \(T=300\) K, molar volume \(1.0\times10^{-5}\) cubic metres, \(\alpha=6.0\times10^{-5}\) per kelvin and \(\kappa_T=1.0\times10^{-11}\) per pascal.

Eliminate the unmeasured mixed entropy derivative

Write \(dS=(C_V/T)dT+(\partial p/\partial T)_VdV\), using the Helmholtz mixed derivative. On the constant-pressure path \(dV=\alpha VdT\). Holding volume fixed in \(dV/V=\alpha dT-\kappa_Tdp\) gives

\[(\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha/\kappa_T.\]

Therefore \(C_p-C_V=TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T=1.08\) J per mole kelvin. Positive isothermal compressibility and positive temperature imply the difference is nonnegative even for negative expansion coefficient.

Tutorial · Infer two unknowns from occupancy measurements

At two distinct temperatures a set of dilute independent adsorption sites gives coverages 0.20 and 0.60. Why can these two readings fail to identify binding energy if the reservoir chemical potentials are not independently known? Express the unknown combination and suggest an experiment that would determine the binding energy.

Write an equation for each observation before counting parameters

The log odds satisfy

\[\log[f/(1-f)]=(\mu+\epsilon)/(k_BT).\]

The measured log odds are minus 1.386 and plus 0.405. They determine the value of \(\mu+\epsilon\) in each run multiplied by its known inverse thermal energy. With two unrelated reservoir potentials there are three unknown dimensional quantities and only two equations. Increasing coverage precision cannot resolve that degeneracy.

A separately calibrated reservoir supplies \(\mu(T,p)\) relative to a consistent energy reference. Then several pressures at one temperature verify the model and several temperatures can test a constant binding energy against an additional binding entropy or site degeneracy. A reported equilibrium constant alone can always absorb a reference-state shift; the reference reservoir and site standard state are part of a physically interpretable adsorption energy.
