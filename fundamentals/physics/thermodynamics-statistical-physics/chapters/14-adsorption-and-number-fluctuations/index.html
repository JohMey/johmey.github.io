Counting occupied sites on a surface

A surface with \(M\) identical sites exchanges particles with a gas. As the gas chemical potential rises, the coverage grows and eventually levels off. The same measurement also predicts the magnitude of number fluctuations, without estimating a time-dependent sticking rate.

Start with one site and a reservoir

An empty site has energy zero and population zero. An occupied site has energy \(-\epsilon\) with \(\epsilon>0\), and population one. Assume each site can bind at most one particle, the sites do not interact, and both states have unit degeneracy. A reservoir that supplies heat and particles assigns a state weight proportional to \(\exp[-\beta(E-\mu N)]\), where \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). One may recover the combination \(E-\mu N\) by expanding the reservoir entropy after removing energy \(E\) and particles \(N\), using \(T\,dS=dU-\mu\,dN\) at fixed volume.

\[\Xi_1=1+e^{\beta(\mu+\epsilon)},\]
\[\theta=P(1)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta(\mu+\epsilon)}}.\]

At \(\mu=-\epsilon\) empty and occupied are equally likely. A more negative chemical potential penalises bringing a particle out of the gas. Positive binding energy counteracts that penalty. The reference for both energies must be the same. For an ideal dilute gas at fixed temperature, changing the pressure from \(p_1\) to \(p_2\) changes the chemical potential by \(k_BT\ln(p_2/p_1)\). Thus doubling the pressure doubles the odds \(\theta/(1-\theta)\), even when the coverage itself grows only slightly.

Factorisation explains the noise

With independent sites every configuration has a product of one-site weights, so summing over all binary choices gives

\[\Xi=\Xi_1^M.\]

Alternatively group configurations by population \(N\). There are \(\binom{M}{N}\) ways to choose the occupied sites. Division by \(\Xi\) gives the binomial probability \(P(N)=\binom{M}{N}\theta^N(1-\theta)^{M-N}\). Since a binary variable satisfies \(n^2=n\), its variance is \(\theta-\theta^2\). Independent sites have zero cross-covariance. Adding their variances gives

\[\langle N\rangle=M\theta,\]
\[\operatorname{var}N=M\theta(1-\theta)=k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu}\right)_T.\]

For \(M=100\) at half coverage the mean is 50 and the standard deviation is 5 particles. Raising the odds to 9 gives mean 90 and deviation 3. On the steep part of the isotherm small changes of reservoir conditions produce the largest response. Thermal noise is largest there for precisely the same reason. A broad distribution near a sharp steepening can also signal collective behaviour, but the independent-site prediction provides a quantitative comparison before invoking interactions.

Measure a shift in binding energy

Imagine comparing two surfaces in the same chamber. Their temperature and chemical potential agree. If the second binds particles more strongly by \(k_BT\ln4\), it multiplies the odds by four. Where the first has coverage \(1/5\), its odds are \(1/4\), so the second reaches exactly one half. Move the binding-energy slider and identify the chemical potential at the centre of the curve. The plot also displays the number variance per site on the same dimensionless scale. At very small coverage the two curves nearly coincide because \(\theta^2\) becomes negligible.

Tutorial on recovering an isotherm

At pressure \(p_0\) a surface has mean coverage 0.75. How much must pressure change for coverage 0.90 at the same temperature? For 400 sites, calculate the initial and final root-mean-square fluctuations in coverage.

Work through the solution

The odds change from 3 to 9, requiring \(p=3p_0\). The variance of the fractional coverage \(N/M\) is \(\theta(1-\theta)/M\). Taking square roots gives 0.0217 initially and 0.0150 finally. These numbers measure instantaneous equilibrium fluctuations. The uncertainty of a time average additionally depends on how long successive readings remain correlated.

Infer two independent predictions from the probability of a completely empty three-site patch at half coverage. What fraction of frames show at least two occupied sites?

Work through the solution

Empty probability is

\[(1/2)^3=1/8.\]

Two occupied sites have probability \(\binom32/8=3/8\), and three have probability \(1/8\), giving one half for at least two. Non-binomial counts at fixed mean are a direct test of equivalence and independence of the sites.

Suppose every occupied site admits two internal states of equal energy while an empty site has only one. Determine the shift of the half-coverage chemical potential.

Work through the solution

The one-site grand sum becomes \(1+2e^{\beta(\mu+\epsilon)}\). Half coverage requires the occupied and empty weights to agree, so \(\mu=-\epsilon-k_BT\ln2\). Internal entropy shifts the measured midpoint without changing the exclusion of a second particle.
