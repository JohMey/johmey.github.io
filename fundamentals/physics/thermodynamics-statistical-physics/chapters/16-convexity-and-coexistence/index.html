Why two coexisting phases share a tangent

A material prepared with an intermediate composition can separate into two regions of different compositions. Its total composition is conserved. How can a free-energy graph predict both final compositions and the amount of each phase?

Minimise the free energy of a mixture

Work at fixed temperature and volume with a constant density of equivalent sites. Let \(f(c)\) be the Helmholtz free energy per site of a spatially uniform state with fraction \(c\) of species B. If the two regions have compositions \(c_1,c_2\) and the second contains fraction \(x\) of all sites, conservation requires

\[\bar c=(1-x)c_1+xc_2.\]

Neglect interface energy per site in the macroscopic limit. The mixture free energy per site is then the weighted sum of the two \(f\)'s, a point on the chord joining them.

Introduce a multiplier \(\mu\) and minimise \((1-x)f(c_1)+xf(c_2)-\mu[(1-x)c_1+xc_2-\bar c]\). Varying \(c_1,c_2\) when both regions are present gives

\[f'(c_1)=f'(c_2)=\mu.\]

This derivative measures the chemical work to replace an A particle by B. Varying \(x\) supplies the missing condition,

\[f(c_2)-f(c_1)=\mu(c_2-c_1).\]

The common slope therefore equals the slope of the connecting chord. A straight line must touch the free energy at both endpoints and lie below competing homogeneous states. Equivalently \(\int_{c_1}^{c_2}[f'(c)-\mu]\,dc=0\). This equal-area condition is the composition version of Maxwell's construction. For liquids plotted as pressure versus specific volume, the conjugate slope acquires the sign from \(p=-\partial F/\partial V\).

Find the coexistence and stability boundaries separately

Take \(f(c)=A[(c-m)^2-d^2]^2+\mu_0c\), with \(A>0\), \(m=0.5\) and \(0<d<0.5\). Subtracting the linear term tilts all trial chords by the same amount. At \(c=m\pm d\) the remaining term vanishes with zero derivative. Hence the common tangent is \(\mu_0 c\), and the coexistence compositions are \(m-d,m+d\). At \(d=0.30\) a sample of average composition 0.45 separates into 0.20 and 0.80 regions. Conservation gives \(x=(0.45-0.20)/(0.80-0.20)=5/12\) for the B-rich region.

Now perturb a uniform state into two equal parts at \(c\pm\eta\). Their average energy differs from the uniform energy by \(f''(c)\eta^2/2+O(\eta^4)\). Here \(f''=4A[3(c-m)^2-d^2]\). Between \(m-d/\sqrt3\) and \(m+d/\sqrt3\) infinitesimal fluctuations reduce the energy. Between either of these limits and the nearest coexistence endpoint a uniform state resists small fluctuations but a sufficiently large rearrangement can still lower the energy. This accounts for metastability and for the distinction between spinodal and coexistence boundaries. The uniform model does not predict a nucleation time because interfaces carry no energy in this construction.

Get a phase fraction from a graph

The display subtracts \(\mu_0c\) and measures energies in \(A\). Move the mean composition across the whole interval. Outside the coexistence window the stable composition is homogeneous. Inside, the endpoints stay put while the rich-region fraction changes linearly. Compare \(c=0.45\) to \(c=0.55\) for the same gap. Reflection symmetry exchanges the two phase fractions and leaves the free-energy saving unchanged.

Tutorial on a composition quench

A homogeneous sample is prepared at \(c=0.26\) with \(d=0.30\). Does an infinitesimal composition fluctuation lower its energy? What fraction of the final mixture occupies the B-rich phase in equilibrium?

Work through the solution

The lower spinodal lies at \(0.5-0.3/\sqrt3=0.327\), so the prepared state lies below it and has positive curvature. Nevertheless 0.26 lies between 0.20 and 0.80. Its equilibrium rich fraction is

\[0.06/0.60=0.10.\]

Local stability and the global free-energy minimum answer different experimental questions.

For \(m=0.50\), \(d=0.30\), compare free-energy gains of demixing at mean compositions 0.20 and 0.50 after subtracting the tilt. Report the gain in units of \(A\).

Work through the solution

At a coexistence endpoint the pure homogeneous material already lies on the common tangent and has zero gain. At the midpoint the homogeneous energy exceeds the tangent by \(d^4 A=0.0081A\) per site. The equilibrium mixture uses equal amounts of the two compositions. The gain tends continuously to zero at either endpoint.

Would adding \(b+mc\) to the free energy change the two selected compositions or spinodals? What would change in the tangent construction?

Work through the solution

Conservation fixes the mean composition, so the new term contributes \(b+m\bar c\) to every candidate mixture at that mean. The minimiser is unchanged. Second derivatives are unchanged, preserving spinodals. Both endpoint tangent slopes, and thus the chemical-potential difference assigned to coexistence, shift by \(m\).
