Can a fluctuation reveal attraction on a surface?

Two surfaces have identical mean coverage at a chosen gas pressure. Repeated microscopy images nevertheless show different event-to-event scatter in the number of occupied sites. Determine whether interactions can account for the excess and design a measurement that distinguishes clustering from instrumental drift.

Predict the independent-site baseline

Each image samples 100 equivalent sites in thermal equilibrium. At half coverage, predict the mean occupied population, its standard deviation and the slope of the mean population with respect to \(\beta\mu\). What changes if independent sites have binding energies larger by \(k_BT\ln3\) at fixed \(\mu\)?

Work through the solution

At \(\theta=1/2\), mean population is 50 and standard deviation \(\sqrt{100/4}=5\). The number fluctuation identity gives slope 25. Multiplying the original odds by three gives coverage \(3/4\), mean 75, variance \(100(3/4)(1/4)=18.75\) and standard deviation 4.33. A change of mean binding at fixed coverage, compensated by the chemical potential, cannot by itself enhance the independent-site half-coverage variance.

Map an interacting row to spins

Model the surface as a ring of sites with occupancies \(n_j=0,1\), attraction \(-w n_jn_{j+1}\) per occupied pair, and grand-energy contribution \(-(\epsilon+\mu)n_j\) per site. Put \(n_j=(1+s_j)/2\). Determine the Ising exchange \(J\) and the chemical potential that gives zero Ising field.

Work through the solution

The pair product becomes \([1+s_j+s_{j+1}+s_js_{j+1}]/4\). On a periodic row each site enters two pair terms. The exchange is \(J=w/4\) and the field coefficient in \(-H\sum_j s_j\) is \(H=(w+\epsilon+\mu)/2\). Choosing \(\mu=-\epsilon-w\) gives \(H=0\), and spin reversal then enforces mean occupation \(1/2\). Pair attraction can therefore be hidden by a shift of the half-coverage chemical potential.

Use the correlation length to predict number noise

Let \(w=4k_BT\) at the adjusted half-coverage point. Calculate the correlation at one and ten bonds and the predicted population deviation for 100 sites. Compare to the independent-site prediction.

Work through the solution

Here \(J/(k_BT)=1\), \(q=\tanh1=0.76159\), and \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle\simeq q^r\), giving 0.7616 and 0.0657. Since \(n=(1+s)/2\), occupancy covariance is \(q^r/4\). Summing the covariance over all site pairs for a ring much longer than \(-1/\ln q=3.67\) gives

\[\operatorname{var}N\simeq(100/4)[1+2\sum_{r=1}^\infty q^r]=25(1+q)/(1-q)=184.7.\]

The deviation is 13.59 sites. Positive spatial covariances account for the extra noise.

Check whether the observation is spatial or temporal

Repeat the experiment with eight sites on a ring. Derive the variance from the finite-ring correlation \(C_r=(q^r+q^{8-r})/(1+q^8)\). What measurements would test the interpretation of the excess variance on the larger sample?

Work through the solution

Translational invariance gives \(\operatorname{var}N=(8/4)\sum_{r=0}^{7} C_r=2(1+q)(1-q^8)/[(1-q)(1+q^8)]\), approximately 11.77 at \(q=\tanh1\). Report a standard deviation of about 3.43 sites. Measure occupancy covariance against spatial separation and repeat after changing the delay between images. Surface correlations predict a distance-dependent decay within individual images. Temporal drift common to all sites can instead give a substantial long-range component and serially correlated frame means.
