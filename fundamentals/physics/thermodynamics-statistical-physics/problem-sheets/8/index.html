Count atoms in a trap and predict the onset of condensation

A dilute cloud of bosons is confined by three harmonic restoring forces. Cooling moves particles between one-particle states while the total atom number stays constant. Start with counted lattice points in quantum-number space and predict the temperature at which the excited states can no longer hold the whole cloud.

Count levels rather than ordering them one by one

Measure energy from the single-particle ground state. Suppose the angular frequencies are \(\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z\). For energies far above one quantum, recover the density of levels \(g(E)\) by finding the volume of the allowed region in \((n_x,n_y,n_z)\). Check the dimensions. Should one divide by another factorial for indistinguishable atoms?

Work through the solution

Each nonnegative integer triple is a distinct one-particle state. Its excitation energy equals \(\hbar(\omega_x n_x+\omega_y n_y+\omega_z n_z)\). States below energy \(E\) fill a tetrahedron with three intercepts \(E/(\hbar\omega_i)\). Its volume is one sixth of their product. Differentiating the integrated count supplies a density per unit energy.

\[G(E)\simeq\frac{E^3}{6\hbar^3\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z},\]
\[g(E)\simeq\frac{E^2}{2(\hbar\bar\omega)^3},\]
\[\bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}.\]

The factor of six came from geometry. Indistinguishability enters later in the count of occupations, through the Bose distribution. Dividing the one-particle density by a factorial of atom number would change the number of available orbitals when one added a second particle. The continuous tetrahedron loses information on its faces and edges, so the estimate is best when \(k_BT\) spans many quanta in every direction.

Find how many atoms the excited cloud can hold

The Bose occupation is \(1/[\exp((E-\mu)/k_BT)-1]\). The chemical potential measured from the lowest state approaches zero from below as the population grows. Evaluate the excited-state population at this limit and determine the condensed fraction as a function of temperature. Explicitly account for the state at zero.

Work through the solution

The smooth density vanishes at zero and loses the one discrete ground orbital. Retain its population separately, \(N=N_0+N_{\rm ex}\). Insert the smooth density only for the excited contribution. Put \(x=E/(k_BT)\). The integrand contains \(x^2/(e^x-1)\). Expanding the reciprocal as \(\sum_{\ell\ge1}e^{-\ell x}\), integrate each nonnegative term. Its integral is \(2/\ell^3\). The result is

\[N_{\rm ex}^{\max}=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\bar\omega}\right)^3,\]
\[T_c=\frac{\hbar\bar\omega}{k_B}\left(\frac{N}{\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3}.\]

Below this temperature the excited cloud has the stated capacity and the balance occupies the ground state. Hence

\[N_0/N=1-(T/T_c)^3.\]

Above \(T_c\) the chemical potential is negative and one must solve a number constraint. Extending the cubic formula above the transition would give a negative condensate fraction. This is an especially clear example of a constraint selecting which branch of a calculation has physical meaning.

A trap calibration as a numerical example

Use \(N=100000\) and cyclic frequencies \(f_x=f_y=80\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(f_z=160\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Compute \(T_c\), the ideal fraction at 140 nK and the result of halving only the axial frequency. Distinguish the quantities kept fixed in these two comparisons.

Work through the solution

Since \(\hbar\omega=hf\), the geometric-mean cyclic frequency is \(100.79\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(h\bar f/k_B=4.84\,\mathrm{nK}\). With \(\zeta(3)=1.20206\) the dimensionless temperature multiplier is about 43.65. Thus \(T_c\) is about 211 nK and the fraction at 140 nK is about 0.71. Halving one frequency at fixed particle number lowers \(T_c\) by \(2^{-1/3}\) to roughly 168 nK. At the same absolute temperature the ideal fraction then falls to about 0.42. At a fixed ratio \(T/T_c\) both clouds would have the same fraction.

The same equations predict the energy below \(T_c\). Multiplying the occupation integrand by \(E\) changes the sum to \(6\zeta(4)\). Thus \(U=3\zeta(4)(k_BT)^4/(\hbar\bar\omega)^3\), measured relative to all atoms in the ground state. Differentiating at fixed trap gives

\[C=12k_B\zeta(4)(k_BT/\hbar\bar\omega)^3.\]

The population and energy probe different moments of the same density of states. A wrong power of energy in the state count changes both predictions.

Test what a continuum approximation misses

Use the isotropic trap experiment below. For frequency \(f\), level \(n\) has energy \(nhf\). Obtain its exact degeneracy before comparing the discrete excited-state sum with the continuum. Vary \(N\) and the frequency separately. Interpret the crossing of each capacity curve with one.

Open this experiment online

Work through the solution

For each \(n_x=0,\ldots,n\), there are \(n-n_x+1\) choices of \(n_y\). Summing gives \((n+1)(n+2)/2\). The total capacity sums this degeneracy times \(1/[\exp(nhf/k_BT)-1]\) for \(n\ge1\). At a given temperature the faces and edges of the lattice supply positive state-count corrections to the leading volume estimate. Consequently the discrete capacity reaches \(N\) at a lower temperature. Increasing \(N\) makes the fractional correction smaller. Changing \(f\) rescales the temperature axis in kelvin while leaving the discrete curve versus \(T/T_c\) unchanged at fixed \(N\). The reported discrete crossing is a saturation convention for a finite ideal sample. A finite set of atoms has a smooth occupation change and interactions introduce further shifts.

Compare the trap with a gas in a box by reading the quantum-gas chapter in David Tong’s statistical physics course. Repeating the level count for a box changes the power of energy in the density of states.
