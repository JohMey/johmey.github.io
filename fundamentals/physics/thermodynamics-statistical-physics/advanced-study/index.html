State, process and the first law for a closed system

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Distinguish properties from path-dependent transfers and determine heat and work for a piston-cylinder process.

From first principles

Obtain closed-system work from the motion of a boundary

Choose a fixed mass of gas enclosed by a piston and cylinder. No mass crosses this boundary. The first law is the empirical balance of energy: with heat positive into the gas and work positive out, change of internal, kinetic and potential energy equals net heat minus net work. For a piston moving normally through \(dx\) against a uniform resisting pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\), force times displacement gives

\[\delta W_b=p_{\rm ext}A\,dx=p_{\rm ext}\,dV,\]
\[\Delta(U+KE+PE)=Q-W .\]

For slow internal equilibration with tiny pressure differences, one can use gas pressure in the integral. If bulk kinetic and potential changes are negligible and no shaft or electrical work crosses the boundary, the integrated heating requirement is

\[Q_{12}=U_2-U_1+\int_{V_1}^{V_2}p_{\rm ext}\,dV .\]

The area under the specified pressure–volume path is therefore part of the heat calculation. For a rigid vessel it vanishes. For a constant-pressure piston heating an ideal gas, \(p\Delta V=mR\Delta T\) adds to the internal-energy change. The end temperatures alone determine the ideal-gas internal-energy change, while the moving boundary determines the work.

Derivation and applications

Determine the heat input when a spring loads the piston

A freely sliding piston does not necessarily hold the gas pressure constant. Attach a linear spring to a frictionless piston of area \(A\) and let its resisting force increase as the gas expands. Consider a closed mass \(m\) of ideal gas, heated slowly enough that piston force balance holds at each intermediate state. Neglect piston kinetic energy, heat leakage and changes of elevation except as already included in the constant external pressure \(p_1\). At the initial position the gas is in equilibrium at absolute pressure \(p_1\) and volume \(V_1\).

The displacement \(x\) produces a volume increment \(Ax\) and an extra resisting force \(kx\). Dividing by piston area gives the pressure as a function of the evolving volume:

\[V-V_1=Ax,\]
\[p(V)=p_1+\frac{k}{A^2}(V-V_1).\]

Boundary work is performed at this changing external pressure. Integrating the straight-line path over the swept volume \(\Delta V=V_2-V_1\) separates work against the initial pressure from work stored in the spring:

\[W_{12}=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}p(V)\,dV=p_1\Delta V+\frac{k}{2A^2}(\Delta V)^2.\]

For constant specific heat \(c_v\) and specific gas constant \(R\), the first law gives the heat required, positive into the gas. State temperatures follow from the ideal-gas relation, including the final spring pressure:

\[Q_{12}=m c_v(T_2-T_1)+W_{12},\]
\[T_i=p_iV_i/(mR).\]

Only the temperature change determines the internal-energy change; the mechanical law selects the work term. Setting \(k=0\) recovers expansion against constant pressure. Abrupt heating that accelerates the piston would invalidate the assumed quasistatic pressure path.

Worked example

Calculate the heater input for an air cylinder

Take \(m=0.0100\) kg, \(V_1=0.0100\) m³, \(p_1=100\) kPa, \(A=0.0100\) m² and \(k=1000\) N/m. Increase volume by \(0.00200\) m³. The final pressure is \(120\) kPa; the work is \((100+120)(0.00200)/2=0.220\) kJ. For \(R=0.287\) and \(c_v=0.718\) kJ/(kg K), \(\Delta T=(1.440-1.000)/(0.0100\times0.287)=153\) K. The gas receives \(1.10+0.220=1.32\) kJ of heat.

Second application

Separate actuator work from stored spring energy

On the same stroke the piston travels \(0.200\) m. The initially unloaded incremental spring stores \(kx^2/2=20.0\) J, exactly the triangular part of the pressure–volume work. The constant load receives \(200\) J. If the spring were removed while retaining the same initial state and swept volume, the final pressure would stay at \(100\) kPa and the temperature rise would be only \(69.7\) K. The two processes end at different thermodynamic states despite having the same final volume.

Decide what crosses the boundary

Draw a boundary around the gas in a piston cylinder. If no gas crosses its surface, the system is closed even though the piston moves. A rigid vessel is a special closed system with fixed volume. An insulated wall prevents heat transfer; a shaft or electrical lead can still transmit work through it. These descriptions impose different constraints and should be identified before choosing an equation.

At equilibrium a simple compressible substance can be described using independent state variables such as temperature and specific volume. Pressure \(p\), volume \(V\), temperature \(T\) and internal energy \(U\) are properties. An equation of state relates some of them. For a dilute ideal gas \(pV=mRT\), where \(R\) is the specific gas constant. Always use absolute pressure and absolute temperature in this relation. Two initial and final states determine changes in properties, but do not specify the heat and work transfers between them.

Construct the process energy balance

Take work done by the system as positive and heat entering it as positive. The first law is \(\Delta U+\Delta KE+\Delta PE=Q-W\). For a stationary vessel ignore changes in kinetic and gravitational energy of the system as a whole. A moving piston of area \(A\) against external pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\) performs incremental work \(p_{\rm ext}A\,dx=p_{\rm ext}dV\). For a quasistatic mechanically reversible motion the boundary pressure can be represented by the gas pressure throughout the process.

If gas expands from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) at constant external pressure, \(W=p_{\rm ext}(V_2-V_1)\). If it instead follows a reversible isothermal ideal-gas path, inserting \(p=mRT/V\) and integrating gives \(W=mRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\). Both expressions have units of energy. Their different volume dependence makes a pressure-volume diagram useful: the area under each pressure path has a direct work interpretation when its boundary-force assumptions hold.

Heat air below a freely moving piston

A weight-loaded, frictionless piston keeps 0.50 kg of ideal air at 150 kPa absolute while a heater raises its temperature from 300 to 360 K. Use \(R=0.287\) and \(c_v=0.718\) kJ/(kg K), treated as constant. The initial and final volumes are \(mRT/p=0.287\) and 0.3444 m³. Boundary work is \(150(0.0574)=8.61\) kJ. For this gas the internal-energy change is \(mc_v\Delta T=0.50(0.718)(60)=21.54\) kJ.

The required heat is \(Q=\Delta U+W=30.15\) kJ. The same result follows from \(mc_p\Delta T\) because for this constant-pressure ideal-gas process with only boundary work \(c_p=c_v+R=1.005\) kJ/(kg K). The extra heat above the internal-energy increase moved the piston weight. Using \(Q=mc_p\Delta T\) without inspecting the constraint would give the wrong heat input to a rigid tank.

Compare a rigid-wall experiment

Lock the piston at its initial volume before heating an identical mass from 300 to 360 K. Now \(W=0\), so only 21.54 kJ of heat is needed. The final pressure is \(150(360/300)=180\) kPa. Equal temperature changes in an ideal gas imply equal changes in internal energy, even though final pressures and energy transfers differ. For a real fluid near a phase boundary, constant ideal-gas heat capacities may fail; use tabulated state properties or a suitable equation of state. In either case the first-law accounting is unchanged once the initial and final internal energies and the actual boundary work are known.

A different path between specified volumes

An ideal gas initially at 300 kPa and 0.020 m³ expands quasistatically and isothermally to twice its initial volume. Since \(pV=p_1V_1\), work out is \(W=p_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)=6\ln2=4.16\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Ideal-gas internal energy depends only on temperature, so \(\Delta U=0\) and heat entering equals 4.16 kJ. A freely expanding ideal gas in an insulated rigid outer vessel can reach the same final temperature and occupied volume while doing zero boundary work. The state variables alone do not record which process occurred.

Determine heat and work along a measured compression path

A measured pressure–volume trace contains more information than its endpoints. For some slow piston processes an empirical polytropic law \(pV^n=\text{constant}\) fits the path. Here \(n\) is a fitted process exponent and need not equal the gas heat-capacity ratio. Assume a closed cylinder with negligible kinetic and potential energy change, a quasistatic pressure load and a calorically perfect ideal gas. Work out of the gas is the area \(W=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}p\,dV\), which is negative for compression. Substitute \(p=p_1V_1^n V^{-n}\) and integrate:

\[W=\frac{p_2V_2-p_1V_1}{1-n}\quad(n\ne1),\qquad W=p_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)\quad(n=1).\]

The logarithmic case is the continuous limit as \(n\) approaches one. The ideal-gas equation also gives \(T_2/T_1=(V_1/V_2)^{n-1}\), so \(\Delta U=mc_v(T_2-T_1)\). With \(Q-W=\Delta U\) and \(pV=mRT\), the heat input is \(Q=m[c_v+R/(1-n)](T_2-T_1)\) for \(n\ne1\). Setting \(n=\gamma=c_p/c_v\) eliminates \(Q\). This inference relies on the ideal-gas, constant-property, quasistatic assumptions. An arbitrary irreversible compression with the same endpoints cannot be called isentropic from the fitted exponent alone.

Recover a cylinder heat loss from three measurements

A test traps air at \(p_1=100\,\mathrm{kPa}\) absolute, \(V_1=0.0100\,\mathrm{m^3}\), \(T_1=300\,\mathrm K\), then compresses it to \(V_2=0.0040\,\mathrm{m^3}\). The fitted exponent is 1.25. Using \(R=0.287,\ c_v=0.718\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\), the trapped mass is \(p_1V_1/(RT_1)=0.01161\,\mathrm{kg}\). The predicted pressure is \(100(2.5)^{1.25}=314.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\), and \(T_2=300(2.5)^{0.25}=377.2\,\mathrm K\). Note that absolute pressure is required in both the fit and the gas law. A pressure transducer referenced to atmosphere would need its reference restored.

The gas does \(W=[314.4(0.0040)-100(0.0100)]/(1-1.25)=-1.03\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Its internal energy rises by \(0.01161(0.718)(77.2)=0.644\,\mathrm{kJ}\), giving \(Q=\Delta U+W=-0.386\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Compression work enters the gas while part leaves as heat through the cylinder wall. If the process were isothermal at 300 K, the same volume change would require only \(-W=0.916\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and the full amount would leave as heat. A mid-stroke measurement gives a stronger check than the end pressure: the fitted law predicts \(100(0.010/0.007)^{1.25}=156.2\,\mathrm{kPa}\) at 7.0 litres. A substantial discrepancy would favour direct numerical integration of the measured trace over using a single exponent.

An apparent exponent can also be estimated directly from pressure ratios: \(n=\ln(p_2/p_1)/\ln(V_1/V_2)\). Compare estimates from the first and second halves of the stroke. Agreement means the same log–log slope describes both halves, while disagreement may indicate changing heat transfer, leakage or phase lag between the pressure and position sensors. It does not identify which of those mechanisms is responsible. As a separate check, suppose a water jacket gains the estimated 0.386 kJ each stroke and the compressor completes ten such strokes per minute. The mean heat removal from these strokes would be \(0.386(10)/60=0.0643\,\mathrm{kW}\). A jacket measurement of the complete machine includes intake and exhaust processes and mechanical friction as well, so experimental comparison requires either an isolated compression interval or a balance around the rest of the cycle.

A change in operating speed can also change the fitted exponent because it changes the time available for heat exchange during a single compression stroke.

Open systems, enthalpy and steady-flow devices

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Use an inlet-outlet balance to distinguish shaft work, kinetic-energy conversion and pressure reduction.

From first principles

Derive the steady-flow energy balance including flow work

Surround a turbine, nozzle or mixing chamber with a stationary control surface. In addition to transporting its internal, kinetic and gravitational energies, an entering fluid is pushed across the inlet by upstream pressure. A mass increment \(dm\) occupies volume \(v\,dm\) and requires pressure work \(pv\,dm\) to enter. The outlet carries the corresponding work away. Combining internal energy \(u\) and flow work defines enthalpy:

\[h=u+pv,\]
\[e_{\rm stream}=h+V^2/2+gz .\]

Here \(V\) denotes speed, \(v\) specific volume and \(z\) elevation. In steady operation, sum these stream contributions and include heat into and shaft work out of the control volume. For one inlet and one outlet with equal mass rate, rearrangement gives

\[\dot Q-\dot W_s=\dot m\,[h_2-h_1+(V_2^2-V_1^2)/2+g(z_2-z_1)] .\]

All terms have units of power. A nozzle can accelerate flow without shaft work by lowering static enthalpy. A throttle with no heat, no shaft work and negligible speed and elevation changes is instead approximately isenthalpic. Omitting the pressure work while also using internal energy would miss both conclusions.

Derivation and applications

Convert a nozzle energy balance into a mass-flow limit

A steady nozzle converts static enthalpy into bulk kinetic energy. A converging passage has a maximum possible mass flow at a specified inlet stagnation state. Derive this for a steady, one-dimensional, adiabatic ideal gas with constant \(c_p\) and \(\gamma\), reversible acceleration and negligible inlet-reservoir velocity. Write reservoir conditions as \(T_0,p_0\) and the local static conditions as \(T,p\). With no shaft work the energy balance gives

\[c_pT_0=c_pT+u^2/2,\]
\[u=\sqrt{2c_pT_0(1-T/T_0)} .\]

Isentropic acceleration links temperature and pressure. Let \(r=T/T_0\), so \(p/p_0=r^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\). The mass flux is \(\rho u\) and ideal-gas density is \(p/(RT)\). Substitution gives a function of just one local variable:

\[G(r)=\frac{p_0}{RT_0}\sqrt{2c_pT_0}\,r^{1/(\gamma-1)}\sqrt{1-r}.\]

To find its maximum differentiate \(\ln G=(\ln r)/(\gamma-1)+\ln(1-r)/2+\text{constant}\). Equating the two derivative terms gives the sonic temperature and pressure ratios:

\[r_*=\frac{2}{\gamma+1},\]
\[p_*/p_0=\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)},\]
\[u_*^2=\gamma R T_* .\]

The flow through a converging exit is choked when downstream pressure is low enough for this state to be reached. Further lowering downstream pressure changes the flow after the exit; the ideal mass flux at that exit remains fixed. The model omits wall friction, heat transfer and discharge-coefficient corrections.

Worked example

Size a sonic air metering aperture

For \(p_0=200\) kPa absolute, \(T_0=300\) K, \(\gamma=1.40\) and \(R=287\) J/(kg K), the critical state has \(T_*=250\) K and \(p_*=105.7\) kPa. Then \(\rho_*=1.47\) kg/m³ and \(u_*=317\) m/s, giving \(G_*\simeq467\) kg/(m² s). A \(20.0\) mm² effective aperture meters about \(9.33\) g/s if the downstream pressure allows choking.

Second application

Decide whether a higher receiver pressure matters

Raise the receiver pressure to \(160\) kPa. Its ratio to reservoir pressure is now \(0.800\), above the critical \(0.528\). An ideal converging nozzle discharges at this pressure with \(r=0.800^{(0.4/1.4)}=0.938\). The energy equation gives \(u\approx193\) m/s, static density about \(1.98\) kg/m³, and mass flux about \(382\) kg/(m² s). Through the same aperture the predicted flow falls to \(7.64\) g/s. A model assuming choked flow at both settings would miss this change.

Account for fluid entering and leaving

A closed-system piston can be followed with its gas. A continuously operating turbine has fresh fluid crossing its boundary every second. Enclose the hardware and its internal flow in a stationary control volume. Flowing mass carries internal energy \(u\), kinetic energy \(v^2/2\) and gravitational energy \(gz\) per kilogram. Upstream pressure also does work pushing fluid in, while downstream pressure opposes its exit. Per unit mass this flow work is \(p/\rho\), motivating the specific enthalpy \(h=u+p/\rho\).

Adding all incoming and outgoing energy rates gives, for one inlet, one outlet and constant stored energy,

\[\dot Q-\dot W_s=\dot m\left[h_2-h_1+(v_2^2-v_1^2)/2+g(z_2-z_1)\right].\]

Here \(\dot W_s\) is shaft power out of the control volume. Pressure work at the flowing boundaries is already contained in enthalpy; adding it once more would count it twice. One inlet and outlet have the same steady mass rate even when their volumetric flow rates differ dramatically because gas expands.

Read the dominant energy conversion

In a short, insulated nozzle there is no shaft, the elevation difference is small and falling enthalpy raises velocity. The property \(h+v^2/2\) is then constant, often called stagnation enthalpy. In an insulated turbine with small inlet and outlet speeds, the enthalpy drop instead appears mainly as positive shaft work. An insulated compressor takes work in, raising enthalpy. An insulated throttling valve has neither shaft power nor a large net change of bulk kinetic energy between distant ports, so its inlet and outlet enthalpies are approximately equal. These components can all drop pressure during expansion, yet their energy effects differ.

For ideal-gas air with nearly constant \(c_p\), an enthalpy difference may be approximated by \(c_p\Delta T\). Keeping h in kJ/kg while using \(v^2/2\) in J/kg is a common factor-of-one-thousand error. At 100 m/s the kinetic energy is 5 kJ/kg. That estimate often decides whether an inlet velocity may be omitted from a turbine work calculation.

Evaluate heat loss and exhaust speed

A turbine passes 2.0 kg/s of gas from \(h_1=820\) to \(h_2=600\) kJ/kg. Inlet speed is 40 m/s, exhaust speed 160 m/s and the casing loses 10 kW of heat. Elevations are equal. The gas loses \(2(820-600)=440\) kW of enthalpy flow. Its kinetic-energy rate grows by \(2(160^2-40^2)/2000=24.0\) kW. The unit delivers \(440-24-10=406\) kW shaft power. Counting all 440 kW as shaft output would overstate the result by both the residual exhaust energy and the heat leak.

A separate nozzle using gas at \(h_1=820\) kJ/kg and 40 m/s could reach \(h_2=800\) kJ/kg without a shaft if its speed rose to \(\sqrt{40^2+2(20\,000)}=204\) m/s. This estimate is an energy condition. The required area ratio, pressure drop and possibility of choking also depend on compressible-flow relations.

Extend the same balance to a mixer

Two insulated water lines mix at negligible speed change. One supplies 0.20 kg/s at 360 K and the other 0.30 kg/s at 300 K. A mass balance gives 0.50 kg/s at exit. With equal, constant \(c_p\) and no shaft, summing the two inlet enthalpy rates yields an outlet temperature \([0.20(360)+0.30(300)]/0.50=324\) K. The temperature lies between the inlet values, closer to the higher mass rate. The mixer conserves energy while creating entropy. The next lecture explains how both statements can hold simultaneously and why reversing the mixer without another effect is impossible.

Interpret a measured stagnation temperature

For an insulated diffuser with negligible shaft work, reducing speed from 200 to 40 m/s raises static enthalpy by \((200^2-40^2)/2=19.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). For air with \(c_p=1.005\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) that corresponds to a 19.1 K static temperature rise. Stagnation enthalpy \(h+V^2/2\) stays constant even if internal friction produces entropy. A stationary temperature probe that slows the arriving air may approach a stagnation reading, so comparison with a predicted static temperature requires knowing where and how the measurement was taken.

Minimise compression work by selecting an intermediate pressure

A continuous air supply can be compressed in two stages and cooled at intermediate pressure. For a steady adiabatic compressor with negligible kinetic and potential changes, shaft work input is the stream enthalpy rise. With an ideal gas, constant \(c_p,\gamma\), and a reversible stage, \(w=c_p T_{\rm in}[(p_{\rm out}/p_{\rm in})^a-1]\), where \(a=(\gamma-1)/\gamma\). This follows by integrating \(ds=c_p\,dT/T-R\,dp/p=0\), then applying \(dh=c_p\,dT\). If both stages use the same isentropic efficiency, divide each ideal rise by that efficiency.

Assume complete intercooling to the original inlet temperature \(T_1\), no pressure losses and prescribed first and last pressures. Put \(r_1=p_i/p_1,\ r_2=p_3/p_i\), so \(r_1r_2=p_3/p_1=r\). Total ideal work is \(c_pT_1[r_1^a+(r/r_1)^a-2]\). Differentiate with respect to \(\ln r_1\); the derivative is \(c_pT_1a(r_1^a-r_2^a)\). The sum has a minimum at equal ratios, giving intermediate pressure \(p_i=\sqrt{p_1p_3}\). This is a geometric mean of absolute pressures. A gauge-pressure ratio would give a completely different design.

Compare two cooled stages with a single compression

Supply air at 100 kPa and 300 K and deliver it at 900 kPa absolute. For \(\gamma=1.4,\ c_p=1.005\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\), an ideal single stage gives \(T_3=300(9^{0.2857})=562.0\,\mathrm K\) and work about 263 kJ/kg. Two equal-ratio stages operate through 300 kPa. Each reaches \(300(3^{0.2857})=410.6\,\mathrm K\) before its outlet or intercooler, requiring \(1.005(110.6)=111.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Total work is about 222.4 kJ/kg, roughly 15.5% lower. The intercooler must remove 111.2 kJ/kg between the stages under the ideal complete-cooling assumption.

With equal stage efficiencies of 0.80, each real enthalpy rise becomes 139.0 kJ/kg and the intermediate discharge temperature is about 438 K. Cooling back to 300 K then removes 139.0 kJ per kilogram, with a corresponding load of 27.8 kW at 0.20 kg/s. The equal-ratio choice survives because both stage work expressions acquired the same multiplier. An intercooler that can cool only to 330 K increases the weight of second-stage compression work; the new optimum shifts towards a larger first-stage pressure ratio. A design comparing only compressor shaft power would also need to account for coolant pumping, fan power and pressure losses before choosing the number of stages.

For an incomplete intercooler the optimal shift can be calculated. Take stage two inlet temperature \(T_m=330\) K independently maintained and identical efficiencies. The variable portion of total work is proportional to \(T_1r_1^a+T_m(r/r_1)^a\). Differentiating with respect to log first-stage ratio gives \(T_1r_1^a=T_mr_2^a\), or \(r_1/r_2=(330/300)^{1/a}=1.40\). At overall ratio nine, \(r_1=\sqrt{9(1.40)}=3.54,\ r_2=2.54\). Compressing farther while the air is colder is energetically preferable within this simplified comparison. These ratios presume the cooler can maintain 330 K over its revised duty and that both discharge temperatures stay within their allowable ranges. A compressor map may impose further flow and speed limits beyond the thermodynamic minimum.

At fixed pressure endpoints, the measured intermediate pressure alone would thus be insufficient for diagnosing whether the cooler or a compressor caused an observed power increase.

Entropy, irreversibility and useful-work loss

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Check a proposed process with the second law and quantify the effect of a finite temperature difference.

From first principles

Separate entropy transfer from entropy generated inside a boundary

For a reversible differential process the thermodynamic definition of entropy is \(dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T\). Irreversible changes reach the same state-function difference but can generate entropy internally. For a closed system exchanging heats \(Q_j\) across boundary portions at known local temperatures \(T_j\), the second law gives an experimentally testable inequality:

\[S_2-S_1=\sum_j\int\frac{\delta Q_j}{T_j}+S_{\rm gen},\]
\[S_{\rm gen}\ge0 .\]

To evaluate the left side independently of the actual path, use a reversible path between the same equilibrium states. For an ideal gas the fundamental relation \(Tds=du+p\,dv\), together with \(du=c_vdT\) and \(pv=RT\), yields

\[s_2-s_1=c_v\ln(T_2/T_1)+R\ln(v_2/v_1)\]

for constant specific heat. Inserting this property change into the balance isolates entropy generation of the actual process. A gas might cool and lose entropy while the combined gas plus surroundings generates entropy. For heat exchanged across a wall separating two reservoirs, the two reservoirs’ boundary transfer terms have opposite signs and different temperature denominators.

Derivation and applications

Count the entropy generated when two ideal gases mix

Two separated ideal gases can have the same temperature and pressure, yet their mixing increases entropy. Consider distinct nonreacting species in insulated rigid compartments. Remove the separator and let them equilibrate in the common volume. Initially both sides have temperature \(T\) and pressure \(p\), and no shaft work is performed. The total internal energy of ideal gases depends only on temperature, so the final equilibrium temperature stays \(T\). Denote mole numbers by \(n_i\) and total volume by \(V\).

For each species, the entropy change can be calculated along a reversible isothermal expansion into its accessible final volume, even though the actual mixing is irreversible. Integrating \(\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T=p_i\,dV/T=n_iR\,dV/V\) gives

\[\Delta S_i=n_iR\ln(V/V_i).\]

The initial volumes were proportional to mole numbers because \(p\) and \(T\) were common. Thus \(V_i/V=n_i/\sum_jn_j=y_i\), the final mole fraction. Add the species changes and use zero boundary entropy transfer:

\[\Delta S_{\rm mix}=S_{\rm gen}=-R\sum_i n_i\ln y_i .\]

For two gases with \(y\) and \(1-y\), the generation per mole of mixture is maximal when both are equally abundant. The derivative and its zero are

\[\frac{d}{dy}\frac{\Delta S_{\rm mix}}n=R\ln[(1-y)/y]=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=\tfrac12.\]

The entropy change tends to zero when either fraction tends to one. If the compartments contain the same gas and the initial temperatures and pressures already agree, removing the partition does not create distinguishable species; assigning artificial labels would predict a spurious mixing term.

Worked example

Estimate the lost work of mixing two samples

Mix \(1.00\) mol of one ideal gas and \(3.00\) mol of another at \(300\) K. The mole fractions are \(0.250\) and \(0.750\). The generated entropy is \(-8.314[\ln0.25+3\ln0.75]=18.7\) J/K. Relative to an environment at \(300\) K, the destroyed work potential is \(300(18.7)=5.61\) kJ. No energy has disappeared from the insulated vessel.

Second application

Compare equal and strongly unequal amounts

For a fixed total of \(4.00\) mol, an equal mixture generates \(4R\ln2=23.1\) J/K. With only \(0.0400\) mol of one species the fractions become \(0.0100\) and \(0.990\). The generation falls to \(4R[-0.01\ln0.01-0.99\ln0.99]=1.86\) J/K. Each rare molecule gains access to much more volume, but there are few such molecules; both contributions must be counted before comparing samples.

Energy bookkeeping is not sufficient

In an insulated two-body experiment, heat lost by one body is gained by the other. That energy balance alone would allow heat to move spontaneously from a colder body to a hotter one. Entropy supplies the missing direction constraint. It is a property of a state. For a simple equilibrium system a reversible differential heat transfer defines \(dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T\). An irreversible process between the same endpoints has the same entropy difference; the actual heat transfer divided by the body temperature need not equal that difference.

For a closed system, the integrated entropy balance reads \(\Delta S=\int \delta Q/T_b+S_{\rm gen}\), where \(T_b\) is the absolute temperature at the boundary where the heat crosses. Entropy generation satisfies \(S_{\rm gen}\ge0\). A system can lose entropy by exporting it in heat or in a mass stream. A negative change in the entropy of a single hot body therefore need not violate the second law.

Calculate a property change with a convenient path

For a calorically perfect ideal gas, combine \(T\,ds=du+p\,dv\) with \(du=c_v\,dT\) and \(p=RT/v\). This gives \(ds=c_v\,dT/T+R\,dv/v\). Replacing \(v\) using the ideal-gas law yields the alternative \(ds=c_p\,dT/T-R\,dp/p\). Integrate between the initial and final equilibrium states. These are property relationships, even when the actual path between states includes friction, throttling or free expansion.

An ideal gas passing through an insulated valve with equal inlet and outlet speeds has \(h_2=h_1\), so its temperatures are equal in the constant-\(c_p\) approximation. Dropping air from 400 to 100 kPa gives \(s_2-s_1=-0.287\ln(100/400)=0.398\) kJ/(kg K). With no heat transfer or other streams, the positive entropy change per kilogram is entirely generated. A reversible insulated expansion turbine would generally produce a lower outlet temperature and shaft work.

Trace entropy across two reservoirs

An insulated conductor transfers 600 W from a reservoir at 400 K to another at 300 K. Its stored energy and entropy are steady. The hot reservoir loses entropy at \(600/400=1.50\) W/K, while the cold one gains \(600/300=2.00\) W/K. The total generation rate is 0.50 W/K. Heat rate is the same at both ends; entropy transfer in that heat differs because the temperatures differ.

\[\dot S_{\rm gen}=\dot Q\left(\frac1{T_c}-\frac1{T_h}\right).\]

If the surroundings defining the available-work reference are at \(T_0=300\) K, the corresponding destruction of available work is \(T_0\dot S_{\rm gen}=150\) W. For these particular reservoirs a reversible engine supplied with 600 W at 400 K could have produced \(600(1-300/400)=150\) W before rejecting the remainder. The conductor instead rejects all 600 W to the cold sink. Energy did not disappear; an opportunity for work was lost.

Locate sources and choose a boundary

A heat exchanger can have little external heat leak yet generate appreciable entropy internally through finite temperature differences and pressure losses. Write \(\dot S_{\rm gen}=\sum_{\rm out}\dot m s-\sum_{\rm in}\dot m s-\sum_j\dot Q_j/T_{b,j}\) for steady operation. Both streams belong inside the same control volume when testing internal heat exchange. If the boundary encloses only one stream, heat crossing the wall carries entropy with it and must appear explicitly. The largest reduction in entropy generation per extra unit of area or pumping effort is a design question. The thermodynamic balance identifies losses without alone determining the economically preferred geometry.

Compare the conditions on a valve and a turbine

Air passes through a steady insulated throttling valve from 400 to 100 kPa with negligible changes of speed. With no shaft work the first law gives equal inlet and outlet enthalpy. For ideal air this implies equal temperature. Its entropy change follows from \(\Delta s=c_p\ln(T_2/T_1)-R\ln(p_2/p_1)=0.287\ln4=0.398\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\). At 0.10 kg/s the entropy generation is 0.0398 kW/K. An ideal adiabatic turbine undergoing the same pressure drop has constant entropy and delivers work as its temperature falls. Pressure ratio alone therefore cannot determine the temperature change across an unfamiliar device.

Obtain a work bound for two finite bodies

The reversible efficiency \(1-T_c/T_h\) describes operation between reservoirs held at fixed temperatures. If the two bodies are finite, each increment of transferred heat changes the temperatures and the instantaneous efficiency. Consider two incompressible blocks in a common insulated enclosure. Their heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\) are taken as constant. A reversible engine operates between them and exports mechanical work until no temperature difference remains. The working fluid returns to its starting state after each incremental cycle, so conservation of entropy for this reversible composite system determines the final temperature.

\[C_h\ln(T_f/T_h)+C_c\ln(T_f/T_c)=0,\qquad T_f=T_h^{C_h/(C_h+C_c)}T_c^{C_c/(C_h+C_c)}.\]

Energy conservation gives maximum work \(W_{\max}=C_hT_h+C_cT_c-(C_h+C_c)T_f\) for consistent internal-energy references. The reversible final temperature is a weighted geometric mean. If the two blocks are simply put in thermal contact without work output, first-law conservation instead gives the weighted arithmetic mean. The arithmetic mean is larger for unequal initial temperatures; additional energy remains as sensible heat after irreversible contact. These different final temperatures matter when comparing two ways of using the same finite resources.

In a real engine running at finite heat-transfer rates there are temperature differences between each block and the working fluid. Total entropy then increases. For a specified common final temperature the entropy change of the blocks is fixed by state properties; measuring it indicates the departure from the ideal reversible construction without needing the working-fluid cycle.

Compare direct contact and a reversible engine quantitatively

Take a hot assembly of heat capacity 2.0 kJ/K at 450 K and a cooler assembly of 1.0 kJ/K at 300 K. Solving the log balance gives \(T_f=(450^2\,300)^{1/3}=393.1\,\mathrm K\). The hot assembly releases about \(2(450-393.1)=113.8\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and the cold one receives 93.1 kJ. Their difference gives approximately 20.7 kJ of maximum work. Acting as though the initial temperatures stayed at 450 and 300 K throughout would assign an incorrectly high efficiency to later parts of the transfer.

By direct contact the common temperature would be \((2\times450+300)/3=400\,\mathrm K\). The entropy generated is \(2\ln(400/450)+\ln(400/300)=0.0521\,\mathrm{kJ/K}\). If instead a device delivers a measured 12 kJ of work before the blocks reach a common temperature, energy fixes that temperature as \((1200-12)/3=396\,\mathrm K\). The measured process then generates \(2\ln(396/450)+\ln(396/300)=0.0220\,\mathrm{kJ/K}\), a positive value between the two limiting cases. Entropy generation multiplied by an environmental temperature gives destroyed exergy relative to that environment; comparing the finite-body work outputs also requires acknowledging their different final stored energies.

The geometric-mean result has an informative limiting case. If the cold assembly is so large that \(C_c/C_h\) tends to infinity, its temperature remains effectively fixed, and the engine work reduces to \(C_h[(T_h-T_c)-T_c\ln(T_h/T_c)]\). This is the integral of the reversible work recovered as the finite hot block approaches a constant sink. It is smaller than applying the initial Carnot efficiency to all of the hot block’s released heat.

Phase equilibrium and steam quality

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Locate a two-phase state from two independent measurements and use mass fractions to find its volume and energy.

From first principles

Derive the slope of a pure-substance coexistence line

At liquid–vapour equilibrium, transferring a small amount of matter between phases cannot lower the total Gibbs energy. For a pure substance this requires equality of the specific Gibbs energies \(g_f=g_g\) at common temperature and pressure. To find how pressure must change when temperature changes while maintaining coexistence, differentiate this equality along the saturation curve. The fundamental differential \(dg=-s\,dT+v\,dp\) gives

\[-s_f\,dT+v_f\,dp=-s_g\,dT+v_g\,dp .\]

Rearranging expresses the measured slope as the entropy change of vaporization divided by its specific-volume change. Reversible phase change at constant temperature absorbs specific enthalpy \(h_{fg}=T(s_g-s_f)\), so

\[\left(\frac{dp}{dT}\right)_{\rm sat}=\frac{s_g-s_f}{v_g-v_f}=\frac{h_{fg}}{T(v_g-v_f)} .\]

The units are Pa/K. No ideal-gas approximation has been made. If the vapour is dilute and the liquid volume negligible, further substitution can simplify the slope, but this fails near the critical point. The slope describes neighbouring coexistence states; determining a system’s vapour mass fraction additionally requires an extensive property such as specific enthalpy or volume.

Derivation and applications

Trace an isentropic pressure change through the saturation dome

A vapour fraction inferred from one property must reproduce the others at the same pressure. Consider a pure substance in local liquid–vapour equilibrium. Let \(x\) be the vapour mass fraction and use subscripts \(f,g\) for saturated liquid and vapour at the current pressure. Neglect interfacial contributions and assume each phase has the tabulated saturation state. Specific extensive properties then obey the lever rule because their totals add over the masses of both phases:

\[s=(1-x)s_f+xs_g=s_f+xs_{fg},\]
\[h=h_f+xh_{fg}.\]

In a steady adiabatic reversible expansion the entropy balance gives \(s_2=s_1\). At the specified exit pressure solve the entropy relation before using the enthalpy relation:

\[x_{2s}=(s_1-s_{f,2})/s_{fg,2},\]
\[h_{2s}=h_{f,2}+x_{2s}h_{fg,2}.\]

This interpolation is physical only when \(0\le x_{2s}\le1\). For an actual adiabatic turbine with negligible kinetic-energy change, use isentropic efficiency \(\eta_t=(h_1-h_2)/(h_1-h_{2s})\) and check the actual phase state separately:

\[h_2=h_1-\eta_t(h_1-h_{2s}),\]
\[x_2=(h_2-h_{f,2})/h_{fg,2}\]

if the actual outlet is still a mixture. Irreversibility reduces extracted work, which can leave the exhaust drier at the same pressure. A drier exit therefore does not by itself demonstrate higher efficiency.

Worked example

Infer equilibrium quality for two expansion histories

At a low-pressure outlet take rounded steam properties \(h_f=192\), \(h_{fg}=2393\) kJ/kg, \(s_f=0.649\) and \(s_{fg}=7.50\) kJ/(kg K). Steam enters with \(h_1=3200\) kJ/kg and \(s_1=6.65\) kJ/(kg K). The reversible quality is \((6.65-0.649)/7.50=0.800\), giving \(h_{2s}=2107\) kJ/kg. For \(\eta_t=0.800\), the actual exit enthalpy is about \(2326\) kJ/kg and the quality \(0.892\). The actual specific work is about \(874\) kJ/kg.

Second application

Detect when a reported quality has no physical meaning

At the same outlet pressure an independent calculation gives \(h_2=2630\) kJ/kg. Interpolating would give \(x=(2630-192)/2393=1.019\). Since the saturated vapour enthalpy is only \(2585\) kJ/kg, the enthalpy lies above the saturation range. The outlet is superheated; finding its temperature requires superheated-vapour properties. Reporting a vapour fraction above unity would hide a change of thermodynamic region.

Temperature can stay fixed while heat enters

In an open pan at a fixed ambient pressure, liquid water begins to boil at a particular temperature. Additional heat can then convert liquid to vapour while the temperature remains close to that value. Specifying both saturation temperature and saturation pressure of a pure substance does not locate a unique mixture state. Along the coexistence curve they are linked, and the fraction of material in each phase supplies the missing information. Outside that curve pressure and temperature can usually be used as independent coordinates for a simple compressible substance.

Define quality \(x=m_g/(m_f+m_g)\) as the vapour mass fraction, using subscripts \(f\) for saturated liquid and \(g\) for saturated vapour. A property such as volume or enthalpy is additive over the two masses when interfacial contributions are negligible. Writing \(V=m_f v_f+m_gv_g\) and dividing by total mass gives the mixing relation. The same derivation applies to specific internal energy, enthalpy and entropy.

\[v=v_f+x(v_g-v_f),\qquad h=h_f+xh_{fg},\qquad h_{fg}=h_g-h_f .\]

Quality lies between zero and one in a saturated mixture. A calculated value beyond either endpoint signals a single-phase state or inconsistent measurements. Temperature does not interpolate: both phases in equilibrium have the same temperature.

Distinguish vapour mass from space occupied

A closed vessel contains 2.00 kg of saturated water and steam at approximately 100 kPa in 0.500 m³. Use the supplied rounded values \(v_f=0.00104,\ v_g=1.694\,\mathrm{m^3/kg}\), \(h_f=417.5,\ h_{fg}=2258\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The measured specific volume is 0.250 m³/kg. Quality follows as \((0.250-0.00104)/(1.694-0.00104)=0.1471\). Thus vapour mass is 0.294 kg while 1.706 kg remains liquid.

The vapour occupies \(0.2941(1.694)=0.4982\,\mathrm{m^3}\), almost the entire vessel. The liquid occupies roughly 0.0018 m³. Specific enthalpy is \(417.5+0.1471(2258)=749.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Specific internal energy follows from \(u=h-pv=749.6-100(0.250)=724.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) when using kPa and m³/kg. A level indicator measures occupied volume; using its reading directly as a mass fraction would give a large error.

Connect latent heat to the coexistence curve

For two pure phases in equilibrium their specific Gibbs free energies are equal. Along the coexistence line this equality persists, so \(dg_f=dg_g\). From \(dg=v\,dp-s\,dT\) one obtains \(dp_{\rm sat}/dT=(s_g-s_f)/(v_g-v_f)=h_{fg}/[T(v_g-v_f)]\). At about 373 K using the same values, the slope is \(2258/[373(1.693)]=3.58\,\mathrm{kPa/K}\). A small increase of boiling pressure by 3.6 kPa therefore raises saturation temperature by about one kelvin locally. Larger changes require integrating the changing properties or reading a saturation table rather than treating this local slope as a constant.

Predict flash after throttling

A saturated liquid with inlet enthalpy 500 kJ/kg passes through an insulated valve to the pressure of the drum above. With negligible work and velocity change, enthalpy remains 500 kJ/kg. The downstream quality is therefore \((500-417.5)/2258=0.0365\). Only about 3.7% of the water flashes by mass, but the large specific volume of the vapour can make its occupied volume dominate the downstream pipe.

Trace a saturated mixture along a constant-volume path

The drum example determines one state. If a sealed rigid vessel is heated, its pressure and temperature change together along saturation while its specific volume remains fixed. Provided equilibrium is maintained and no other phase appears, the quality at every pressure is \(x=(v-v_f)/(v_g-v_f)\). The energy equation for the vessel is \(Q=\Delta U\) when it does no boundary or shaft work. Replacing internal-energy change by enthalpy change would add the spurious \(V\Delta p\) term of a rigid container.

Quality does not change solely because latent heat has entered. Differentiate \(v=(1-x)v_f+xv_g\) along the saturation curve at fixed \(v\). This gives

\[\frac{dx}{dT}=-\frac{(1-x)\,dv_f/dT+x\,dv_g/dT}{v_g-v_f}.\]

Saturated liquid usually expands as temperature rises while saturated vapour specific volume decreases over the ordinary water range. The sign therefore depends on the initial mixture and local property slopes. A vessel containing almost all liquid by volume can, under some conditions, follow the saturated-liquid boundary on heating rather than drying out. Determine the actual phase region from the property data after each change. When \(v\) lies outside the interval between \(v_f\) and \(v_g\), a saturated quality formula is no longer applicable.

Find how much energy changes phase in a sealed vessel

Take 1.00 kg of water in a rigid 0.300 m³ vessel. Use rounded saturation data at 100 kPa: \(v_f=0.00104,\ v_g=1.694\,\mathrm{m^3/kg},\ u_f=417.4,\ u_g=2506\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). At 200 kPa use \(v_f=0.00106,\ v_g=0.8857,\ u_f=504.5,\ u_g=2529\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). The volume bounds contain 0.300 m³/kg at both pressures. Initial quality is \((0.300-0.00104)/(1.694-0.00104)=0.1766\); final quality is \((0.300-0.00106)/(0.8857-0.00106)=0.3379\). Thus about 0.161 kg additionally evaporates.

Mixture internal energies are \(417.4+0.1766(2506-417.4)=786.3\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) and \(504.5+0.3379(2529-504.5)=1188.6\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\), giving heat input about 402 kJ. Merely multiplying evaporated mass by one latent heat would overlook the sensible energy changes in each phase and the variation of saturation properties. As an independent check, \(\Delta H=\Delta U+V\Delta p\) exceeds the required heat by \(0.300(200-100)=30.0\,\mathrm{kJ}\). If additional heating brings saturated vapour specific volume down to 0.300 m³/kg, the last liquid disappears. Beyond that state the fixed-volume path enters the superheated region and one must determine temperature using a single-phase equation of state or superheated-vapour data.

The vapour volume fraction in the second state is \(m x v_g/V=0.3379(0.8857)/0.300=0.998\) approximately. Although about one third of the mass is vapour, almost every part of the vessel volume is vapour-filled. A level probe that detects a very small liquid volume is consistent with the presence of substantial residual liquid mass. This has a direct consequence for rounding: replacing \(v_f\) by zero only gives a modest error in calculated quality here, but using that same approximation to predict the remaining liquid depth gives a nonsensical zero. Retain the finite liquid volume whenever the question concerns an interface height or the mass recovered through a bottom drain.

The units of the property table matter just as much as the phase labels when predicting a small liquid inventory.

Can an equation of state predict energy changes during expansion?

A caloric model \(du=c_v\,dT\) works for an ideal gas. To discover the extra term for a real fluid, let \(v\), \(s\) and \(u\) denote molar volume, entropy and energy for a single homogeneous phase. At a fixed composition the fundamental relation is \(du=T\,ds-p\,dv\). Introduce the Helmholtz energy \(f=u-Ts\). Then \(df=-s\,dT-p\,dv\). Equality of the two mixed partial derivatives of the smooth function \(f(T,v)\) gives the Maxwell relation \((\partial s/\partial v)_T=(\partial p/\partial T)_v\).

Now expand \(ds\) in \(T,v\). The term \(T(\partial s/\partial T)_v\) equals \(c_v\), obtained directly by holding \(v\) fixed in the fundamental relation. Substituting both coefficients gives

\[du=c_v\,dT+\left[T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_v-p\right]\,dv.\]

The bracket has units of pressure. For \(p=RT/v\) it vanishes. For a van der Waals model \(p=RT/(v-b)-a/v^2\), the repulsive volume correction cancels between the two terms and the attraction term leaves \(a/v^2\). Assuming constant \(c_v\), integration yields \(\Delta u=c_v(T_2-T_1)+a(1/v_1-1/v_2)\).

An insulated, rigid container contains a gas on one side of a removable internal partition and vacuum on the other. Removing the partition causes no external boundary work and exchanges no heat. States before and after settling therefore have equal internal energy. Let \(T_1=300\,\mathrm K\), \(v_1=0.00200\,\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}\), \(v_2=0.00400\,\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}\), \(a=0.150\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}\), \(b=4.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(c_v=24.0\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}\). The volume contribution is \(0.150(500-250)=37.5\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\). To keep energy constant the temperature falls by \(37.5/24.0=1.56\,\mathrm K\), to \(298.44\,\mathrm K\). The differential identity links the equilibrium endpoints even though the actual expansion was irreversible. The value of \(b\) matters for their pressures and cancels from this energy change.

Gas mixtures, humidity and condensation

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Translate relative humidity into a water mass balance and determine how much condensate a cooling coil removes.

From first principles

Obtain humidity ratio from partial pressures and dry-air mass

Treat moist air as an ideal mixture of dry air and water vapour occupying the same volume \(V\) at temperature \(T\). Dalton's law gives total pressure \(p=p_{da}+p_v\). For each component \(m_i=p_iV/(R_iT)\), so dividing vapour mass by dry-air mass cancels the shared volume and temperature:

\[\omega=\frac{m_v}{m_{da}}=\frac{R_{da}}{R_v}\,\frac{p_v}{p-p_v}\simeq0.622\frac{p_v}{p-p_v}.\]

Relative humidity is a different observable. It compares actual vapour partial pressure with the saturation pressure at the same temperature. Cooling without water removal keeps \(\omega\) constant, and approximately constant total pressure then keeps \(p_v\) constant until the saturation curve reaches it:

\[\varphi=p_v/p_{\rm sat}(T),\]
\[p_{\rm sat}(T_{\rm dew})=p_v .\]

Below the dew point some water can leave the gas as liquid, while dry-air mass is conserved. A steady coil receiving humidity ratio \(\omega_i\) and delivering \(\omega_o\) therefore removes condensate at rate \(\dot m_{da}(\omega_i-\omega_o)\). Inferring the same removal from relative humidity alone would ignore its dependence on temperature.

Derivation and applications

Measure the bypass fraction of a cooling and dehumidifying coil

Humid air crossing a coil may leave warmer and wetter than air in equilibrium with the coldest effective surface. A common reduced model treats a fraction \(b\) of the dry-air stream as bypassing the surface while the remainder reaches a saturated state at the apparatus dew-point temperature. Consider a steady coil at nearly constant total pressure, neglect fan work and heat gains, and use dry-air mass as the conserved basis. Denote inlet, saturated-surface and mixed outlet states by \(i,s,o\), with humidity ratio \(\omega\) and moist-air enthalpy \(h\) per kilogram of dry air.

Water-vapour and mixed-stream enthalpy balances give two independent ways to estimate the same bypass fraction:

\[\omega_o=b\omega_i+(1-b)\omega_s,\]
\[h_o=bh_i+(1-b)h_s.\]

Solving either for \(b\) exposes a consistency check on measured data and the assumed single surface state:

\[b=\frac{\omega_o-\omega_s}{\omega_i-\omega_s}=\frac{h_o-h_s}{h_i-h_s}.\]

The condensed water remains a separate outlet from the coil. With condensate leaving at liquid enthalpy \(h_\ell\) in the same reference convention, an overall balance gives positive heat removal

\[\dot m_\ell=\dot m_{da}(\omega_i-\omega_o),\]
\[\dot Q_{\rm out}=\dot m_{da}(h_i-h_o)-\dot m_\ell h_\ell .\]

The mixed-state model requires \(0\le b\le1\). It also requires incoming air wet enough for condensation on the assumed surface. A dry surface with solely sensible cooling cannot be represented by forcing a saturated leaving branch.

Worked example

Evaluate air and water leaving a coil

Use \(\omega_i=0.0160\), \(\omega_s=0.00760\) kg/kg dry air, \(h_i=71.0\), \(h_s=29.3\) kJ/kg dry air and \(b=0.250\). Then \(\omega_o=0.00970\) and \(h_o=39.7\) kJ/kg dry air. With \(1.20\) kg/s dry air, the condensate rate is \(0.00756\) kg/s. For \(h_\ell=42.0\) kJ/kg, heat removal is \(1.20(71.0-39.725)-0.00756(42.0)=37.2\) kW.

Second application

Test whether three measurements share one apparatus dew point

On another run with the same inlet and assumed surface, measured outlet values are \(\omega_o=0.01096\) and \(h_o=41.8\) kJ/kg dry air. The humidity ratio implies \(b=0.400\), while enthalpy gives \(b=0.300\). At \(b=0.400\) the model would predict \(h_o=46.0\) kJ/kg. The \(4.2\) kJ/kg discrepancy indicates that measurement uncertainty, a more distributed surface temperature or another heat transfer must be considered before inferring a unique bypass fraction.

Choose a quantity that survives condensation

A ventilation duct can carry an unchanged mass flow of dry air while water evaporates into it or condenses out. It is therefore convenient to express water content and mixture energy per kilogram of dry air. Model the dry air and water vapour as ideal gases sharing a common temperature and total volume. The partial pressure of each gas is the pressure it would exert if it occupied this volume alone. Dalton’s law gives \(p=p_a+p_v\).

Relative humidity \(\phi=p_v/p_{\rm sat}(T)\) describes proximity to saturation at the current temperature. It is not the mass fraction of water. For humidity ratio \(w=m_v/m_a\), divide the two ideal-gas mass equations. The common temperature and volume cancel, leaving a ratio of molar masses multiplied by the partial-pressure ratio.

\[w=\frac{M_v}{M_a}\frac{p_v}{p-p_v}\simeq0.622\frac{p_v}{p-p_v},\qquad w_{\rm sat}(T)=0.622\frac{p_{\rm sat}(T)}{p-p_{\rm sat}(T)} .\]

Before any water condenses during cooling at constant total pressure, the humidity ratio and vapour partial pressure remain fixed. Relative humidity rises because saturation pressure falls. The dew point is the temperature at which saturation pressure equals that fixed vapour pressure. Once that point is crossed, further cooling can remove water.

Find water removal and the associated cooling duty

Air enters a coil at 30 °C, 100 kPa and 60% relative humidity, and leaves saturated at 12 °C. Use \(p_{\rm sat}(30)=4.24\) kPa and \(p_{\rm sat}(12)=1.40\) kPa. The inlet vapour partial pressure is 2.544 kPa, giving \(w_1=0.01624\) kg water per kg dry air. The outlet ratio is \(w_2=0.00883\). For dry-air flow 0.50 kg/s the condensate flow is \(0.50(0.01624-0.00883)=0.00371\,\mathrm{kg/s}\), about 13.3 kg/h.

Using a 0 °C liquid-water reference, approximate moist-air enthalpy per kilogram dry air by \(h=1.005T+w(2501+1.86T)\) kJ/kg dry air with \(T\) in °C. The duct streams carry about 71.7 and 34.3 kJ/kg dry air. Condensate leaving at 12 °C carries \(h_l\simeq4.18(12)=50.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Energy conservation gives heat removed per kilogram dry air as \(h_1-h_2-(w_1-w_2)h_l\), approximately 37.0 kJ/kg dry air, or 18.5 kW.

Separate humidity ratio from relative humidity

Suppose the outlet stream is reheated to 20 °C without adding water. Its vapour pressure remains 1.40 kPa at the same total pressure. Since \(p_{\rm sat}(20)=2.34\,\mathrm{kPa}\), the new relative humidity is 59.8%. Neither evaporating water nor drying the air was required during this reheat. A sensor reporting 60% at both the inlet and reheated outlet therefore describes two air streams containing rather different masses of water. The coil water balance, written on the dry-air basis, explains the apparent coincidence.

Bracket an unknown dew point

At the original inlet the vapour pressure was 2.544 kPa. Suppose a saturation table gives 2.487 kPa at 21 °C and 2.644 kPa at 22 °C. Linear interpolation over this short interval puts the dew point at \(21+(2.544-2.487)/(2.644-2.487)=21.36^\circ\mathrm C\). A coil surface warmer than this value cannot start condensing the entering air locally under the stated conditions.

Balance an adiabatic spray chamber

A direct evaporative cooler behaves differently from a refrigerated cooling coil. Water is added to an airstream, the dry-bulb temperature falls, and the humidity ratio increases. Assume steady operation, negligible shaft work and heat leakage, unchanged total pressure, and no liquid droplets carried out. Express enthalpy per kilogram dry air. The dry-air flow is conserved; added liquid flow per kilogram dry air equals \(w_2-w_1\). The resulting energy balance is

\[h_1+(w_2-w_1)h_{\ell,\rm in}=h_2.\]

Use the same enthalpy reference for liquid and moist air. If the added water has low liquid enthalpy on that reference, the moist-air enthalpy is approximately constant. Often the recirculated liquid is near the leaving-air temperature; the more accurate energy equation above can be used without that approximation. An ideal infinitely long saturated contactor has \(\phi_2=1\), but a finite cooler generally leaves unsaturated air. An outlet temperature or a contact effectiveness is needed in addition to the balances unless saturation is specified.

With constant heat capacities one may write \(h_2=c_{pa}T_2+w_2(h_{g0}+c_{pv}T_2)\). Substituting this form into the balance and grouping the unknown \(w_2\) shows explicitly which measured quantities are needed. The process cannot cool below the adiabatic saturation limit for the given inlet state and water supply merely by increasing the spray rate.

Check outlet humidity and water consumption

Useful work and exergy destruction

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Evaluate the useful-work potential of a steady stream and distinguish energy conservation from the destruction of useful-work potential.

From first principles

Derive maximum useful work relative to an environment

For a closed body changing toward equilibrium with surroundings at \(T_0,p_0\), consider the reversible path that gives the largest possible useful work. Neglect bulk kinetic and gravitational energy. With heat \(Q_0\) positive into the body and total boundary plus shaft work \(W\) positive out, the first law gives \(W=U_i-U_f+Q_0\). Reversible heat exchange with this environment requires \(Q_0=T_0(S_f-S_i)\). Part of total work simply displaces ambient pressure, so

\[W_{\rm useful,rev}=U_i-U_f+T_0(S_f-S_i)-p_0(V_f-V_i).\]

Choose the final state as the dead state, denoted by subscript zero. Grouping the available initial differences defines nonflow physical exergy:

\[B=(U-U_0)+p_0(V-V_0)-T_0(S-S_0).\]

If the process generates entropy, the same first and second law combination lowers the useful work by \(T_0S_{\rm gen}\). The expression depends on the specified surrounding state and assumes no other heat reservoirs or chemical changes. A stream crossing a control volume carries its pressure flow work as well, giving a distinct enthalpy-based expression for flow exergy.

Derivation and applications

Derive the work potential of a compressed flowing gas

A stream can deliver useful work by cooling or by expanding, even when no fuel or chemical reaction is involved. Choose a steady reference environment at \(T_0,p_0\) with the same gas composition. Consider one kilogram taken reversibly from its inlet state to the environmental state. Neglect kinetic and potential energy. Let \(q_0\) be heat supplied from the environment, positive into the device, and \(w\) shaft work delivered. The steady energy balance includes flow work in enthalpy:

\[w=h-h_0+q_0 .\]

The only external heat exchange occurs with the environment. Reversibility then gives \(s_0-s=q_0/T_0\), eliminating the unmeasured heat transfer. The maximum work is the specific physical flow exergy

\[b=(h-h_0)-T_0(s-s_0).\]

For an ideal gas with constant \(c_p\) and \(R\), insert \(dh=c_p\,dT\) and \(ds=c_p\,dT/T-R\,dp/p\). Integrate relative to \(T_0,p_0\):

\[b=c_p[(T-T_0)-T_0\ln(T/T_0)]+RT_0\ln(p/p_0).\]

The pressure term survives at ambient temperature. Near \(T=T_0\) the thermal term is quadratic in the temperature difference because energy and the ambient heat contribution cancel to first order. The formula describes work relative to a specified reference and assumes the inlet and outlet composition is the same. An actual dissipative path destroys part of the reversible work potential.

Worked example

Estimate the work available from warm compressed air

Take air at \(T=360\) K, \(p=300\) kPa entering an environment at \(T_0=300\) K and \(p_0=100\) kPa. With \(c_p=1.005\) and \(R=0.287\) kJ/(kg K), the thermal contribution is \(1.005[60-300\ln1.2]=5.33\) kJ/kg. The pressure contribution is \(0.287(300)\ln3=94.6\) kJ/kg. Total available work is approximately \(99.9\) kJ/kg, mostly associated with pressure.

Second application

Account for loss in an ambient-temperature throttle

Let \(0.100\) kg/s of ideal air throttle adiabatically from \(300\) to \(100\) kPa, both at \(300\) K. Enthalpy stays constant, no shaft work is extracted, and the exergy destruction is \(0.100(94.6)=9.46\) kW. Independent entropy accounting gives \(\dot S_{\rm gen}=\dot mR\ln3=0.0315\) kW/K. Its product with the ambient \(300\) K recovers the same \(9.46\) kW. A device balancing energy perfectly can therefore expend substantial work potential.

Specify the surroundings before rating a resource

A warm stream contains energy relative to a chosen reference. The amount that could become useful work depends on the surroundings available to receive heat. If stream and environment reach mutual thermal and mechanical equilibrium, there is no further physical work potential associated with their difference. This equilibrium is the physical dead state at \(T_0,p_0\). Exclude chemical reactions here and consider a steady flow with constant composition.

Imagine a reversible device that takes a stream from its current state to the dead state while interacting thermally only with the environment. The steady first law relates delivered work to changes in stream enthalpy, kinetic and potential energy, and heat. The reversible entropy balance gives heat received from the environment \(q_0=T_0(s_0-s)\). Eliminating that heat gives the specific flow exergy

\[\psi=(h-h_0)-T_0(s-s_0)+V^2/2+g(z-z_0).\]

Use consistent units for the kinetic and potential terms. The entropy contribution reduces the fraction of enthalpy that a reversible device can turn into useful work. At the dead state, with the same velocity and elevation as the reference, that work potential vanishes.

Cool a liquid to the environment

Take a stream of liquid water at approximately ambient pressure, 360 K, with negligible motion and elevation change. It flows at 0.20 kg/s towards a large environment at 300 K. Assume \(c_p=4.18\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\) constant. Integration gives \(h-h_0=c_p(T-T_0)\) and \(s-s_0=c_p\ln(T/T_0)\). The stream’s specific physical exergy is therefore \(4.18[60-300\ln(360/300)]=22.2\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\). Its enthalpy difference is much larger, 250.8 kJ/kg.

Cooling all the way to 300 K makes at most \(0.20(22.2)=4.44\,\mathrm{kW}\) of useful work available, even though the stream releases 50.16 kW of heat. Direct heat exchange with the environment delivers no shaft work. The environment entropy gain is \(50.16/300=0.1672\,\mathrm{kW/K}\), while the stream loses \(0.20(4.18)\ln(360/300)=0.1524\,\mathrm{kW/K}\). The difference, about 0.0148 kW/K, is the entropy production rate. Multiplication by \(T_0\) gives the lost 4.44 kW of work potential.

Include heat crossing another temperature boundary

Heat \(Q\) supplied reversibly by a reservoir at a uniform boundary temperature \(T_b\) carries exergy \(Q(1-T_0/T_b)\). This follows by comparing its energy with the environmental heat required to discharge the associated entropy \(Q/T_b\). A heat input of 10 kW from a 600 K reservoir in a 300 K environment carries 5 kW of exergy. A transfer from a finite cooling stream has changing source temperature and requires integration, as in the water example. Irreversibility in the device destroys exergy at rate \(T_0\dot S_{\rm gen}\); directing heat to a useful secondary process can retain part of the exergy that direct cooling would destroy.

Include the work potential of pressure

For an ideal gas already at \(T_0\), an elevated pressure still gives physical flow exergy \(RT_0\ln(p/p_0)\). Air at 300 K and twice environmental pressure has about \(0.287(300)\ln2=59.7\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}\) of pressure-related work potential although its ideal-gas enthalpy equals that at the dead state.

Derive the least work needed to heat a finite stream

Heat pumps are commonly compared by heat delivered per unit of electrical work. A second-law comparison additionally accounts for the temperature at which heat is delivered. Consider a steady stream heated from \(T_i\) to \(T_o\) at nearly environmental pressure, with constant heat capacity rate \(C=\dot m c_p\). A reversible heat pump extracts heat from surroundings at \(T_0\) and delivers it through a temperature-matched sequence of infinitesimal condensers. For one increment \(dQ=C\,dT\) at stream temperature \(T\), the reversed Carnot relation gives \(dW_{\min}=dQ(1-T_0/T)\).

Integrating over the stream yields \(W_{\min}=C[(T_o-T_i)-T_0\ln(T_o/T_i)]\). This is the increase of its physical flow exergy, and is smaller than evaluating the Carnot work factor at the final high temperature for the whole heat duty. A device that condenses at a single temperature above the warmest outlet generates entropy during delivery to the colder portions. The result assumes that the source of heat is the environment at \(T_0\); a colder source would introduce an additional resource requirement.

For steady equipment receiving only electricity and environmental heat, with negligible parasitic flows, a meaningful second-law efficiency is the stream’s exergy increase divided by actual electrical input. Electric power is entirely work potential, whereas a kilowatt of heat near room temperature carries only a small fraction of a kilowatt of exergy.

Compare a heat pump with a resistance heater

Water flows at 0.10 kg/s from 293 K to 333 K in a 293 K environment. With \(c_p=4.18\,\mathrm{kJ/(kg\,K)}\), heat duty is \(0.418(40)=16.72\,\mathrm{kW}\). The exergy increase is \(0.418[40-293\ln(333/293)]=1.05\,\mathrm{kW}\) approximately. This relatively small figure reflects the modest average temperature lift. A well-insulated heat pump drawing 4.20 kW from the electrical supply has heating COP \(16.72/4.20=3.98\) and exergy efficiency about 25%. Its surroundings provide 12.52 kW of heat under the stated energy balance.

A resistance heater supplying the identical water service draws 16.72 kW when its losses to the room are negligible. It is nearly 100% efficient on the simple electrical-energy-to-heat basis, yet its exergy efficiency is only about 6.3%. For the heat pump the implied destruction of work potential is \(4.20-1.05=3.15\,\mathrm{kW}\); for resistance heating it is about 15.67 kW. Electricity used by a circulating pump, external heat leaks or a source below environmental temperature belongs in the appropriate device boundary before drawing a practical comparison. The heat-duty equality ensures that the machines are judged against the same delivered temperature service.

For the resistance case the entropy balance can be checked without using the exergy formula. Electrical work carries no entropy across the control surface, and assume no heat leakage. The stream increases its entropy at \(0.418\ln(333/293)=0.0535\,\mathrm{kW/K}\). Multiplying by the 293 K environment gives 15.7 kW destroyed, consistent with the difference between input work and stream exergy gain. This agreement depends on keeping the heater and heated stream in the same chosen boundary.

Recover the pressure dependence of enthalpy

To obtain its pressure derivative, differentiate \(g=h-Ts\). Its differential is \(dg=-s\,dT+v\,dp\). Equality of mixed derivatives gives \((\partial s/\partial p)_T=-(\partial v/\partial T)_p\). Connect composition to the equilibrium ratios

For a real mixture K can depend on phase composition and require an outer property iteration. Here use fixed positive K values to isolate the material balance.

Eliminate the component flows

Define \(\beta=V/F\). Component conservation gives \(z_i=(1-\beta)x_i+\beta y_i=x_i[1+\beta(K_i-1)]\). Thus \(x_i=z_i/[1+\beta(K_i-1)]\). For both compositions to sum to one, enforce \(\sum_i(y_i-x_i)=0\), giving the Rachford–Rice equation:

\[R(\beta)=\sum_i{z_i(K_i-1)\over1+\beta(K_i-1)}=0,\]
\[R'(\beta)=-\sum_i{z_i(K_i-1)^2\over[1+\beta(K_i-1)]^2}.\]

The denominators are positive for \(0\le\beta\le1\). With at least one non-unity K of positive feed fraction, R decreases strictly, so at most one physical root exists. A positive R(0) and a negative R(1) bracket two phases. If \(R(0)\le0\) the stable fixed-K split is all liquid; if \(R(1)\ge0\) it is all vapour, with equality corresponding to incipient phase appearance. Why one voltage can support several measured polarisations

The high-temperature phase may be symmetric under inversion so that states with polarisation \(+P\) and \(-P\) are equivalent in zero field. Near a continuous transition, treat one component of polarisation as an order parameter. A local free-energy density compatible with the symmetry is \(f(P)=a(T-T_c)P^2/2+bP^4/4\) with \(a,b>0\). For a device whose applied electric field \(E\) is controlled, the appropriate potential in this simple model is \(g(P;E)=f(P)-EP\). Vibrations of monoatomic and diatomic chains

From first principles

Turn a local spring law into a phonon dispersion relation

Consider identical atoms of mass \(m\) spaced by \(a\) along a chain. Write \(u_j\) for the displacement from the equilibrium position of atom \(j\). For small changes of bond length, expand the bond energy to second order about its minimum. Its first derivative vanishes and its positive second derivative \(K\) is the measured bond stiffness. The bond on the right pulls with force \(K(u_{j+1}-u_j)\); the left bond contributes \(K(u_{j-1}-u_j)\). Newton’s law gives

\[m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1}) .\]

Translation of a normal mode by one site can change its phase but not its shape. Insert \(u_j=Ue^{i(kja-\omega t)}\), divide by the common amplitude, and simplify:

\[-m\omega^2=K(e^{ika}+e^{-ika}-2)=-4K\sin^2(ka/2),\]
\[\omega=2\sqrt{K/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.\]

For wavelengths much larger than \(a\), \(\sin(ka/2)\simeq ka/2\). The slope gives the sound speed \(a\sqrt{K/m}\). At \(k=0\) all atoms move together and no bond stretches, which explains the zero frequency. More than one atom per primitive cell permits internal relative motion even at \(k=0\), the ingredient needed for an optical branch.

Derivation and applications

Why can a narrow frequency bin near the top of a vibration band contain a substantial share of all modes? Take a monoatomic chain of \(N\) equal masses \(m\) with one longitudinal displacement each. Impose periodic ends and use identical harmonic nearest-neighbour springs of stiffness \(K\) with spacing \(a\). The frequency on the positive half of the first zone rises monotonically to \(\omega_m=2\sqrt{K/m}\). Inverting that branch gives the wavevector bounding the states below a trial frequency,

\[k(\omega)=\frac{2}{a}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_m}\right),\]
\[0\leq\omega\leq\omega_m .\]

Allowed wavevectors are spaced by \(2\pi/(Na)\). Counting the interval of width \(2k(\omega)\) includes both propagation directions. In a large chain the fractional count and its derivative per atom are therefore

\[F(\omega)=\frac{2k(\omega)}{2\pi/(Na)}\frac{1}{N}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_m}\right),\]
\[g(\omega)=\frac{dF}{d\omega}=\frac{2}{\pi\omega_m\sqrt{1-(\omega/\omega_m)^2}}.\]

Here \(g\,d\omega\) is a fraction of the modes. The normalization follows from \(F(\omega_m)=1\), as required by the count of independent displacements. Near the edge the slope of the dispersion tends to zero, so evenly spaced wavevectors map into progressively smaller frequency intervals. This causes an integrable singularity in the continuum density. A finite spectrometer bin always contains a finite count. A measured spectrum can also weight modes differently through a probe’s coupling to atomic motion.

Worked example

Count modes within the upper tenth of the band

For \(N=10{,}000\), the interval between \(0.9\omega_m\) and the upper edge contains \(N[1-(2/\pi)\arcsin(0.9)]\simeq2871\) modes, allowing a mode or two for endpoints. A uniform distribution in frequency would give 1000. A nearly stationary wave packet near the upper edge and a large number of modes per unit frequency have the same origin in the small group velocity.

Second application

Use the spectral median to infer a spring stiffness

An ideal chain with atomic mass \(5.00\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}\) has half of its longitudinal modes below an ordinary frequency of \(4.00\,\mathrm{THz}\). Setting \(F=1/2\) gives \(\omega_{1/2}/\omega_m=\sin(\pi/4)\). The upper frequency is \(5.66\,\mathrm{THz}\), and \(K=m(2\pi f_m)^2/4=15.8\,\mathrm{N/m}\). The frequency ratio is unchanged by conversion to angular frequency, but the final stiffness requires that conversion.

From bond extension to a travelling displacement

Let atoms of mass \(m\) on a line be coupled by springs of stiffness \(K\), spaced \(a\) apart. The spring to the right pulls site \(n\) by \(K(u_{n+1}-u_n)\); the left contributes \(K(u_{n-1}-u_n)\). A uniform shift produces no force. Translation symmetry suggests \(u_n=u e^{i(kna-\omega t)}\). Substitution gives

\[\omega^2(k)=\frac{2K}{m}(1-\cos ka)=\frac{4K}{m}\sin^2(ka/2).\]

The sound speed at long wavelength is \(a\sqrt{K/m}\), consistent with a continuum rod whose one-dimensional stiffness is \(Ka\) and whose mass per length is \(m/a\). At \(k=\pi/a\) neighbouring atoms move in opposition and the slope of the dispersion vanishes. A wave packet made of nearby \(k\)'s moves with the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\), which can differ greatly from the phase velocity \(\omega/k\) near the boundary.

Introduce an internal coordinate

Place masses \(m,M\) alternately, with spacing \(a/2\) between neighbours and cell period \(a\). Let their displacements in cell \(n\) be \(Ue^{ikna}\) and \(Ve^{ikna}\) times the same time factor. The two force balances contain sums of neighbouring amplitudes. For example the A mass sees B in cell \(n\) and in cell \(n-1\), giving \(m\omega^2 U=2KU-K(1+e^{-ika})V\). Exchanging the masses and conjugating the phase gives the second row. Requiring a nonzero solution yields

\[mM\omega^4-2K(m+M)\omega^2+4K^2\sin^2(ka/2)=0 .\]

There are two nonnegative solutions for \(\omega^2\) per \(k\). With \(N\) cells under periodic boundary conditions this gives \(2N\) longitudinal modes, exactly matching the number of independent displacements. At \(k=0\) the lower branch has \(U=V\) and zero frequency. For the upper branch the centre of mass stays fixed, \(mU+MV=0\), with \(\omega^2=2K(1/m+1/M)\). An electric field can excite some optical modes in polar crystals; the branch name alone does not guarantee optical activity.

A gap and a sound speed answer different questions

At the zone boundary \(1+e^{ika}=0\), so the sublattices decouple. If \(M=4m\), the two edge frequencies are \(\sqrt{K/(2m)}\) and \(\sqrt{2K/m}\), a factor of two apart. When the masses become identical the two folded branches meet, recovering the original monoatomic chain in a doubled cell. An apparent branch boundary created by plotting in a smaller zone need not be a physical frequency gap.

Expand the determinant for small \(k\) on the lower branch. Since \(\omega^2\) scales as \(k^2\), discard the term of order \(k^4\). This gives \(v_s=a\sqrt{K/[2(m+M)]}\). The two springs in a cell act in series for long waves, while both masses contribute to inertia. The sound speed alone determines a stiffness-to-mass ratio; measuring the upper branch supplies independent information about the internal motion.

Convert a mode count into thermal predictions

Quantisation assigns a harmonic mode levels \(\hbar\omega(j+1/2)\). The thermal part of its mean energy is \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\). Low-temperature excitations thus select long-wavelength acoustic modes while high-frequency optical modes remain scarcely populated. Counting states in a three-dimensional sphere gives a low-frequency density proportional to \(\omega^2\) for linear branches. Rescaling the energy integral by \(\hbar\omega/k_BT\) gives thermal energy proportional to \(T^4\) and heat capacity proportional to \(T^3\). Dimensionality and linear dispersion are essential assumptions behind that power.

Count thermally generated vacancies

A mode calculation for a perfect crystal starts with one atom on every site. Heating a real sample can leave some atoms at a surface or reservoir and some lattice sites empty. How large should that defect population be even at thermal equilibrium? Consider \(N\) equivalent sites and \(n_v\) independent vacancies. At fixed temperature and pressure let \(g_f\) be the Gibbs cost of making one vacancy and transferring its atom to the reservoir. It may contain local vibrational contributions. We count the separate entropy of choosing the missing sites explicitly.

There are \(W=N!/[n_v!(N-n_v)!]\) such arrangements. With \(x=n_v/N\) and macroscopic occupations, Stirling’s formula gives

\[\frac{G-G_0}{N}=xg_f+k_BT\left[x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right].\]

Minimizing with respect to \(x\) gives \(g_f+k_BT\ln[x/(1-x)]=0\). The second derivative is positive. Thus \(x=1/(1+e^{g_f/(k_BT)})\). For positive \(g_f\gg k_BT\) there are few vacancies and \(x\simeq e^{-g_f/(k_BT)}\). Each vacancy costs energy, but at tiny \(x\) the number of possible positions grows fast enough to favour a nonzero population. A finite microscopic specimen can still contain zero vacancies at a given instant.

Take \(g_f=0.800\,\mathrm{eV}\) as constant over the chosen range and \(k_B=8.6173\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV/K}\). At \(900\,\mathrm K\), \(k_BT=0.07756\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(x=3.31\times10^{-5}\). A region with \(10^{15}\) sites contains an expected \(3.31\times10^{10}\) empty sites. At \(600\,\mathrm K\) the equilibrium fraction drops to \(1.91\times10^{-7}\). Rapid cooling may leave an excess because vacancies must migrate to sinks before the concentration relaxes. The formation free energy in this calculation sets an equilibrium population. A migration barrier would enter the time to attain it.

A light substitution can localize a vibration

An infinite monatomic chain has a continuous vibration band, while a single altered atom can support a vibration spatially concentrated near one site. Let identical springs of stiffness \(K\) connect masses \(m\) on a chain of spacing \(a\), except the central mass at site zero is \(M\). Seek a symmetric localized solution with alternating neighbouring amplitudes \(u_n=A(-q)^{|n|}e^{-i\omega t}\) for \(0<q<1\). Alternation is motivated by the top of the perfect-chain band where adjacent atoms oscillate out of phase. Exponential decay supplies the localization.

At any ordinary site away from the impurity, the equation \(-m\omega^2u_n=K(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_n)\) gives \(m\omega^2=K(2+q+q^{-1})\). Since \(q+q^{-1}>2\), this frequency lies above the perfect-chain band edge \(\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{K/m}\). At site zero both neighbours have displacement \(-q u_0\), giving \(M\omega^2=2K(1+q)\). Eliminating \(\omega\) and cancelling \(1+q\) yields

\[q=\frac{M}{2m-M},\qquad \omega^2=\frac Km(2+q+q^{-1}).\]

The physical condition \(0<q<1\) requires a lighter central mass \(0<M<m\). A heavy isotope can still scatter travelling waves or produce resonant response within the band. This ansatz predicts no exponentially localized symmetric mode above the band for the heavier substitution. Its absence follows from the admissible decay factor rather than an assumption that a heavy impurity cannot move.

Predict a localized spectral peak

Take host atomic mass \(m=4.00\times10^{-26}\) kg, spring stiffness \(K=40.0\) N/m, lattice spacing \(a=0.300\) nm and impurity \(M=m/2\). The host upper angular frequency is \(2\sqrt{40.0/(4.00\times10^{-26})}=6.32\times10^{13}\) rad/s. For the impurity \(q=1/3\), so \(\omega^2=(16/3)K/m\), giving \(\omega=7.30\times10^{13}\) rad/s or \(f=11.6\) THz. The ordinary chain ends near \(10.1\) THz.

The neighbour amplitude is minus one third of the central amplitude, and the second neighbour has plus one ninth. Expressing the absolute envelope as \(e^{-|n|a/\xi}\) gives localization length \(\xi=a/\ln3=0.273\) nm. As \(M\) approaches \(m\) from below, \(q\) approaches one, localization length diverges and the extra frequency merges with the band edge. A finite-chain calculation should therefore vary chain length before claiming that a very weak impurity mode is spatially localized: a box shorter than its decay length cannot separate that broad tail from a standing host mode.

Spatially resolved displacement data can provide two independent estimates of impurity mass. Invert the decay-factor relation to obtain \(M/m=2q/(1+q)\) from neighbouring displacement amplitudes. Separately insert the measured localized frequency and the same host force constant in the central equation of motion. Agreement tests the assumption that the impurity changes only inertia. A substituted atom may also change the two neighbouring springs: in that case the observed frequency and decay factor can no longer be fitted by altering mass alone. This inversion distinguishes an isotope-like substitution from a stronger local bonding perturbation.

A weak second impurity far away perturbs the localized mode only through its exponentially small tail. Bringing two such impurities within a few decay lengths allows the modes to mix and split in frequency, offering another test of their spatial localization.

Filling electronic states and measuring the Fermi surface

Condensed matter and band theory

Use exclusion and state counting to predict carrier energies, thermal response and the effect of dimensionality.

From first principles

Count the states in a filled Fermi sphere

Take independent spin-one-half electrons in a large cube of volume \(V=L^3\) with periodic boundary conditions. The condition \(e^{ik_xL}=1\) permits \(k_x=2\pi n_x/L\), with the same spacing in the other directions. Thus each allowed wave vector occupies reciprocal volume \((2\pi)^3/V\). For the free-particle energy \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\), all states below energy \(E\) lie in a sphere. Counting its volume and two spin states gives

\[N(E)=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.\]

At zero temperature the exclusion principle fills these single-particle states up to \(E_F\). Writing \(n=N/V\), solve the counting formula at that upper limit:

\[k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\]
\[E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2 n)^{2/3}.\]

The number of available states in a thin energy shell is the derivative \(D(E)dE\). In three dimensions \(D(E)\propto\sqrt E\) because the shell area grows with \(k^2\) while \(dk/dE\) falls as \(1/k\). This counting assumes an isotropic parabolic band. Additional valleys or a different effective dimension change the result.

Derivation and applications

Obtain the pressure of a filled Fermi sea by compressing its states

Even at zero temperature a box of mobile electrons exerts a pressure on its boundaries. Consider \(N\) noninteracting spin-one-half fermions in a three-dimensional volume \(V\), with fixed isotropic mass \(m\) and quadratic energy. Take the bottom of the band as a volume-independent energy reference. Every state below \(E_F\) is occupied once per allowed spin state. Since the density of states including spin has the form \(D(E)=C\sqrt E\), dividing two elementary integrals eliminates the normalization constant,

\[\frac{U}{N}=\frac{\int_0^{E_F} E\,C\sqrt E\,dE}{\int_0^{E_F} C\sqrt E\,dE}=\frac35 E_F .\]

State counting gives \(E_F=(\hbar^2/2m)(3\pi^2 N/V)^{2/3}\). During a uniform compression at fixed \(N\) the upper occupied energy rises. At zero entropy the energy derivative with respect to box volume is the negative pressure, so

\[P=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac{2U}{3V}=\frac25 nE_F,\]
\[n=\frac NV .\]

Density appears both explicitly and through \(E_F\). A second volume derivative gives the positive resistance to a small fractional compression,

\[B=-V\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac53P=\frac23 nE_F .\]

Both \(P\) and the bulk modulus \(B\) have units of energy per volume. The factor \(5/3\) distinguishes the change of pressure from pressure itself. An actual solid also has electrostatic and lattice contributions. Extracting the electronic contribution from a measured solid modulus requires information about these other stresses and the volume dependence of the band parameters.

Worked example

Estimate the electronic stiffness of a simple metal

Take \(n=5.00\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(E_F=5.00\,\mathrm{eV}\). With \(1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J\), the product \(nE_F\) is \(4.01\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The pressure contribution is \(16.0\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and the modulus is \(26.7\,\mathrm{GPa}\). A one percent volume decrease would raise this pressure by approximately \(0.267\,\mathrm{GPa}\) in the linear response regime.

Second application

Predict a finite compression without linearizing

Suppose a separate trapped gas starts at pressure \(12.0\,\mathrm{kPa}\) and is compressed slowly to \(0.800\) of its original volume at fixed particle number and negligible entropy. Its pressure becomes \(12.0(1/0.800)^{5/3}=17.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Its energy per particle rises by only \((1/0.800)^{2/3}=1.16\) times. The larger pressure ratio also contains the increase in particles per unit volume.

Count occupied states at zero temperature

Consider a cubic box of volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries and free electrons of effective model mass \(m\). Allowed wavevectors occupy a volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) per state for each spin. At zero temperature each state below the chemical potential is filled. For \(\epsilon=\hbar^2 k^2/2m\) the filled region is a sphere and each \(k\) has two spin states. Hence

\[N=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3},\qquad k_F=(3\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2 k_F^2}{2m}.\]

With \(n=8.5\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ and the free electron mass, \(k_F\) is about \(1.36\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\) about 7.0 eV. The corresponding temperature \(E_F/k_B\) is roughly 81,000 K. Ordinary room temperature therefore changes occupation only near the outer surface of this sea in a simple metal. This is a model estimate; anisotropic bands and multiple pockets change its interpretation.

The density of states is a derivative

Replacing \(k_F\) by \(k(E)=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) gives the integrated state count below energy \(E\). Its derivative is

\[D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E,\qquad D(E_F)=\frac{3N}{2E_F}.\]

Check dimensions by integrating \(D(E)\,dE\) to recover a count. In two dimensions the occupied area grows as \(k^2\) and the density per energy is constant for a parabolic band. In one dimension the count grows as \(k\) and the density has an integrable \(E^{-1/2}\) singularity at its bottom. A large density of states means many states fit in a narrow energy window. It does not imply a large group velocity: a flat dispersion may create the large density.

Why only a shell contributes to heat capacity

At finite temperature the occupation is \(f(E)=[1+e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT}]^{-1}\). Heating can promote an electron only into a vacant state. Deep in the filled sea all nearby destinations are occupied. The active shell has width of order \(k_BT\), involving about \(D(E_F)k_BT\) states; the typical energy change is another factor \(k_BT\). Thus the thermal energy increases quadratically and the heat capacity rises linearly with \(T\).

A coefficient follows by expanding the smooth density of states across that shell. At fixed particle number, the relevant excess energy to leading order is \(\int (E-E_F)D(E_F)[f(E)-\Theta(E_F-E)]\,dE\). The filled and empty sides contribute equally to this order. Substituting \(x=|E-E_F|/k_BT\) and using \(\int_0^\infty x/(e^x+1)\,dx=\pi^2/12\) gives \(C_{\rm el}=(\pi^2/3)D(E_F)k_B^2T\). This expansion requires the density to be smooth on the thermal scale; a band edge at the chemical potential needs separate treatment.

Separate two low-temperature contributions

In a three-dimensional simple metal combine \(C_\mathrm{el}=\gamma T\) with the phonon result \(C_\mathrm{ph}=\beta T^3\). Plot \(C/T\) against \(T^2\); the intercept estimates \(\gamma\), and the slope estimates \(\beta\). If two points are \((4,3)\) and \((16,6)\) in K² and mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻², respectively, \(\beta=0.25\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴ and \(\gamma=2.0\) mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻². Curvature across the fitted interval would be evidence against this two-term description. To go beyond a free sphere we next retain the periodic potential of the ions.

Count charges and electronic compressibility in two dimensions

A two-dimensional electron layer illustrates how changing available spatial dimensions alters the density of states and an electrical measurement. Assume one parabolic subband \(E=E_c+\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)/(2m^*)\), spin degeneracy two and no valley degeneracy. A square sample of area \(A\) with periodic boundary conditions supplies one state per spin per reciprocal area \((2\pi)^2/A\). Counting the disk up to \(k_F\) gives areal density \(n_s=k_F^2/(2\pi)\), hence

\[E_F-E_c=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m^*},\qquad D_A(E)=\frac{dn_s}{dE_F}=\frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}\]

above the subband edge at zero temperature. Unlike the square-root density in three dimensions, each occupied parabolic two-dimensional subband contributes a constant step. When an additional subband begins to fill it supplies another step, possibly with different degeneracy or mass. Temperature and disorder broaden the step in a real measurement.

An electrostatic gate must both create a field across the insulating spacer and raise the layer’s chemical potential. For areal capacitance \(C_g'\) of the spacer and increment of sheet charge magnitude \(dQ'=e\,dn_s\), the required voltage is \(dV=dQ'/C_g'+dE_F/e\). The second term defines the quantum capacitance \(C_q'=e^2D_A\). Their inverse capacitances add in series. For a small density of states the electronic contribution measurably lowers the total capacitance even when the geometric dimensions are known precisely.

Measure which capacitance limits a device

Take \(m^*=0.067m_e\) and \(n_s=2.00\times10^{15}\) m⁻². Then \(k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_s}=1.12\times10^8\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F-E_c=7.15\) meV. At 4 K the thermal scale \(k_BT=0.345\) meV is smaller than this separation, supporting the degenerate estimate. The electronic capacitance per area is \(0.0448\) F/m². For a spacer with \(C_g'=0.0100\) F/m², the measured series value is \((1/0.0100+1/0.0448)^{-1}=0.00818\) F/m².

Over a gate area of \(100\) μm², the predicted capacitance is \(0.818\) pF rather than the purely geometric \(1.00\) pF. Treating that measured value as a change of dielectric thickness would misidentify an electronic effect. In the limit of a very high density of states, the quantum capacitance becomes large and the geometric value is recovered. Near depletion the assumptions of a sharp occupied subband and uniform layer require scrutiny. Comparing capacitance with an independent sheet-density measurement can test whether a low reading reflects depleted electronic states or simply a different insulating spacer.

Using the series result, a small gate swing can be split into two measurable energy changes. A 10.0 mV swing at the stated operating point changes sheet charge magnitude by \(8.18\times10^{-5}\) C/m², or sheet density by \(5.10\times10^{14}\) m⁻². The geometric voltage increment is \(8.18\) mV and the chemical-potential contribution is \(1.82\) mV, corresponding to a Fermi-level shift of 1.82 meV. Their sum recovers the imposed swing. This decomposition assumes the layer remains in the same subband and equilibrates through its electrical contact on the capacitance measurement timescale.

A slow measurement with significant leakage instead mixes capacitance with conductance and can corrupt both estimated voltage contributions.

Count states added by raising the Fermi level

For a parabolic band, states within a radius \(k\) fill a length, area or volume in reciprocal space. Since \(k\) scales as \(\sqrt E\), the integrated count varies as \(E^{d/2}\). Normalise at one energy to compare exponents independently of the sample dimensions.

Magnetic moments and susceptibility

Condensed matter and band theory

Derive thermal magnetisation for a two-state moment and distinguish its signature from the response of a filled electronic sea.

From first principles

Obtain the Curie law by counting two magnetic orientations

Take many identical, independent localized moments per volume \(n\), each with component \(+\mu\) or \(-\mu\) along an applied induction \(B\). The magnetic energies are \(-\mu B\) and \(+\mu B\). At temperature \(T\) their probabilities are proportional to their Boltzmann factors. With \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\), normalize the two weights and subtract their contributions to the mean:

\[Z=e^x+e^{-x},\]
\[\langle m_z\rangle=\mu\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}=\mu\tanh x .\]

Multiply by number density to get magnetization. In weak fields or at high temperatures \(x\ll1\), so the leading term in \(\tanh x\) gives

\[M\simeq\frac{n\mu^2 B}{k_BT},\]
\[\chi=\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\simeq\frac{\mu_0 n\mu^2}{k_BT}.\]

The susceptibility expression uses a weakly magnetic sample with \(B\simeq\mu_0 H\) and negligible demagnetizing correction. At low temperature and fixed nonzero field both the expansion and the \(1/T\) extrapolation fail. The exact two-state average saturates at \(n\mu\) because no moment can contribute more than \(\mu\). Exchange between neighbours can also invalidate the independent weights.

Derivation and applications

Distinguish the saturation moment from the Curie moment

An ion with angular momentum quantum number \(J\) can have more than two projections on a magnetic field. Consider independent identical ions of number density \(n\) in thermal equilibrium, with isotropic Landé factor \(g\) and negligible splitting at zero field. Label the levels \(m=-J,\ldots,J\) so that their moments along the applied induction \(B\) are \(g\mu_Bm\). Adjacent Zeeman levels are then separated by \(g\mu_B B\). Introduce \(x=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)\). Weighing each level with its Boltzmann factor gives

\[Z=\sum_{m=-J}^{J}e^{mx},\]
\[M=ng\mu_B\,\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial x}.\]

In weak fields each zero-field state is almost equally likely. The zeroth-order sum of \(m\) vanishes by symmetry. The next term in the numerator is \(x\sum m^2\), while the denominator is \(2J+1\) to this order. Using the sum of squares of the allowed projections gives

\[\langle m\rangle\simeq x\frac{\sum_{m=-J}^J m^2}{2J+1}=\frac{J(J+1)}{3}x .\]

For a dilute specimen take \(B\simeq\mu_0H\). The dimensionless volume susceptibility is the derivative of \(M\) with respect to magnetic field strength \(H\). In strong fields the highest projection alone survives. These limits identify two different combinations of the microscopic parameters,

\[\chi=\frac{\mu_0 n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_BT},\]
\[M_{\rm sat}=ng\mu_B J .\]

The Curie-law effective moment is \(g\mu_B\sqrt{J(J+1)}\), whereas the aligned projection is \(g\mu_BJ\). Their ratio approaches one for large \(J\). A susceptibility fit requires \(Jx\ll1\); a saturation measurement requires the opposite tendency and sufficiently weak interactions between moments.

Worked example

Predict the response of dilute spin-one ions

Take \(J=1\), \(g=2.00\), \(n=1.00\times10^{26}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), and \(T=10.0\,\mathrm K\). The aligned magnetization is \(1.85\times10^3\,\mathrm{A/m}\). The Curie formula gives \(\chi=2.09\times10^{-4}\). At \(B=0.100\,\mathrm T\), \(Jx=0.0134\), and the predicted magnetization is only \(\chi B/\mu_0=16.6\,\mathrm{A/m}\), consistent with weak polarization.

Second application

Identify a moment from two calibrated measurements

Suppose independent concentration measurements in another material yield a saturation moment of \(3.00\mu_B\) per ion and an effective Curie moment of \(3.87\mu_B\). The squared ratio is \(1+1/J=(3.87/3.00)^2=1.66\), giving \(J\simeq1.50\). The saturation value then gives \(g\simeq2.00\). Susceptibility alone fixes only a product of the factor \(g\) and the size of the angular momentum.

A moment has an energy as well as a direction

Consider \(N\) independent identical magnetic moments in volume \(V\), each with allowed projection \(\pm\mu\) along a uniform applied induction \(B\). The energies are \(\mp\mu B\); the state aligned with the field has lower energy. For \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) the one-moment partition function is \(Z_1=2\cosh(\beta\mu B)\). From \(\langle m_z\rangle=k_BT\,\partial_B\ln Z_1\) obtain

\[M=n\mu\tanh(\mu B/k_BT),\qquad n=N/V .\]

The full curve approaches \(n\mu\) at high field or low temperature. In the weak-field regime \(\mu B\ll k_BT\), expansion of the hyperbolic tangent gives \(M\simeq n\mu^2 B/(k_BT)\). Under weak magnetisation and negligible demagnetising corrections use \(B\simeq\mu_0H\); the dimensionless SI susceptibility \(\partial M/\partial H\) is then \(\chi=\mu_0n\mu^2/(k_BT)\). Be explicit about differentiating with respect to \(B\) or \(H\), which carry different units.

Work a moment count from two measurements

Suppose a dilute two-state sample exhibits saturation magnetisation \(9.27\times10^3\) A/m and the moments are known to have \(\mu=\mu_B=9.27\times10^{-24}\) J/T. The implied density is \(1.00\times10^{27}\) m⁻³. At 10 K and 1 T the argument \(\mu B/k_BT\) is 0.0672, safely in the linear range for a rough estimate: \(M\simeq622\) A/m. At the same temperature a field of about 14.9 T gives argument one and \(M/M_\mathrm{sat}=0.762\). Fitting a straight line up to that field would underestimate the zero-field slope.

If the moment magnitude is unknown, saturation determines \(n\mu\) and the small-field slope supplies \(n\mu^2\). Taken together they can separate density from moment within this model. A mixture of moment species, orbital contributions or interactions can defeat this two-equation inference. Plotting curves at several temperatures against \(B/T\) tests the independent-moment scaling without needing to know the overall density beforehand.

Compare with itinerant spin response

For a weakly perturbed degenerate metal, the two spin species shift by \(\mp\mu B\). Almost all states below \(E_F\) remain doubly occupied, so only a thin shell at the Fermi surface changes its net spin. At low temperature and nearly constant total density of states \(D_V(E_F)\), the spin imbalance is approximately \(D_V(E_F)\mu B\), giving \(M\simeq\mu^2D_V(E_F)B\). The leading Pauli response is nearly temperature independent, in contrast to the \(1/T\) scaling of independent localised moments. This argument addresses spin susceptibility; orbital diamagnetism also contributes to a real measured susceptibility.

Diagnose a small impurity contribution

If bulk data show a roughly constant offset plus a low-temperature upturn, test a simple additive model \(\chi=\chi_0+C/T\) over a weak-field interval. Plot \(\chi\) against \(1/T\) rather than taking an inverse before subtracting the offset. An upturn can arise from a dilute impurity population even when the host electronic sea dominates heat capacity. If scaling fails systematically, examine interactions between moments; the next chapter shows how cooperative ordering changes the small-field susceptibility.

Cool local moments by adiabatic demagnetization

Independent moments have an entropy that depends on the ratio of magnetic field to temperature. For a two-state moment of magnitude \(\mu\), energies are \(\mp\mu B\). Define \(x=\mu B/(k_BT)\). The one-moment partition function is \(2\cosh x\), average energy is \(-\mu B\tanh x\), and the magnetic entropy follows from \(S=k_B\ln Z+U/T\),

\[\frac{S_{\rm spin}}{Nk_B}=\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x .\]

At zero field for fixed positive temperature the moment has two equiprobable orientations and contributes \(k_B\ln2\). At large \(x\) nearly all moments favour the lower state and the spin entropy tends to zero. If field is increased at fixed temperature, this reduction of spin entropy expels heat to a bath. Now isolate the material and reduce the field quasistatically. In the ideal spin-dominated limit entropy remains fixed, so \(x\) remains fixed and \(T/B\) is constant.

Thermal isolation and sufficiently slow variation are both required. The first prevents external heat from entering, and the second allows internal degrees of freedom to follow equilibrium. Other stores of entropy, particularly lattice vibrations or mobile electrons, participate in an actual specimen. The pure spin scaling can then be modified even when the process as a whole is perfectly adiabatic.

Estimate temperature and cooling capacity

Take \(N=1.00\times10^{21}\) independent moments each of magnitude \(\mu_B\), initially at 2.00 K. Magnetize isothermally from negligible field to 3.00 T, using \(\mu_B/k_B=0.6717\) K/T. The final magnetic parameter is \(x=1.01\), giving spin entropy per particle about \(0.362k_B\) compared with the initial \(0.693k_B\). The heat passed to the bath in that reversible isothermal step is \(T N k_B(0.693-0.362)=9.14\) mJ. Work is supplied by the field source during preparation.

Isolate and reduce field from 3.00 T to 0.300 T. Holding \(x\) fixed predicts \(T_f=2.00(0.300/3.00)=0.200\) K. Further field reduction in the ideal noninteracting equations would approach zero temperature. Real moments interact, however, and internal effective fields or ordering lift their near-zero-field degeneracy. Thermal leaks and finite relaxation rates also become increasingly important. A practical test records temperature against field on several ramp rates; dependence on the rate can distinguish irreversible heating from a reproducible correction to the equilibrium entropy model. The final cold sample can absorb only finite heat before its moments become disordered, even if the predicted endpoint temperature is very low.

A second check comes from magnetization along the ideal isolated sweep. Since \(M=N\mu\tanh x\) at fixed volume and \(x\) is conserved, the spin magnetization should stay almost constant while both the external field and the temperature fall. In contrast, an isothermal field decrease reduces \(x\) and magnetization. Measuring temperature alone cannot establish that the sample really followed an isentropic spin path. A simultaneous magnetic reading tests the fixed-population interpretation. Deviations concentrated at the weakest fields can signal interactions between moments rather than a general failure of the high-field entropy estimate.

Exchange, spontaneous magnetisation and critical response

Condensed matter and band theory

Use a mean-field model and a free-energy expansion to explain how a macroscopic moment can arise in zero applied field.

From first principles

How an interaction permits spontaneous magnetization

Put an Ising spin \(s_i=\pm1\) on each lattice site and use a ferromagnetic bond energy \(-J s_i s_j\) with \(J>0\). If every site has \(z\) neighbours, replacing their actual spins by the common mean \(m=\langle s\rangle\) assigns a trial spin energy \(-s(zJm+\mu B)\). Thermal averaging this two-level problem must reproduce the same mean used to construct the local field:

\[m=\frac{\sum_{s=\pm1}s\, e^{s(zJm+\mu B)/(k_BT)}}{\sum_{s=\pm1}e^{s(zJm+\mu B)/(k_BT)}}=\tanh\!\left(\frac{zJm+\mu B}{k_BT}\right).\]

At zero applied field the right-hand side is odd, so \(m=0\) always solves the equation. To decide when two nearby nonzero solutions appear, compare the slope at the origin with one, then retain the cubic correction. Write \(a=zJ/(k_BT)\):

\[m=am-\tfrac13a^3m^3+\cdots,\]
\[T_c^{\rm MF}=zJ/k_B,\]
\[m^2\simeq3(a-1)/a^3 .\]

Just below this mean-field critical temperature either sign of \(m\) is possible even in zero applied field. The approximation replaces correlated neighbours by an average. In short-range one-dimensional systems that omission can even change whether a finite-temperature transition exists.

Derivation and applications

Calculate a correlation length without a mean-field transition

Mean-field theory replaces the neighbours of each spin by a common average. A chain gives a case in which the energy of a local reversal can instead be counted exactly. Take an open chain of Ising spins \(s_i=\pm1\), zero external field, and ferromagnetic nearest-neighbour interaction \(J>0\). The energy is \(-J\sum_i s_is_{i+1}\). Specify the first spin and all bond variables \(b_i=s_i s_{i+1}\). This is a one-to-one description of every configuration. Since the energy is a sum over these independent binary bonds, each bond has weights \(e^{K}\) and \(e^{-K}\), where \(K=J/(k_BT)\).

\[p_{\rm wall}=P(b_i=-1)=\frac{1}{1+e^{2K}},\]
\[\langle b_i\rangle=1-2p_{\rm wall}=\tanh K .\]

A wall costs \(2J\) compared with an aligned bond. To compare two spins separated by \(r\) bonds, multiply the intervening \(b_i\). Every interior spin then occurs twice and drops out. Bond independence makes the average of this product particularly simple,

\[\langle s_i s_{i+r}\rangle=\left\langle\prod_{j=i}^{i+r-1} b_j\right\rangle=(\tanh K)^r .\]

Writing the result as an exponential in the physical separation \(ra\), where \(a\) is the site spacing, gives

\[\xi=-\frac{a}{\ln(\tanh K)},\]
\[\xi\simeq\frac a2 e^{2K}\quad (K\gg1).\]

At low temperature the last expression follows by expanding \(\tanh K\simeq1-2e^{-2K}\). At every positive temperature the probability of a wall remains positive. Thus widely separated spins lose correlation in this short-range one-dimensional model. A finite sample shorter than \(\xi\) can still look ordered during a measurement.

Worked example

Estimate decorrelation over a microscope field

Let \(J/k_B=20.0\,\mathrm K\), \(T=10.0\,\mathrm K\) and \(a=0.400\,\mathrm{nm}\). The wall probability is \(1/(1+e^4)=0.0180\), giving about 18 walls per 1000 bonds on average. The exact correlation length is \(10.9\,\mathrm{nm}\). Across \(20.0\,\mathrm{nm}\), or 50 bonds, the measured spin correlation should be approximately \((\tanh2)^{50}=0.160\).

Second application

Infer exchange from a measured spatial decay

In a different chain the correlation drops to one half over eight bonds at \(T=30.0\,\mathrm K\). Each step has correlation \(2^{-1/8}=0.9170\). Inverting \(K=\tfrac12\ln[(1+\langle b\rangle)/(1-\langle b\rangle)]\) gives \(J/k_B=47.1\,\mathrm K\). A local image counting opposite neighbours should then find a fraction \((1-0.9170)/2=0.0415\), an independent test using adjacent spins.

Replace neighbouring moments by their average

An independent moment has zero mean at zero field for any positive temperature. In a ferromagnet aligned neighbours can lower the exchange energy. Consider Ising variables \(s_i=\pm1\) with energy \(-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_i s_j-\mu B\sum_i s_i\), \(J>0\). Each distinct nearest-neighbour bond is counted once. In a uniform mean-field approximation replace a neighbour's spin by \(m=\langle s\rangle\). For coordination number \(z\), an individual spin then feels an effective energy bias \(zJm+\mu B\). Applying the two-state result to that spin requires self-consistency:

\[m=\tanh[(zJm+\mu B)/(k_BT)] .\]

At \(B=0\), \(m=0\) always solves the equation. The slope of the right-hand side at the origin is \(zJ/(k_BT)\). Above the mean-field transition temperature \(T_c=zJ/k_B\) only the disordered solution exists. Below it two symmetry-related nonzero stable solutions appear. The sign selected in a macroscopic region can depend on a small field and on its history.

Approach the transition from either side

For small field above \(T_c\), expand \(\tanh x\simeq x\). This gives \(m\simeq\mu B/[k_B(T-T_c)]\), a Curie–Weiss susceptibility. A positive intercept of an inverse-susceptibility plot can indicate dominant ferromagnetic exchange over a suitable high-temperature interval. It is not an exact measurement of the true ordering temperature, particularly when fluctuations, geometry or competing interactions matter.

Just below \(T_c\), at zero field, expand through the cubic term. Writing \(t=T_c/T\), the nonzero solution has \(m^2\simeq3(t-1)/t^3\), approaching \(3(T_c-T)/T_c\) sufficiently near the transition. The square-root onset is a mean-field prediction. A finite-temperature transition of this type does not exist for a one-dimensional short-range Ising chain in the thermodynamic limit, despite the same simple self-consistency calculation predicting one. Neglecting the cost and entropy of fluctuations can qualitatively change a prediction.

Interpret the curvature of a free energy

A local free energy even under \(m\mapsto-m\) at zero field may be expanded as \(f=\tfrac12a(T-T_c)m^2+\tfrac14 b m^4-hm\), \(a,b>0\). At \(h=0\), the minima sit at 0 above the transition and at \(m=\pm\sqrt{a(T_c-T)/b}\) below it. A positive second derivative indicates local stability, and the differential response \(dm/dh=1/f''\) grows as the curvature weakens. For \(a(T-T_c)=-1,b=4\) in scaled units, the minima are at \(\pm0.5\), have curvature 2 and are lower than the intervening maximum by 0.0625 in free-energy density units.

From a local moment to a macroscopic measurement

Different regions can select opposite stable magnetisations. Domain walls cost exchange and anisotropy energy, while domain formation can reduce the magnetic field energy outside a specimen. A small measured zero-field net moment can therefore coexist with substantial local order. A static uniform free-energy graph alone cannot predict coercivity: wall pinning, nucleation, defects and measurement times affect a hysteresis loop. Temperature-dependent susceptibility, local probes and field-cycling measurements answer complementary questions about the same ordered sample.

Derive the profile and energy of a domain wall

Two domains magnetized in opposite easy-axis directions can coexist, but rotating the magnetization in between costs exchange and anisotropy energy. Consider a uniaxial ferromagnet with magnetization magnitude fixed and angle \(\theta(x)\) relative to the easy axis varying along one spatial coordinate. Assume a planar 180-degree wall in a geometry where additional magnetostatic costs can be neglected for this profile. With exchange stiffness \(A\) in J/m and anisotropy density \(K\) in J/m³, the wall energy per unit area is

\[\sigma_w=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[A(\partial_x\theta)^2+K\sin^2\theta]\,dx .\]

A narrow wall saves anisotropy energy but makes the spatial gradient steep. A broad wall saves exchange energy but places more material away from the preferred axis. Variation gives \(2A\theta''=2K\sin\theta\cos\theta\). Multiply by \(\theta'\) and integrate once. Since both gradient and anisotropy term vanish deep inside either domain, the integration constant is zero, yielding \(A(\theta')^2=K\sin^2\theta\). Exchange and anisotropy costs are locally equal on the minimum-energy profile.

Take increasing \(\theta\) between zero and \(\pi\) and define \(\Delta=\sqrt{A/K}\). Separation gives \(d\theta/\sin\theta=dx/\Delta\). With the wall centred at zero, \(\tan(\theta/2)=\exp(x/\Delta)\). Inserting the first integral back into the wall energy and changing variable from \(x\) to \(\theta\) gives \(\sigma_w=2K\Delta\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta=4\sqrt{AK}\). The profile has long tails, so a quoted wall width must state its convention; \(\pi\Delta\) is a frequently used slope-based width rather than a sharp boundary.

Compare two anisotropies at fixed exchange stiffness

Use \(A=1.00\times10^{-11}\) J/m and \(K=1.00\times10^5\) J/m³. The scale \(\Delta\) is 10.0 nm and conventional width \(\pi\Delta\) is 31.4 nm. Wall energy is \(4.00\) mJ/m². A planar patch of area \(1.00\) μm² costs \(4.00\times10^{-15}\) J, even though the two far domains have the same zero-field bulk energy.

If a second specimen at the same temperature has four times the anisotropy and the same exchange stiffness, the predicted width halves while the cost per area doubles. The combination of width and energy could therefore independently estimate \(A\) and \(K\), whereas either measurement by itself fixes only a ratio or a product. In the limit \(K\to0\) the preferred axis loses meaning and the wall spreads indefinitely in this model. Finite sample size, magnetostatic fields, boundaries and defects then set spatial scales omitted from the one-dimensional calculation.

One can also express the measured magnetization components explicitly. With the local moment in a fixed rotation plane, the easy-axis component is proportional to \(\cos\theta=-\tanh(x/\Delta)\) and the transverse component to \(\sin\theta=1/\cosh(x/\Delta)\). The easy-axis profile passes smoothly through zero even though the moment magnitude remains fixed throughout the wall. A probe sensitive only to that component might incorrectly interpret the central zero as suppression of ferromagnetism. Measuring a transverse component or a quantity sensitive to the full moment tests the distinction between a rotating order parameter and a vanishing one.

Distinguish a soft magnetic response from a loss of stability

In zero applied field the lowest stabilising even expansion is \(f=a m^2/2+m^4/4\) in these units. Vary the quadratic coefficient through zero. Equilibrium requires both stationarity and positive curvature at a minimum.

Pairing, excitation gaps and what a spectrum measures

Condensed matter and band theory

Use a two-component excitation model to predict spectroscopic signatures, then distinguish three physically different gaps encountered across the course.

From first principles

See how pairing opens a spectroscopic gap

Begin with a single normal-state band and let \(\xi\) be its energy measured from the chemical potential. A minimal mean-field model for a uniform paired state mixes an electron amplitude with a hole amplitude through a complex pairing energy \(\Delta\). The two diagonal energies have opposite signs. To find its stationary excitations write the eigenvalue condition:

\[H=\begin{pmatrix}\xi&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\det(H-EI)=(\xi-E)(-\xi-E)-|\Delta|^2=0 .\]

It follows that the two branches are \(E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi^2+|\Delta|^2}\). At the former Fermi surface \(\xi=0\), even the cheapest positive excitation costs \(|\Delta|\). To predict a tunnelling spectrum for constant isotropic gap and nearly constant normal density of states, count the normal states mapping into an energy interval \(dE\). The Jacobian from \(\xi=\pm\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) gives the normalized density, with the electron and hole weights combined:

\[\frac{D_s(E)}{D_n(0)}=\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}}\quad (|E|>|\Delta|).\]

There are no states within the ideal gap at this level of description. Finite temperature, lifetime broadening and instrumental resolution change the measured conductance near its edges. The model assumes a nonzero \(\Delta\); it predicts consequences of pairing without deriving the attractive interaction that supports it.

Derivation and applications

Predict the weights of a particle peak and its partner

A pairing gap determines excitation energies, but a momentum-resolved spectrum also measures how strongly an electron couples to each peak. Consider a clean, uniform superconductor with a real positive gap \(\Delta\), at temperature low enough to neglect thermal broadening. Measure the normal-state energy \(\xi\) at one wavevector from the chemical potential. In the electron and hole basis the positive-energy eigenvector has components \(u,v\). The first row of its eigenvalue equation requires

\[(\xi-E)u+\Delta v=0,\]
\[\frac{v}{u}=\frac{E-\xi}{\Delta},\]
\[E=\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}.\]

Normalize the vector to unit probability. Since \((E-\xi)(E+\xi)=\Delta^2\), the squared amplitude ratio can be written without subtracting two nearly equal energies. The resulting coherence factors are

\[|u|^2=\frac{1}{1+(E-\xi)/(E+\xi)}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{\xi}{E}\right),\]
\[|v|^2=\frac12\left(1-\frac{\xi}{E}\right).\]

The negative-energy eigenvector is orthogonal to the positive one and consequently has electron weight \(|v|^2\). In the ideal single-particle spectral function at this momentum the two sharp peaks have unit total weight,

\[A(\epsilon)=|u|^2\delta(\epsilon-E)+|v|^2\delta(\epsilon+E),\]
\[\int A(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon=1 .\]

Thus the weights exchange as the wavevector passes through the normal Fermi surface. At \(\xi=0\) the two weights are equal. Well above the surface the positive-energy peak carries almost all the weight. To compare both sides quantitatively an experiment must account for occupation and matrix elements; ordinary photoemission alone samples the occupied side most directly.

Worked example

Resolve a weak partner peak

At a selected momentum let \(\xi=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=4.00\,\mathrm{meV}\). The spectral energies are \(+5.00\) and \(-5.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), with respective electron weights \(0.800\) and \(0.200\). At a momentum with the opposite \(\xi\) and the same gap the two peak energies stay fixed while their weights reverse. Such a weight exchange tests particle–hole mixing more specifically than the peak separation alone.

Second application

Reconstruct a gap using corrected peak areas

In a different sample the peaks are separated by \(6.00\,\mathrm{meV}\), and corrected electron spectral areas have positive-to-negative ratio \(3:2\). Their normalized weights are \(0.600\) and \(0.400\), so \(\xi/E=0.200\). With \(E=3.00\,\mathrm{meV}\) this gives \(\xi=0.600\,\mathrm{meV}\) and \(\Delta=\sqrt{3.00^2-0.600^2}=2.94\,\mathrm{meV}\). Half the separation overestimates the gap here because the probed momentum lies away from the normal Fermi surface.

Build excitations on a paired state

For a conventional fully gapped superconductor, a mean-field description mixes particle and hole amplitudes near the Fermi surface. With \(\xi_k=\epsilon_k-\mu\) and gap parameter \(\Delta\), a simplified matrix for a time-reversed pair of states is

\[\begin{pmatrix}\xi_k&\Delta\\\Delta^*&-\xi_k\end{pmatrix}\binom{u_k}{v_k}=E\binom{u_k}{v_k},\qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta|^2}.\]

A positive-energy quasiparticle has minimum energy \(|\Delta|\) where the normal band crossed the chemical potential. Breaking a pair into two excitations can cost at least \(2|\Delta|\). These are different thresholds, so the probe and transition being discussed must be specified. The matrix displays level repulsion, reminiscent of the nearly free electron calculation, but its basis states and the physical origin of the coupling are different.

Transform a density of states

Suppose the normal-state density is approximately constant over the gap scale and the gap is isotropic. For \(E>|\Delta|\), \(|\xi|=\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) and \(d|\xi|/dE=E/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\). Counting the paired branches relative to the normal-state convention yields \(D_s(E)/D_n=|E|/\sqrt{E^2-|\Delta|^2}\) outside the gap and zero inside in the ideal zero-broadening model. The edge singularity is integrable. States removed from the gap reappear near its edges. Thermal broadening, disorder, nodes or an anisotropic gap can change the measured shape.

A low-temperature tunnel junction with a normal counterelectrode of nearly constant density can use differential conductance as a probe of this spectrum, subject to matrix-element and broadening effects. Peaks at biases \(\pm1.5\) mV suggest \(|\Delta|\simeq1.5\) meV in this geometry. Peak separation on the voltage axis is 3.0 mV. Using the weak-coupling BCS ratio \(2|\Delta(0)|/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.53\) gives \(T_c\simeq9.86\) K. Measure \(T_c\) independently; agreement with this estimate supports the simple model, while deviation calls for closer investigation.

Select a second observation that distinguishes the mechanism

A nearly free electron band gap separates eigenvalues near a zone boundary. For conduction it also matters where the chemical potential sits. A superconducting gap opens at the Fermi surface on entering an ordered state, and has magnetic screening and a characteristic temperature dependence. A photonic stop band instead excludes real propagating wavevectors in a frequency range for the specified periodic optical medium. All three can produce missing spectral weight or reduced transmission. Their energies, frequency units, occupation rules and control parameters differ.

Return to the numerical optical stack with a prediction: making both layers identical closes its first stop band even though the artificial zone boundary remains. For a proposed superconducting gap, repeat the tunnelling and magnetisation measurements as temperature crosses the transition. For an electronic band edge, relate spectroscopy to filling, dispersion and transport. This combination of an analytic limit, a controlled change and an independent observable is what makes an interpretation of a spectrum persuasive.

Read the phase difference across a superconducting weak link

A single-particle tunnelling spectrum can reveal an excitation gap. A weak junction between two superconductors can also carry a coherent pair current that responds directly to their relative condensate phase. Use the gauge-invariant phase difference \(\varphi\), including the vector-potential line integral across the junction. For a simple tunnel junction in the low-transparency regime, its leading coupling energy is \(U_J=-E_J\cos\varphi\). Its \(2\pi\) periodicity reflects invariance under an integer phase winding and the energy minimum at aligned phase for a conventional junction.

A potential difference \(V\) between the condensates causes their relative phase to advance at \(\dot\varphi=2eV/\hbar\), choosing a consistent orientation of current and voltage. Combining electrical power \(IV\) with the rate of change of junction energy gives \(IV=E_J\sin\varphi\,\dot\varphi\), hence

\[I=I_c\sin\varphi,\qquad I_c=\frac{2eE_J}{\hbar}.\]

Under a steady subcritical current the phase can remain constant while \(V=0\). At constant nonzero voltage the phase winds and the ideal coherent current oscillates with frequency \(f_J=2e|V|/h\). These two statements distinguish a DC pair current with no terminal voltage from an AC pair response under bias. A real junction includes quasiparticle conduction and capacitance, so its observed total current can contain additional components.

Convert a microwave frequency into a voltage

A junction biased at 5.00 μV has Josephson oscillation frequency \(f_J=(2e/h)V=2.42\) GHz. Illuminate the junction with a stable microwave reference of frequency 10.0 GHz. If its mean phase advance locks to one full winding per reference cycle, then \(\langle\dot\varphi\rangle=2\pi f_{\rm ref}\), requiring mean voltage \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)=20.7\) μV. Locking to two windings per cycle gives 41.4 μV. Such voltage steps probe phase dynamics and the charge associated with coherent transfer.

Suppose the same device has measured critical current \(I_c=30.0\) nA at the operating temperature. Its coupling energy is \(\hbar I_c/(2e)=9.87\times10^{-24}\) J, corresponding to \(E_J/k_B=0.715\) K. At a bath temperature of 50 mK thermal energy is appreciably smaller, although phase fluctuations can still depend on capacitance and the surrounding circuit. If either electrode is driven normal, the equilibrium phase-link energy disappears and the ideal supercurrent relation ceases to apply. Observing quantized phase locking and an excitation gap therefore accesses two different signatures of the superconducting state; their characteristic frequencies need not be numerically identical.

The two Josephson relations yield a further calibration test that does not require measuring energy directly. At fixed microwave frequency, the voltage separation of successive locking steps is \(hf_{\rm ref}/(2e)\) independently of junction area in the ideal phase model. Increasing junction area can change total critical current and the visibility or width of current intervals over which locking persists, yet should leave the quantized voltage spacing fixed. Looking at spacing and current width separately distinguishes uncertainty in the reference frequency from a change in the junction coupling or its electromagnetic environment.

How the same electrons carry heat and charge

Condensed matter and band theory

A metal with high electrical conductivity often also conducts heat well. The quantitative relation involves an open-circuit measurement, the thermal width of the Fermi surface, and the energy dependence of the states able to carry current.

Weigh the states that can carry a current

Use an isotropic band of quasiparticles with energy \(E\), velocity magnitude \(v(E)\), density of states per volume \(D(E)\) and elastic relaxation time \(\tau(E)\). In a weak electric field or temperature gradient the departure from local equilibrium is proportional to the relaxation time times the applied driving term. For a given transport direction the angular average gives \(\langle v_x^2\rangle=v^2/3\) in three dimensions. Define \(\Sigma(E)=D(E)v(E)^2\tau(E)/3\). Electrical current weights a particle by its charge \(q=-e\), while heat current weights it by \(E-\mu\). Three moments consequently suffice for the two linear responses:

\[K_j=\int\Sigma(E)(E-\mu)^j[-\partial_E f(E)]\,dE,\]
\[j=0,1,2.\]

Let \(\mathcal E\) denote the electric/electrochemical field with any chemical-potential gradient properly included. The resulting currents along one direction are \(j_e=q^2K_0\mathcal E-qK_1\nabla T/T\) and \(j_Q=qK_1\mathcal E-K_2\nabla T/T\). The factor \(E-\mu\) in the thermal drive follows from differentiating the Fermi function with respect to temperature. The repeated first moment is a manifestation of reciprocal coupling of electrical and thermal transport in this zero-field model.

Impose the actual electrical boundary condition

A voltage probe draws negligible charge current. Set \(j_e=0\) to obtain \(\mathcal E=S\nabla T\) with \(S=K_1/(qTK_0)\). Substitute this field into the heat current to get \(\kappa_e=(K_2-K_1^2/K_0)/T\). Under the positive measure \(\Sigma(-f')dE/K_0\), the bracket divided by \(K_0\) is the variance of \(E-\mu\). Hence the open-circuit electronic thermal conductivity is nonnegative. Measuring with zero imposed electric field would retain a thermally driven charge current and a different heat-flow coefficient.

For a degenerate metal with smooth \(\Sigma\), the weight \(-f'\) is a narrow even peak. Replacing \(\Sigma\) by \(\Sigma(\mu)\) gives \(K_1=0\), \(K_0=\Sigma(\mu)\), and \(K_2=(\pi^2/3)(k_BT)^2\Sigma(\mu)\). The dimensionless second moment follows by integrating \(x^2/[4\cosh^2(x/2)]\) over the real axis. With \(\sigma=q^2K_0\),

\[\frac{\kappa_e}{\sigma T}=L_0=\frac{\pi^2k_B^2}{3e^2}=2.44\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,\Omega/K^2}.\]

At \(T=100\,\mathrm K\), a metal with \(\sigma=2.0\times10^7\,\mathrm{S/m}\) has estimated electronic \(\kappa_e=48.8\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\). A total measured conductivity of 60 would leave about \(11\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\) for other carriers if the assumptions hold. Phonons, inelastic scattering or strong energy dependence may change that inference.

Select a narrow range of transport energies

A model energy filter \(\Sigma\propto\exp[-(x-c)^2/(2w^2)]\), with \(x=(E-\mu)/(k_BT)\), \(c,w\) dimensionless, introduces a tunable departure from this metallic limit. The Seebeck coefficient is \((k_B/q)\langle x\rangle\) and the normalised Lorenz ratio is \(\operatorname{var}(x)/(\pi^2/3)\). A very narrow filter centred above the chemical potential produces strong signed thermopower but little heat transport at zero charge current, because current-carrying particles have almost identical excess energies. Those statements refer to the ratio and thermal moments; the absolute conductivity also depends on the overall transmission.

The experiment integrates these moments numerically and plots their normalised weight against the reference Fermi derivative. Vary \(c\) from positive to negative at fixed width. Predict which observable reverses sign and which is invariant before reading the numerical results.

Why a polar crystal has two optical phonon frequencies

Condensed matter and band theory

A uniform relative displacement of positive and negative ions creates an electric polarisation. A longitudinal vibration induces a macroscopic restoring field while a transverse displacement has a different electrical boundary condition. An infrared spectrum can reveal both frequencies.

Separate the ionic and electronic contributions

For one infrared-active optical mode let \(u\) be relative displacement per primitive cell, \(M\) the effective reduced mass, \(Z^*\) the effective charge in coulombs, and \(N\) the number of cells per volume. The oscillator equation driven by the macroscopic field is \(M(\ddot u+\gamma\dot u+\omega_{\rm TO}^2u)=Z^*E\). The zero-macroscopic-field stiffness defines the transverse optical frequency in this oscillator model. Bound electronic response at these frequencies supplies a real relative background permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\). The ionic polarisation is \(P_{\rm ion}=NZ^*u\), so \(D=\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty E+P_{\rm ion}\).

With time convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\), solving for \(u\) gives

\[\epsilon_r(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{S}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\]
\[S=\frac{N(Z^*)^2}{\epsilon_0 M}.\]

The signed effective charge appears twice, once as drive and once in the dipole density. Its square therefore sets oscillator strength. At zero frequency the ions can follow the field and \(\epsilon_s=\epsilon_\infty+S/\omega_{\rm TO}^2\). Far above the phonon resonance but below electronic absorption the ionic term is small, making \(\epsilon_\infty\) the observed plateau.

Apply Gauss's law to a longitudinal vibration

Consider a long-wavelength, nonzero wavevector \(\boldsymbol k\) with no external free charge. Gauss's law gives \(\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol D=0\). For a longitudinal field \(D_L=0\), so the ions create \(E_L=-NZ^*u/(\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty)\). Substituting this opposing field into the undamped oscillator equation adds a restoring term. Thus

\[\omega_{\rm LO}^2=\omega_{\rm TO}^2+S/\epsilon_\infty,\]
\[\frac{\omega_{\rm LO}^2}{\omega_{\rm TO}^2}=\frac{\epsilon_s}{\epsilon_\infty}.\]

Equivalently the longitudinal collective mode is the zero of the lossless dielectric function. The transverse optical frequency is its pole. For a single isotropic mode this is the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation. With several infrared modes, anisotropy, damping or mobile free carriers, assigning one measured peak and one dielectric ratio to a single oscillator needs further justification.

Connect a reflectance band with the two modes

In the lossless limit rewrite \(\epsilon_r=\epsilon_\infty(\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega^2)/(\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2)\). Between the two frequencies this is negative. A transverse electromagnetic wave satisfies \(k^2=\epsilon_r\omega^2/c^2\), so its wavenumber becomes imaginary there in a nonmagnetic lossless crystal. At a planar vacuum boundary a semi-infinite crystal therefore has unit ideal reflectance over this interval. Finite damping makes the refractive index complex and allows absorption, smoothing the edges. The two mode frequencies have been deduced from mechanical response and electric boundary conditions; an electromagnetic wave interacting with the lattice is a coupled phonon–polariton excitation.

For \(f_{\rm TO}=6.0\,\mathrm{THz}\), \(\epsilon_\infty=4\) and \(\epsilon_s=9\), \(f_{\rm LO}=9.0\,\mathrm{THz}\). At 7.5 THz in the lossless limit \(\epsilon_r=4(81-56.25)/(36-56.25)=-4.89\). Below the mode, normal-incidence static-index reflectance is \([(\sqrt9-1)/(\sqrt9+1)]^2=0.25\). Above the phonon plateau the corresponding background reflectance approaches \(1/9\). Exchange and two-particle energies

Quantum mechanics

Build allowed two-electron states and calculate how exchange symmetry changes the expectation of a repulsive interaction.

From first principles

Construct the allowed two-electron spatial wave functions

Consider identical electrons prepared from two orthonormal spatial orbitals \(a,b\). A product \(|ab\rangle\) labels which coordinate slot contains which orbital. Exchanging the slots maps it to \(|ba\rangle\); the same exchange squared is the identity, so its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). Form the normalized eigenvectors

\[\Phi_\pm=(|ab\rangle\pm|ba\rangle)/\sqrt2,\]
\[P_{12}\Phi_\pm=\pm\Phi_\pm .\]

The fermionic exchange postulate applies to the full state. A spatially symmetric pair therefore combines with an antisymmetric spin state, and a spatially antisymmetric pair with symmetric spin. For a symmetric interaction \(W\), expand its expectation in both components. The two diagonal terms coincide and the off-diagonal terms are complex conjugates, so

\[\langle\Phi_\pm|W|\Phi_\pm\rangle=J\pm K,\]
\[J=\langle ab|W|ab\rangle,\]
\[K=\operatorname{Re}\langle ab|W|ba\rangle .\]

The difference of \(2K\) is an interference effect in the two-particle amplitude. It can affect the spin-dependent spectrum even when \(W\) is itself independent of spin. If the orbitals cease to be orthogonal, their two-particle normalization must be recomputed before applying this expectation value.

Derivation and applications

Exchange a pair of particles prepared in overlapping orbitals

The familiar factor of \(1/\sqrt2\) for a two-particle superposition assumes orthogonal one-particle states. Localized orbitals centred near each other can overlap. Let \(a(x),b(x)\) be normalized orbitals and \(S=\langle a|b\rangle\). Temporarily label the two coordinate slots 1 and 2 to evaluate inner products. The overlap of \(|a(1)b(2)\rangle\) with its exchange is the product of two one-particle inner products:

\[\langle ab|ba\rangle=\langle a|b\rangle\langle b|a\rangle=|S|^2 .\]

Symmetrize or antisymmetrize the coordinate wave function and normalize only after including this overlap. For \(|S|<1\) the two allowed candidates are

\[\Psi_\pm(x_1,x_2)=\frac{a(x_1)b(x_2)\pm b(x_1)a(x_2)}{\sqrt{2(1\pm|S|^2)}} .\]

The appropriate spatial symmetry depends on the particles and their spin preparation. To connect the result to a measurable energy, consider a weak one-dimensional short-range interaction modelled by \(W=g\delta(x_1-x_2)\). First-order perturbation theory evaluates the probability density on the coincidence line. With \(D=\int|a(x)|^2|b(x)|^2dx\) it gives

\[\langle W\rangle_+=\frac{2gD}{1+|S|^2},\]
\[\langle W\rangle_-=0.\]

At equal coordinates the two exchanged terms have the same value, so the antisymmetric amplitude cancels exactly. This cancellation applies to an ideal contact interaction. A force with finite range can still sample nonzero amplitude on either side of the coincidence line. In the limit \(|S|=1\) the antisymmetric candidate itself disappears.

Worked example

Estimate the interaction splitting in separate traps

Two localized orbitals have overlap magnitude \(0.500\) and measured density overlap \(D=0.120\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}\). For a weak repulsion \(g=2.00\,\mathrm{meV\,\mu m}\) the symmetric spatial state acquires a shift \(2(2.00)(0.120)/1.25=0.384\) meV. The antisymmetric state has zero first-order contact shift. A distinguishable pair in the unsymmetrized product would give \(gD=0.240\) meV. All estimates assume orbital shapes can be held fixed as the interaction is turned on.

Second application

Put both particles in the same orbital

For two bosons occupying the same normalized orbital, \(S=1\) and \(\Psi_+=a(x_1)a(x_2)\). A box orbital of uniform density over \(L=5.00\) micrometres has \(D=1/L=0.200\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}\). At the same coupling the shift is \(g/L=0.400\) meV. Using the orthogonal-orbital normalization here would double this energy. Two spin-polarized identical fermions cannot prepare this shared-orbital state.

Exchange acts on the entire state

Two identical particles observed at different locations cannot be assigned enduring physical labels by a measurement of position alone. The operator \(P_{12}\) swaps all coordinates of the two particles, including spin. Since a second swap restores the original state, \(P_{12}^2=1\) and exchange eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). In three dimensions identical electrons are fermions, so their total state is antisymmetric under exchange. For a spin-independent Hamiltonian, spatial and spin factors can often be treated separately while enforcing the required product symmetry.

Take two normalized orthogonal spatial orbitals \(a\) and \(b\). Both products \(a(1)b(2)\) and \(b(1)a(2)\) represent the same occupied orbitals. Their symmetric and antisymmetric combinations are

\[\Phi_\pm=\frac{a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)}{\sqrt2}.\]

The spin singlet is antisymmetric, requiring \(\Phi_+\) for the spatial part. All three spin triplets are symmetric, requiring \(\Phi_-\). Two electrons can share one spatial orbital in a singlet because they occupy different spin orbitals. In contrast the antisymmetric spatial combination vanishes if one sets \(b=a\). The physical constraint applies to the full one-electron state.

Direct and exchange matrix elements

Suppose \(a,b\) are different eigenstates of a single-particle Hamiltonian with energies \(\epsilon_a,\epsilon_b\). For their two-particle sum both spatial combinations have energy \(\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b\). Now include a weak symmetric interaction \(W(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)\). Expanding \(\langle\Phi_\pm|W|\Phi_\pm\rangle\) gives two identical diagonal terms and two conjugate off-diagonal terms. Their combination is

\[\langle W\rangle_\pm=J\pm K,\qquad J=\langle ab|W|ab\rangle,\quad K=\mathrm{Re}\langle ab|W|ba\rangle .\]

For the Coulomb interaction \(K\) is nonnegative. The triplet spatial state has suppressed coincidence probability, since \(\Phi_-(\mathbf r,\mathbf r)=0\). A repulsive interaction can therefore cost less in the triplet than in the corresponding singlet. This splitting is produced by interference of exchanged amplitudes in the interaction expectation, even when the interaction does not directly depend on spin.

A measured splitting

For two orbitals suppose the independent-particle energy sum is \(-8.00\) eV, the direct integral is \(1.20\) eV and exchange is \(0.070\) eV. In the fixed-orbital estimate the singlet has energy \(-6.73\) eV and the triplet \(-6.87\) eV. The separation is \(2K=0.140\) eV, corresponding to frequency \(3.39\times10^{13}\) Hz. This energy difference need not produce a strong electric-dipole line between the two states. A spin-independent electric dipole preserves the orthogonality of singlet and triplet spin factors.

Orbitals localized far apart can still have appreciable direct electrostatic interaction because charge densities at different sites interact. The exchange integral also requires orbital overlap at a given coordinate and falls rapidly as overlap is removed. Holding sites apart while monitoring the singlet–triplet splitting can consequently distinguish the exchange contribution from the shared direct energy shift. Strong correlation or substantial orbital rearrangement would require a larger basis than the two fixed configurations used here.

Thermal population measurements require one more factor. With negligible magnetic field the three triplet spin projections share the same energy. At temperature well above the splitting, their combined population can approach three times that of the singlet. To extract an energy splitting from population ratios, include this multiplicity before taking a Boltzmann logarithm.

Interfere two identical bosons at a beam splitter

Exchange symmetry has measurable consequences even when there is no interaction energy. A balanced lossless optical beam splitter mixes two incident spatial modes \(a,b\) into outputs \(c,d\). Choose phases so creation operators transform as \(a^\dagger\to(c^\dagger+d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\) and \(b^\dagger\to(c^\dagger-d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\). The relative sign enforces orthogonality of the two output combinations, as required for a unitary transformation. Prepare one photon in each input with identical spectrum, time profile and polarization.

The input is \(a^\dagger b^\dagger|0\rangle\). Applying the two transformations yields terms for both photons leaving \(c\), both leaving \(d\), or one leaving each output. The two one-per-output terms have opposite signs. Boson creation operators commute, so they cancel exactly:

\[|\psi_{\rm out}\rangle=\frac{(c^\dagger)^2-(d^\dagger)^2}{2}|0\rangle=\frac{|2,0\rangle-|0,2\rangle}{\sqrt2}.\]

The alternatives that cancel are both photons transmitted and both photons reflected into the one-per-output event. Photon number is conserved, but ideally the coincidence probability vanishes. If the photons have orthogonal polarization or arrive at distinguishable times, the corresponding internal states retain information about the alternatives. Their one-per-output probabilities then add to one half. This is a two-particle interference measurement, distinct from ordinary interference fringes in a single detector intensity.

Predict a delay scan for Gaussian wave packets

Let normalized temporal field amplitudes be \(f(t)\) and \(g(t)\) and let their overlap be \(s=\int f^*(t)g(t)\,dt\). Retaining these internal labels while adding the two exchange amplitudes gives coincidence probability \(P_{\rm coin}=(1-|s|^2)/2\) for an ideal balanced splitter with one photon in each input. Choose otherwise identical transform-limited Gaussian pulses whose individual arrival-time intensity distributions have standard deviation \(\sigma_t\). Shifting one by delay \(\tau\) makes \(|s|^2=\exp[-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)]\), obtained by completing the square in the overlap integral.

Take \(\sigma_t=100\) fs. At \(\tau=200\) fs the expected coincidence probability is \((1-e^{-1})/2=0.316\), between zero for perfect overlap and 0.500 for widely separated pulses. For 10,000 incident pairs and ideal detection one expects about 3160 coincidences. At a delay \(2\sigma_t\sqrt{\ln2}=167\) fs the coincidence probability has recovered halfway from its minimum to the distant baseline. The full width between those two half-depth delays is 333 fs. Dark counts, multiple-pair generation or unequal beam-splitter transmission can raise the measured minimum even at zero delay. Recording the far-delay baseline separates overall detector efficiency from the normalized depth of the dip.

It is important here that indistinguishability includes every degree of freedom capable of preserving information about the two input alternatives. Two photons with the same central wavelength and similar arrival times may still have orthogonal polarizations or distinguishable frequency profiles. Including polarization overlap multiplies the temporal overlap by the appropriate inner product for separable polarization and time states. Rotating one linear polarization continuously therefore provides an independent control of coincidence depth, even with the delay held at zero. It can test whether residual coincidences come from distinguishability or from an unequal optical splitting ratio.

Density matrices and loss of phase

Quantum mechanics

Distinguish a coherent superposition from a statistical mixture and reconstruct a dephasing rate from measurements in two bases.

From first principles

Turn a preparation ensemble into a density matrix

Suppose a preparation delivers normalized state \(|\psi_j\rangle\) with classical probability \(w_j\), where the weights sum to one. The Born probabilities for a measurement outcome with projector \(\Pi\) must be averaged over the unrecorded preparation. Use the identity \(\langle\psi_j|\Pi|\psi_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|\Pi)\) and linearity of the trace:

\[P(\Pi)=\sum_jw_j\langle\psi_j|\Pi|\psi_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi),\]
\[\rho=\sum_jw_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j| .\]

In an energy basis the diagonal entries determine energy populations. To measure coherence, project onto \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Multiplication and unit trace give

\[P(+)=\tfrac12(\rho_{00}+\rho_{11}+\rho_{01}+\rho_{10})=\tfrac12+\operatorname{Re}\rho_{01}.\]

Thus equal energy populations can correspond either to perfectly coherent superposition or to an incoherent mixture. For unitary individual evolutions \(\rho(t)=U\rho(0)U^\dagger\); random relative phases across the ensemble can suppress the average off-diagonal entry while preserving both populations. Tomography in a further phase-shifted basis accesses the imaginary component.

Derivation and applications

Separate reversible phase dispersion from irreversible decay with an echo

An ensemble of spins can lose average phase coherence even when every individual spin evolves unitarily. Start each spin in an equal-amplitude superposition. In a frame rotating at the reference frequency, assume each spin has a time-independent angular detuning \(\delta\) drawn from a Gaussian with zero mean and standard deviation \(\sigma\). Ignore energy relaxation during the measurement. Under free evolution, the two components accumulate a phase difference \(\delta t\). The normalized transverse coherence is the characteristic function of the detuning distribution:

\[C(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\delta}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp[-\delta^2/(2\sigma^2)]e^{-i\delta t}.\]

Completing the square in the Gaussian exponent or Fourier transforming the normalized Gaussian gives

\[C_{\rm free}(t)=e^{-\sigma^2t^2/2},\]
\[P_{+x}(t)=\tfrac12[1+\operatorname{Re}C_{\rm free}(t)].\]

Now apply an ideal instantaneous \(\pi\) rotation about \(x\) at time \(\tau\). It exchanges the two levels, reversing the sign of subsequent relative phase accumulation in the toggling frame. An individual spin accumulates \(\delta\tau-\delta(t-\tau)\) before readout at \(t\ge\tau\). Gaussian averaging yields

\[|C_{\rm echo}(t)|=e^{-\sigma^2(2\tau-t)^2/2}.\]

At \(t=2\tau\) the static phase dispersion refocuses for every detuning. A separate independent exponential homogeneous decay factor \(e^{-t/T_2}\) would survive in the echo amplitude. Detunings changing appreciably between the two halves also spoil the cancellation. The echo therefore measures which part of an apparent decay remembers its earlier phase.

Worked example

Predict recovery in a broadened sample

Take \(\sigma/(2\pi)=1.00\) kHz. At \(t=0.500\) ms the unrefocused coherence is \(e^{-\pi^2/2}=0.00719\), so \(P_{+x}=0.5036\). With a \(\pi\) pulse at \(0.250\) ms, the same static ensemble instead has ideal echo magnitude one at \(0.500\) ms. The two experiments distinguish rapid loss of ensemble contrast from loss of the individual phase information.

Second application

Infer a homogeneous decay and a timing tolerance

Suppose echoes at \(2.00\) and \(4.00\) ms have normalized magnitudes \(0.800\) and \(0.640\). Their ratio is \(0.800\), giving \(T_2=-2.00\,\mathrm{ms}/\ln0.800=8.96\) ms. If the readout is displaced by \(0.100\) ms from the true echo, static broadening with the previous \(\sigma\) reduces its contrast by another factor \(e^{-(2\pi\,0.100)^2/2}=0.821\). Timing and homogeneous decay enter separately in this inference.

Two preparations with identical energy populations

Prepare a two-level system with states \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\). One source always prepares \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Another prepares \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) at random with equal probability. Measuring in the displayed basis gives identical frequencies for the two sources. After projection onto \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\), however, the first source always gives plus and the second gives plus on half the trials. An adequate description must store more than the populations seen in one basis.

Define \(\rho=\sum_jp_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|\) for a preparation ensemble. Probabilities follow from \(P(Q)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho Q)\) for a projector \(Q\). The trace is one, Hermiticity makes expectation values real and positivity ensures probabilities are nonnegative. The first preparation has all matrix entries equal to \(1/2\). The second has diagonal entries \(1/2\) and off-diagonal entries zero. Although a density matrix can be decomposed into ensembles in multiple ways, every measurement prediction depends only on the matrix itself.

Coherence can be lost to an unobserved system

Consider a pure joint state \((|0\rangle|e_0\rangle+|1\rangle|e_1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) of system and environment. Average over any complete environment basis, equivalently take the partial trace. The system retains equal populations, and its off-diagonal element is \(\rho_{01}=\langle e_1|e_0\rangle/2\). If the environment states become orthogonal, they carry enough information to distinguish the two alternatives and system-only interference disappears. The combined system can still be pure.

A simple dephasing model multiplies off-diagonal elements by \(e^{-t/T_2}\), leaving populations fixed. In a rotating frame that removes the known level splitting, initial plus preparation then gives

\[P_+(t)=\frac{1+e^{-t/T_2}}2,\qquad \mathrm{Tr}\,\rho^2=\frac{1+e^{-2t/T_2}}2 .\]

The first expression follows by projecting onto plus; the second is the sum of diagonal entries of \(\rho^2\). Purity ranges from one for a pure state to one half for the completely mixed two-level state. Population relaxation would change the diagonal entries too. Measuring both the energy basis and the interference basis helps decide whether the simpler model is adequate.

An interference record

Following a calibrated plus preparation, a measurement in the rotating frame gives \(P_+=0.700\) after 30.0 μs. The contrast is \(2P_+-1=0.400\), giving \(T_2=-30.0/\ln(0.400)=32.7\) μs. The inferred purity is \((1+0.400^2)/2=0.580\). At 60.0 μs an exponential model gives contrast \(0.160\) and \(P_+=0.580\). A different next value can signal nonexponential noise or a misestimated coherent phase.

A single interference quadrature cannot distinguish reduced visibility from uncompensated precession. To test the rotating frame assumption, vary the final projection phase and fit the full fringe. Its phase reports the coherent rotation; its amplitude reports visibility. Combining this with an energy-basis measurement determines the full Bloch vector and makes the purity estimate meaningful even when populations drift.

Purity estimates from finite counts can also be misleading when each fitted probability is treated independently. The reconstructed Bloch vector must have length at most one for a physical density operator. Longer vectors indicate sampling fluctuations or inconsistent calibrations. Increasing repetitions narrows counting uncertainty, while rotating a known reference state helps diagnose systematic errors among measurement axes.

Energy relaxation places a bound on phase memory

Pure dephasing leaves populations fixed. A two-level system coupled to a cold environment can also lose energy by relaxing from excited state \(|1\rangle\) to ground state \(|0\rangle\). Suppose the probability for an initially excited system to survive time \(t\) is \(s=e^{-t/T_1}\). A simple system–environment evolution sends \(|1,E_0\rangle\) to \(\sqrt s|1,E_0\rangle+\sqrt{1-s}|0,E_1\rangle\) and leaves \(|0,E_0\rangle\) unchanged. The two environment states represent absence or presence of a released excitation and are orthogonal.

For an initial superposition only its unrelaxed excited amplitude shares the original environment state with the ground amplitude. Partial tracing consequently multiplies the off-diagonal system element by \(\sqrt s\), while the excited-state probability is multiplied by \(s\). Additional independent Markovian phase noise can multiply the off-diagonal element by \(e^{-t/T_\phi}\). In a rotating frame the resulting evolution is

\[\rho_{11}(t)=e^{-t/T_1}\rho_{11}(0),\qquad \rho_{01}(t)=e^{-t/T_2}\rho_{01}(0),\qquad \frac1{T_2}=\frac1{2T_1}+\frac1{T_\phi}.\]

Thus even a perfectly stable transition frequency cannot make coherence last indefinitely when population relaxes. In this two-level model \(T_2\le2T_1\), with equality if there is no additional pure dephasing. At finite bath temperature upward jumps also occur and populations approach a nonzero equilibrium excitation. A multilevel environment or nonexponential noise can require a different fitting model.

Separate two decay mechanisms using independent preparations

First prepare the excited state and measure population decay; suppose it gives \(T_1=40.0\) μs. Prepare \(|+\rangle\) independently and fit fringe contrast to obtain \(T_2=50.0\) μs. The pure-dephasing rate is \(1/50.0-1/80.0=0.00750\) μs⁻¹, corresponding to \(T_\phi=133\) μs. At \(t=40.0\) μs after plus preparation, the excited population is \(e^{-1}/2=0.184\), and the real coherence in the calibrated rotating frame is \(e^{-0.8}/2=0.225\). Then \(P_+=1/2+\mathrm{Re}\rho_{01}=0.725\).

The reconstructed purity is \(\rho_{00}^2+\rho_{11}^2+2|\rho_{01}|^2=0.801\) to rounding. As time grows, coherence vanishes but the cold-bath model returns the system to the pure ground state, so purity ultimately rises back to one. This differs qualitatively from pure dephasing of an initial plus state, whose limiting purity is one half. An independently measured \(T_2>2T_1\) would call for checking the data and assumptions about temperature, state assignment, fit functions and time-dependent noise before assigning an unphysical negative pure-dephasing rate.

The two independent preparations can be checked against a single density matrix at an additional time. Starting in plus, a general measurement along a transverse direction gives a cosine fringe whose offset is one half regardless of the energy populations, while its amplitude measures the transverse coherence. A separate energy-basis measurement supplies the population imbalance. Together they determine the full Bloch-vector length, which should never exceed one. If independently fitted transverse and longitudinal decay curves imply a greater length at some time, the combined fitted model cannot represent a physical quantum state there even if each measured curve looks plausible in isolation.

Checking positivity across the fitted interval also constrains long extrapolations from records that cover only a small fraction of a relaxation time.

Thermal radiance, étendue and detector calibration

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A square millimetre of a heated object emits thermal radiation into an entire hemisphere. A detector sees only the rays accepted by its optics and only wavelengths to which it responds. Derive the equilibrium spectrum, follow it through an optical collection system and infer what temperature or emissivity a calibrated reading can actually determine.

Count optical states in a large equilibrium cavity

Take a cubic airless cavity of volume \(V\) in thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\). For its bulk mode density we can use periodic boundary conditions. Allowed wavevectors are separated by \(2\pi/L\) in each coordinate. Count a spherical shell in wavevector space, divide by the volume per state and include two independent transverse polarisations. Substituting \(k=2\pi\nu/c\) gives \(g(\nu)d\nu/V=8\pi\nu^2d\nu/c^3\). Using standing waves in an ideally reflecting large box gives the same leading volume density when directions and polarisations are counted consistently.

One oscillator mode of frequency \(\nu\) can hold \(m=0,1,2,\ldots\) photons. Its thermal occupation has probabilities proportional to \(e^{-mh\nu/k_BT}\). Summing the geometric series and taking its logarithmic derivative gives \(\langle m\rangle=1/(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)\). Multiplying by the density of modes and energy per photon gives spectral energy density \(u_\nu\). The zero-point contribution of a harmonic oscillator is not part of the thermal power exchanged between these black surfaces. At low frequency the result approaches an energy \(k_BT\) per mode; extending that classical limit to arbitrarily high frequency would make the integrated power diverge.

An isotropic distribution of energy travelling at speed \(c\) produces a normal flux through a small aperture weighted by the direction cosine. Per unit solid angle its radiance is \(B_\nu=cu_\nu/(4\pi)\). Transform to equal wavelength intervals using \(B_\lambda d\lambda=B_\nu|d\nu|\). The two useful spectral forms are

\[B_\nu=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac1{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1},\]
\[B_\lambda=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.\]

The wavelength expression has units of watts per square metre per steradian per metre of wavelength. To express it per micrometre multiply by one millionth. At 400 K and 10 micrometres the result is 33.56 W per square metre per steradian per micrometre. At 295 K it is 9.14 in the same units. Equal wavelength and equal frequency bins carry different widths, so their distributions reach their maxima at different converted wavelengths. A spectrum's peak should always name its horizontal variable and spectral-density convention.

Distinguish emitter power from brightness in a chosen direction

For a black surface with angle-independent radiance into its outward hemisphere, integrate \(\cos\theta\,d\Omega\). The result is \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\). The emitted spectral power per unit area is \(\pi B_\lambda\). Integrate over frequency using \(x=h\nu/(k_BT)\). The remaining integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) follows by expanding \(1/(e^x-1)\) as a sum of exponentials and integrating term by term. This yields Stefan–Boltzmann exitance \(M=\sigma T^4\) and identifies the constant \(\sigma=2\pi^5k_B^4/(15h^3c^2)\).

A black patch of 1.0 square millimetre at 400 K emits about 1.45 mW into its entire outward hemisphere over all wavelengths. The same patch at 295 K emits about 0.429 mW. Replacing one by the other changes all-wavelength hemispherical power by about 1.02 mW. This is not the power a small infrared receiver sees through a narrow filter. The integrated emitter number is valuable for energy conservation, but by itself contains no receiver area, direction, passband or optical transmission.

The surface spectrum of a body in thermal equilibrium has directional spectral emissivity equal to its absorptivity in the corresponding reciprocal optical modes under the usual reciprocal-material conditions. An opaque diffuse grey surface with emissivity \(\epsilon\) in an isotropically illuminated background can be modelled by \(\epsilon B_\lambda(T)+(1-\epsilon)B_\lambda(T_{\rm bg})\). It emits less than a black surface but also reflects background. A shiny object viewed in a warm room may consequently display a radiance temperature nearer the reflected scene than its own physical temperature. Specular surfaces require explicit directional knowledge of that scene.

Derive the geometric acceptance without counting the angle twice

An area element \(dA\) normal to the collection axis emits into \(d\Omega\) at angle \(\theta\) a spectral power \(B_\lambda\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\). For a uniformly filled cone of half-angle \(\alpha\) and a uniform patch of area \(A\), integrating gives \(A\pi\sin^2\alpha\). This is its geometrical throughput in air when there is no vignetting. Optical étendue more generally uses \(n^2dA\cos\theta\,d\Omega\). A lossless passive optical relay in geometrical optics preserves this phase-space measure; correspondingly \(L/n^2\) is conserved along a ray in transparent media under the usual stationary conditions.

A direct lens example makes the restriction tangible. An image relay enlarging a uniformly bright patch by a factor of two in each transverse direction forms an image four times the area. In a lossless paraxial design filling the original pupil, each image point then occupies one quarter the solid angle. A detector covering the whole enlarged image and accepting that cone can receive the original power. Placing a fixed small sensor at the image plane intercepts less area instead. Higher magnification has not created additional radiance, even if it makes a small detail easier to resolve.

For the same 1.0 square millimetre patch seen within ten degrees, the projected acceptance is \(9.47\times10^{-8}\) square metre steradian. A wavelength-independent combined transmission and detector absorption of 0.65 leaves effective acceptance \(6.16\times10^{-8}\) square metre steradian. Integrating the Planck curve from 8 to 12 micrometres gives source and background band radiances 133.74 and 35.43 W per square metre per steradian. Their difference yields only 6.05 microwatts in the receiver. Keeping a single midpoint radiance times four micrometres happens to be fairly close here, but there is no fixed universal midpoint correction across temperatures or filters.

Open the live experiment

Set source and ambient temperatures equal. The emitted and ambient curves remain finite while the differenced instrument reading vanishes. Increase the cone half-angle from ten to twenty degrees and predict the scale factor \(\sin^2 20^\circ/\sin^2 10^\circ=3.88\). The spectral shape should stay fixed if transmission is separable from angle. If real data shift spectral shape with aperture, inspect filter angle dependence, source nonuniformity and stray light. The integral in the readout is computed on the specified 8–12 micrometre band even though the graph shows wavelengths beyond it.

Convert received power to calibrated voltage or counts

A thermal detector may respond to absorbed power through a temperature rise and an electrical responsivity. A photon-counting detector measures absorbed photons, so its count rate contains a factor \(\lambda/(hc)\) multiplying spectral power before integration. Two receivers with identical geometric acceptance and nominal quantum efficiency can respond differently to a changing source temperature if one output is proportional to energy and the other to photon number. Calibrate the actual electrical transfer and make the time interval explicit for accumulated counts.

For an ideal linear power detector let \(V=V_0+gP\), with offset \(V_0\) and gain \(g\). Two calibrated sources spanning the use range determine both. A black reference port at the same ambient temperature can also reject a common background in a chopped measurement. A filter, window or warm lens that absorbs thermal radiation also emits it. If these optics remain unchanged and stable, part of their own emission cancels in a reference difference. A reference traversing a different path generally leaves a residual input that is not captured by an electronic dark measurement.

Suppose effective source-minus-ambient powers of 2.00 and 8.00 microwatts yield 0.120 and 0.420 V. The fitted slope is 0.0500 V per microwatt with an offset of 0.020 V. A reading of 0.320 V implies 6.00 microwatts after offset correction. Substituting the same reading directly into a single-point gain with zero assumed offset would bias the inferred temperature. Rechecking a third reference near six microwatts tests linearity, while repeated zero-input readings at realistic instrument temperature test offset drift.

Invert the radiance and propagate uncertainties

A narrowband blackbody radiance measurement can be inverted analytically by solving Planck's expression for \(T\). In a broad passband use numerical quadrature across the calibrated spectral response and solve a scalar monotone equation when emissivity and background are known. The derivative at a single wavelength is particularly informative for estimating precision. With \(x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)\),

\[\frac{\partial\log B_\lambda}{\partial\log T}=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}},\]
\[T=\frac{hc/(\lambda k_B)}{\log[1+2hc^2/(\lambda^5B_\lambda)]}.\]

At 10 micrometres and 400 K the logarithmic sensitivity is 3.70. A one-percent relative radiance uncertainty therefore gives about 1.08 K temperature uncertainty if all other quantities are exact. An unknown five-percent emissivity error can dominate that estimate. A two-colour temperature ratio may reduce uncertainty from an emissivity that is constant across the two narrow bands, but cannot cancel an arbitrary spectral emissivity or a large reflected ambient component.

As an example consider a grey object at 400 K and ambient 295 K with emissivity 0.70. At ten micrometres its outgoing radiance is \(0.70(33.56)+0.30(9.14)=26.24\) in the earlier spectral units. Inverting as a perfect blackbody gives a brightness temperature of about 375 K. The difference is a physical radiance–temperature degeneracy. Repeat at known ambient temperatures while keeping the object thermally stabilised to constrain the reflected fraction. If heating the background changes the actual object temperature, a second independent temperature sensor or a transient thermal model is necessary.

When detector heat capacity sets the measured signal

Consider a thermal receiver of heat capacity \(C_d\) coupled to its surroundings with thermal conductance \(G_d\). For small excursions its temperature obeys \(C_d\dot\theta+G_d\theta=P_{\rm abs}(t)\), where \(\theta\) is the excess above its operating point. A voltage thermometer with responsivity \(r_T\) converts this into output. Its frequency-dependent power responsivity is \(r_T/(G_d-i\omega C_d)\). The geometric and spectral calculations determine absorbed optical power, while this thermal equation determines how much of its modulation appears at the electrical output.

Take \(C_d=2.0\) microjoules per kelvin and \(G_d=0.10\) milliwatt per kelvin. The thermal time constant is 20 ms and ordinary corner frequency is 7.96 Hz. A sinusoidal absorbed-power amplitude of 6.0 microwatts would give a static temperature rise of 0.060 K. At 20 Hz the amplitude is reduced by \(1/\sqrt{1+(2\pi f\tau)^2}=0.370\), leaving about 22.2 mK with a phase lag magnitude near 68 degrees. A receiver calibrated with a steady optical power is not automatically calibrated for this chopped experiment.

A square-wave chopper also changes the relation between time-domain peak differences and a lock-in's fundamental reading. For equal hot and reference dwell times, a square wave switching between powers \(P_1,P_0\) has fundamental sinusoidal peak amplitude \(2(P_1-P_0)/\pi\), with different factors for rms and peak reports. The thermal transfer attenuates this component at the chopper frequency and the odd harmonics at their respective frequencies. Substituting the full hot-minus-reference power directly into the first-harmonic response would overestimate the expected signal.

Measure a frequency sweep on a stable reference and test whether amplitude and phase share a single time constant. A geometrical error rescales the amplitude but ordinarily leaves this thermal phase unchanged. A second thermal mass or an electronic filter can introduce another pole. Lowering chop frequency increases thermal signal until the static limit but can expose low-frequency environmental drift. Choose the frequency by comparing the calibrated response with the measured noise spectrum.

Finally, a reference and an unknown source must illuminate the same active area if responsivity varies across the receiver. Imaging one source as a small spot and the other as a filled patch can create a calibration bias even when total spectral powers agree. Translating a reference spot across the detector or imaging the same aperture successively at both temperatures tests that spatial uniformity assumption.

If the sensor is used on a source much smaller than the calibrated reference aperture, record an image or a field-stop scan to determine its filling fraction. Otherwise even exact knowledge of filter transmission and thermodynamic temperature leaves an unknown geometrical scale factor. Aperture filling is part of the measurement equation, not an electronic gain setting.

Steady conduction, thermal resistance and insulation

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Derive the heat leak through a cylindrical wall and identify when increasing insulation thickness can increase heat transfer.

From first principles

Derive series thermal resistance from conservation of heat rate

Consider conduction along a prescribed path through a stationary material, with local area \(A(x)\) normal to the heat flow and conductivity \(k(x)>0\). Assume a one-dimensional steady state, no side losses and no heat generation. A balance on an elementary slice gives a single heat rate \(\dot Q\) at every position. Fourier's measured constitutive relation links that heat rate to the temperature gradient:

\[\frac{d\dot Q}{dx}=0,\]
\[\dot Q=-k(x)A(x)\frac{dT}{dx}.\]

Separate variables and integrate from the hot to the cold surface. An exterior fluid film described by \(\dot Q=h A_s(T_s-T_\infty)\) introduces an additional drop proportional to the same heat rate:

\[T_{\rm hot}-T_\infty=\dot Q\left[\int_{\rm solid}\frac{dx}{k(x)A(x)}+\frac{1}{h A_s}\right].\]

The bracket is a thermal resistance in K/W. For a plane homogeneous layer it gives \(L/(kA)\); for radial conduction in a cylinder the local area \(2\pi rL\) varies, giving a logarithm. Adding resistances is justified by a common heat rate. Heat-generating layers or side branches require a separate balance before using a series formula.

Derivation and applications

How the changing area sets resistance in a spherical shell

Heat passing through the insulation of a spherical vessel spreads over a larger surface as it travels outward. Treat an inner surface at radius \(r_i\) as isothermal, a concentric outer radius \(r_o\), constant thermal conductivity \(k\), and uniform outside convection coefficient \(h\). Assume steady radial conduction with no internal heat generation, perfect interfaces and a uniform ambient temperature \(T_\infty\). With no accumulation between concentric spheres, the heat rate is constant even though local heat flux decreases outward.

Fourier’s empirical law specifies flux from temperature gradient. Multiply it by the local area and rearrange so that each radial slice contributes its own temperature drop:

\[\dot Q=-4\pi r^2 k\,dT/dr,\]
\[T_i-T_o=\frac{\dot Q}{4\pi k}\int_{r_i}^{r_o}\frac{dr}{r^2}.\]

The outer convective drop equals the same heat rate divided by the outer film conductance. Adding both drops gives a series relation:

\[\frac{T_i-T_\infty}{\dot Q}=R=\frac{1}{4\pi k}\!\left(\frac1{r_i}-\frac1{r_o}\right)+\frac1{4\pi h r_o^2}.\]

Increasing \(r_o\) adds conduction resistance but also raises film area. The sign of the derivative decides which effect dominates:

\[\frac{dR}{dr_o}=\frac{1}{4\pi k r_o^2}-\frac{2}{4\pi h r_o^3},\]
\[r_{\rm critical}=2k/h .\]

For an initially smaller radius, adding a thin layer can reduce total resistance; after this minimum the resistance grows. The result presumes fixed \(h\) and surface temperature \(T_i\). If changes in geometry substantially change natural convection or radiation, the assumed film law must be revisited.

Worked example

Evaluate an insulated sensor bead

Take \(r_i=3.00\) mm, \(r_o=6.00\) mm, \(k=0.0400\) W/(m K) and \(h=10.0\) W/(m² K). The shell contributes \(331.6\) K/W and the film \(221.0\) K/W. A \(40.0\) K inner-to-ambient difference gives \(0.0724\) W. The uncoated bead had outer resistance \(884\) K/W and lost only \(0.0452\) W. At \(6\) mm the coating is still below the \(8.00\) mm critical radius.

Second application

Determine whether a change in airflow reverses the trend

With forced airflow raising \(h\) to \(40.0\) W/(m² K), the critical radius falls to \(2.00\) mm, inside the bare bead. The bare and coated resistances become \(221\) and \(331.6+55.3=386.9\) K/W. The coating now reduces heat loss from \(0.181\) to \(0.103\) W. The same layer can change the sign of its effect because the surrounding film is part of the experiment.

Turn heat flux into a temperature field

Energy moves through a stationary homogeneous solid down its temperature gradient. For isotropic conduction Fourier’s law is \(\mathbf q=-k\nabla T\), where \(\mathbf q\) is heat flux in W/m² and \(k\) is thermal conductivity. A steady one-dimensional wall with no internal heat generation passes the same heat rate at every cut. Heat flux is uniform only when the area of the cuts is uniform. That distinction matters for pipes and spherical shells.

Let an inner cylindrical surface at radius \(r_i\) be warmer than the outer one at \(r_o\). Assume a long concentric wall of length \(L\), constant \(k\), negligible end loss and axisymmetry. Fourier’s law gives \(\dot Q=-2\pi rLk\,dT/dr\), with outward rate taken positive. The area grows with radius, so the magnitude of \(dT/dr\) decreases outward even though the heat rate is unchanged.

Derive and combine thermal resistances

Separate and integrate over the cylindrical wall. This yields \(T_i-T_o=\dot Q\ln(r_o/r_i)/(2\pi k L)\). The cylindrical conduction resistance is the factor multiplying \(\dot Q\). At an outer convection boundary \(\dot Q=h\,2\pi r_oL(T_o-T_\infty)\), giving another resistance between the surface and the bulk surroundings. For a prescribed inner surface temperature,

\[\dot Q=\frac{T_i-T_\infty}{\ln(r_o/r_i)/(2\pi kL)+1/(2\pi r_oLh)}.\]

For multiple concentric layers, add the conduction resistance of each. The drops across series resistances sum because the same rate passes through them all. Poor contact between layers can add another temperature jump. A parallel heat leak bypassing the insulation carries part of the total heat under a common temperature difference, so its conductance adds instead.

Estimate the heat leak from a warm pipe

Take \(L=1.0\) m, \(r_i=0.020\) m and \(r_o=0.040\) m. The insulation has \(k=0.050\) W/(m K) and the surroundings have \(h=10\) W/(m² K), \(T_\infty=293\) K. The inner insulation surface is held at 353 K. The conduction resistance is \(\ln2/[2\pi(0.050)(1)]=2.206\) K/W, and the outer film resistance is \(1/[10(2\pi)(0.040)(1)]=0.398\) K/W. Total heat loss is \(60/(2.206+0.398)=23.0\) W per metre of pipe.

The outer surface is \(293+(23.0)(0.398)=302.2\) K. The rest of the 60 K difference is within the insulation. The heat flux at the outer surface is half that at the inner surface, because the heat-transfer area is twice as large. Assigning the outer circumference to the entire wall in a flat-wall formula would miss this variation. When thickness is very small relative to radius, expanding the logarithm recovers the plane-wall approximation.

Explain the critical radius

Increasing \(r_o\) makes the conduction resistance grow but makes the convection resistance shrink. At fixed \(T_i,T_\infty,h,k\), differentiating the total resistance gives \(dR/dr_o=1/(2\pi Lk r_o)-1/(2\pi Lh r_o^2)\). Its minimum occurs at \(r_c=k/h\); that radius maximises heat loss. Here \(r_c=0.0050\) m, already below the bare pipe radius, so adding this insulation reduces loss from the outset. A much thinner wire could initially lose more heat because its exposed area increases faster than the wall resistance. This conclusion assumes the same external coefficient as the diameter changes. If natural convection or radiation changes appreciably, the boundary model and its temperature dependence must be reconsidered.

Recover an unknown contact resistance

A 0.010 m² test sandwich passes 8 W at steady state between measured outer face temperatures of 50 and 40 °C. Its measured total resistance is \(10/8=1.25\,\mathrm{K/W}\). The two known solid layers together account for 0.75 K/W, leaving 0.50 K/W attributable to the interface if other parasitic paths are small. The area-normalised contact resistance is \(R''=A R=0.0050\,\mathrm{m^2K/W}\), corresponding to conductance \(1/R''=200\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\). A pair of thermocouples on opposite sides of the interface should then show a 4 K temperature difference. That independent measurement helps distinguish contact resistance from an unaccounted external heat loss.

Derive the temperature field along a cooling fin

A fin adds area for convection but must first conduct heat from its base to that area. Treat a straight fin with constant cross-sectional area \(A_c\), exposed perimeter \(P\), conductivity \(k\) and uniform film coefficient \(h\). Its base is held at \(T_b\) and surrounding air at \(T_\infty\). Assume the temperature is nearly uniform across each fin section; a small transverse Biot number supports this approximation. Take the tip as adiabatic, appropriate when tip area is small or insulated. Define excess temperature \(\theta=T-T_\infty\).

A slice \(dx\) loses heat \(hP\theta\,dx\) to convection. Its decrease in axial heat rate must supply that loss. Fourier’s law \(Q_x=-kA_c\,d\theta/dx\) then yields \(\theta''-m^2\theta=0\), where \(m=\sqrt{hP/(kA_c)}\). The boundary conditions are \(\theta(0)=\theta_b,\ \theta'(L)=0\). Using a hyperbolic cosine referenced to the insulated end gives

\[\theta(x)=\theta_b\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)},\qquad Q_b=\sqrt{hPkA_c}\,\theta_b\tanh(mL).\]

If the whole fin stayed at base temperature, heat removal would be \(hPL\theta_b\). The ratio \(\eta_f=\tanh(mL)/(mL)\) is the fin efficiency under the stated tip model. A very long fin can have low efficiency although it removes more heat than a short one; efficiency measures use of installed area, while total heat removal measures cooling duty.

Decide whether doubling fin length is worthwhile

An aluminium fin has rectangular section 20 mm by 2 mm, length 50 mm and conductivity 180 W/(m K). Air at 25 °C flows over it with \(h=35\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\), and the base is at 75 °C. Thus \(A_c=4.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2},\ P=0.044\,\mathrm m,\ \theta_b=50\,\mathrm K\). The transverse length \(A_c/P=0.909\,\mathrm{mm}\) gives \(hA_c/(kP)=0.000177\), consistent with small cross-sectional gradients. The fin parameter is \(m=\sqrt{35(0.044)/[180(4\times10^{-5})]}=14.6\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\); hence \(mL=0.731\) and \(\tanh(mL)=0.624\).

The base heat rate is \(\sqrt{35(0.044)(180)(4\times10^{-5})}(50)(0.624)=3.29\,\mathrm W\). Fin efficiency is \(0.624/0.731=0.854\). Doubling length makes \(mL=1.46\) and raises heat removal to about \(5.27\tanh(1.46)=4.73\,\mathrm W\). Twice the material therefore gives only 44% more cooling. The original covered base area by itself would convect \(35(4\times10^{-5})(50)=0.070\,\mathrm W\), so the fin still offers a large improvement. Adding several fins can change the film coefficient through crowding, and finite contact resistance can lower the effective base temperature. Those effects must be checked before multiplying a single-fin heat rate by the number installed.

A surface thermometer offers a second way to test the heat-transfer model. The insulated-tip solution gives \(T(L)=25+50/\cosh(0.731)=64.1^\circ\mathrm C\). If a thin thermocouple instead measures nearly 75 °C all along a fin while the base still sheds only the predicted few watts, the effective film coefficient may be smaller than assumed. A much colder measured tip could indicate strong local tip convection or poor base contact. Interpret a profile together with the base heat rate: several combinations of conductance, base temperature and film conditions can reproduce one measured temperature, but they need not reproduce both the profile and the total cooling duty.

The root of the fin offers a particularly direct simultaneous measurement of axial temperature gradient and supplied heat rate.

Infer wall cooling from a measured skin-friction force

The film coefficient in a thermal-resistance calculation carries information about transport within the flow. For a smooth isothermal plate, take a steady parallel free stream of speed \(U\) and temperature \(T_e\). Define \(\theta=(T-T_w)/(T_e-T_w)\). Both \(u/U\) and \(\theta\) rise from zero at the wall to one in the outer stream. If momentum and heat diffuse at equal rates, their transport equations and boundary conditions match for a constant-property, zero-pressure-gradient boundary layer starting at the same leading edge. Under this approximation their profiles and their normalized wall gradients coincide. In turbulent flow the analogy additionally relies on similar turbulent diffusivities.

For a wall hotter than the stream the heat flux into the fluid and the magnitude of skin friction are \(q''=k(T_w-T_e)\partial_y\theta|_w\) and \(\tau_w=\mu U\partial_y(u/U)|_w\). Divide the equations. At \(Pr=\mu c_p/k=1\), the unknown gradient cancels. With \(q''=h(T_w-T_e)\) the Reynolds analogy becomes

\[h\simeq\frac{c_p\tau_w}{U},\qquad St=\frac{h}{\rho Uc_p}\simeq \frac{C_f}{2},\qquad C_f=\frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U^2}.\]

A small heat-transfer tunnel has effective \(Pr\) close to one. At \(U=10.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the stream has \(\rho=1.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and \(c_p=1000\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}\). A force balance isolates the skin friction on one flush plate face of area \(0.50\,\mathrm{m^2}\), and measures \(0.180\,\mathrm N\). The mean shear is \(0.360\,\mathrm{Pa}\), hence \(\bar C_f=0.00600\) and \(\bar h\simeq36.0\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}\). A uniform wall 20 K above the stream loses approximately \(36(0.50)(20)=360\,\mathrm W\) by convection. Average drag produces an average coefficient because both quantities are integrated over the same face. Pressure drag from a protruding instrument, an unheated entry length or a fluid with a very different Prandtl number would invalidate that identification.

Thermal diffusion and transient surface cooling

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Use diffusion time, Biot number and penetration depth to choose between a uniform-body model and a spatial temperature field.

From first principles

Derive thermal diffusion from stored energy and conductive flux

Let a stationary homogeneous solid have density \(\rho\), specific heat \(c\) and thermal conductivity \(k\). Assume no heat generation and consider one-dimensional temperature variation. A slice of area \(A\) and thickness \(dx\) stores energy at rate \(\rho c A\,dx\,\partial_tT\). The difference between the conductive flux entering and leaving gives

\[\rho c A\,dx\,\partial_tT=A[q''(x)-q''(x+dx)]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho c\,\partial_tT=-\partial_xq'' .\]

Fourier's law \(q''=-k\partial_xT\) closes this balance. For constant properties the diffusion equation and its characteristic time over distance \(L\) follow:

\[\partial_tT=\alpha\,\partial_{xx}T,\]
\[\alpha=\frac{k}{\rho c},\]
\[t_{\rm diff}\sim L^2/\alpha .\]

Initial temperatures and boundary heat-flux or temperature conditions are needed to select a specific solution. If conduction inside the solid is sufficiently rapid relative to transfer at its surface, one can integrate the balance using an almost uniform interior temperature. Comparing the internal and external resistance scales yields the Biot number \(hL_c/k\) used for that simplification.

Derivation and applications

Predict how a thermal probe follows a changing fluid temperature

A sensor calibrated by an abrupt temperature step can lag behind a fluid that is itself heating steadily. Take a body of mass \(m\), heat capacity \(c\), exposed area \(A\) and film coefficient \(h\) with negligible internal temperature gradients. This lumped description requires internal conduction to be fast compared with surface heat transfer. Treat properties and \(h\) as constant and neglect self-heating, radiation and lead conduction. Denote the body temperature by \(T\), fluid temperature by \(T_f\), and set \(\tau=mc/(hA)\).

An instantaneous energy balance gives a first-order differential equation. For a linearly increasing fluid temperature \(T_f=T_0+\beta t\), define the lag \(D=T_f-T\):

\[mc\,\dot T=hA(T_f-T),\]
\[\dot D + D/\tau=\beta .\]

Suppose the probe and fluid start in equilibrium at \(T_0\). Multiply the lag equation by \(e^{t/\tau}\) and integrate from zero. The integrating factor converts the left side to a single derivative:

\[e^{t/\tau}D(t)=\beta\int_0^t e^{s/\tau}\,ds=\beta\tau(e^{t/\tau}-1).\]

Subtract the resulting lag from the prescribed fluid history to obtain the reading:

\[T(t)=T_0+\beta[t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})] .\]

For \(t\ll\tau\) the rise begins quadratically, since the initial film temperature difference was zero. For \(t\gg\tau\) the probe warms at the same rate as the fluid but reads low by \(\beta\tau\). Cooling ramps reverse the sign. A measured constant lag is therefore evidence for this model only after the startup transient has decayed.

Worked example

Evaluate the reading during a ramp calibration

A probe has \(mc=0.800\) J/K and \(hA=0.100\) W/K, hence \(\tau=8.00\) s. Start at \(20.0^\circ\)C and heat the fluid by \(0.500\) K/s. At \(t=16.0\) s the fluid is at \(28.0^\circ\)C and the lag is \(4.00(1-e^{-2})=3.46\) K, so the probe reads \(24.5^\circ\)C. Simply subtracting the eventual \(4.00\) K would overcorrect during startup.

Second application

Estimate a time constant without an abrupt temperature step

During a long calibration ramp of \(0.200\) K/s, a second probe remains \(1.20\) K below an independent fluid reference. Its inferred time constant is \(\tau=1.20/0.200=6.00\) s. If the fluid is then held fixed, the remaining lag decays exponentially. Waiting \(6.00\ln(1.20/0.100)=14.9\) s brings the error below \(0.100\) K. The hold and the ramp test two different responses using the same fitted parameter.

Allow energy to accumulate in the material

A surface thermocouple responds rapidly to a cold wash, while the interior of a thick part can remain hot. To predict the time needed to cool the centre, steady thermal resistance alone is insufficient. Enclose a differential slice of material. Incoming conductive heat minus outgoing heat plus local generation equals the rate of change of its stored energy. Substituting Fourier’s law in a homogeneous stationary solid gives

\[\rho c_p\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=k\nabla^2T+\dot q_v,\qquad \alpha=\frac{k}{\rho c_p}.\]

Thermal diffusivity \(\alpha\) in m²/s compares a material’s ability to spread heat with its ability to store it. A large conductivity does not alone guarantee fast adjustment if volumetric heat capacity is also large. Without heat generation, a length \(L\) gives a characteristic diffusion time \(t_d=L^2/\alpha\). The dimensionless Fourier number \(Fo=\alpha t/L^2\) measures how long diffusion has acted relative to that scale.

Decide whether the interior stays nearly uniform

At a convecting boundary \(-k\partial T/\partial n=h(T_s-T_\infty)\). Comparing the resistance of the interior with a fluid film leads to \(Bi=hL_c/k\), often using \(L_c=V/A_s\) for a lumped model. If this ratio is small, commonly below about 0.1 as a first engineering check, the internal temperature difference is small compared with that across the film. Integrating energy over a uniform body then gives \(\rho c_p V\,dT/dt=-hA_s(T-T_\infty)\) and \((T-T_\infty)/(T_0-T_\infty)=e^{-t/\tau}\), with \(\tau=\rho c_pV/(hA_s)\).

The Biot and Fourier numbers answer different questions. A small Fourier number says a disturbance has penetrated only a short distance compared with \(L\). A small Biot number says the boundary transfers heat slowly enough for internal gradients to remain small. Choose the length for each comparison explicitly, especially for a slab cooled on one side rather than both.

Solve a sudden prescribed-temperature change

Consider initially uniform material occupying \(x>0\) whose surface is suddenly held at \(T_s\), with its far interior still at \(T_i\). Neglect internal generation and use constant properties. The initial and boundary conditions have no geometric length scale. Try the similarity coordinate \(\eta=x/(2\sqrt{\alpha t})\) and set \(\Theta=(T-T_s)/(T_i-T_s)\). Differentiating and substituting into the diffusion equation yields \(\Theta''+2\eta\Theta'=0\), with derivatives in \(\eta\). Integrating once gives \(\Theta'=C e^{-\eta^2}\). The limits \(\Theta(0)=0,\ \Theta(\infty)=1\) give \(\Theta=\operatorname{erf}(\eta)\).

At \(x=0\) the imposed surface temperature holds exactly. At a fixed depth and very early time \(\eta\) is large, so the temperature tends to its initial value. The distance of any fixed fractional temperature change grows as \(\sqrt{t}\). Quadrupling exposure time doubles that distance, a scaling quite different from a body travelling at a constant speed.

Predict depth and validate the time window

A thick solid initially at 373 K is held at 293 K at its surface. With \(\alpha=1.0\times10^{-5}\) m²/s and \(t=10\) s, \(2\sqrt{\alpha t}=0.020\) m. At depth 10 mm, \(\eta=0.50\) and \(\operatorname{erf}(0.50)=0.5205\), giving \(T=293+80(0.5205)=334.6\) K. At 20 mm, \(\operatorname{erf}(1)=0.8427\) gives 360.4 K. The surface has cooled by 80 K while the deeper sensor has cooled by only 12.6 K.

At 40 s the 20 mm sensor reaches the same fraction previously measured at 10 mm. This prediction assumes the remote boundary has not begun to influence the measurement and that the surface temperature is actually prescribed. A finite slab or a convective wash may require a finite-domain diffusion solution instead. Record surface and interior histories to distinguish uncertain boundary conditions from uncertain diffusivity.

Interpret a penetration depth

If two embedded sensors are at 2 and 6 mm in the same semi-infinite specimen, the deeper sensor reaches the same normalised temperature at nine times the shallow sensor’s time because \(x/\sqrt{\alpha t}\) must match. An observed factor much smaller than nine can indicate a heat leak along a probe or a far boundary that has begun to influence the temperature field.

Resolve a slab with a finite surface film

A prescribed surface temperature and a uniformly cooling lump are two useful limits. A finite slab exchanging heat through a finite film generally satisfies neither. Let \(L\) be half-thickness, \(x=0\) the midplane, and \(T_i\) the initial uniform temperature. Both sides suddenly see fluid at \(T_\infty\) with constant \(h\). Using \(X=x/L,\ Fo=\alpha t/L^2\) and \(\Theta=(T-T_\infty)/(T_i-T_\infty)\), the diffusion equation becomes \(\Theta_{Fo}=\Theta_{XX}\), with symmetry \(\Theta_X(0)=0\) and film condition \(-\Theta_X(1)=Bi\,\Theta(1)\), where \(Bi=hL/k\).

Seek a separated mode \(\Theta=\cos(\lambda X)e^{-\lambda^2Fo}\). Its derivative vanishes at the midplane. Imposing the convective surface condition gives \(\lambda\tan\lambda=Bi\). The uniform initial condition fixes modal amplitudes by cosine orthogonality on \(0<X<1\):

\[\Theta=\sum_n A_n\cos(\lambda_n X)e^{-\lambda_n^2Fo},\qquad A_n=\frac{4\sin\lambda_n}{2\lambda_n+\sin(2\lambda_n)}.\]

For \(Bi\ll1\), the smallest root has \(\lambda_1^2\simeq Bi\), its shape approaches a constant, and \(A_1\simeq1\). The decay becomes \(e^{-Bi\,Fo}\), exactly the lumped time scale because \(Bi\,Fo=h t/(\rho cL)\). For a large film coefficient the first root tends towards \(\pi/2\), corresponding to a prescribed surface temperature. The same boundary-value model thus recovers both limiting behaviours.

Predict when the core and surface meet different targets

A 40 mm thick flat slab with \(k=2.0\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\) and \(\alpha=1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\) is cooled on both sides by \(h=50\,\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}\). It starts at 100 °C in fluid at 20 °C. Here \(L=0.020\) m and \(Bi=0.50\). The first root of \(\lambda\tan\lambda=0.5\) is 0.6533 and \(A_1=1.070\). After 600 s, \(Fo=1.50\). The first term gives centre excess ratio \(1.070\exp[-(0.6533)^2(1.50)]=0.564\), hence \(T_c=65.1^\circ\mathrm C\). At the surface multiply by \(\cos(0.6533)=0.794\), giving about 55.9 °C. Higher roots are strongly attenuated at this Fourier number.

A process requiring the centre to fall below 50 °C needs centre ratio \((50-20)/(100-20)=0.375\). Solve \(Fo=\ln(1.070/0.375)/0.6533^2=2.46\), or approximately 984 s. By contrast a lumped estimate gives \(Fo=-\ln(0.375)/0.5=1.96\), about 785 s. It underestimates the core cooling time by over three minutes because the film and internal gradients both matter. An early-time surface prediction requires more modes than this late-time centre criterion. In particular the first term alone gives a centre ratio above unity at time zero, signalling that the remaining terms are necessary to reconstruct the original uniform state.

If product quality depends on average energy content rather than the hottest spot, average the first-mode shape over half the thickness. Its factor \(\int_0^1\cos(\lambda_1 X)dX=\sin\lambda_1/\lambda_1=0.930\) gives mean excess ratio \(0.564(0.930)=0.525\) after 600 s. The predicted bulk temperature is therefore about 62.0 °C, between centre and surface as expected. A calorimetric measurement made after removing and mixing the material is closer to the mean than to either point thermometer. A decision to stop cooling based on such a reading can therefore satisfy an average enthalpy requirement while leaving the centre several kelvins above a local temperature limit.

The surface flux can be checked against the rate of thermal energy loss. At 600 seconds each face transfers \(50(55.9-20)=1795\,\mathrm{W/m^2}\) to the air. Per square metre of plan area the slab therefore loses approximately 3590 W. The volumetric heat capacity obtained independently from \(k/\alpha\) is \(2.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{J/(m^3K)}\). Multiplying by total 0.040 m thickness gives an areal capacity of 80000 J/(m² K), and a predicted instantaneous mean cooling rate \(3590/80000=0.0449\,\mathrm{K/s}\). Differentiating the one-term mean temperature gives the same decay rate to within rounding.

This independent balance is especially useful if measurements suggest two different film coefficients on opposite sides. An unequal pair of measured fluxes cannot be fitted by a strictly symmetric half-slab model even if it reproduces one centre temperature. The full-thickness conduction problem then requires both surface boundary conditions; simply replacing them by their arithmetic mean may match total heat over one interval while shifting the hottest location away from the midplane.

Heat-exchanger effectiveness and finite capacity rates

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

Derive the counterflow effectiveness relation and determine outlet temperatures when neither stream temperature is fixed.

From first principles

Derive exchanger effectiveness from the limiting heat balance

Consider a steady two-stream heat exchanger with no external heat gain, constant specific heats and no phase change. Each stream has a capacity rate \(C=\dot m c_p\), the power required for one kelvin of temperature change. Applying the first law independently to each stream gives a common transferred heat rate

\[\dot Q=C_h(T_{h,i}-T_{h,o})=C_c(T_{c,o}-T_{c,i}).\]

In passive exchange neither exit can cross the opposite stream’s inlet temperature. Therefore the maximum available temperature change on either stream is bounded by the same inlet difference \(T_{h,i}-T_{c,i}\). The smaller capacity rate limits the largest conceivable common duty. Define effectiveness from this physically accessible upper scale:

\[\dot Q_{\max}=C_{\min}(T_{h,i}-T_{c,i}),\]
\[\varepsilon=\dot Q/\dot Q_{\max},\]
\[\mathrm{NTU}=UA/C_{\min}.\]

The bound need not be reached by a finite exchanger or by every flow arrangement. Local conductance \(U\,dA\) and the changing local temperature difference determine the actual effectiveness. Knowing the two capacity rates and \(\varepsilon\) suffices to reconstruct both outlet temperatures using the separate energy balances.

Derivation and applications

Where the logarithmic mean temperature difference comes from

A local heat-transfer coefficient multiplies the temperature difference at that location. Integrating one exchanger therefore requires the changing driving difference across its whole area. Take a steady counterflow exchanger with no phase change, constant positive capacity rates \(C_h=\dot m_h c_h\), \(C_c=\dot m_c c_c\), constant overall coefficient \(U\) and no external heat loss. Measure area coordinate \(A'\) along the hot flow. Let \(d\dot Q\) be the positive heat transfer from hot to cold over the increment. Both fluid temperatures decrease along this chosen coordinate because the cold stream flows the other way.

\[d\dot Q=U(T_h-T_c)\,dA',\]
\[dT_h=-d\dot Q/C_h,\]
\[dT_c=-d\dot Q/C_c .\]

Thus \(\Delta=T_h-T_c\) changes linearly with cumulative heat transfer. Write its two physical terminal values as \(\Delta_1=T_{h,i}-T_{c,o}\) and \(\Delta_2=T_{h,o}-T_{c,i}\):

\[d\Delta=(1/C_c-1/C_h)d\dot Q,\]
\[\Delta_2-\Delta_1=(1/C_c-1/C_h)\dot Q .\]

To recover total area, integrate \(dA'=d\dot Q/(U\Delta)\) using the preceding linear relation. Eliminating the reciprocal capacity difference leaves

\[\dot Q=UA\,\frac{\Delta_1-\Delta_2}{\ln(\Delta_1/\Delta_2)} .\]

For equal terminal differences the apparent zero divided by zero has limit \(\Delta_1\). When one positive approach tends toward zero at a fixed heat duty, the necessary area diverges. Terminal differences must pair the temperatures at the same physical end, not two inlet temperatures chosen from opposite ends.

Worked example

Size an exchanger from measured terminal temperatures

A hot stream cools from \(90\) to \(55^\circ\)C while the counterflow cold stream warms from \(20\) to \(60^\circ\)C. The terminal differences are \(30\) and \(35\) K, so their logarithmic mean is \(5/\ln(35/30)=32.4\) K. For a duty of \(70.0\) kW and \(U=500\) W/(m² K), the required area is \(4.32\) m². The capacities \(2.00\) and \(1.75\) kW/K independently deliver the same \(70.0\) kW.

Second application

Compare equal duty at a tighter terminal approach

A redesign has measured terminal differences of \(5.00\) and \(35.0\) K at the same duty and coefficient. The logarithmic mean drops to \(30/\ln7=15.4\) K and the required area rises to \(9.08\) m². Using the arithmetic mean \(20.0\) K would give only \(7.00\) m². The region with low driving difference consumes disproportionately more area per kilowatt transferred.

Set the maximum possible heat rate

In a two-stream heat exchanger, the hot temperature falls as the cold temperature rises. Treat each stream as single phase with constant heat capacity rate \(C=\dot m c_p\) in watts per kelvin. Neglect heat leakage, axial conduction and changes in kinetic energy. A steady overall balance gives \(Q=C_h(T_{hi}-T_{ho})=C_c(T_{co}-T_{ci})\). Performing this balance alone leaves both outlet temperatures and the transferred heat rate unknown.

The stream with smaller capacity rate would undergo the larger temperature change for a given heat rate. Even in an ideal counterflow exchanger it cannot pass the inlet temperature of the other stream at its nearest end. Thus \(Q_{\max}=C_{\min}(T_{hi}-T_{ci})\). Effectiveness is \(\varepsilon=Q/Q_{\max}\), which immediately gives both outlet temperatures once the heat transfer law fixes \(\varepsilon\).

Apply a balance to an area element

Let \(A\) increase along the hot stream and write the local temperature difference \(\Delta=T_h-T_c\). An element transfers \(dQ=U\Delta\,dA\). In counterflow both temperature profiles decrease when followed in the hot-stream direction, giving \(dT_h=-dQ/C_h\), \(dT_c=-dQ/C_c\). Hence \(d\ln\Delta/dA=U(1/C_c-1/C_h)\). Integrate over area and express each end difference in terms of inlet temperatures and total heat rate using the stream balances.

\[NTU=UA/C_{\min},\quad C_r=C_{\min}/C_{\max},\quad \varepsilon=\frac{1-\exp[-NTU(1-C_r)]}{1-C_r\exp[-NTU(1-C_r)]}.\]

The sign in the differential balance follows from the chosen spatial direction. In parallel flow the cold stream warms along that coordinate and the local temperature difference decays more rapidly. For equal capacity rates the counterflow formula has a removable zero over zero. Its limit is \(NTU/(1+NTU)\), which can also be obtained from the constant temperature difference in that special case.

Predict both outlet temperatures

A counterflow cooler receives a hot stream at 90 °C with \(C_h=2.0\,\mathrm{kW/K}\) and a cold stream at 20 °C with \(C_c=1.0\,\mathrm{kW/K}\). Its conductance \(UA\) is 1.50 kW/K. Thus \(C_r=0.5\), \(NTU=1.5\), and the exponential is \(e^{-0.75}=0.4724\). Effectiveness is 0.6908. Consequently \(Q=0.6908(1.0)(90-20)=48.4\,\mathrm{kW}\), \(T_{ho}=90-48.4/2=65.8^\circ\mathrm C\) and \(T_{co}=20+48.4=68.4^\circ\mathrm C\).

The cold outlet is warmer than the hot outlet. They are at opposite physical ends, so this does not imply a local reversal of heat flow. The end differences are \(90-68.4=21.6\) K and \(65.8-20=45.8\) K, both positive. Their logarithmic mean is approximately \((45.8-21.6)/\ln(45.8/21.6)=32.2\,\mathrm K\), independently recovering \(Q=UA\Delta T_{\rm lm}\simeq48.3\,\mathrm{kW}\). This local check catches errors caused by swapping the terminal temperatures of a counterflow exchanger.

Predict the counterflow experiment

A smaller stream capacity rate raises NTU when the exchanger conductance is held fixed, yet it can reduce heat transfer in watts because less thermal capacity passes each second. For inlet temperatures 350 and 290 K and \(C_r=0.60\), use the counterflow expression to predict the effectiveness at NTU 1.5 and 3.0. Then halve both capacity rates, as the model does to obtain the second NTU, and predict the ratio of heat rates. Check both outlet temperatures and the hot-stream heat rate, paying attention to which stream has the smaller capacity. Open the heat-exchanger experiment.

Identify which side controls an exchanger’s conductance

The effectiveness relation uses a single conductance \(UA\), but a designer may need to determine whether the hot film, cold film or fouling dominates it. Write the total thermal resistance in K/W as \(R_{\rm tot}=1/(h_hA_h)+R_w+R_f+1/(h_cA_c)\). Areas must be kept explicit if the two sides use different reference areas. Suppose a test can vary hot-side flow while holding cold-side flow, wall resistance and deposit condition fixed. Let a supported correlation in the tested region imply \(h_h\propto\dot m_h^{\,n}\). With \(m_r=\dot m_h/\dot m_{\rm ref}\), the relation takes the linear form

\[(UA)^{-1}=R_{\rm fixed}+R_{h,\rm ref}\,m_r^{-n}.\]

This is the basis of a Wilson-plot style separation. Each measured \(UA\) can be obtained from balanced heat duty and the correct logarithmic mean temperature difference. The intercept estimates the combined fixed resistance; the slope estimates the hot-film resistance at reference flow. One set of measurements alone does not separate cold film from wall and deposits individually. Temperature-dependent material properties and changes in the opposite stream can spoil the assumed linear relation.

Treating \(UA\) as proportional to flow whenever convection rises would ignore the series resistances left in its path. As \(m_r\) becomes large the hot-film term tends to zero, leaving an asymptote \(1/R_{\rm fixed}\) rather than unbounded conductance. Pumping power typically rises over this same change of flow.

Use a flow sweep to distinguish fouling from film resistance

A clean counterflow rig at reference hot-side mass flow has \(UA=500\,\mathrm{W/K}\), giving total resistance \(0.00200\,\mathrm{K/W}\). At twice the flow it has \(UA=600\,\mathrm{W/K}\), or \(0.001667\,\mathrm{K/W}\). Over this interval use measured exponent \(n=0.80\), so \(2^{-0.8}=0.5743\). Subtracting the two equations gives \(R_{h,\rm ref}=(0.00200-0.001667)/(1-0.5743)=0.000783\,\mathrm{K/W}\). The combined fixed resistance is \(0.001217\,\mathrm{K/W}\). Even an ideal hot film could raise conductance only to about 822 W/K under the unchanged fixed-side assumptions.

A later test at reference flow gives \(UA=400\,\mathrm{W/K}\), so total resistance has grown to 0.00250 K/W. If a second flow sweep confirms the same hot-film slope, the added fixed resistance is 0.00050 K/W. For a heat-transfer area of 2.0 m² on the chosen basis this corresponds to fouling resistance \(R_f''=0.0010\,\mathrm{m^2K/W}\). If instead the slope has changed, assigning the entire difference to deposits is unwarranted; changed flow distribution or fluid properties may contribute. Temperature measurement also matters strongly when the two terminal differences are close to sensor uncertainty. Compare the hot and cold enthalpy duties before fitting the conductance and retain the residual across all measured flow settings.

A third clean test can challenge the fitted exponent. At four times reference hot flow, the predicted resistance is \(0.001217+0.000783(4^{-0.8})=0.001475\,\mathrm{K/W}\), or \(UA\simeq678\,\mathrm{W/K}\). A systematic departure that grows at high flow could reflect changing cold-side properties, thermocouple bias or failure of the hot-film correlation. Two calibration points always fit a two-parameter straight line; the independent third point actually tests the assumed dependence.

A fin adds surface area, but heat must travel down its length before it can reach that area. Derive the temperature profile from an energy balance and quantify how much of the nominal area contributes to cooling.

Balance a short slice with distributed convection

Consider a straight prismatic fin of conductivity k, cross-section A, exposed perimeter P and length L. Hold the root at \(T_b\), let a fluid at \(T_\infty\) surround the fin and use a uniform convection coefficient h. Temperature is assumed nearly uniform across each cross-section. A small transverse Biot number \(h(A/P)/k\) supports that assumption. Ignore radiation and heat loss from the tip. Define \(\theta=T-T_\infty\). The axial heat entering a slice, less the heat leaving its far face, must equal \(hP\theta\,dx\).

\[q_x=-kA\,\theta',\]
\[-{dq_x\over dx}=hP\theta,\]
\[\theta''-m^2\theta=0,\]
\[m=\sqrt{hP/(kA)}.\]

A positive temperature excess makes the heat flow diminish downstream. For an insulated tip \(q_x(L)=0\), so a hyperbolic cosine centered at the tip satisfies the zero-slope condition. Its coefficient follows from the root temperature.

\[{\theta(x)\over\theta_b}={\cosh[m(L-x)]\over\cosh(mL)},\]
\[Q_b=kAm\theta_b\tanh(mL).\]

Efficiency follows by comparing with an isothermal fin

If every part of the lateral area stayed at \(T_b\), the heat transfer would be \(hPL\theta_b\). The actual-to-ideal ratio is therefore \(\eta_f=\tanh(mL)/(mL)\). For small mL the temperature falls little along the wall and doubling the area nearly doubles the heat flow. For large mL the root heat flow approaches \(kAm\theta_b\). Near the long-fin limit increasing conductivity or the root cross-section can buy more cooling than adding length. Efficiency alone does not measure total heat removal because it contains the nominal area in its denominator.

Two fins made from the same stock

Use a rectangular aluminium strip, 20 mm wide, 2 mm thick, \(k=200\) W/(m K), \(h=25\) W/(m² K) and \(\theta_b=50\) K. All four long faces are exposed. With \(A=4.0\times10^{-5}\) m² and \(P=0.044\) m, \(m=11.73\) m⁻¹. At 50 mm length, \(mL=0.586\), \(Q_b=2.47\) W and \(\eta_f=0.899\). The tip remains about 42.5 K above ambient.

At 150 mm, the heat rate is 4.43 W and the efficiency drops to 0.536. The material and exposed area tripled, while the heat transfer increased by about 79%. A fin root bonded through a finite contact resistance \(R_c\) would run cooler than the heated base. Replacing the temperature excess by \(T_{\rm base}-T_\infty-Q_b R_c\) in the root heat equation gives a direct way to include the bond in a thermal circuit.

Choose a short fin and inspect how far the infinite-fin approximation is from the insulated-tip temperature. Increase length until the root heat rate is almost saturated. Estimate the added cooling from another centimetre at that point.

A moving boundary in a freezing layer

Engineering thermodynamics and heat transfer

A flat cold surface grows a layer of ice from water initially at its melting temperature. Estimate when it will reach a specified thickness, then discover how a finite heat-transfer resistance changes its early growth.

Write a balance at a boundary whose position is unknown

Let the solid occupy \(0<x<s(t)\). The boundary at x=0 is held at \(T_w<T_m\), the liquid remains at \(T_m\), and the solid-liquid interface is at its equilibrium melting temperature. Treat density, latent heat of fusion \(L_f\) and solid conductivity k as constants. Ignore volume change and fluid convection. When the front moves by ds, each unit area releases latent energy \(\rho L_f\,ds\). In this one-phase problem conduction into the cold side removes that energy. Taking the magnitude of heat extracted, the interface condition reads

\[\rho L_f\,{ds\over dt}=k\,{\partial T\over\partial x}\bigg|_{s^-}.\]

The derivative is positive because temperature increases towards the liquid. Solving the full problem requires time-dependent conduction in a domain with a moving outer end. A useful approximation emerges when the sensible heat needed to cool the new solid is small beside its latent heat. Measure that ratio with the Stefan number \({\rm Ste}=c_p(T_m-T_w)/L_f\).

Derive the square-root law and check the approximation

For small Ste the temperature profile in the solid adjusts quickly compared with front growth. Approximate the gradient by \((T_m-T_w)/s\). Multiplying the interface balance by s separates the variables. Integrating from an initial layer \(s_0\) gives

\[s\,ds={k(T_m-T_w)\over\rho L_f}\,dt,\]
\[s^2=s_0^2+{2k(T_m-T_w)\over\rho L_f}t .\]

With zero initial thickness the predicted speed starts unbounded because the model inserts a finite temperature difference across zero resistance. A finite surface heat-transfer coefficient removes this singularity. If coolant at \(T_c\) reaches the wall through a film \(1/h\), the heat flux is \((T_m-T_c)/(1/h+s/k)\). For a layer growing from zero, integration gives

\[t={\rho L_f\over T_m-T_c}\left({s\over h}+{s^2\over2k}\right).\]

The linear term dominates while the film presents the larger resistance. Once the solid is thick, the quadratic term recovers diffusion-controlled growth. These two regimes can be distinguished by recording thickness at equal time intervals.

An ideal wall and a finite film

For water freezing at 0 °C take \(k=2.2\) W/(m K), \(\rho=917\) kg/m³, \(L_f=334\) kJ/kg and \(c_p=2.1\) kJ/(kg K). With a wall at −5 °C the Stefan number is 0.0314. The ideal-wall time to form 10 mm is \(917(334000)(0.01)^2/[2(2.2)(5)]=1392\) s, about 23 minutes. Reaching 20 mm requires four times that duration. The increasing conduction distance accounts for the slowing front.

With coolant at −5 °C and \(h=200\) W/(m² K), the added time for 10 mm is 3063 s. The resulting total is about 74.2 minutes. At that point the film resistance is 0.005 m² K/W and the ice contributes 0.00455 m² K/W. Both terms are of the same order. Vary h and find when a second thickness measurement would provide more information about ice conductivity than a first one.

Choose the reference frame for a flux

Let A be the evaporating species and B an insoluble carrier. A planar film of thickness L lies between the evaporating surface, at mole fraction \(y_s\), and a well-mixed reservoir at \(y_\infty<y_s\). Keep total pressure, temperature, total molar concentration \(c=p/(RT)\) and binary diffusivity D uniform. In a stationary laboratory frame the total species flux equals diffusion relative to the molar-average velocity plus transport by that velocity. If positive z points towards the reservoir, write

\[N_A=-cD{dy_A\over dz}+y_A(N_A+N_B).\]

Steady conditions in one dimension make \(N_A,N_B\) independent of z. Carrier B cannot enter the liquid and has zero net flux there. This fixes \(N_B=0\) throughout the film. B still diffuses towards the interface relative to the moving mixture. Its diffusive flux balances its outward convective flux. The constraint \(N_A+N_B=0\) would instead describe equimolar counterdiffusion between two exchanging species.

Separate variables and enforce both boundary concentrations

Collect the terms in \(N_A\), divide by \(1-y_A\) and integrate from the surface to the reservoir. The remaining integral is a logarithm of the carrier mole fraction.

\[N_A(1-y_A)=-cD{dy_A\over dz},\]
\[N_A={cD\over L}\ln{1-y_\infty\over 1-y_s}.\]
\[1-y_A(z)=(1-y_s)\left({1-y_\infty\over1-y_s}\right)^{z/L}.\]

The concentration profile curves because a given net flux requires a different concentration gradient at different carrier fractions. A dilute expansion of the logarithm gives \(N_A\simeq(cD/L)(y_s-y_\infty)\). The exact-to-dilute flux ratio exceeds unity when the surface emits A. The discrepancy becomes measurable long before the carrier disappears. At fractions very close to unity, the assumptions of a stagnant, isothermal film also deserve new scrutiny.

Small and large amounts of vapour at the surface

At 300 K and 1 bar, \(c=40.09\) mol/m³. Take \(D=2.0\times10^{-5}\) m²/s and \(L=5\) mm. The coefficient \(cD/L=0.1604\) mol/(m² s). With a vapour-free reservoir and \(y_s=0.05\), the logarithm is 0.05129 and the predicted flux is 0.00823 mol/(m² s). A linear gradient would give 0.00802, about 2.5% lower.

For \(y_s=0.50\), the flux rises to 0.111 mol/(m² s), versus 0.0802 for the dilute estimate. At mid-depth the exact fraction is \(1-\sqrt{0.5}=0.293\), while linear interpolation gives 0.25. An experimental check can therefore use either a mass-loss measurement or a local concentration measurement. Evaporative cooling changes the saturation mole fraction at the surface, so a measured surface temperature matters even if room temperature seems stable.

Measure how quickly a dielectric follows an electric field

Fields and the response of matter

Derive the time and frequency responses of a relaxing polarisation, locate the loss maximum and decide whether a measured sample contains more than one relaxation process.

From a delayed polarisation to a constitutive equation

A slab of insulating material is placed between parallel electrodes. At first the measured charge increases very quickly when a small voltage is switched on. It then continues to rise slowly even though the applied voltage is fixed. A constant real relative permittivity can describe the final charge but cannot predict this record. The experiment reveals at least two relevant time scales. For a measurement window that resolves only the slower process, treat the fast electronic response as instantaneous and describe the slower polarisation \(P\) by its approach to an equilibrium value.

At fixed weak field the equilibrium slow polarisation is \(\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E\), with \(\Delta\epsilon>0\). Assume the rate of change is proportional to the distance from that equilibrium, with one positive relaxation time \(\tau\). Then \(\tau\dot P+P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E(t)\). This is a phenomenological material law. Its assumptions include linear response about the prepared state, a time-independent \(\tau\), and a sample whose constituents can be described by one dominant relaxation. Testing those assumptions is part of the experiment.

For \(E(t)=E_s\) switched on at zero from an unpolarised state, solve using the integrating factor \(e^{t/\tau}\). The result is \(P=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon E_s(1-e^{-t/\tau})\). After one \(\tau\), 63.21% of the slow response has developed; after \(3\tau\), 95.02%. Measuring an absolute fraction requires subtracting the fast initial jump and knowing the eventual plateau. A long baseline drift can look like a second relaxation if those references are not controlled.

Derive the complex dielectric response

Now use peak phasors with time dependence \(e^{-i\omega t}\). Differentiation contributes \(-i\omega\), so \(P/E=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon/(1-i\omega\tau)\). Include the high-frequency relative permittivity \(\epsilon_\infty\), where “high” means above the slow process but below additional electronic resonances. The real and imaginary parts of relative permittivity are

\[\epsilon'=\epsilon_\infty+\frac{\Delta\epsilon}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\]
\[\epsilon''=\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.\]

At fixed field amplitude the polarisation current is \(\dot P=-i\omega P\). The mean electrical work per unit time and volume is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E^*\dot P)=\tfrac12\omega\epsilon_0\epsilon''|E|^2\). Loss therefore has positive \(\epsilon''\) in the chosen time convention. Using \(e^{+i\omega t}\) reverses the sign of the imaginary part of permittivity but leaves mean absorbed energy positive when the current convention is changed consistently.

Set \(u=\omega\tau\). Differentiating \(u/(1+u^2)\) gives \((1-u^2)/(1+u^2)^2\). Hence \(\epsilon''\) peaks at \(u=1\), with height \(\Delta\epsilon/2\), and the real part lies halfway between its low- and high-frequency plateaux. The peak identified here is in the imaginary *permittivity*. A plot of dissipated power at fixed field includes an additional factor of frequency and need not have a peak at the same location.

Tie a frequency sweep to a step test

Suppose a material has \(\epsilon_\infty=2\), \(\Delta\epsilon=6\), and a measured loss peak at 1 kHz. Then \(\tau=1/(2\pi\,1000)\simeq159\ \mu\mathrm s\). At the peak the relative permittivity is \(5+3i\) in our convention. A time-domain voltage step should show a slow charge transient reaching 63.2% of its added charge in roughly 159 μs. This comparison connects two experiments that weight electrode offsets and parasitic impedances differently.

An independent shape check follows by eliminating \(u\). Let \(X=\epsilon'-\epsilon_\infty\) and \(Y=\epsilon''\). From the formulas \(X^2+Y^2=\Delta\epsilon X\). Thus \((X-\Delta\epsilon/2)^2+Y^2=(\Delta\epsilon/2)^2\): a single Debye relaxation traces a semicircle in the complex plane. Departures from a semicircle can reflect several relaxation times, spatially inhomogeneous samples, leakage or imperfect fixture correction. They do not uniquely identify the microscopic mechanism.

Measure equilibrium field fluctuations with a capacitor

Fields and the response of matter

Derive an electrical noise measurement in two ways, determine what fraction reaches a finite-bandwidth detector and use agreement to calibrate an experiment.

A zero-average voltage can store measurable energy

Connect a capacitor \(C\) across a resistor \(R\) at a uniform absolute temperature \(T\), with no external drive. Ideal circuit equations predict that any deliberately deposited charge decays on time scale \(RC\). At thermal equilibrium, microscopic fluctuations continually exchange charge and energy with the capacitor. Symmetry gives zero mean voltage, but the stored energy involves \(V^2\). In the classical limit a quadratic degree of freedom has mean energy \(k_BT/2\), giving \(\langle V^2\rangle=k_BT/C\). The resistor sets the time scale of equilibration; equilibrium mean-square voltage does not depend on resistance in this ideal model.

Check the dimensions: \(k_BT\) is an energy in joules and capacitance has units coulombs per volt. Their ratio is volts squared. At \(T=300\) K and \(C=100\) pF the RMS voltage is \(\sqrt{k_BT/C}\simeq6.44\ \mu{\rm V}\). A voltmeter that averages the signed voltage for a long time reports a value near zero. Measuring the noise requires an RMS, variance or appropriately calibrated spectral measurement.

Derive the same variance from a spectral measurement

Use a one-sided voltage power spectral density per hertz at positive frequencies. The classical Johnson–Nyquist open-circuit result for a resistor is \(S_R(f)=4k_BTR\) V²/Hz. Its square root is an amplitude spectral density in V/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\), not a voltage variance. Use an equivalent series voltage noise source and the RC transfer function \(H(f)=1/[1+2\pi i fRC]\). A stationary linear system multiplies the voltage power spectrum by \(|H|^2\), so

\[S_C(f)=\frac{4k_BTR}{1+(2\pi fRC)^2},\qquad\]

\int_0^\infty S_C(f)\,df=\frac{k_BT}{C}.

The integral follows by \(u=2\pi RCf\) and \(\int_0^\infty du/(1+u^2)=\pi/2\). It furnishes a detailed check on the factor four, the use of one-sided density, the conversion from hertz to angular frequency and the amplitude-versus-power distinction. If your result disagrees by a constant factor of two or \(2\pi\), audit these conventions before assigning a new noise temperature.

For \(R=10\) kΩ, \(T=300\) K, the low-frequency amplitude spectral density is approximately 12.9 nV/\(\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). The RC corner at \(C=100\) pF is \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\simeq159\) kHz. The open-circuit low-frequency density rises with \(R\) while the bandwidth shrinks in inverse proportion. Their integrated product gives the earlier resistance-independent variance.

Include a real observation bandwidth

Suppose a first measurement adds an ideal low-pass cutoff \(B\). The recorded fraction of equilibrium variance is \(F=(2/\pi)\arctan(B/f_c)\). Half of the noise variance lies below the RC corner. That may be surprising if the corner was introduced as the frequency at which the spectral density reaches half its zero-frequency value. These two properties coincide for this single-pole spectrum for different mathematical reasons.

At \(B=0.1f_c\), \(F\simeq0.06345\), so the recorded RMS is about 25.2% of the infinite-band RMS. At \(B=10f_c\), \(F\simeq0.93655\), giving about 96.8% of the RMS. In an experiment, taking a square root one step too early changes this comparison. A real detector transfer function replaces the sharp cutoff by an integral of \(S_C(f)|H_\mathrm{det}(f)|^2\).

In the classical regime spectral density and integrated variance are proportional to \(T\), while RMS voltage grows only as \(\sqrt T\). A plot labelled “noise” without units cannot distinguish these two dependencies.

A physical check and a calibration check

An RC time-domain experiment provides a second observable beyond integrated power. Under the same single-pole equilibrium model the voltage autocovariance is \(\langle V(t)V(t+\tau)\rangle=(k_BT/C)e^{-|\tau|/(RC)}\). Its variance at zero lag agrees with equipartition, and its decay time is obtained from the homogeneous charge-relaxation equation. Repeating the measurement with a known extra parallel capacitance lowers the RMS and lengthens the correlation time. Seeing only one of those changes suggests that the observed noise may include substantial amplifier input voltage or an independent background.

The classical approximation assumes \(hf\ll k_BT\) over the relevant band and a resistor that can be treated as frequency independent. A MHz room-temperature experiment is far inside this regime. At sufficiently high frequencies or cryogenic temperatures, a quantum noise model, an explicit definition of the measured ordering of the spectrum and the detector's energy exchange are needed. Extending an ideal classical white source to infinite frequency is only permissible as an approximation inside the convergent low-frequency RC calculation; it is not a microscopic model at arbitrarily high frequency.

Measuring magnetic core loss by a hysteresis loop and by calorimetry

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

A closed loop on a graph of magnetic induction against field encloses an area with units of energy per unit volume. A thermometer measures kelvins. Bring these two observations onto the same scale, account for the winding heat, and determine when the two experiments actually test the same material loss.

Infer the field and the induction from independent windings

Wind a toroidal specimen with a primary winding of \(N_p\) turns and a lightly loaded pickup of \(N_s\) turns. Let \(A\) be the representative iron cross-sectional area, \(\ell\) the mean magnetic path length and \(V_c=A\ell\) the magnetic volume. Neglect a gap and leakage initially. Ampère's law around a path through the core gives \(H\simeq N_pi_p/\ell\). This relation is accurate when the toroid is slender enough that the spread of path radii is small and the pickup current is negligible. In a thick toroid \(H(r)=N_pi_p/(2\pi r)\), so one point on a nominal loop can actually represent a range of local field values.

Faraday's law supplies the second observable. Choose winding polarity so the reported voltage is \(v_s=N_sA\,dB/dt\). Integrating the voltage gives the change in cross-section-averaged magnetic induction. The pickup cannot determine a constant induction offset, because static flux induces no voltage. A closed loop area is insensitive to adding a constant to \(B\) or \(H\), but a dc offset in the voltage integrates into a time-dependent ramp and prevents the reconstructed trajectory from closing. In steady periodic operation one can subtract the voltage mean over a complete period before integration. Applying that correction to a genuinely evolving magnetisation process would remove part of a real flux change.

Multiply the two measured field quantities in the appropriate order. The field work supplied per unit material volume on a small induction change is \(H\,dB\). The source term associated with the magnetising flux is \(i_p v_{p,{\rm induced}}\,dt\). Using \(v_{p,{\rm induced}}\simeq(N_p/N_s)v_s\), its value is \(i_pN_pA\,dB=V_cH\,dB\). Integrating around a settled cycle gives the magnetic loss

\[W_c=V_c\oint H\,dB,\]
\[P_c=fV_c\oint H\,dB.\]

The field can absorb positive work and return some of it later in the same cycle. The area measures the algebraic remainder. Reversing the polarity of a single signal flips the loop orientation. A passively heated specimen must receive positive net energy when signs are defined for power entering the core. Simply taking the absolute value of all instantaneous products would count reversible storage twice and can grossly overestimate the heat.

Work an elliptical loop from time-domain signals

Suppose a nearly sinusoidal field is \(H=H_p\cos\omega t\) and the induction lags it by a positive angle \(\delta\), \(B=B_p\cos(\omega t-\delta)\). Differentiate the latter and multiply by \(H\). Over one cycle \(\cos\omega t\sin\omega t\) averages to zero, and \(\cos^2\omega t\) averages to one half. The resulting mean power density is \(\omega H_pB_p\sin\delta/2\), giving \(\oint H\,dB=\pi H_pB_p\sin\delta\). With zero lag the two signals trace a line and the area vanishes, even if the peak values are large. A saturation-distorted loop need not be elliptical; the original time integral remains valid with synchronised data.

Take \(H_p=120\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_p=0.200\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta=12^\circ\). The energy per cycle and volume is \(15.68\,{\rm J/m^3}\). A toroid with \(\ell=0.20\,{\rm m}\), \(A=2.0\times10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2}\) and \(V_c=40\,{\rm cm^3}\) dissipates \(0.627\,{\rm mJ}\) per cycle. At \(f=200\,{\rm Hz}\) its power is \(0.125\,{\rm W}\). With \(N_p=100\), the necessary current peak is \(0.240\,{\rm A}\). With \(N_s=50\), the pickup voltage has peak \(N_sA(2\pi f)B_p=2.51\,{\rm V}\). These last two predictions allow the raw oscilloscope traces to be checked before a calculated loss in watts is trusted.

For sampled periodic data use \(w=\sum_n(H_n+H_{n+1})(B_{n+1}-B_n)/2\), including the segment from the last point back to the first. This trapezoidal line integral computes an oriented polygon area. Plot the starting and ending points before adding the closing segment. Connecting widely separated endpoints by hand hides drift and can generate a fictitious wedge whose area depends on acquisition timing. Repeat at higher sampling rates to see whether corners or switching features carry appreciable extra area. When a periodic excitation has harmonics the time increment must resolve the highest significant harmonic in both measured signals.

Compare a thermal measurement with the magnetic area

Consider first a calorimeter that receives heat only from the core. Let the heat capacity of specimen, holder and sensor be \(C_{\rm eff}\). Let the leakage conductance to an ambient bath be \(G\), and let \(\Delta T=T-T_a\). The thermal balance \(C_{\rm eff}\dot{\Delta T}=P_c-G\Delta T\) predicts a steady offset \(P_c/G\) and an initial heating slope \(P_c/C_{\rm eff}\). A small thermometer lag or poor thermal contact can make the earliest measured slope misleading even though environmental losses are then small. A separate electrical heater placed where the core deposits its heat can calibrate the effective thermal response.

For \(C_{\rm eff}=20\,{\rm J/K}\), \(G=0.015\,{\rm W/K}\), and a \(2.0\,{\rm K}\) rise above ambient, the magnetic loss \(0.125\,{\rm W}\) would give a slope of about \(0.00477\,{\rm K/s}\). The heat already leaking to the bath is \(0.030\,{\rm W}\), too large to discard in a ten-percent comparison. If the winding is inside the same calorimeter, its Ohmic heat must enter the balance as well. With primary resistance \(1.50\,\Omega\) and the computed sinusoidal current, winding heating is \((0.240/\sqrt2)^2(1.50)=0.0432\,{\rm W}\). The expected slope of the combined package at the same temperature is now \(0.00693\,{\rm K/s}\). Subtraction of winding heat recovers the core result only when the estimated resistance represents the winding at its operating temperature and frequency.

A thermal estimate drawn from a whole interval is often more robust than a derivative from two adjacent temperature samples. Integrating gives \(Q_{\rm deposited}=C_{\rm eff}[\Delta T(t_2)-\Delta T(t_1)]+G\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Delta T\,dt\). Divide by the duration for mean power, then remove other measured heat sources. A drifting ambient must be recorded explicitly because the calorimeter stores energy relative to its own absolute temperature. If the core and coil equilibrate slowly with each other, a single fitted heat capacity can depend on the fitting time window. A two-temperature model or a longer dwell then supplies an independent diagnostic.

Separate reversible exchange from hysteretic and induced currents

A single measured loop at finite frequency gives the total energy dissipated by the core at that prescribed waveform and temperature. The loop can include irreversible domain processes, eddy currents and other rate-dependent effects. It does not by itself split these contributions. To probe their different scaling hold the peak induction and waveform shape fixed, then change frequency. Under a restricted low-frequency model the quasi-static hysteresis energy per cycle is approximately constant, while classical thin-lamination eddy-current power scales as \(f^2B_p^2\). Its energy per cycle therefore scales roughly as \(f\). Plotting the measured loss per cycle and volume against \(f\) tests whether such an intercept-plus-slope description fits the chosen range.

Suppose the area is \(12\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(100\,{\rm Hz}\) and \(20\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(300\,{\rm Hz}\), at the same peak induction. A straight line gives intercept \(8\,{\rm J/m^3}\) and slope \(0.040\,{\rm J\,s/m^3}\). It predicts \(16\,{\rm J/m^3}\) at \(200\,{\rm Hz}\), near the elliptical-loop calculation. Two points always determine a line. A third and fourth frequency, together with uncertainties, are required to test the law. Different losses can share similar frequency scaling over a short interval, and temperature-dependent conductivity can bend a nominally simple curve.

Use the pickup to reject the reversible part of terminal power

Measuring primary terminal voltage and current with a power analyser provides a separate electrical route to loss, but the wanted number can be a small difference between terms. The applied voltage contains winding resistance, induced magnetising voltage, leakage-inductance voltage and possible parasitic contributions. In steady periodic operation the energy stored in a fixed linear leakage inductance returns after each cycle, while winding resistance dissipates energy permanently. The secondary pickup preferentially measures the core-linked flux. Forming \(p_c(t)=(N_p/N_s)i_p(t)v_s(t)\) and averaging over a full cycle estimates core power without directly including the primary's copper voltage drop. It relies on both windings linking the same relevant flux and on negligible power drawn by the pickup.

For the elliptical example the primary magnetising voltage has peak \(5.03\,{\rm V}\) and leads the induction by ninety degrees. Its phase relative to the current is \(90^\circ-\delta=78^\circ\). The core branch's apparent power is \(V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\approx0.603\,{\rm VA}\); its mean dissipative part is about \(0.125\,{\rm W}\), and the reactive part is about \(0.590\,{\rm var}\). Only the mean part contributes net heating of the magnetic volume. Including the winding voltage adds about \(0.043\,{\rm W}\) of real power to the terminal reading. A simple product of peak current and peak secondary voltage, without the turns ratio, factor one half and phase factor, has no reason to agree with either measured loss.

Consider a nearly ideal core with the same peak signals but a lag of only one degree. Its loss is about \(10.5\,{\rm mW}\), while the much larger reversible power exchange is almost unchanged. A time offset of \(10\,\mu{\rm s}\) at \(200\,{\rm Hz}\) adds \(0.72^\circ\), causing a relative loss error of approximately \(72\%\) for that small lag. Calibrate the relative channel delays using a reference signal through the actual current transducer and voltage chain, including their filters. Adjusting traces on screen until a core loop appears narrow assumes the very loss one is trying to measure. A synchronous measurement over many periods suppresses random noise but does not remove a persistent delay bias.

Test reconstruction on a waveform with a third harmonic

Let \(H(t)=H_1\cos\omega t+H_3\cos3\omega t\) while \(B(t)=B_1\cos(\omega t-\delta_1)+B_3\cos(3\omega t-\delta_3)\). With these definitions \(\delta_n\) is the phase lag of the \(n\)th harmonic in its own sinusoid. Multiplying \(H\) by \(\dot B\) and integrating over an integer number of fundamental periods eliminates products of different harmonics. Each surviving harmonic contributes the number of its own cycles in one fundamental period. The area per fundamental period is therefore \(\pi[H_1B_1\sin\delta_1+3H_3B_3\sin\delta_3]\). This identity tests whether a numerical area calculation tracks the full waveform. It does not assume that a real material is linear; here the two signals are supplied as measured periodic functions.

Use the earlier fundamental values and add \(H_3=20\,{\rm A/m}\), \(B_3=0.030\,{\rm T}\) and \(\delta_3=30^\circ\). The third harmonic contributes \(2.83\,{\rm J/m^3}\) in each fundamental period. This represents an additional \(0.0226\,{\rm W}\) for the chosen specimen and frequency. A fundamental-only sine fit would miss nearly fifteen percent of the resulting total area. An apparent jagged loop can also arise from asynchronous sampling or an offset. Distinguish genuine harmonics by checking that they are phase locked to the drive and persist under a change in sampling rate and analogue bandwidth.

Sweep phase and read the oriented loop

The live experiment reconstructs the \(H,B\) trajectory and compares it with a zero-lag reference. It uses the same peak field and induction for the two curves. At fixed peaks changing frequency leaves an assigned phase lag and therefore the prescribed energy per cycle unchanged, while watts rise with the number of cycles per second. A real core can change its lag and waveform with frequency; the graph separates this independent variable so that the meaning of the two scaled quantities is clear. Optical trapping and Brownian calibration of a dielectric bead

Electromechanics, optics and measurement

An image of a bead remaining near a focus does not establish the strength of its trap. Derive the force pulling a small dielectric sphere into a bright region, compare it with the forward force from scattering and learn what a motion record can tell you about stiffness, drag and temperature. The model here uses an inert dielectric sphere in a homogeneous Newtonian liquid.

From two dielectric boundaries to the energy of an induced dipole

A sphere of radius \(a\) and relative permittivity \(\epsilon_b\) sits in a liquid of relative permittivity \(\epsilon_m\). Assuming the sphere is small compared with a wavelength in the liquid, solve its local electric problem in an almost uniform applied field \(E\). Rotational symmetry about the field admits an interior potential proportional to \(r\cos\theta\). The exterior consists of the imposed potential \(-Er\cos\theta\) and an induced dipolar term proportional to \(\cos\theta/r^2\). Equality of potential and of normal displacement at the surface gives the local-field and dipole factors

\[E_{\rm in}=\frac{3\epsilon_m}{\epsilon_b+2\epsilon_m}E,\]
\[p=\alpha E,\]
\[\alpha=4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_m a^3\frac{\epsilon_b-\epsilon_m}{\epsilon_b+2\epsilon_m}.\]

The field doing the polarising work is the external field in the host at the particle position before inserting it. To turn that work into a potential, build up the dipole from zero rather than assuming a permanent dipole moment. Quasistatic assembly gives \(-\int_0^E\alpha E'\,dE'=-\alpha E^2/2\). For a harmonic peak field average the square over an optical period. In a nearly lossless liquid of refractive index \(n_m\), the peak field is related to travelling-wave intensity by \(I=n_m\epsilon_0c|E|^2/2\). The resulting conservative force is

\[U=-\frac{\operatorname{Re}\alpha}{4}|E|^2=-\frac{\operatorname{Re}\alpha}{2n_m\epsilon_0 c}I,\]
\[\mathbf F_{\rm grad}=-\nabla U .\]

The two factors of two have different origins: induced-dipole assembly and time averaging of a sinusoid. Using a permanent-dipole potential with a peak field would miss both. A bead of higher refractive index than the liquid has positive real polarizability and is attracted to increasing intensity. A lower-index inclusion reverses this local tendency. For large particles, strong absorption or rapidly structured fields, a dipole with real static polarizability no longer specifies the force; a field or multipole calculation of the complete momentum balance is required.

Investigate the small-sphere dipole response and its dependence on particle size

Compute a transverse stiffness and test how local it is

Assume the beam near its focal plane has intensity \(I(x)=I_0e^{-2x^2/w^2}\), with power \(P\), waist \(w\) defined at \(1/e^2\) intensity and \(I_0=2P/(\pi w^2)\). Put \(U_0=\operatorname{Re}\alpha I_0/(2n_m\epsilon_0c)>0\). The potential measured above its minimum is \(U_0[1-e^{-2x^2/w^2}]\). Expanding to second order and equating its leading term to \(\kappa_xx^2/2\) gives \(\kappa_x=4U_0/w^2\). Stiffness therefore scales linearly with power, as the third power of sphere radius and as the inverse fourth power of waist at fixed indices. Each scaling applies within the stated approximations.

For a 40 nm radius bead with \(n_b=1.59\), take \(n_m=1.33\), \(w=0.65\) micrometres, vacuum wavelength 1064 nm, \(P=90\) mW and \(T=298\) K. The index contrast factor is 0.1252 and the waist intensity is \(1.36\times10^{11}\) watts per square metre. The predicted depth \(3.03\times10^{-20}\) J is 7.36 times the thermal energy. The lateral stiffness is \(2.87\times10^{-7}\) N/m. A displacement of 50 nm then requires approximately 14.3 fN of transverse balancing force. Extract stiffness from fluctuations or from a relaxation time

Near a stable point in one Cartesian direction, let \(x\) denote displacement from the mean and \(\zeta\) the viscous drag. At sufficiently low frequencies the inertial term is negligible and the overdamped Langevin equation is \(\zeta\dot x=-\kappa x+\xi\). At equilibrium the fluctuating force has zero mean and white covariance \(2k_BT\zeta\delta(t-t')\) over the frequencies considered. The deterministic relaxation time is \(\tau=\zeta/\kappa\). Multiplying the equation at positive delay by \(x(0)\) and averaging removes the uncorrelated later noise and gives the decaying covariance. Equipartition sets its initial value.

\[C(t)=\frac{k_BT}{\kappa}e^{-|t|/\tau},\]
\[f_c=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi\zeta},\]
\[S_x^{(1)}(f)=\frac{4k_BT\zeta}{\kappa^2+(2\pi f\zeta)^2},\]
\[f\ge0 .\]

The stated spectrum is one-sided in ordinary frequency. Its integral from zero to infinity equals \(C(0)\); doubling another time would double the variance. In an unbounded fluid with viscosity 1.00 mPa s, Stokes drag is \(6\pi\eta a=7.54\times10^{-10}\) N s/m. The default predicts \(f_c=60.5\) Hz and rms displacement 120 nm. These local equilibrium predictions apply to a sufficiently long-lived, locally sampled trapped state.

Work in laboratory coordinates with a fixed optical trap and with fluid moving uniformly at velocity \(u(t)\) near the bead, well away from walls in the idealised model. The drag is now \(-\zeta(\dot x-u)\). Superposing a stage displacement \(A\cos\omega t\) on thermal fluctuations gives a deterministic response whose complex amplitude satisfies \((\kappa-i\omega\zeta)\widetilde x=-i\omega\zeta A\) in the negative-exponential convention. Therefore its amplitude ratio is

\[\frac{|\widetilde x|}{A}=\frac{\omega\tau}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2\tau^2}} .\]

At the trap corner the bead displacement amplitude is the known stage amplitude divided by the square root of two. A calibrated 80.0 nm stage oscillation there produces a predicted bead amplitude of 56.6 nm. A frequency-dependent stage calibration changes the actual fluid displacement, and motion of the optics relative to the cell generates a direct detector background even with the bead immobilised. Liouville flow and what an ensemble can remember

Classical mechanics

A cloud of identical noninteracting beads is released with slightly different initial positions and momenta. Its position distribution widens. Does the system occupy more phase space, or has its uncertainty merely changed shape?

Follow a small phase-space element

For one canonical degree of freedom the velocity of a point \((q,p)\) is \((\partial_pH,-\partial_qH)\). A short-time map takes \(\boldsymbol z\) to \(\boldsymbol z+\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol z,t)\,dt\). Its Jacobian matrix is \(I+(\partial\boldsymbol v/\partial\boldsymbol z)dt\); expanding the determinant gives \(1+(\nabla_z\cdot\boldsymbol v)dt+O(dt^2)\). Hamilton's equations make the divergence zero because the two mixed second derivatives of a smooth Hamiltonian cancel. Each additional canonical pair contributes a similar cancellation. A time-dependent Hamiltonian may change energy but still preserve the instantaneous phase-space volume along the flow.

Conservation of the number of ensemble members in a moving phase-space box also gives the continuity equation \(\partial_t f+\nabla_z\cdot(f\boldsymbol v)=0\). The vanishing divergence leaves \(df/dt=0\) on each trajectory. A stationary mechanical system can admit \(f=F(H)\) as a stationary distribution because trajectories stay on energy surfaces. Such stationarity alone does not imply that one trajectory explores an entire energy surface. For example, further integrals of motion restrict regular multidimensional trajectories.

Propagate a cloud released into free flight

For mass \(m\), the exact map is \(p=p_0,\ q=q_0+tp_0/m\). Its determinant is one regardless of time. An initially uncorrelated cloud with variances \(s_q^2,s_p^2\) has evolved covariance \(C_{qp}=ts_p^2/m\). The new position variance is \(s_q^2+(ts_p/m)^2\), while the momentum variance stays fixed. Compute the determinant of this covariance matrix:

\[\sigma_q^2\sigma_p^2-C_{qp}^2=s_q^2s_p^2 .\]

For a Gaussian the ellipse at a fixed multiple of the covariance radius thus retains its area while it shears. With \(m=1\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(s_q=1\,\mathrm m\), \(s_p=0.5\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\), and \(t=4\,\mathrm s\), the observed position standard deviation is \(\sqrt5=2.236\,\mathrm m\). The momentum standard deviation remains 0.5 and the covariance becomes \(1.0\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\). Their uncorrected width product is 1.118 in action units. The square root of the covariance determinant is still 0.5. Multiplication of measured marginal widths alone falsely suggests an area increase.

Compare reversible shear and real drag

Now apply a linear drag \(\dot p=-\gamma p\) to each bead with constant \(\gamma>0\). Its phase-space divergence is \(-\gamma\), hence the map determinant after time \(t\) is \(e^{-\gamma t}\). Direct integration gives \(p=e^{-\gamma t}p_0,\ q=q_0+(1-e^{-\gamma t})p_0/(m\gamma)\). The cloud now genuinely loses area in the reduced bead description. A larger Hamiltonian model of bead plus environment can remain volume preserving. Check there is a smooth zero-drag limit rather than substituting zero into a quotient.

Time-dependent diffusion

Numerical partial differential equations

Derive the diffusion time-step restriction from two independent arguments and compare explicit and implicit decay of spatial modes.

From first principles

Derive the explicit heat step and its grid-dependent time scale

Take the heat equation \(T_t=\alpha T_{xx}\) with constant positive diffusivity on a uniform one-dimensional grid. Fourier conduction through each side of a cell produces a temperature update proportional to the difference of the two neighbour gradients. With forward Euler timing, collect those fluxes to obtain

\[T_i^{n+1}=T_i^n+r(T_{i-1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i+1}^n),\]
\[r=\alpha\Delta t/h^2 .\]

When \(0\le r\le1/2\) the new temperature is a convex average of three old ones, preserving bounds on a source-free grid. To check whether a higher step could still be stable for all periodic grid disturbances, substitute \(T_j^n=g^n e^{ij\theta}\), where \(j\) is the node index. The amplification simplifies to

\[g=1+r(e^{-i\theta}-2+e^{i\theta})=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2).\]

The alternating mode at \(\theta=\pi\) gives \(g=1-4r\), so \(|g|\le1\) again requires \(r\le1/2\). Halving spatial spacing therefore quarters the largest explicit time step for this discretization. Implicit stepping changes this restriction but still has to resolve any transient relevant to the measurement.

Derivation and applications

Can an energy-stable heat step create an alternating transient?

A diffusion mode should decay rather than oscillate. To distinguish decay of a norm from preservation of sign, examine the finite-element or finite-volume semidiscrete system \(M\dot u+Ku=0\), with symmetric positive definite mass matrix \(M\) and thermal stiffness \(K\) positive semidefinite. An eigenvector satisfying \(Kv=\lambda Mv\) has scalar amplitude \(y(t)\) obeying \(y'=-\lambda y\). A generalized \(\theta\) step applied to this mode gives

\[[1+\theta z]y^{n+1}=[1-(1-\theta)z]y^n,\]
\[z=\Delta t\,\lambda .\]

For Crank–Nicolson take \(\theta=1/2\). The factor \(G=(1-z/2)/(1+z/2)\) has \(|G|\le1\) for all \(z\ge0\) but changes sign when \(z>2\). An initial grid-scale component can therefore alternate in time. To test a remedy, replace the first full step by two half backward-Euler steps. Their combined factor is

\[G_{\rm start}=(1+z/2)^{-2}.\]

Thereafter return to the second-order Crank–Nicolson update. After \(n\) further full steps the mode amplitudes, with and without the initial damping, are

\[y_{\rm damped}=G_{\rm start}G^ny^0,\]
\[y_{\rm plain}=G^{n+1}y^0 .\]

For small \(z\), both startup strategies agree through first order, while for large \(z\) the two half steps strongly suppress the unresolved mode. This argument explains a startup treatment for nonsmooth initial data; sign preservation of every physical grid value is a stronger matrix property than sign preservation of one mode.

Worked example

Check damping of a near-grid-scale eigenmode

Take \(\lambda=2000\) s⁻¹ and \(\Delta t=0.0100\) s. Then \(z=20\). A single Crank–Nicolson step leaves \(-9/11=-0.818\) of the amplitude; two backward-Euler half steps leave \(1/121=0.00826\). The exact amplitude factor is \(e^{-20}=2.06\times10^{-9}\). Both discrete results are bounded, but the startup treatment removes almost all of a mode that the time grid cannot resolve.

Second application

Check the cost for a physically slow component

For the same mesh and step, a slow mode with \(\lambda=2.00\) s⁻¹ has \(z=0.0200\). Crank–Nicolson gives \(0.99/1.01=0.980198\), the startup gives \(1/1.01^2=0.980296\) and the exact factor is \(0.980199\). The slow component changes by less than \(10^{-4}\) in this comparison. A timestep must still resolve a transient that is itself part of the desired output.

Discretise space and time separately

A heat pulse in a thin insulated rod smooths over time according to \(u_t=D u_{xx}\) for constant positive diffusivity \(D\). Use a uniform grid of spacing \(h\), and initially examine interior points or periodic boundaries so the stencil is unchanged everywhere. Replacing the second derivative by a centred difference gives a system of ordinary differential equations for the nodal temperatures. The time method still has to resolve the fastest rate that this spatial operator can generate.

An explicit Euler step yields \(u_i^{n+1}=r u_{i-1}^n+(1-2r)u_i^n+r u_{i+1}^n\) with \(r=D\Delta t/h^2\). If \(0\leq r\leq1/2\), the update is a convex combination of three temperatures. The maximum cannot rise and the minimum cannot fall in a source-free interior step. Starting with one hot node at 1 and neighbours at zero, \(r=0.4\) gives a centre value 0.2 and neighbours 0.4 after a single step. At \(r=0.6\) the centre would become negative although the continuum heat equation preserves nonnegativity.

Identify the spatial modes that set the limit

Insert \(u_j^n=g^n e^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}\), where \(\mathrm{i}\) is the imaginary unit and \(j\) indexes the grid. The two neighbouring values contribute factors \(e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\theta}\), yielding \(g=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Long waves have \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\simeq\theta^2/4\), so their decay approaches the continuum rate as the mesh is refined. The alternating grid mode \(\theta=\pi\) has the most negative amplification and enforces \(r\leq1/2\) for bounded magnitude.

At the limiting value \(r=1/2\) the alternating mode flips sign without losing amplitude. A strict margin damps it. For \(D=10^{-4}\) m²/s and \(h=2\) mm, the maximum explicit step is \(h^2/(2D)=0.020\) s. Refining to 1 mm reduces that limit to 0.005 s. The mesh gives a sharper temperature profile yet can cost four times more steps over the same physical interval, besides the increased number of spatial degrees of freedom.

Compare backward Euler and Crank–Nicolson

Writing the semidiscrete system as \(u'=-A_hu\), backward Euler solves \((I+\Delta t A_h)u^{n+1}=u^n\). Crank–Nicolson averages the old and new diffusive fluxes, giving \((I+\Delta t A_h/2)u^{n+1}=(I-\Delta t A_h/2)u^n\). For a Fourier mode put \(s=4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Their factors are \(1/(1+s)\) and \((1-s/2)/(1+s/2)\), respectively. Both remain bounded at arbitrarily large \(s\), but Crank–Nicolson may alternate for under-resolved sharp features.

With \(s=20\), backward Euler retains only \(1/21\) per step. Crank–Nicolson yields \(-9/11\), retaining much more of the fast component and reversing its sign. For a smooth low-frequency mode Crank–Nicolson generally gives more accurate temporal decay because it is second order. Decide whether early sharp transients or later smooth behaviour is the relevant physical quantity. To identify spatial and temporal error separately, refine \(\Delta t\) first on a fixed mesh, then refine space with time error kept well below spatial error. Merely halving both at once cannot reveal which term dominated the original discrepancy.

Apply a prescribed heat input without confusing it with temperature

An insulated periodic rod with uniform heating rate \(s\) measured in K/s has an average temperature that must rise by \(s\Delta t\) each step. Sum the centred second differences over the periodic indices: each nodal value appears with coefficients \(1,-2,1\), which cancel. The source alone changes the mean. For \(s=3\) K/s and a step of 0.02 s the numerical average must increase by 0.06 K, independently of the diffusivity and initial profile.

A code can pass this global test while still mishandling short-wave damping, since an alternating error has zero spatial mean. Pair a mean-temperature test with a nonconstant mode-decay test. Use a homogeneous periodic run for isolates of diffusion, and a uniform temperature with prescribed source for the storage term. A failure in one test then identifies a different part of the mathematical balance than a failure in the other.

Prove decay of a discrete thermal energy

Let a closed semidiscrete diffusion model be \(M\dot u+Ku=0\), with \(M\) symmetric positive definite and \(K\) symmetric positive semidefinite. Assume \(K{\bf1}=0\), expressing zero flux for a uniform temperature. Backward Euler satisfies \(M(u^{n+1}-u^n)+\Delta t Ku^{n+1}=0\). Multiplying by the constant vector and using symmetry gives \({\bf1}^TMu^{n+1}={\bf1}^TMu^n\). Thus the capacity-weighted sum, proportional to stored sensible heat for constant capacities and a fixed reference, is conserved for any step size.

Now multiply by \(u^{n+1}\). The identity \(a^TM(a-b)=[\|a\|_M^2-\|b\|_M^2+\|a-b\|_M^2]/2\) yields the exact balance

\[\tfrac12\|u^n\|_M^2-\tfrac12\|u^{n+1}\|_M^2=\Delta t\,(u^{n+1})^TKu^{n+1}+\tfrac12\|u^{n+1}-u^n\|_M^2 .\]

Both terms are nonnegative. The quadratic quantity therefore decays although total sensible heat is conserved. Subtracting the conserved mean first yields the same decay statement for temperature variation around equilibrium. The first term represents spatial smoothing measured at the new time, and the second reflects the numerical time update. This derivation gives a stability certificate on nonuniform grids without assuming Fourier modes.

Audit two cells with different heat capacities

Choose capacities 1 and 3 J/K, conductance 2 W/K, and initial temperature deviations \((4,0)^T\) K. The conserved sensible heat is 4 J, so the final equilibrium in a closed system must be one kelvin above reference. At \(\Delta t=0.5\) s the matrix \(M+\Delta tK\) has rows \((2,-1)\) and \((-1,4)\), and the right-hand side is \((4,0)^T\). The new temperatures are \((16/7,4/7)^T\). Checking their weighted sum gives \(16/7+3(4/7)=4\), as required. The unweighted average changes, which is physically appropriate for unequal capacities.

The quadratic quantity at the start is \(4^2/2=8\). After the step it is \([(16/7)^2+3(4/7)^2]/2=152/49\). Its drop is \(240/49\). The conductive contribution is \((0.5)(2)(12/7)^2=144/49\); the time-update contribution is \([(-12/7)^2+3(4/7)^2]/2=96/49\). Their sum recovers the measured drop exactly. Neither contribution equals a loss of the 4 J of sensible heat. The quadratic measure depends on squared temperatures and has different physical units.

A positive heat supply or a prescribed exterior temperature introduces a boundary or source work term into these identities. Applying the closed-system decay test to an externally heated experiment would therefore diagnose the wrong physical model. Verify which conserved or dissipated quantity is actually implied by the capacity matrix, boundary conditions and source terms before comparing numerical integrators.

The conserved mean suggests another check after many large steps. The steady state must have zero conductance dissipation, which for two connected cells requires equal temperatures. Together with the weighted heat total this fixes both temperatures at one kelvin above reference. A scheme that instead approaches the unweighted initial average of two would be converging to the wrong conserved quantity despite producing a visually smooth, uniform endpoint. The measured quadratic variation about the correct mean tends to zero; the uncentred squared quantity tends to a positive constant because the remaining uniform state still has temperature above the chosen reference.

What a periodic grid does to a decaying Fourier mode

Numerical partial differential equations

A periodic diffusion solver predicts the shape of a sampled wave while its amplitude decays too slowly. Fourier analysis separates spatial differentiation error, an unresolved physical mode and errors caused by the time integrator.

Diagonalise the differential operator on the periodic interval

Use a dimensionless coordinate \(0\le x<2\pi\) and solve \(u_t=\kappa u_{xx}\) with periodic values and periodic derivatives. The complex exponentials \(e^{imx}\), with integer m, satisfy both conditions and are eigenfunctions of the second derivative. Put \(u(x,t)=A_m(t)e^{imx}\) and cancel the common exponential. The scalar amplitude equation is \(A'_m=-\kappa m^2 A_m\). Thus every cosine or sine mode decays by \(\exp(-\kappa m^2t)\), with no phase shift, while the m=0 mean is conserved.

On a uniform grid \(x_j=2\pi j/N\), the samples of \(m\) and \(m+\ell N\) agree for every integer \(\ell\). A discrete Fourier transform returns coefficients in one representative band. For the real cosine test with even N we can fold a positive physical mode into \(k=\min(r,N-r)\), \(r=m\bmod N\). A Fourier spectral Laplacian multiplies that coefficient by \(-k^2\). For a resolved mode it differentiates the trigonometric interpolant exactly, up to arithmetic. For unresolved data it differentiates the aliased representative; no operation using only those samples can identify the original m.

Apply the centred difference stencil to a single wave

The standard second difference is \((u_{j+1}-2u_j+u_{j-1})/h^2\), \(h=2\pi/N\). Put the grid wave \(u_j=e^{ikjh}\) into the stencil. Factor out the common phase and simplify with \(e^{i\xi}+e^{-i\xi}=2\cos\xi\). This gives the symbol and its small-h expansion:

\[{e^{ikh}-2+e^{-ikh}\over h^2}=-{4\over h^2}\sin^2(kh/2)=-k^2+{k^4h^2\over12}+O(k^6h^4).\]

The effective squared wavenumber is smaller than \(k^2\) for every nonconstant mode in the resolved band, so this spatial approximation decays too slowly if the time equation is integrated exactly. The error is modest when \(kh\ll1\), but at the Nyquist mode \(kh=\pi\) the ratio is \(4/\pi^2=0.4053\). A stable time integrator cannot repair this spatial attenuation error without additional modeling. The experiment exponentiates both discretizations' symbols exactly, isolating space and sampling here.

Measure separate errors with the same sampled initial data

Choose \(\kappa=0.5,\ m=3,\ N=16\). The true squared wavenumber is 9 and the difference stencil gives 8.006. Starting at unit amplitude, at \(t=0.20\) exact and spectral amplitudes are 0.40657; finite differences give 0.44906. Doubling N to 32 changes the difference result to 0.41716. The absolute amplitude error falls from 0.04249 to 0.01059, a ratio of 4.01 consistent with the second-order expansion. This is a spatial-convergence study because time evolution introduces no truncation error.

Now take \(m=12,\ N=16\). Every sampled cosine equals the low mode \(k=4\) at \(t=0\). At \(t=0.02\), the continuum amplitude for the true physical mode is \(e^{-1.44}=0.237\), whereas the spectral grid predicts \(e^{-0.16}=0.852\). A spectral computation can therefore agree exactly with its trigonometric interpolant and disagree strongly with the evolution of the intended signal. Sampling on a finer grid or obtaining prior bandwidth information resolves that ambiguity. The mean mode is particularly revealing when a nonconstant physical cosine aliases to a constant.

Start at mode 3 and successively double N from 8 to 32. Compute two amplitude-error ratios at the same time. Then use mode 12 on 16 points to observe an alias, and increase N until the Fourier grid represents the intended mode.

Splitting a stiff relaxation from a slowly varying drive

Numerical ordinary differential equations

A sensor tries to follow a changing reference while a fast relaxation damps departures. An explicit method may oscillate wildly even though the underlying transient decays monotonically. Treating the restoring term implicitly can resolve the slow motion using steps longer than the transient time. Whether those steps measure the transient accurately is a different question.

Construct an exact benchmark with two time scales

Let \(s(t)=\sin t\) and choose a relaxation time \(\varepsilon>0\). Consider \(y'=s'(t)+(s(t)-y)/\varepsilon\), with \(y(0)=1\). Set \(e=y-s\). Subtraction of \(s'\) gives \(e'=-e/\varepsilon\), hence the reference solution \(y(t)=\sin t+e^{-t/\varepsilon}\). The slow reference changes over times of order one, while the initial mismatch decays significantly over \(\varepsilon\). We know both pieces exactly and can diagnose errors even when a stable result looks visually smooth.

Explicit Euler applied to a perturbation of the state multiplies that perturbation by \(1-r\), with \(r=h/\varepsilon\). It damps monotonically only for \(0<r\le1\), changes sign while damping for \(1<r<2\), and grows for \(r>2\). These statements compare nearby solutions at equal drive. The full signal also contains the time variation of \(s\). At the boundary \(r=2\) a state perturbation alternates without decaying. Refining the step to follow a slow input alone can therefore give the wrong choice of \(h\).

Solve only the fast part implicitly

Split \(f_{\rm slow}(t)=\cos t\) from \(f_{\rm fast}(t,y)=(\sin t-y)/\varepsilon\). Evaluate the slow part at \(t_n\) and the fast part at \(t_{n+1},y_{n+1}\). This first-order additive implicit-explicit Euler step needs one scalar linear solve:

\[(1+r)y_{n+1}=y_n+h\cos t_n+r\sin t_{n+1}.\]

The state perturbation now multiplies by \(1/(1+r)\). It is damped at every positive step and tends to zero as \(r\) grows. Subtract \(\sin t_{n+1}\) from both sides after solving. The mismatch obeys \(e_{n+1}=[e_n+h\cos t_n-(\sin t_{n+1}-\sin t_n)]/(1+r)\). The bracketed forcing defect is \(O(h^2)\) for smooth input. In the stiff limit at fixed \(h\), the endpoint approaches the current reference. Moving the reference in the fast term to the old time would instead drag it one sample behind in that limit. The choice of what to treat implicitly includes the time at which a fast equilibrium is imposed.

Test early-time accuracy and late-time stability

For \(\varepsilon=0.02\), \(h=0.05\), \(r=2.5\). At the first step the exact value is \(\sin(0.05)+e^{-2.5}=0.1321\). Explicit Euler gives \(1+0.05(1-50)=-1.45\). The implicit-explicit value is \([1+0.05+2.5\sin(0.05)]/3.5=0.3357\). The latter is stable but visibly overestimates the surviving transient. Its damping factor \(1/3.5=0.286\) differs substantially from \(e^{-2.5}=0.0821\). A question about peak rate or initial settling requires shorter steps even if the late-time reference is recovered.

The experiment holds the final time at one while the number of steps changes. The vertical coordinate is the base-ten logarithm of absolute error, with values below \(10^{-15}\) drawn at that floor. Negative heights therefore correspond to errors less than one. Track the maximum error separately from the final error as the transient dies out. Try an initially unstable explicit run, then double the number of steps. The stiffness criterion depends on the ratio \(h/\varepsilon\), while accuracy on the reference also depends on \(h\) itself.

Learning a drifting temperature from a sequence of readings

Inverse problems and least squares

Derive the scalar Kalman update by completing the square and see how process uncertainty controls memory of the past.

Propagate a distribution through time

A drifting offset follows \(x_k=x_{k-1}+w_k\). A thermometer reports \(z_k=x_k+v_k\). Assume independent, zero-mean Gaussian process increments of variance \(Q\) and sensor noise of variance \(R\), independent at each time and of the initial state. Before the next reading, the mean stays at \(m^-=m_{k-1}\) and the variance adds, \(P^-=P_{k-1}+Q\).

The negative log posterior, up to constants, is \((x-m^-)^2/(2P^-)+(z_k-x)^2/(2R)\). Collect the coefficient of \(x^2\) and its linear term. The result can be written in two equivalent forms:

\[P_k=(1/P^-+1/R)^{-1},\]
\[m_k=m^-+K(z_k-m^-),\]
\[K=P^-/(P^-+R).\]

Thus the fraction of the innovation used is a model prediction. When sensors are noisy the gain falls. When the model permits rapid drift, uncertainty grows between readings, giving the new measurement a larger role. These are variances, so a stated standard deviation must be squared first.

A reading with units and a repeated reading

A prior says \(20.0\pm2.0\) °C (one standard deviation), with negligible intervening drift. A sensor of standard deviation 1 °C reads 23 °C. The gain is \(4/(4+1)=0.8\), giving \(22.4\) °C and variance \(0.8\) °C². A second independent reading of 23 °C gives gain \(0.8/1.8\) and mean \(22.6667\) °C. Treating a duplicate record as independent would falsely claim the same information gain.

In a long random walk with \(Q=0.25\) and \(R=1\) in squared units, a steady posterior variance satisfies \(P=(P+Q)R/(P+Q+R)\). Rearrangement gives \(P^2+QP-QR=0\), so the physically positive root is about 0.3904. New uncertainty replenishes the information squeezed out by measurement.

Predicting a new measurement when the fitted parameters are correlated

Inverse problems and least squares

Propagate the full posterior covariance to a quantity of interest, distinguish latent and measured uncertainty and select the observation that reduces the uncertainty of the actual prediction.

One model supplies many possible targets

For parameters \(\theta\) with posterior mean \(m\) and covariance \(C\), a linear target \(s=h^T\theta\) has mean \(h^Tm\). Subtract the mean and expand the quadratic product to obtain \(\operatorname{Var}(s)=h^TCh\). An independent sensor with variance \(R\) changes the variance of a future measured value to \(h^TCh+R\). Repeated fits of noisy datasets and repeated physical measurements are distinct sources of variation.

A candidate new observation \(z=a^T\theta+\epsilon\) with variance \(R_a\) has covariance \(h^TCa\) with the target. For a Gaussian posterior the conditional covariance update is \(C_+=C-Caa^TC/(a^TCa+R_a)\), found either by completing the square or projecting out the optimal multiple of the innovation. The target variance decreases by

\[\Delta\operatorname{Var}(s)=\frac{(h^TCa)^2}{a^TCa+R_a}.\]

A precise sum and an uncertain difference

Let \(C\) have diagonal entries 1 and correlation \(-0.9\). A target \(h=(1,1)\) has variance \(2-1.8=0.2\). The difference \(h=(1,-1)\) has variance 3.8. Ignoring correlations would predict 2 for both. With sensor variance 0.25, a newly measured sum has variance 0.45 although the latent sum variance remains 0.2.

For the target sum, measuring the first coordinate with variance 0.25 reduces variance by \((1-0.9)^2/(1+0.25)=0.008\). Measuring the sum itself at the same variance reduces it by \(0.2^2/(0.2+0.25)=0.0889\). These two sensors have differently normalised sensitivity vectors. Their stated noise levels refer to their own output units and must be calibrated before a cost comparison.

For a fairer geometric comparison, restrict both measurement and prediction directions to unit length. Fix the target along the normalised sum. Sweep candidate observation angles and read the remaining prediction variance. Change the parameter correlation: when does a coordinate sensor improve the target appreciably?
