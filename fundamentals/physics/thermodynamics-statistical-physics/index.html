    How the course fits together

    1. Chapters 1–4

      Describe a system by its equilibrium state, then keep accounts of energy exchanged as heat and work. Entropy selects accessible changes. Counting microscopic states explains why two bodies in contact approach a common temperature.

    2. Chapters 5–8

      Build a partition sum from permitted energies to predict averages. Particle statistics change the occupancy of those levels. Following moving particles and repeated collisions then connects equilibrium averages to transport rates.

    3. Chapters 9–12

      Apply state counting to photons, lattice vibrations and chemical composition. Finally add a finite transfer rate to the equilibrium description and predict both the direction of change and the time taken to relax.

    Further chapters and interactive tutorials

    1. How a finite heat bath changes a fluctuation

      A tiny sample exchanges heat with its mounting plate inside an insulated chamber. A thermometer samples the same mean temperature whether the plate is large or small. Can the time record of the sample energy reveal the size of the plate?

    2. Counting occupied sites on a surface

      A surface with \(M\) identical sites exchanges particles with a gas. As the gas chemical potential rises, the coverage grows and eventually levels off. The same measurement also predicts the magnitude of number fluctuations, without estimating a time-dependent sticking rate.

    3. How a pair potential changes the gas pressure

      A low-density gas can measure its own intermolecular forces. At a prescribed temperature the first deviation from ideal behaviour is a volume. Its sign tells us whether overlap exclusion or attraction makes the larger contribution.

    4. Why two coexisting phases share a tangent

      A material prepared with an intermediate composition can separate into two regions of different compositions. Its total composition is conserved. How can a free-energy graph predict both final compositions and the amount of each phase?

    5. Extracting a length from thermal spin fluctuations

      A one-dimensional row of magnetic moments favours local alignment. At low temperature a snapshot can contain a long row of identical spins. An exact calculation distinguishes a large correlated domain from a thermodynamic phase with permanent order.

    6. Why only a narrow shell of a Fermi gas carries heat

      Heating a filled Fermi sea does not give every particle a classical thermal energy. Deep below the chemical potential, nearby states are already occupied. Quantifying the accessible states predicts both the low-temperature heat capacity and the shift of the chemical potential at fixed particle number.

    7. Mean-field ordering, response and the critical point

      Interactions can make a magnet favour a nonzero moment even when the applied field vanishes. Averaging the local exchange field gives a solvable model whose critical predictions can be compared with the exact one-dimensional chain.

    8. Design a thermometer with a countable energy spectrum

      How precisely can a single equilibrated degree of freedom measure temperature? Derive the reading distribution from the canonical ensemble, find the gap that makes it most informative, and analyse a detector that records no excitations at all.

    Applied study

    Connect heat exchange and thermal equilibrium to energy conservation, entropy and microstates.

    Which temperature will two bodies reach?

    Touch two metal blocks together inside an insulated container. Their final temperature is constrained by conservation of energy. That law alone also permits the hot block to heat up at the expense of the cold one. A second idea, entropy, selects the direction we actually observe. A third, thermal conductance, sets the time it takes.

    Assume that each block is internally close to equilibrium and has a constant total heat capacity \(C_i\) in J/K. The blocks exchange heat but do no external work. A transfer \(dQ\) from block 1 to block 2 produces \(C_1dT_1=-dQ\) and \(C_2dT_2=dQ\). Adding and integrating gives

    \[C_1T_1+C_2T_2=\text{constant},\]
    \[T_*=\frac{C_1T_{1,0}+C_2T_{2,0}}{C_1+C_2}.\]

    The second expression assumes the bodies have reached a common temperature. A larger heat capacity puts more weight on that block’s starting temperature.

    Count the ways to share energy

    At fixed total energy \(U\), suppose block 1 has energy \(U_1\). Weakly coupled blocks have \(\Omega_1(U_1)\Omega_2(U-U_1)\) accessible combinations of microscopic states. For an isolated equilibrium system with equally likely microstates, this multiplicity determines the probability of an energy partition. Taking \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\) turns the product into a sum. At its maximum

    \[\frac{dS_{\rm total}}{dU_1}=\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}=0,\]
    \[\frac{1}{T_i}=\frac{\partial S_i}{\partial U_i}.\]

    Positive heat capacities make the curvature negative, since \(\partial^2S_i/\partial U_i^2=-1/(C_iT_i^2)\). Equal temperature thus maximises total entropy. Before equilibrium a small transfer from hot to cold increases entropy by \(dQ(1/T_{\rm cold}-1/T_{\rm hot})>0\). Temperatures here must be absolute.

    From an isolated pair to a canonical ensemble

    When a small system contacts a much larger reservoir, its temperature is effectively set by the reservoir. The chance of observing a particular microstate of energy \(E_j\) is proportional to the number of reservoir states at energy \(U-E_j\). Expand the reservoir entropy about \(U\):

    \[S_R(U-E_j)\simeq S_R(U)-E_j/T.\]

    Since multiplicity is exponential in entropy, the probability becomes

    \[p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z,\]

    with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) and \(Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\).

    For one unit with levels \(0\) and \(\Delta\),

    \[Z=1+e^{-\beta\Delta}.\]

    If \(\Delta=k_BT\ln 3\), the excited state has probability \(1/4\) and mean energy is \(\Delta/4\). Raising temperature at fixed gap drives the two probabilities toward one half. This microscopic account relates heat capacity to the response of mean energy to temperature.

    Same change of state, different heat and work

    For a closed system the first law reads \(dU=\delta Q-\delta W\), with work positive when the system does it. Internal energy and entropy are state functions. Heat and work depend on the path. To see this, compare two ways for one mole of ideal gas initially at 300 K to expand to twice its volume.

    During reversible isothermal expansion, \(p=RT/V\) and \(W=\int p\,dV=RT\ln2=1.73\) kJ. The ideal gas’s internal energy depends only on temperature, so \(Q=W\). Free expansion into an evacuated chamber inside an insulated shell instead gives

    \[W=Q=0.\]

    The final temperature is again 300 K. Both paths give the same gas entropy increase \(R\ln2=5.76\) J/K. In the reversible case the reservoir loses this much entropy, while in free expansion the insulated gas produces it internally. In the two-block problem the entropy production likewise measures irreversibility inside the chosen boundary.

    Give the contact a conductance

    Model a short thermal bridge by a constant conductance \(G\) in W/K with negligible heat storage. Its heat flow is \(\dot Q=G(T_1-T_2)\). Subtract the two heat balances to obtain

    \[d(T_1-T_2)/dt=-G(1/C_1+1/C_2)(T_1-T_2).\]

    The difference decays exponentially with \(\tau=C_1C_2/[G(C_1+C_2)]\). Meanwhile the entropy production rate is \(G(T_1-T_2)^2/(T_1T_2)\), which falls to zero at equilibrium.

    The equilibrium temperature is independent of the bridge conductance, provided heat transfer can continue. The same energy balance for a body in contact with a reservoir gives a different time constant because the reservoir temperature stays fixed. Cooling a body in a room.

    A hot block and a larger cold block

    Take \(C_1=500\) J/K at 360 K, \(C_2=1500\) J/K at 300 K and \(G=2.0\) W/K. The common equilibrium temperature is \(315\) K. Block 1 eventually releases \(500(360-315)=22.5\) kJ and block 2 absorbs \(1500(315-300)=22.5\) kJ. The initial heat current is 120 W and \(\tau=187.5\) s.

    To find the entropy change between the initial and final states, integrate \(dS=C\,dT/T\) for each block along a reversible reference path. The sum is

    \[500\ln(315/360)+1500\ln(315/300)=6.42\,\mathrm{J/K}.\]

    Energy transfer cancels between the two bodies. Their entropy changes have different magnitudes because they receive or lose heat at different temperatures.

    Exercises on microscopic and macroscopic equilibrium

    1. Count a small system by hand

    Two groups each contain four distinguishable two-level units. Distribute four identical excitation quanta over the eight units, at most one quantum per unit, with all microstates equally likely. Which partition is most probable? Does it occur every time?

    Count the microscopic arrangements

    If group 1 has \(j\) quanta, the multiplicity is \(\binom4j\binom4{4-j}\). For \(j=0,1,2,3,4\) this gives \(1,16,36,16,1\), summing to 70. Equal sharing is the most probable partition, with probability \(36/70\). A small system shows substantial fluctuations around this partition. In macroscopic systems the relative spread of the energy share is typically much smaller.

    2. Diagnose an energy-conserving prediction

    From the worked initial state a proposed model raises the hot block by 3 K and lowers the colder block by 1 K. Test its energy and entropy changes for an isolated, passive pair.

    Apply both thermodynamic laws

    The net energy change is

    \[500(3)+1500(-1)=0.\]

    But \(500\ln(363/360)+1500\ln(299/300)=-0.86\) J/K to two decimals. The macroscopic change contradicts the second law for a passive isolated pair. An external device moving heat uphill would require a larger system boundary to account for its operation.
