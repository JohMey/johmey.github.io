When does the long-solenoid formula work?

Building a coil from current loops

The usual solenoid formula depends only on turns per metre and current. A short coil, however, can have a noticeably weaker field than that formula predicts. To see where the length enters, start with one circular loop of radius \(R\) carrying steady current \(I\) in air. On its axis, every wire element has a partner across the loop. Their radial field components cancel and their axial components add.

At distance \(u\) from the plane of the loop, each element lies a distance \(\sqrt{R^2+u^2}\) from the observation point. The axial projection of the Biot–Savart law is

\[dB_z=(\mu_0 I/4\pi)R\,d\ell/(R^2+u^2)^{3/2}.\]

Integrating \(d\ell\) around the circumference \(2\pi R\) gives

\[B_{\rm loop}(u)=\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(R^2+u^2)^{3/2}}.\]

What the two ends contribute

Let the coil occupy \(-L/2\leq z'\leq L/2\) and distribute \(N\) turns uniformly over its length. Assume a thin winding, no magnetic core and free space outside. A slice of width \(dz'\) contains \((N/L)\,dz'\) loops. At the centre, sum their fields using the loop result and the antiderivative \(z'/\sqrt{R^2+z'^2}\) for \(R^2/(R^2+z'^2)^{3/2}\). The two limits give

\[B_{\rm centre}=\frac{\mu_0 NI}{2\sqrt{R^2+(L/2)^2}}=\frac{\mu_0(N/L)I}{\sqrt{1+(2R/L)^2}}.\]

With the turn density \(N/L\) fixed and \(L/R\) large, the denominator approaches one. The familiar long-solenoid result applies deep inside such a coil. Holding \(N\) fixed and shortening the coil gives a different limit, the field \(\mu_0 NI/(2R)\) of \(N\) coincident loops.

Field at the centre of a short solenoid

A 200-turn winding has radius 25 mm, length 30 mm and current 1.0 A. The long-coil estimate is \(\mu_0(200/0.030)(1.0)=8.38\,\mathrm{mT}\), with \(\mu_0\approx4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{H/m}\). Here \(2R/L=1.67\), so the finite-length factor is

\[1/\sqrt{1+1.67^2}=0.514.\]

The centre field is 4.31 mT. Much of the field spreads around the ends of this short winding.

Tutorial · Testing a long-coil estimate

  1. An engineer wants the long-solenoid formula to overestimate the actual centre field by at most 5% of the actual value. What minimum length is needed at \(R=25\) mm?

    Worked solution

    The ratio of estimated to actual field is \(\sqrt{1+(2R/L)^2}\). Requiring it to be at most 1.05 gives

    \[L/R\geq 2/\sqrt{1.05^2-1}=6.25.\]

    The threshold is 156.2 mm, so a whole-millimetre design needs at least 157 mm. This tests the single centre point. A requirement covering the ends would need the field at additional positions.

  2. Place the probe at one end of the original 30 mm coil. Integrate the contributions from loops at distances \(0\) to \(L\). What fraction of the centre field reaches the probe?

    Worked solution

    Inserting the new limits into the same antiderivative gives

    \[B_{\rm end}=(\mu_0 NI/2L)L/\sqrt{R^2+L^2}=3.22\,\mathrm{mT}.\]

    Its ratio to \(B_{\rm centre}\) is

    \[3.22/4.31=0.747.\]

    For a very long coil the ratio tends to one half, since turns then extend far away on only one side of an end probe.
