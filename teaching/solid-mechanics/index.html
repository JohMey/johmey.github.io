Stress, strain and equilibrium

For small displacement gradients, the strain tensor is \(\boldsymbol\epsilon=(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^\mathsf T)/2\). In small-strain isotropic elasticity, \(\boldsymbol\sigma=\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol\epsilon)\mathbf I+2\mu\boldsymbol\epsilon\), where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé moduli. Static force balance is \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma+\mathbf b=0\).

A displacement condition constrains motion and produces a reaction. A traction condition sets \(\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\); its surface integral is force. Constrain the rigid-body modes without clamping additional faces. Verify global equilibrium by comparing applied forces and reactions.

Beam theory and a 3D continuum

For a slender prismatic beam under end load \(F\), the Euler–Bernoulli theory gives tip deflection \(FL^3/(3EI)\), with \(I=bh^3/12\) for a rectangular section loaded in its height direction. The reference neglects transverse shear and local end effects.

Static displacement usually converges more smoothly than local stress near concentrated loads or idealised sharp constraints. Apply load as a reproducible face traction and compare stresses at fixed physical locations under refinement.

Worked example and lab

For \(L=0.2\) m, \(b=0.02\) m, \(h=0.01\) m, \(E=70\) GPa and \(F=1\) N, the beam reference is 22.86 µm. The nominal extreme-fibre bending stress at midspan is 0.30 MPa from \(Mc/I\), with moment \(F L/2\) and \(c=h/2\).

  1. Run the clamped-beam notebook and recover the expected sign of displacement and the net reaction force.
  2. Compare tip deflection and midspan bending stress on two meshes. For displacement use the same output point or surface average each time.
  3. Repeat for a more slender beam and discuss whether the difference to Euler–Bernoulli is dominated by mesh error or beam assumptions.

Model the physics in OneSimulation

onesim.physics.solid_mechanics

LinearElasticity uses small-strain elasticity and an elastic material. FixedDisplacement, Traction, TractionFree and BodyForce describe supports and loads. Remove rigid-body modes through physical constraints and evaluate stress inside the domain. Use the cantilever for a force, deflection and mesh-refinement workflow. For thermal strain, couple a temperature field and set a stress-free reference. Inspect stress component ordering before selecting the first normal component or constructing an invariant.

For global dynamics, StructuralOscillator takes mass, stiffness, damping and force. MaxwellRelaxation evolves stress under a strain-rate input, FatigueCrackGrowth uses ParisLaw, HertzImpact uses HertzContactLaw, and CohesiveOpening uses BilinearCohesiveLaw. Initialize each law's state, follow its units and check the relevant quantity such as dissipated energy, contact force, crack length or traction versus opening. Use a resolved time step around rapid contact or softening. The vibration example in Chapter 2 gives a complete extraction and plotting pattern.

6.18.1 Stress relaxation in a Maxwell element

Hold strain rate at zero and initialize stress. The relaxation time equals viscosity divided by Young's modulus.

from onesim.physics.solid_mechanics import MaxwellRelaxation

domain = LumpedDomain()
model = MaxwellRelaxation(
    domain=domain.system,
    youngs_modulus=4,
    viscosity=2,
    strain_rate=0,
)
t, data = history_case(
    domain,
    model,
    dict(stress=1.0),
    ("stress",),
    end=2,
    dt=0.01,
)
np.testing.assert_allclose(data["stress"], np.exp(-2 * t), atol=1e-7)
fig = history_plot("Stress relaxation in a Maxwell element", model, t, data)
plt.show()

6.18.2 Paris-law crack growth

Use a constant stress range and cycling frequency. At exponent two the stress-intensity law gives exponential crack growth for a fixed geometry factor.

from onesim.physics.solid_mechanics import FatigueCrackGrowth, ParisLaw

domain = LumpedDomain()
model = FatigueCrackGrowth(
    domain=domain.system,
    stress_range=1,
    frequency=2,
    growth_law=ParisLaw(coefficient=0.02, exponent=2),
)
t, data = history_case(
    domain,
    model,
    dict(crack_length=0.3),
    ("crack_length",),
    end=2,
    dt=0.01,
)
np.testing.assert_allclose(
    data["crack_length"], 0.3 * np.exp(0.04 * np.pi * t), atol=1e-9
)
fig = history_plot("Paris-law crack growth", model, t, data)
plt.show()

6.18.3 Elastic Hertz contact pulse

Initialize positive approach velocity at first contact. The force follows positive indentation to the three-halves power and the isolated energy stays nearly constant.

from onesim.physics.solid_mechanics import HertzImpact, HertzContactLaw

domain = LumpedDomain()
model = HertzImpact(
    domain=domain.system,
    reduced_mass=2,
    contact_law=HertzContactLaw(effective_modulus=3, effective_radius=0.25),
)
t, data = history_case(
    domain,
    model,
    dict(indentation=0.0, approach_velocity=0.4),
    ("indentation", "contact_force", "mechanical_energy"),
    end=2,
    dt=0.005,
)
np.testing.assert_allclose(
    data["contact_force"], 2 * np.maximum(data["indentation"], 0) ** 1.5, atol=1e-10
)
fig = history_plot("Elastic Hertz contact pulse", model, t, data)
plt.show()

6.18.4 Traction and damage in cohesive opening

Drive the opening monotonically through the elastic and softening regimes. The work integral accumulates from the traction and the prescribed opening rate.

from onesim.physics.solid_mechanics import CohesiveOpening, BilinearCohesiveLaw

domain = LumpedDomain()
model = CohesiveOpening(
    domain=domain.system,
    opening_rate=0.3,
    cohesive_law=BilinearCohesiveLaw(
        peak_traction=2, onset_opening=0.2, final_opening=1
    ),
)
t, data = history_case(
    domain,
    model,
    dict(opening=0.0, work_per_area=0.0),
    ("opening", "traction", "damage"),
    end=4,
    dt=0.01,
)
assert data["damage"][-1] > data["damage"][0] and data["traction"].max() > 1.9
fig = history_plot("Traction and damage in cohesive opening", model, t, data)
plt.show()
