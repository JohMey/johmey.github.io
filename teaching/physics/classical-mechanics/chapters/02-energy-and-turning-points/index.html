Potential energy and turning points

Use a potential-energy curve to distinguish allowed motion, local stability and escape over a barrier.

Chapter 2 of 12

Replace time with position

A small cart travels through a shaped track or along a nonlinear spring. Its acceleration changes continuously with position, yet one can often predict where it will reverse direction before solving for its position as a function of time. Consider a single coordinate \(x\) in an inertial frame, a constant mass and a force that depends on position alone. Then \(F=m\ddot x\) times \(\dot x\) gives \(F(x)\dot x=d(m\dot x^2/2)/dt\). Define the potential difference by \(U(x)-U(x_0)=-\int_{x_0}^xF(s)\,ds\). It follows that \(E=m\dot x^2/2+U(x)\) is constant.

The zero of \(U\) is conventional. Turning points and speed depend on differences, so adding the same constant to \(E\) and \(U\) has no observable consequence. Allowed positions satisfy \(U(x)\le E\). Where the inequality is strict, two velocities have the same energy, one in each direction. Specifying a position and energy therefore leaves a choice of initial direction.

Examine a pair of wells

Take an illustrative bistable spring with potential \(U(x)=A(x^2-b^2)^2\), where \(A>0\) has units J/m⁴ and \(b\) is a length. Differentiation gives \(F=-4Ax(x^2-b^2)\). The equilibrium positions are \(0\) and \(\pm b\). At either outer equilibrium the curvature \(U''=4A(3x^2-b^2)\) is \(8Ab^2>0\). At the origin the curvature is negative. A small displacement from the central equilibrium grows because the force points away from it.

An energy below \(Ab^4\) confines motion to one well, assuming a start there. At higher energy the cart can cross the centre. At exactly the barrier energy, a cart approaching the top from the side slows asymptotically in this frictionless model. Near the top \(E-U(x)\) is proportional to \(x^2\), so the time integral \(\int dx/\sqrt{2(E-U)/m}\) diverges logarithmically as \(x\) approaches zero. A test that checks only \(U=E\) misses this distinction between an ordinary reversal and an unstable equilibrium.

Extract the local frequency

Write \(x=b+\xi\) and expand the potential about the right-hand minimum. Its constant and linear contributions vanish, leaving \(U\simeq (8Ab^2)\xi^2/2\). The equation of motion is harmonic to this order, with \(\omega=\sqrt{8Ab^2/m}\). This curvature estimate uses small displacements compared with \(b\). For excursions sampling a significant fraction of the well, the oscillation need not be sinusoidal and its period can differ substantially.

At an arbitrary energy with adjacent turning points \(x_-,x_+\), a full oscillation takes twice the outward transit time. Thus \(T=2\int_{x_-}^{x_+}dx/\sqrt{2(E-U(x))/m}\). For an ordinary turning point with nonzero slope of \(U\), the denominator vanishes as the square root of distance and the integral remains finite. This expression supplies a direct route from a measured force curve to the period without measuring acceleration.

Interpret a threshold experiment

Let \(m=0.200\) kg, \(A=4000\) J/m⁴ and \(b=0.0500\) m. The barrier height is 0.0250 J and the effective spring stiffness at either minimum is 80.0 N/m. A weak disturbance oscillates at 20.0 rad/s, with period 0.314 s. From the minimum an initial speed of 0.400 m/s supplies 0.0160 J, below the crossing threshold. Solving \(x^2=b^2\pm\sqrt{E/A}\) gives right-well turning points 0.0224 and 0.0671 m. The displacement is asymmetric about the minimum even though the local harmonic approximation is symmetric.

A launch exactly at \(\sqrt{2(0.0250)/0.200}=0.500\) m/s is marginal. Crossing the barrier in finite time requires greater speed in this idealisation. In an experiment, dissipation raises the energy the cart needs at launch. Measuring the speed at several known positions tests conservation along the path and helps distinguish a wrong potential shape from an actual energy loss.

Time measurements give a second access to the shape of the potential. Between ordinary turning points the speed is \(\sqrt{2[E-U(x)]/m}\). The half-period is therefore the integral of its reciprocal over the allowed interval. The integrand diverges at a simple turning point, but only as the inverse square root of distance, giving a finite travel time. At an energy that just approaches a smooth unstable maximum, the divergence becomes logarithmic. Extremely slow passage near the barrier can easily be mistaken for a stopped cart.
