The observer changes the acceleration

Imagine a puck launched directly outwards from the centre of a horizontal rotating table. The nearly frictionless puck moves in a straight line in the laboratory after release. To a camera fixed to the table, it moves sideways. The two descriptions refer to the same sequence of points in space and time, but their coordinate axes evolve differently. A discrepancy in reported acceleration alone does not imply that a new physical interaction acted on the puck.

Let the table rotate with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). During a small time \(dt\), a vector fixed to the table changes by \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A\,dt\) as seen from the laboratory. If a vector also changes its components relative to the table, the two contributions add. Thus \((d\mathbf A/dt)_{\rm lab}=(d\mathbf A/dt)_{\rm rot}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A\). The rule applies to any vector, including the velocity measured with the rotating basis.

Differentiate twice

Permit the rotating origin to translate with acceleration \(\mathbf a_O\). Write \(\mathbf r\) for the position relative to that origin. Differentiating the position once adds the frame velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r\). Differentiating a second time and collecting the contributions yields

\[\mathbf a_{\rm lab}=\mathbf a_O+\mathbf a_{\rm rot}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot}+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).\]

The two equal contributions to the velocity-dependent term arise from differentiating the moving basis of \(\mathbf v_{\rm rot}\) and from the rotating-frame derivative of \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r\). Dropping either loses a factor of two. To solve Newton’s equation for the rotating acceleration, transfer all frame terms to the force side with their signs reversed. For steady rotation at a fixed origin the remaining extra terms are the Coriolis and centrifugal forces.

Distinguish velocity-dependent deflection from a static load

For vertical rotation, the centrifugal force points horizontally away from the axis and has magnitude \(m\Omega^2r_\perp\). An object at rest on the rotating table experiences this term in its rotating-frame balance. In the laboratory a force directed towards the axis supplies its centripetal acceleration. Both descriptions predict the same required friction or tension. By contrast, the Coriolis force vanishes when the rotating-frame velocity is zero. When present it is perpendicular to that velocity, so its instantaneous power in the rotating frame is zero.

A static rotating liquid displays the centrifugal term directly. With gravity downwards and angular speed constant, equilibrium gives \(\partial_r p=\rho\Omega^2r\) and \(\partial_zp=-\rho g\). On the free surface the pressure is constant, hence \(dz/dr=\Omega^2r/g\). Integration gives a parabolic surface with rise \(\Omega^2R^2/(2g)\) from centre to container radius \(R\). With \(\Omega=3.0\) rad/s and \(R=0.20\) m the rise is 18.3 mm. If the container stops suddenly, the preceding static argument ceases to describe the transient flow.

Reconstruct the camera image

Let the puck leave the common origin at speed \(u=0.80\) m/s along the positive laboratory \(x\) axis when the two frames coincide. If the table turns counterclockwise at \(\Omega=0.50\) rad/s, transforming the laboratory position \((ut,0)\) gives \(x'=ut\cos(\Omega t)\) and \(y'=-ut\sin(\Omega t)\). After 0.40 s the rotating camera sees \(x'=0.314\) m and \(y'=-0.0636\) m. The puck lags behind the rotating radial line.

For \(\Omega t\ll1\) the transverse displacement is \(y'\simeq-u\Omega t^2\). Its initial acceleration is therefore \(-2u\Omega=-0.80\) m/s², just the Coriolis term. At launch the centrifugal term is zero because the puck sits at the axis. Later both frame terms contribute. Extrapolating the initial acceleration indefinitely would give a parabola, whereas the exact coordinate transformation yields the rotating trace for any elapsed time that the puck stays on the table.

A further check follows from work. The Coriolis term is perpendicular to instantaneous relative velocity, since \((\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf v')\cdot\mathbf v'=0\). It can turn a track without directly changing speed in the rotating frame. For constant angular velocity the centrifugal term can be derived from the potential \(-m|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2\). A time-dependent rotation also introduces the Euler term, whose work generally cannot be folded into that same static potential. This explains why an experiment on a spinning-up table differs from one performed at the same instantaneous angular speed after settling.
