Chapter 1 · Equations of state and response functions

Use compressibility and thermal expansion to predict what a pressure gauge will record when a material is heated in a closed vessel.

Decide which variables specify a state

An equilibrium sample of one simple phase and fixed composition can be described by pressure, volume and temperature related by an equation of state. A piston experiment can prescribe pressure while changing volume; a sealed rigid vessel instead fixes volume. The distinction matters because the same temperature change can lead to expansion in one apparatus and a large pressure increase in the other. Assume quasistatic measurements so that a single pressure and temperature describe the sample.

An equation of state is a constitutive statement. The ideal-gas relation \(pV=nRT\) treats particles as dilute enough for their mutual interactions to have negligible effect on the measured pressure. An incompressible-liquid approximation often gives an adequate volume balance at a chosen pressure but can make a constrained heating problem ill posed if applied literally. Both small thermal expansion and small but finite compressibility may be needed to compute the pressure response.

Describe a neighbourhood of equilibrium

Treat volume as a differentiable function \(V(T,p)\) near a reference state. Its total differential contains the two independent slopes \((\partial V/\partial T)_p\) and \((\partial V/\partial p)_T\). Define the volume expansion coefficient \(\alpha=(1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_p\) and the isothermal compressibility \(\kappa_T=-(1/V)(\partial V/\partial p)_T\). For ordinary mechanically stable material the latter is positive. The local relation is

\[\frac{dV}{V}=\alpha\,dT-\kappa_T\,dp.\]

Both terms are dimensionless. Expansion has units of inverse kelvin, while compressibility has units of inverse pascal. For fixed volume the pressure slope is \((\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha/\kappa_T\). This relation is a consequence of using the same volume differential. It does not assume a particular molecular model and it remains informative for a liquid for which the ideal-gas equation would give a poor answer.

For an ideal gas at fixed amount, differentiating \(V=nRT/p\) gives \(\alpha=1/T\) and \(\kappa_T=1/p\). Their ratio is \(p/T\), as follows independently from \(p=nRT/V\). At 300 K and 100 kPa, warming a sealed ideal gas by 3.0 K raises the pressure by 1.0 kPa. At fixed pressure instead the relative volume increase is one percent.

Separate intensive, extensive and measured responses

Doubling the amount of material at the same temperature and pressure doubles volume but leaves the two normalised coefficients unchanged. Such intensive coefficients can be compared across samples. A vessel’s own volume, however, can respond to both pressure and temperature. If its expansion coefficients are appreciable, the measured pressure slope describes the coupled sample and vessel. One cannot simply substitute the unconstrained expansion of the sample into a rigid-vessel formula.

To see this, let the vessel’s fractional volume response be \(\alpha_v\,dT+C_v^{\rm vessel}dp\), where its mechanical compliance is positive for an internal pressure increase. Equal fractional changes of vessel and sample give \((\alpha-\alpha_v)dT=(\kappa_T+C_v^{\rm vessel})dp\). A compliant wall reduces the pressure rise, and a vessel that expands just as much as the sample can nearly cancel the temperature signal. Here the symbol for vessel compliance denotes a mechanical quantity and has different units from heat capacity.

Compare liquid and container data

Take a representative liquid with \(\alpha=2.5\times10^{-4}\) K⁻¹ and \(\kappa_T=5.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹ near its operating point. In an ideally rigid container, a 2.0 K increase gives \(\Delta p\simeq1.0\) MPa. The unconstrained volume change at fixed pressure would be only 0.050%. This combination explains how a small fractional expansion can produce a significant pressure response when the fluid is only weakly compressible.

If the container has \(\alpha_v=5.0\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and \(C_v^{\rm vessel}=3.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹, the same warming gives about 0.50 MPa. Inverting a measured slope without the vessel data could thus give a misleading estimate of compressibility. These are local linear predictions. Over a broad pressure or temperature range the coefficients must be evaluated along the path, and crossing a phase boundary can invalidate the single-phase differential altogether.

Chapter 2 · Heat, work and the limits of a cycle

Track changes of internal energy around a gas cycle and derive the temperature bound on the efficiency of a reversible engine.

Put signs on a physical boundary

Enclose a gas inside a movable cylinder. When the gas expands, it can raise an external weight. When heated at fixed volume, it can increase its temperature while doing no boundary work. To fix signs, take heat \(Q\) into the gas as positive and work \(W\) done by the gas as positive. The first law is \(\Delta U=Q-W\). Internal energy is a state property; the other two quantities describe transfer during a process. A cyclic device returns to its initial state, so its net work equals its net heat input.

For a quasistatic expansion, a piston moving distance \(dx\) under external pressure \(p_{\rm ext}\) receives work \(p_{\rm ext}A\,dx=p_{\rm ext}dV\). In mechanical equilibrium \(p_{\rm ext}\) approaches the gas pressure, giving the usual area under a pressure–volume curve. A suddenly expanding gas is not generally uniform or in mechanical equilibrium. Extending the equilibrium pressure formula to an unconstrained free expansion would predict work when no piston or other boundary force actually receives it.

Follow an ideal gas along two paths

For a calorically ideal gas, \(dU=nC_{V,m}dT\) with constant molar heat capacity. Reversible isothermal expansion from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) therefore gives zero internal energy change and \(Q=W=nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\). Revise the process so that the gas is thermally insulated but still expands reversibly. Then \(nC_{V,m}dT=-p\,dV=-nRT\,dV/V\). Integrating and introducing \(\gamma=C_{p,m}/C_{V,m}=1+R/C_{V,m}\) gives \(TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant}\). Temperature now falls because the gas supplies work from its energy store.

The two paths can pass through a common starting state but they generally end at different pressures and temperatures for a given final volume. If one wants to compare work between identical endpoints one must specify a connecting step as well. This prevents a common misreading of a pressure–volume plot: an isotherm and an adiabat through the same point have different slopes and cannot serve as alternate paths to the same later point without additional processes.

Construct a reversible two-reservoir cycle

Let the gas expand isothermally at \(T_h\), then expand adiabatically down to \(T_c\), compress isothermally there and return adiabatically to the original state. If the first isotherm changes volume by a factor \(r\), the two adiabatic relations force the cold compression to change volume by the reciprocal factor. The hot heat input is \(Q_h=nRT_h\ln r\). The magnitude of heat expelled during cold compression is \(Q_c=nRT_c\ln r\). Thus

\[W=Q_h-Q_c,\qquad \eta=\frac W{Q_h}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.\]

The working gas provides one explicit construction. More generally a reversible cycle exchanges entropy \(Q_h/T_h\) with the hot reservoir and returns exactly that entropy to the cold one. For an irreversible cyclic engine between the same two reservoirs, the reservoir entropy change \(Q_c/T_c-Q_h/T_h\) is nonnegative. This gives the same expression as an upper bound on efficiency. The temperatures must be absolute and must refer to the reservoirs actually exchanging heat.

Test a proposed engine

An engine receives 1.50 kJ per cycle from a 450 K reservoir and rejects heat to a 300 K sink. Reversibility permits at most 0.500 kJ of useful work. A measured cycle delivering 0.360 kJ must reject 1.140 kJ by the first law. The working fluid’s entropy returns to its original value, while the two reservoirs together gain \(1140/300-1500/450=0.467\) J/K per cycle.

The energy and entropy balances answer different design questions. Energy accounting gives the amount of rejected heat. Entropy generation explains why some of the available work has been lost relative to the reversible limit. Raising the running frequency can increase power even at lower efficiency, since power depends on work per unit time. The reversible limit describes a thermodynamic bound, and achieving a useful power output additionally depends on finite rates of heat transfer and mechanical motion.

Problem sheet 1 · State variables and energy conversion

Apply the local material response of Lecture 1 and the path-dependent energy balances of Lecture 2.

Correct a pressure thermometer

A sealed cell warms by 4.0 K and the pressure increases by 0.80 MPa. The vessel has expansion coefficient \(4.0\times10^{-5}\) K⁻¹ and pressure compliance \(2.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹. The liquid has expansion coefficient \(2.0\times10^{-4}\) K⁻¹. Infer its compressibility and predict the pressure rise in a truly rigid cell.

Require the two volumes to coincide

The observed slope is \(2.0\times10^5\) Pa/K. Equality of sample and vessel volume changes gives \(\kappa_T=(\alpha-\alpha_v)/(dp/dT)-C_v^{\rm vessel}=6.0\times10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹. In a rigid cell the rise is \(4\alpha/\kappa_T=1.33\) MPa. Treating the original vessel as rigid would have given an apparent compressibility \(1.0\times10^{-9}\) Pa⁻¹, overstating the intrinsic response.

Compare isothermal and adiabatic expansion

One mole of monatomic ideal gas at 400 K expands reversibly to twice its initial volume. Find the final temperatures, work and heat for an isothermal path and a thermally insulated path. For which path is the final pressure lower?

Track work and internal energy

Isothermally \(T_f=400\) K and \(Q=W=RT\ln2=2.31\) kJ. Adiabatically \(\gamma=5/3\) gives \(T_f=400\times2^{-2/3}=252\) K. The work is the drop in internal energy, \(W=(3R/2)(400-252)=1.85\) kJ, with \(Q=0\). Both paths end at the same volume, so the colder adiabatic path also gives a lower final pressure. They are distinct final states, despite the same expansion ratio.

Diagnose a refrigerator claim

A cyclic refrigerator takes 600 J from a reservoir at 270 K and gives heat to a room at 300 K. Find the minimum work. A prototype claims to use 50 J per cycle. Test the claim by computing total reservoir entropy change.

Apply both laws to one full cycle

At reversible operation the equality \(Q_h/300=600/270\) gives \(Q_h=666.7\) J and minimum work \(Q_h-Q_c=66.7\) J. The claimed input gives \(Q_h=650\) J by energy conservation. Reservoir entropy would change by \(650/300-600/270=-0.0556\) J/K per cycle, incompatible with the two-reservoir cyclic description. A hidden heat source or an incorrect energy measurement would need to be included.

Chapter 3 · Thermodynamic potentials and phase boundaries

Select the appropriate potential for a controlled experiment and infer the slope of a coexistence line from latent heat and volume change.

Change which variables are controlled

Consider a closed simple compressible system that can exchange heat with a reservoir and work by moving a boundary. In equilibrium its fundamental energy differential for fixed amount is \(dU=T\,dS-p\,dV\). This expression makes entropy and volume natural independent variables, yet laboratory experiments more frequently control temperature and pressure. A change of thermodynamic potential exchanges one independent variable for its conjugate without losing any equilibrium information.

For the Helmholtz free energy \(F=U-TS\), differentiation gives \(dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV\). Its slope with respect to volume at fixed temperature is minus the pressure. For the Gibbs free energy \(G=U+pV-TS\), the differential is \(dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp\). At fixed temperature and pressure a spontaneous change in a closed sample with only pressure–volume work tends to lower its total \(G\). The external reservoir can exchange heat during that change, so a fall in sample energy by itself would not be the correct selection criterion.

Extract a measurable cross derivative

The entropy of a gas is harder to measure directly than its thermal expansion. Since \(G(T,p)\) is a state function with commuting mixed derivatives inside a smooth single phase, \(-(\partial S/\partial p)_T=(\partial V/\partial T)_p\). This is one Maxwell relation. It converts an entropy response to a derivative accessible from volume measurements. The equality applies to the same material and the same choice of fixed variables on both sides.

For one mole of ideal gas \((\partial V/\partial T)_p=R/p\). Compressing isothermally therefore changes its entropy by \(\Delta S=-R\ln(p_2/p_1)\). Doubling pressure gives \(-5.76\) J/(mol·K), in agreement with an independent reversible-heat calculation. The Maxwell relation obtains the state difference without requiring the actual compression process to be reversible. Only an equilibrium path used to evaluate it has to connect well-defined states.

Derive a coexistence-line slope

For one pure substance the molar Gibbs energy is the chemical potential. At two-phase equilibrium both phases must have the same chemical potential; otherwise transferring matter to the phase of lower value decreases total \(G\). Label the phases 1 and 2. Along their coexistence curve a small change in \(T\) and \(p\) changes their chemical potentials equally. Substituting \(d\mu=-s_m dT+v_m dp\) for each yields

\[\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{s_{m,2}-s_{m,1}}{v_{m,2}-v_{m,1}}=\frac{L_m}{T(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})}.\]

Here \(L_m\) is the molar latent heat for the transition from phase 1 to 2. The slope can be negative for melting when the liquid has smaller molar volume than the solid. For vaporisation the volume difference is usually positive and large at modest pressure. A pressure reading and a thermometer together can therefore constrain a latent heat through measurements of the slope and molar volumes.

Estimate a boiling temperature near atmospheric pressure

For vaporising water near 373 K use \(L_m=40.7\) kJ/mol, a vapour volume of 0.0306 m³/mol and a much smaller liquid volume. The coexistence slope is approximately \(40700/[373(0.0306)]=3.57\) kPa/K. An increase of pressure by 10 kPa therefore raises the boiling temperature by roughly 2.8 K over this limited interval. The dimensional check is J/(m³·K), equivalent to Pa/K.

Over a larger interval, approximate the vapour as ideal, neglect the liquid volume and treat \(L_m\) as constant. The slope becomes \(d\ln p/dT=L_m/(RT^2)\), giving \(\ln(p_2/p_1)=-(L_m/R)(1/T_2-1/T_1)\). This form clarifies why a straight line may appear on a logarithmic-pressure versus reciprocal-temperature plot. Curvature in measured data can carry information about temperature-dependent latent heat and departures from ideal-gas behaviour. Close to a critical point the assumptions behind the simple integrated expression cease to be reliable.

For a substance whose solid is less dense than its liquid, melting can have a negative coexistence slope even though the latent heat of melting is positive. In the Clapeyron relation the sign then comes from the volume difference. Increasing pressure at fixed temperature favours the phase taking less volume. This sign check connects the derivative calculation to an independently measurable density contrast.

Chapter 4 · Counting states and defining temperature

Use a model of discrete energy sharing to derive thermodynamic temperature and distinguish a most probable configuration from one microscopic state.

State exactly what is counted

Consider \(N\) independent distinguishable vibrational modes, all of angular frequency \(\omega\), with a common energy quantum \(\varepsilon=\hbar\omega\). Suppose they contain \(q\) excitation quanta in total. A microstate is an ordered list of occupation numbers \((q_1,\ldots,q_N)\) with nonnegative integers adding to \(q\). Giving two quanta to mode 1 differs from giving the same two quanta to mode 2, while exchanging two identical quanta within one mode creates no new microstate. An additive ground-state energy plays no role in the counting below.

Represent a distribution by writing \(q\) dots and separating the modes with \(N-1\) dividers. The locations of the dividers among all \(q+N-1\) symbols determine the occupations, including a zero when two dividers coincide in succession. The multiplicity is therefore \(\Omega(N,q)=\binom{q+N-1}{q}\). It counts the microscopic realisations of a specified macroscopic energy, not different possible values of that energy.

Allow subsystems to exchange energy

Put two such solids into weak thermal contact but isolate the pair from the environment. For a fixed division \(q_A\) and \(q_B=q-q_A\), weak coupling makes the joint multiplicity the product \(\Omega_A(q_A)\Omega_B(q_B)\). Under the microcanonical hypothesis of equal probabilities for accessible microstates of the pair, this product sets the probability weight for an observed division. The most probable division maximises the product, though for finite systems other divisions retain nonzero probability.

As a concrete small system take \(N_A=N_B=2\) and \(q=4\). Each solid has multiplicity \(q_i+1\). The weights for \(q_A=0,1,2,3,4\) are \(5,8,9,8,5\) with sum 35. Equal sharing is the most probable single division, yet it occurs only \(9/35=0.257\) of the time. Any one specific occupation list still has probability \(1/35\). The distinction between a microstate and a macrostate resolves the apparent tension between equal probabilities and a preferred energy division.

Obtain temperature from an entropy slope

Introduce \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\) so that independent multiplicities produce additive entropy. For macroscopic \(N\) and \(q\), use Stirling’s approximation \(\ln n!\simeq n\ln n-n\) and drop corrections small relative to \(N\). Writing \(U=q\varepsilon\) and holding \(N\) fixed gives

\[S/k_B\simeq(q+N)\ln(q+N)-q\ln q-N\ln N,\qquad \frac1T=\frac{k_B}{\varepsilon}\ln(1+N/q).\]

This derivative defines an equilibrium temperature from how quickly the number of ways to store energy grows. The entropy maximum of two systems in contact then requires equal inverse temperatures, even if the systems have different numbers of modes. Heat flows towards the subsystem gaining more entropy per added energy. The equilibrium criterion concerns equality of slopes; the two subsystems need not contain the same total energy.

Solving the derivative for energy per mode yields \(U/N=\varepsilon/[\exp(\varepsilon/k_BT)-1]\). At high temperature the exponential in the denominator expands to first order, giving energy near \(k_BT\) for one harmonic mode. At low temperature only a small fraction of modes carry excitations. The energy and heat capacity above their ground values are then strongly suppressed.

Connect the count with a thermometer

For a set of modes with \(\varepsilon/k_B=240\) K, an observed mean occupation \(q/N=0.50\) gives \(T=240/\ln 3=218\) K. Packing the same excitations into twice as many identical modes changes \(q/N\) to 0.25 and gives \(T=240/\ln5=149\) K. Equal quantities of excitation energy therefore need not indicate equal temperatures. The reservoir of available modes matters.

These estimates assume the modes have a common frequency and have explored accessible distributions sufficiently for the statistical description to apply. A newly driven single mode could contain nearly all the energy while a thermometer responds differently from the occupation-based estimate. As the sample size increases the spread of typical energy per mode narrows, but finite-time preparation and weak internal relaxation still determine whether equilibrium counting is an appropriate description of a measurement.

The entropy curvature also determines whether sharing energy is stable. Near the maximum, expand the combined entropy to second order in a small transfer between subsystems. A negative summed curvature makes the probability fall on either side of the most likely energy. For ordinary positive heat capacities, each entropy curvature is negative, so the two bodies return statistically towards equal temperature after a small redistribution.

Problem sheet 2 · Potentials, phase lines and counting

Relate the equilibrium derivatives of Lecture 3 to the microscopic state counts in Lecture 4.

Infer a latent heat

At 350 K a pure liquid’s vapour pressure is 80 kPa. Near this point the measured slope is 3.0 kPa/K. Estimate the latent heat under the ideal-vapour approximation and predict the local temperature increase for a 4.0 kPa pressure rise. Identify the neglected material quantity.

Rearrange the coexistence equation

Neglecting liquid molar volume gives \(v_g\simeq RT/p=0.0364\) m³/mol. Then \(L_m=T(v_g-v_l)\,dp/dT\simeq350(0.0364)(3000)=38.2\) kJ/mol. Locally \(\Delta T\simeq4.0/3.0=1.33\) K. A measured nonideal vapour molar volume or appreciable liquid volume would change the inferred heat; both appear in the exact Clapeyron slope.

Count excitations without assigning names to them

Three distinguishable modes share three identical quanta. Find the multiplicity, the probability that the first mode is empty and its mean occupation. Then explain why putting a label on every quantum would answer a different counting problem.

Enumerate by the first mode’s occupation

There are \(\binom{5}{3}=10\) states. For first occupancies 0, 1, 2 and 3, the numbers of ways to distribute the remainder over the other two modes are 4, 3, 2 and 1. The empty probability is \(4/10\) and mean occupation is \((0+3+4+3)/10=1\). Symmetry also requires a mean of one per mode. Distinguishable quanta would attach extra multiplicities to assignments within these same occupation lists.

Read entropy from volume measurements

One mole of liquid at 300 K occupies 25 cm³ and has thermal expansion coefficient \(6.0\times10^{-4}\) K⁻¹. Approximate the isothermal entropy change when pressure is increased by 2.0 MPa. Predict what the sign would do for a material with negative thermal expansion.

Use the Maxwell relation at fixed temperature

The derivative \((\partial V/\partial T)_p=\alpha V=1.50\times10^{-8}\) m³/K for this sample. Hence \(\Delta S\simeq-\alpha V\Delta p=-0.030\) J/K. The estimate treats the measured expansion response as nearly constant across the interval. For negative expansion the same pressure increase gives a positive entropy change. The sign follows the measured derivative and need not be inherited from an ideal-gas analogy.

Chapter 5 · Partition functions and thermal fluctuations

Calculate the occupation, heat capacity and energy fluctuations of a two-level system from a single partition function.

Put a small system in a heat bath

A spectroscopic probe may resolve two energy levels even when a bulk thermometer gives only one temperature. Consider many independent units with ground energy zero and excitation energy \(\Delta>0\). Each unit has two nondegenerate states and is weakly coupled to a much larger bath at temperature \(T\). Weak coupling lets us assign energies to the unit while still permitting energy exchange over the measurement time.

Under the canonical distribution the probability of a microstate \(j\) is \(p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). The factor \(Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\) normalises probabilities. This form comes from expanding the bath entropy about its energy before the exchange: the number of bath states compatible with system energy \(E_j\) is proportional to \(\exp[-E_j/(k_BT)]\). A bath with appreciable temperature change after one exchange would call for a finite-reservoir correction.

Differentiate the normalisation

The mean energy is \(\sum_j p_j E_j\). Differentiating \(\ln Z\) with respect to \(\beta\) inserts a factor \(-E_j\) in the weighted sum, giving \(\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\ln Z\). Differentiating a second time produces \(\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2\), which is the energy variance. Changing variables from \(\beta\) to \(T\) produces the fluctuation relation

\[C=\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T}=\frac{\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2}{k_BT^2}.\]

Hold fixed the external parameters that set the energy levels while taking this derivative. A change of magnetic field or volume can shift the levels and introduce an additional energy response. For independent identically prepared units, variances add. The standard deviation of total energy grows as the square root of the number of units, while its mean grows in proportion to that number. Relative fluctuations consequently shrink as a macroscopic sample is assembled.

Locate the heat-capacity peak

For one two-level unit, \(Z=1+e^{-x}\) with \(x=\Delta/(k_BT)\). Its excited-state probability is \(p=1/(1+e^x)\). Hence \(\langle E\rangle=\Delta p\), the variance is \(\Delta^2p(1-p)\) and the heat capacity is \(C/k_B=x^2e^x/(1+e^x)^2\). At low temperature the excited population is exponentially small and little energy can be absorbed by a small temperature increase. At high temperature the two populations approach one half, so changing temperature redistributes them only weakly. A maximum must occur between these limits.

Setting the derivative of \(\ln C\) with respect to \(x\) to zero gives \(2/x=\tanh(x/2)\). Its positive solution is about 2.40, placing the peak at \(T\simeq0.417\Delta/k_B\). This broadened heat-capacity feature is the Schottky anomaly of a two-level population. No collective phase transition is required to produce it. Degeneracy of either level would change the population ratio and modify the peak position.

Infer an energy scale and observe a fluctuation

Take \(\Delta=12.0\) meV, so \(\Delta/k_B\simeq139\) K. A peak near 58 K is expected. At \(T=139\) K one has \(x\simeq1\), \(p=0.269\), mean energy 3.23 meV and \(C\simeq0.197k_B\) per unit. For a single unit the standard deviation of the energy is \(\Delta\sqrt{p(1-p)}=5.32\) meV. The standard deviation exceeding the mean is possible because most single observations return the ground energy and a minority return the full excitation energy.

For a million independent units at the same temperature, the mean total energy scales up by a million and the standard deviation by a thousand. A calorimeter measuring the bulk value will therefore see a much smoother response than a single-unit spectroscopic probe. If measured fluctuations scale faster than this independent-unit prediction, correlations, common temperature fluctuations or instrumental noise deserve investigation before assigning a microscopic heat capacity.

For a noninteracting collection the total partition function factorizes into one factor per unit. Taking its logarithm converts that product into a sum, so mean energy and heat capacity become extensive. The variance of total energy also grows in proportion to the number of units when correlations vanish. The standard deviation grows only as the square root of that number, explaining how a large specimen can display a reproducible macroscopic energy while its microscopic constituents exchange energy continuously with the bath.

Chapter 6 · Indistinguishable particles and quantum gases

Use single-state occupancy to derive Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions and estimate when classical gas statistics cease to apply.

Decide which exchanges leave the state unchanged

An ideal classical gas can often be treated with Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. Cooling the gas or increasing its density eventually makes wave packets overlap appreciably. For indistinguishable quantum particles, merely assigning names to exchanged particles must not create extra states. In an ideal gas one can instead specify the occupation of each single-particle state. Identical fermions occupy a given quantum state at most once, including its spin label. Identical bosons can accumulate in the same state.

Use a reservoir that exchanges both energy and particles with the system. Its temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\) are fixed. A microstate with energy \(E\) and particle number \(N\) has grand-canonical weight proportional to \(\exp[-\beta(E-\mu N)]\). This follows by applying the thermodynamic entropy differential to a reservoir that loses both \(E\) and \(N\). The chemical potential sets the mean particle number through these statistical weights.

Sum the allowed occupancies of one state

For a single-particle state of energy \(\epsilon\), define \(z_\epsilon=\exp[-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)]\). An occupancy of \(n\) contributes weight \(z_\epsilon^n\). For fermions the single-state grand partition sum is \(1+z_\epsilon\), so the mean occupancy is \(z_\epsilon/(1+z_\epsilon)\). For bosons the sum runs over all nonnegative integers and equals \(1/(1-z_\epsilon)\), provided \(z_\epsilon<1\). Differentiating the logarithm of each sum with respect to \(\ln z_\epsilon\) gives

\[\bar n_F=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad \bar n_B=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.\]

These expressions describe one state, not the total population within an energy interval. To get the latter, multiply by the density of states and integrate. If the exponential is large then both occupancies reduce to \(e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}\). The upper sign suppresses double occupancy while the lower sign enhances occupation near the lowest available energy.

Count the occupied sphere of a cold electron gas

In a large cubic volume \(V\) with periodic boundaries, allowed wave vectors fill reciprocal space with one state per volume \((2\pi)^3/V\) for each spin. At zero temperature the noninteracting electron gas fills all states inside a sphere of radius \(k_F\) and leaves higher ones empty. Including two spin states per wave vector gives \(N=2V(4\pi k_F^3/3)/(2\pi)^3\), so \(k_F=(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}\). For electrons of mass \(m_e\), the Fermi energy is \(\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m_e)\).

With density \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(k_F=1.33\times10^{10}\) m⁻¹ and \(E_F\simeq6.77\) eV. The associated temperature \(T_F=E_F/k_B\) is approximately \(7.9\times10^4\) K. At room temperature \(k_BT\) is small compared with this energy, so only a narrow region around the Fermi surface changes occupation noticeably. A classical equipartition estimate of the electron contribution to heat capacity would ignore this restriction.

Test the dilute limit with a cold atomic gas

A useful scale for translational wave packets is the thermal wavelength \(\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}\). For a single internal state the dimensionless product \(n\lambda_T^3\) compares the volume per particle with the thermal wave volume. Small values support the dilute classical approximation. Near unity the occupancy of low-energy states can become appreciable and the statistics of indistinguishability matter.

For particles of mass \(6.6\times10^{-27}\) kg at 1.0 K the thermal wavelength is approximately 0.88 nm. At density \(10^{25}\) m⁻³, \(n\lambda_T^3\simeq0.0068\). Reducing temperature to 0.040 K at unchanged density multiplies that product by \(25^{3/2}=125\), giving about 0.85. This is a degeneracy estimate, not a prediction of the phase of a real interacting gas. Once quantum statistics matter, particle interactions, dimensionality and confinement can also affect which phase the experiment actually finds.

Internal degeneracy enters these comparisons through the density per available internal state. Suppose the same total particle density is spread equally across two spin states with identical spatial dispersion. Each can fill the same momentum levels independently. In a cold ideal Fermi gas the Fermi wavevector at fixed total density is then smaller than in a fully spin-polarized gas. A measured pressure change after altering spin populations can therefore contain a statistical contribution even if the mass, volume and total particle count remain fixed.

A level with two ways to be excited

Chapter 7 · Pressure and effusion from molecular motion

Use velocity distributions to predict gas pressure and determine why particles passing through a pinhole have a different average energy.

Pressure counts momentum flux

Consider a dilute gas at equilibrium with number density \(n\), molecular mass \(m\) and no mean flow. Molecular diameters are small compared with intermolecular spacing. At a stationary wall an elastic specular collision reverses the normal component of velocity, transferring \(2mv_x\) to the wall when \(v_x>0\). In time \(dt\) only molecules within a slab of thickness \(v_xdt\) can reach it. Thus fast molecules strike the wall more frequently even at the same concentration.

With normalized velocity density \(f(\mathbf v)\) the pressure is \(2nm\int_{v_x>0}v_x^2 f\,d^3v\). Reflection symmetry converts this to \(nm\langle v_x^2\rangle\). Isotropy makes each Cartesian variance a third of total mean square speed, giving \(p=nm\langle v^2\rangle/3\). Combining with the ideal gas equation gives \(\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle=3k_BT/2\). Internal rotations and vibrations can store energy but do not directly carry translational momentum to the wall in this model.

Count particles crossing a hole

A small opening in a thin wall allows the crossing molecules to escape. Its diameter must be small compared with the mean free path, and the gas on the receiving side must be sufficiently dilute to neglect backflow. The outgoing flux per unit area is \(J=n\int_{v_x>0}v_x f\,d^3v\). The positive-half integral of the Maxwellian normal component is \(\sqrt{k_BT/(2\pi m)}\), equivalently

\[J=n\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m}}=\frac{n\overline v}{4},\qquad \overline v=\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}} .\]

The factor of one quarter combines the half of velocity directions facing the hole with their angular projection. The emerging speed distribution is weighted by one extra power of speed relative to the equilibrium speed distribution, since faster particles cross the hole more often. For a Maxwell gas it is proportional to \(v^3e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}\). Dividing its energy-weighted integral by its normalization gives mean translational energy \(2k_BT\) per escaping molecule. A thermally isolated remaining gas can consequently cool as it leaks.

Estimate a molecular leak

Nitrogen of molecular mass \(4.65\times10^{-26}\) kg is at \(300\) K and pressure \(1.00\) Pa. The number density is \(p/(k_BT)=2.41\times10^{20}\) m⁻³ and mean speed is \(476\) m/s. A circular hole of diameter \(20.0\) μm has area \(3.14\times10^{-10}\) m². The emission rate \(JA\) is approximately \(9.0\times10^{12}\) molecules/s. This large count corresponds to only \(4.2\times10^{-13}\) kg/s. The small-hole condition must be checked independently using the collision mean free path.

At equal pressures and temperatures two pure gases have the same number density. Their effusion-rate ratio therefore depends only on inverse square root of molecular mass. If back pressure develops, apply the flux equation separately to both sides and subtract opposing fluxes. A pressure difference across a wide tube requires a flowing-gas model instead.

A pressure gauge attached to the receiving chamber reports a quantity affected by the pumping speed as well as by the leak. Weighing a collected condensate, measuring the source pressure decay in a known constant-temperature volume, or calibrating the pump independently can provide an absolute flux determination. Each method tests a different piece of the chain from measured pressure to inferred molecular rate.

Chapter 8 · Diffusion from a random walk

Derive a transport coefficient from a microscopic model and separate a diffusion length from a relaxation time in a finite container.

A spreading cloud without a mean velocity

Release a narrow band of neutral tracer particles into a stationary uniform medium. Repeated collisions erase velocity memory. At times long compared with the collision time, labelled particles can spread while the mean tracer velocity remains zero. Start with an ideal symmetric random walk on a line. During a time step \(\tau\) every particle moves a distance \(\ell\) left or right with equal probabilities, independently of previous steps.

The concentration in cell \(x\) after a step is the mean of the two neighbouring concentrations before that step. Expanding the left side in \(\tau\) and the right side in \(\ell\) cancels all odd spatial derivatives, leaving

\[\partial_t c=D\,\partial_x^2c,\qquad D=\ell^2/(2\tau).\]

After \(N=t/\tau\) independent steps the variance is \(N\ell^2=2Dt\), confirming the same coefficient without a continuum expansion. The exact prefactor depends on which microscopic step model is used. In an isotropic gas the directional velocity correlations and distribution of free-flight lengths matter; an order-of-magnitude estimate is \(D\sim\overline v\ell_{\rm mfp}/3\). Substituting a fixed one-dimensional step length into this three-dimensional estimate would mix incompatible models.

Why a gradient drives current

Consider the cell boundary between \(x\) and \(x+\ell\). The two crossing populations are proportional to the concentrations on their respective sides. Outgoing minus incoming probability current is therefore proportional to \(c(x)-c(x+\ell)\). Taylor expansion gives Fick's law \(j=-D\partial_xc\). Local conservation \(\partial_tc=-\partial_xj\) recovers diffusion for constant \(D\) and remains useful when the transport coefficient varies in space. The continuum model assumes concentration changes little over one microscopic step.

In an unbounded domain the response to a narrow initial band is Gaussian at late times with variance increasing by \(2Dt\). In a finite closed channel the final concentration is instead uniform. Reflecting ends impose zero gradient, so a prepared concentration modulation \(c(x,0)=c_0+a\cos(\pi x/L)\) relaxes through a single amplitude \(a(t)\). Substitution into diffusion gives \(a(t)=a(0)e^{-D\pi^2t/L^2}\). Spatial wavelength matters quadratically because a broad concentration feature supplies only a weak gradient for the mass that must move.

Measure a coefficient two ways

Suppose a dyed band in water broadens from variance \(0.250\) mm² to \(0.610\) mm² in 300 s, with negligible flow. Taking the difference removes the initial width and gives \(D=0.360/(600)=0.000600\) mm²/s, or \(6.00\times10^{-10}\) m²/s. In a 2.00 mm sealed channel, the longest nonuniform cosine mode then decays by \(1/e\) in \(L^2/(\pi^2D)=675\) s. The prediction connects an open-domain variance measurement to a finite-domain contrast decay.

Bulk drift shifts the measured band centroid and can be removed by centring each profile. Shear, by contrast, can spread the band even after centring. Independent checks of the variance law and of the finite-channel decay help distinguish molecular diffusion from unresolved fluid motion.

Over times short compared with a collision time, individual tracer trajectories are approximately straight and the displacement variance initially grows as the square of time. A fit to a diffusion coefficient should exclude this ballistic region. At long times finite walls can likewise limit the spread.

Chapter 9 · Thermal radiation in a cavity

Count electromagnetic states, derive the temperature scaling of radiation energy and connect a cavity measurement to emitted power.

Photons in thermal equilibrium

An empty cavity with walls at temperature \(T\) contains electromagnetic radiation. The walls absorb and emit photons, so equilibrium does not constrain their number. The chemical potential of equilibrium photons is zero. Each mode of angular frequency \(\omega\) carries a thermal mean energy \(\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) above its temperature-independent vacuum energy. The spectrum follows by multiplying this occupation by the number of modes in a frequency interval.

Take a large box of volume \(V\) and periodic boundary conditions for counting. One state per polarization occupies a reciprocal-space cell \((2\pi)^3/V\). A thin spherical shell contributes \(4\pi k^2dk\) times two independent transverse polarizations. Using \(\omega=ck\) gives the state density per volume \(\omega^2/(\pi^2c^3)\). Hence

\[u_\omega(T)=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}.\]

At low frequency the mean mode energy approaches \(k_BT\) as in classical equipartition. Extending that classical formula to arbitrarily high frequencies would produce an infinite integral. Quantum occupation suppresses these modes exponentially when a single photon costs much more than the thermal energy.

Energy, pressure and flux

Integrate over positive \(\omega\), putting \(x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\). The integral \(\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15\) gives energy density \(u=aT^4\) with \(a=\pi^2k_B^4/(15\hbar^3c^3)\). Pressure follows from radiation momentum: a photon carries momentum \(E/c\), while crossing a plane weights directions by their projected speed. Evaluating the normal momentum flux over isotropic directions introduces \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3\), hence \(p=u/3\).

A small hole in a black cavity admits only outgoing directions. The half-sphere average of the normal speed gives net emitted flux \(cu/4\), which is the Stefan–Boltzmann law \(\sigma T^4\) with \(\sigma=ac/4\). The different factors of three and four describe different angular averages and observables. A wall facing another large black surface also receives thermal radiation, so its net radiative heat loss depends on the fourth powers of both temperatures.

Observe cooling power

A 1.00 cm² aperture faces a large black surrounding enclosure at 300 K. The cavity temperature is 600 K. The outgoing power is \(\sigma A T^4=0.735\) W while incoming power is \(0.0459\) W. The difference, \(0.689\) W, must be replaced to maintain temperature if all other thermal exchanges are negligible. The interior radiation energy density is only \(9.81\times10^{-5}\) J/m³; repeated replenishment at the walls sustains the outgoing flux.

A wavelength spectrometer and a frequency spectrometer plot different spectral densities. To convert use \(u_\lambda=u_\nu|d\nu/d\lambda|\), since both must assign the same energy to corresponding intervals. Merely substituting \(\nu=c/\lambda\) into a density per hertz shifts the plotted maximum incorrectly. The integrated energy and the predicted net aperture power are independent of which spectral coordinate is chosen.

The photon equation of state also predicts a reversible adiabatic temperature change. Write \(U=aVT^4\) and \(p=aT^4/3\), then set \(dU=-p\,dV\). Collecting terms gives \(dT/T=-dV/(3V)\), or \(TV^{1/3}=\mathrm{constant}\). Halving the linear dimension of a cubical equilibrium radiation cavity would double its temperature. This scaling agrees with the shift of every electromagnetic mode frequency under uniform contraction.

Chapter 10 · Vibrations and the Debye heat capacity

Count vibrational modes in a solid and explain why a crystal’s low-temperature heat capacity follows a cube law.

What a solid can store

Consider an electrically insulating three-dimensional crystal of \(N\) atoms, volume \(V\), sufficiently cold that its structure remains fixed and normal-mode frequencies vary weakly with temperature. In a harmonic approximation displacements about equilibrium separate into \(3N\) independent oscillators. Their excitations are called phonons. Phonon number is not conserved when the solid exchanges heat, so each mode has a Bose mean occupation with zero chemical potential. The modes exist because the crystal can deform, including at very long wavelengths.

A model assigning the same frequency to every oscillator predicts an exponentially small low-temperature heat capacity, since exciting any mode would require a fixed nonzero energy. Acoustic vibrations instead approach zero frequency as their wavenumber approaches zero. Even in a cold macroscopic crystal, long-wavelength modes can remain thermally accessible. The key quantity is how many have energies of order \(k_BT\) or less.

Count to a cutoff

Use a continuum approximation with three acoustic polarizations and a common sound speed \(v\). The modes in a shell of wavevectors grow as \(k^2dk\), as for photons. Real crystals generally have distinct longitudinal and transverse speeds. An effective Debye speed averages their inverse cubes. Converting via \(\omega=vk\) gives density \(g(\omega)=3V\omega^2/(2\pi^2v^3)\). The continuum would supply infinitely many modes, whereas a crystal with \(N\) atoms has exactly \(3N\). Choose an upper cutoff \(\omega_D\) to match that count,

\[\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N,\qquad g(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3 .\]

This cutoff is an approximate representation of the lattice at high wavenumber. The low-temperature prediction depends on the long-wave acoustic spectrum and is insensitive to precise details near the cutoff. In a multi-atom cell additional optical branches change the intermediate-temperature behaviour, which cannot generally be reconstructed with a single effective speed.

Two limits and a measurement

The thermal energy is the integral of \(g(\omega)\hbar\omega/[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]\) over the band. Define \(\Theta_D=\hbar\omega_D/k_B\). When \(T\ll\Theta_D\), extend the upper limit of the dimensionless integral to infinity. The resulting energy scales as \(T^4\) and differentiation yields

\[C_V=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3.\]

At high temperature each mode instead approaches \(k_BT\) of thermal energy and the heat capacity tends to \(3Nk_B\). The acoustic model has the correct count by construction. The constant zero-point energy never enters either heat capacity because it disappears upon differentiation.

For one mole of atoms with \(\Theta_D=300\) K, the low-temperature prediction at 10.0 K is \(0.0720\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below the classical \(3R=24.9\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 20.0 K the cubic asymptote predicts \(0.576\) J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Measuring several temperatures and plotting \(C/T^3\) tests the constant coefficient directly. In a conducting solid an electronic term proportional to \(T\) can dominate at low temperatures. Separating that term through a plot of \(C/T\) against \(T^2\) allows the intercept and slope to reveal the two physically distinct energy stores.

Finite size eventually supplies a lower frequency cutoff related to the crystal dimensions, so the continuum counting argument presupposes a specimen large enough to have many wavelengths populated throughout the temperature interval.

Chapter 11 · Chemical equilibrium and reaction extent

Minimize Gibbs energy along a permitted reaction and predict a composition change when the system is compressed.

A reaction has one constrained direction

A closed vessel can change particle species while conserving the total number of atoms of each element. At fixed temperature and pressure the equilibrium state minimizes Gibbs energy. Introduce a reaction extent \(\xi\) in moles and stoichiometric coefficients \(\nu_i\), negative for reactants and positive for products. A small reaction advance changes amounts by \(dn_i=\nu_i\,d\xi\). The Gibbs differential at fixed \(T,p\) therefore gives

\[dG=\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i=\Delta_r G\,d\xi,\qquad \Delta_rG=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i .\]

An interior minimum satisfies \(\Delta_rG=0\). A negative value means that a small positive advance lowers \(G\). Reversibility of the microscopic chemistry is assumed for equilibrium to be accessible. Thermodynamic preference alone does not provide the time needed to reach it.

Activities and the equilibrium constant

For ideal gases \(\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ(T)+RT\ln(p_i/p^\circ)\). The activity is the dimensionless ratio \(a_i=p_i/p^\circ\). Inserting this expression into the reaction Gibbs energy gives \(\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q\), with \(Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}\). The equilibrium value of \(Q\) is \(K=\exp[-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)]\). Standard pressure supplies the reference that makes the logarithm and the equilibrium constant dimensionless even when the total molecular count changes.

For the dimerization \(2A\rightleftharpoons A_2\), begin with one mole of monomer units and define \(\alpha\) as the fraction of units bound into dimers. Then \(n_A=1-\alpha\), \(n_{A_2}=\alpha/2\) and total gas amount is \(1-\alpha/2\) moles on this basis. Partial pressures follow from mole fractions times total pressure. Substitution into the reaction quotient gives

\[Q=\frac{\alpha(2-\alpha)}{4(1-\alpha)^2}\frac{p^\circ}{p}.\]

The ratio of activities is determined by both composition and total pressure. Compression at the original equilibrium composition immediately lowers \(Q\), making the forward reaction favourable. The subsequent association reduces the number of gas particles while conserving monomer units.

A pressure change at fixed temperature

Suppose \(K=0.750\) at the chosen temperature. Let \(y=1-\alpha\). Because \(\alpha(2-\alpha)=1-y^2\), solving \(Q=K\) gives \(y=[1+4Kp/p^\circ]^{-1/2}\). At \(p=p^\circ\) this gives \(\alpha=0.500\), with one half of all units bound and only one third of gas particles being dimers. At \(p=4p^\circ\) the bound-unit fraction is \(1-1/\sqrt{13}=0.723\). The distinction between fractions of conserved units and fractions of gas molecules matters for interpreting a spectroscopic composition measurement.

A temperature change can also move equilibrium. Differentiating \(\ln K=-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)\) and using the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation gives \(d\ln K/dT=\Delta_rH^\circ/(RT^2)\). For an exothermic association increasing temperature decreases \(K\) locally. Pressure changes at fixed temperature leave \(K\) itself fixed under the same standard-state convention. It is the composition-dependent quotient that adjusts to the imposed mechanical conditions.

The distinction between a chemical potential per mole and one per particle is essential in this derivation. Here partial pressures were combined with \(R\) because the extent was measured in moles. Molecular statistical mechanics instead uses \(k_B\) with energies per molecule, but both conventions give the same dimensionless ratio of equilibrium activities. A useful numerical check is to scale every initial amount and the container volume by the same factor at fixed temperature. Mole fractions and partial pressures stay fixed, so the equilibrium fraction is unchanged even though twice as many molecules may react.

Chapter 12 · Entropy production and relaxation

Derive a measurable rate of entropy production during contact between finite bodies and test it using temperature histories.

Two bodies with no external heat exchange

Connect two bodies through a weak thermal contact inside a perfectly insulated enclosure. Assume each equilibrates internally much faster than they exchange heat, so each has a single instantaneous temperature \(T_h\) or \(T_c\). Take their heat capacities \(C_h,C_c\) as positive constants over the range, with no work or phase change. Let \(J\) denote heat flowing per second from the initially hot body into the initially cold one. Energy conservation gives \(C_h\dot T_h=-J\) and \(C_c\dot T_c=J\). This immediately fixes the conserved weighted mean temperature.

A body's entropy change for this model depends only on its successive internal equilibrium states, so \(dS_i=C_i\,dT_i/T_i\). The bridge can carry irreversible heat while that state relation remains valid inside each nearly uniform body. Adding both entropy rates gives

\[\dot S_{\rm total}=J(1/T_c-1/T_h).\]

Positive heat flow down the temperature difference produces positive entropy. Equilibrium sets the thermodynamic force \(1/T_c-1/T_h\) to zero. Thermodynamics constrains the sign of \(J\), yet a constitutive heat-transfer law is still needed to predict how quickly temperatures change.

Identify a rate law experimentally

Suppose \(J=G(T_h-T_c)\) with temperature-independent thermal conductance \(G\). Subtracting the two temperature balances shows that \(\Delta=T_h-T_c\) satisfies \(\dot\Delta=-G(1/C_h+1/C_c)\Delta\). The decaying difference and conserved weighted mean determine both temperature histories. The entropy-production rate becomes \(G\Delta^2/(T_hT_c)\). Near final temperature \(T_f\), the temperature difference decays exponentially while the entropy-production rate decays approximately twice as fast because it depends on the square.

The total entropy increase can also be obtained without integrating this rate. Integrating each state differential from its initial temperature to the common final value gives

\[\Delta S=C_h\ln(T_f/T_{h,0})+C_c\ln(T_f/T_{c,0}).\]

This expression is independent of conductance so long as the enclosure is isolated and the final state is the same. Larger conductance produces entropy faster at early times but shortens the duration of the process. Replacing a sliding-contact resistance by a smaller one changes the temperature record even when the equilibrium entropy gain is identical.

Predict a quantity beyond the equilibrium temperature

Take \(C_h=500\) J/K, \(C_c=1500\) J/K, \(T_{h,0}=360\) K, \(T_{c,0}=300\) K and \(G=2.00\) W/K. Conservation gives \(T_f=315\) K. The initial heat current is 120 W, and \(\dot S(0)=120(1/300-1/360)=0.0667\) W/K. The relaxation time is 187.5 s. The net entropy gain is \(500\ln(315/360)+1500\ln(315/300)=6.42\) J/K to two decimal places.

Use the two-body temperature histories to estimate the initial entropy-production rate and sum changes in \(C_i\ln T_i\) over the record. Doubling the conductance should double the initial rate and halve the measured relaxation time. Extending both runs to the same final tolerance should recover the same total entropy gain. A discrepancy that decreases as the sampling interval shrinks points to time resolution in the estimated initial slope.

At a finite recorded time the entropy increase is smaller than its asymptotic endpoint value. Evaluate the state-function expression at the last recorded temperatures before attributing a shortfall to the numerical algorithm. The remaining difference measures entropy still to be produced during subsequent equilibration.

The final entropy gain depends only on endpoints, while initial rate and time history depend on conductance.

Determine whether a drift is heat leakage

A trace from two strongly insulated bodies has equal capacities of 150 J/K. Initially the measured temperatures are 330 and 290 K. After the difference has vanished both read 309 K. Estimate the apparent net heat exchange with the surroundings. Can increasing internal contact conductance explain it? Give an independent instrumental diagnosis.

Show the energy inventory

For equal capacities the initial arithmetic mean is 310 K and must stay fixed under pure internal heat exchange. Final readings imply \(\Delta U=150(309-330)+150(309-290)=-300\) J. Finite contact conductance can change the approach rate, but its internal current cancels in the total energy balance. A common thermometer drift of \(-1\) K between beginning and end would create the same apparent loss. Calibration against an independent temperature reference, or an enclosure heat-flux measurement, distinguishes these possibilities.

Applied study

Connect heat exchange and thermal equilibrium to energy conservation, entropy and microstates.

Compare the experimental runs

Which temperature will two bodies reach?

Touch two metal blocks together inside an insulated container. Their final temperature is constrained by conservation of energy. That law alone also permits the hot block to heat up at the expense of the cold one. A second idea, entropy, selects the direction we actually observe. A third, thermal conductance, sets the time it takes.

Assume that each block is internally close to equilibrium and has a constant total heat capacity \(C_i\) in J/K. The blocks exchange heat but do no external work. A transfer \(dQ\) from block 1 to block 2 produces \(C_1dT_1=-dQ\) and \(C_2dT_2=dQ\). Adding and integrating gives

\[C_1T_1+C_2T_2=\text{constant},\qquad T_*=\frac{C_1T_{1,0}+C_2T_{2,0}}{C_1+C_2}.\]

The second expression assumes the bodies have reached a common temperature. A larger heat capacity puts more weight on that block’s starting temperature.

Count the ways to share energy

At fixed total energy \(U\), suppose block 1 has energy \(U_1\). Weakly coupled blocks have \(\Omega_1(U_1)\Omega_2(U-U_1)\) accessible combinations of microscopic states. For an isolated equilibrium system with equally likely microstates, this multiplicity determines the probability of an energy partition. Taking \(S=k_B\ln\Omega\) turns the product into a sum. At its maximum

\[\frac{dS_{\rm total}}{dU_1}=\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}=0,\qquad \frac{1}{T_i}=\frac{\partial S_i}{\partial U_i}.\]

Positive heat capacities make the curvature negative, since \(\partial^2S_i/\partial U_i^2=-1/(C_iT_i^2)\). Equal temperature thus maximises total entropy. Before equilibrium a small transfer from hot to cold increases entropy by \(dQ(1/T_{\rm cold}-1/T_{\rm hot})>0\). Temperatures here must be absolute.

From an isolated pair to a canonical ensemble

When a small system contacts a much larger reservoir, its temperature is effectively set by the reservoir. The chance of observing a particular microstate of energy \(E_j\) is proportional to the number of reservoir states at energy \(U-E_j\). Expand the reservoir entropy about \(U\): \(S_R(U-E_j)\simeq S_R(U)-E_j/T\). Since multiplicity is exponential in entropy, the probability becomes \(p_j=e^{-\beta E_j}/Z\), with \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\) and \(Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}\).

For one unit with levels \(0\) and \(\Delta\), \(Z=1+e^{-\beta\Delta}\). If \(\Delta=k_BT\ln 3\), the excited state has probability \(1/4\) and mean energy is \(\Delta/4\). Raising temperature at fixed gap drives the two probabilities toward one half. This microscopic account relates heat capacity to the response of mean energy to temperature.

Same change of state, different heat and work

For a closed system the first law reads \(dU=\delta Q-\delta W\), with work positive when the system does it. Internal energy and entropy are state functions. Heat and work depend on the path. To see this, compare two ways for one mole of ideal gas initially at 300 K to expand to twice its volume.

During reversible isothermal expansion, \(p=RT/V\) and \(W=\int p\,dV=RT\ln2=1.73\) kJ. The ideal gas’s internal energy depends only on temperature, so \(Q=W\). Free expansion into an evacuated chamber inside an insulated shell instead gives \(W=Q=0\). The final temperature is again 300 K. Both paths give the same gas entropy increase \(R\ln2=5.76\) J/K. In the reversible case the reservoir loses this much entropy, while in free expansion the insulated gas produces it internally. In the two-block problem the entropy production likewise measures irreversibility inside the chosen boundary.

Give the contact a conductance

Model a short thermal bridge by a constant conductance \(G\) in W/K with negligible heat storage. Its heat flow is \(\dot Q=G(T_1-T_2)\). Subtract the two heat balances to obtain \(d(T_1-T_2)/dt=-G(1/C_1+1/C_2)(T_1-T_2)\). The difference decays exponentially with \(\tau=C_1C_2/[G(C_1+C_2)]\). Meanwhile the entropy production rate is \(G(T_1-T_2)^2/(T_1T_2)\), which falls to zero at equilibrium.

The equilibrium temperature is independent of the bridge conductance, provided heat transfer can continue. The same energy balance for a body in contact with a reservoir gives a different time constant because the reservoir temperature stays fixed. Cooling a body in a room.

A hot block and a larger cold block

Take \(C_1=500\) J/K at 360 K, \(C_2=1500\) J/K at 300 K and \(G=2.0\) W/K. The common equilibrium temperature is \(315\) K. Block 1 eventually releases \(500(360-315)=22.5\) kJ and block 2 absorbs \(1500(315-300)=22.5\) kJ. The initial heat current is 120 W and \(\tau=187.5\) s.

To find the entropy change between the initial and final states, integrate \(dS=C\,dT/T\) for each block along a reversible reference path. The sum is \(500\ln(315/360)+1500\ln(315/300)=6.42\) J/K. Energy transfer cancels between the two bodies. Their entropy changes have different magnitudes because they receive or lose heat at different temperatures.

Exercises on microscopic and macroscopic equilibrium

1. Count a small system by hand

Two groups each contain four distinguishable two-level units. Distribute four identical excitation quanta over the eight units, at most one quantum per unit, with all microstates equally likely. Which partition is most probable? Does it occur every time?

Count the microscopic arrangements

If group 1 has \(j\) quanta, the multiplicity is \(\binom4j\binom4{4-j}\). For \(j=0,1,2,3,4\) this gives \(1,16,36,16,1\), summing to 70. Equal sharing is the most probable partition, with probability \(36/70\). A small system shows substantial fluctuations around this partition. In macroscopic systems the relative spread of the energy share is typically much smaller.
