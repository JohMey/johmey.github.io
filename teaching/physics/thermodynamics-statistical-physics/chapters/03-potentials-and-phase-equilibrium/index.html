Thermodynamic potentials and phase boundaries

Select the appropriate potential for a controlled experiment and infer the slope of a coexistence line from latent heat and volume change.

Change which variables are controlled

Consider a closed simple compressible system that can exchange heat with a reservoir and work by moving a boundary. In equilibrium its fundamental energy differential for fixed amount is \(dU=T\,dS-p\,dV\). This expression makes entropy and volume natural independent variables, yet laboratory experiments more frequently control temperature and pressure. A change of thermodynamic potential exchanges one independent variable for its conjugate without losing any equilibrium information.

For the Helmholtz free energy \(F=U-TS\), differentiation gives \(dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV\). Its slope with respect to volume at fixed temperature is minus the pressure. For the Gibbs free energy \(G=U+pV-TS\), the differential is \(dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp\). At fixed temperature and pressure a spontaneous change in a closed sample with only pressure–volume work tends to lower its total \(G\). The external reservoir can exchange heat during that change, so a fall in sample energy by itself would not be the correct selection criterion.

Extract a measurable cross derivative

The entropy of a gas is harder to measure directly than its thermal expansion. Since \(G(T,p)\) is a state function with commuting mixed derivatives inside a smooth single phase, \(-(\partial S/\partial p)_T=(\partial V/\partial T)_p\). This is one Maxwell relation. It converts an entropy response to a derivative accessible from volume measurements. The equality applies to the same material and the same choice of fixed variables on both sides.

For one mole of ideal gas \((\partial V/\partial T)_p=R/p\). Compressing isothermally therefore changes its entropy by \(\Delta S=-R\ln(p_2/p_1)\). Doubling pressure gives \(-5.76\) J/(mol·K), in agreement with an independent reversible-heat calculation. The Maxwell relation obtains the state difference without requiring the actual compression process to be reversible. Only an equilibrium path used to evaluate it has to connect well-defined states.

Derive a coexistence-line slope

For one pure substance the molar Gibbs energy is the chemical potential. At two-phase equilibrium both phases must have the same chemical potential; otherwise transferring matter to the phase of lower value decreases total \(G\). Label the phases 1 and 2. Along their coexistence curve a small change in \(T\) and \(p\) changes their chemical potentials equally. Substituting \(d\mu=-s_m dT+v_m dp\) for each yields

\[\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{s_{m,2}-s_{m,1}}{v_{m,2}-v_{m,1}}=\frac{L_m}{T(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1})}.\]

Here \(L_m\) is the molar latent heat for the transition from phase 1 to 2. The slope can be negative for melting when the liquid has smaller molar volume than the solid. For vaporisation the volume difference is usually positive and large at modest pressure. A pressure reading and a thermometer together can therefore constrain a latent heat through measurements of the slope and molar volumes.

Estimate a boiling temperature near atmospheric pressure

For vaporising water near 373 K use \(L_m=40.7\) kJ/mol, a vapour volume of 0.0306 m³/mol and a much smaller liquid volume. The coexistence slope is approximately \(40700/[373(0.0306)]=3.57\) kPa/K. An increase of pressure by 10 kPa therefore raises the boiling temperature by roughly 2.8 K over this limited interval. The dimensional check is J/(m³·K), equivalent to Pa/K.

Over a larger interval, approximate the vapour as ideal, neglect the liquid volume and treat \(L_m\) as constant. The slope becomes \(d\ln p/dT=L_m/(RT^2)\), giving \(\ln(p_2/p_1)=-(L_m/R)(1/T_2-1/T_1)\). This form clarifies why a straight line may appear on a logarithmic-pressure versus reciprocal-temperature plot. Curvature in measured data can carry information about temperature-dependent latent heat and departures from ideal-gas behaviour. Close to a critical point the assumptions behind the simple integrated expression cease to be reliable.

For a substance whose solid is less dense than its liquid, melting can have a negative coexistence slope even though the latent heat of melting is positive. In the Clapeyron relation the sign then comes from the volume difference. Increasing pressure at fixed temperature favours the phase taking less volume. This sign check connects the derivative calculation to an independently measurable density contrast.
