Semiclassical quantization

Use a local wavelength to construct the quantization rule for a smooth well and recognize where that construction breaks down.

A locally oscillating solution

Consider one-dimensional bound motion of mass \(m\) in a smoothly varying potential. In allowed regions the stationary equation is \(\psi''+p(x)^2\psi/\hbar^2=0\) with \(p=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}\). If the potential changes little over a local wavelength, a small interval resembles a free-particle region. Accumulating its local phase produces the action integral \(\int p\,dx/\hbar\). Yet a wave with changing local momentum must also change amplitude in order to conserve current.

Substitute \(\psi=A(x)\exp[iS(x)/\hbar]\). The leading real terms give \(S'^2=p^2\). The next imaginary terms give \(2A'S'+AS''=0\), or \((A^2S')'=0\). Therefore each travelling contribution has amplitude proportional to \(1/\sqrt p\). Equivalently a classical particle dwells longer where it is slow, and the smoothed semiclassical probability mirrors this dwell time. The neglected derivative terms are small away from turning points when \(\hbar|p'|\ll p^2\).

Match through a turning point

At an ordinary turning point \(V(x_t)=E\) with nonzero slope, the local wavelength diverges and the previous criterion fails. Linearizing \(V\) there gives the Airy equation. Its decaying forbidden-side solution continues to an oscillatory allowed-side standing wave with a phase offset of \(\pi/4\). Matching decaying exterior tails on both sides of a well bounded by two such turning points imposes

\[\int_{x_-}^{x_+}p(x)\,dx=\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots .\]

The offset encodes the two smooth reflections. An infinite hard wall instead imposes a node directly and has a different reflection phase. Applying this rule unchanged to a hard box would count its levels incorrectly. For slowly varying barriers the forbidden action also controls exponential attenuation, but the two-turning-point integral above determines the energies of a classically allowed well.

A trap with measurable spacings

For \(V=m\omega^2x^2/2\) let \(x_t=\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}\). Put \(x=x_t\sin u\) in the action integral. The integrand becomes \((2E/\omega)\cos^2u\,du\) between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), yielding \(\pi E/\omega\). The rule gives \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)\), exceptionally exact even for the oscillator’s lowest levels.

For a mechanical trap at frequency 100 kHz, \(E_4=4.5hf=2.98\times10^{-28}\) J, equivalent to \(21.6\) μK on division by \(k_B\). The spacing is \(hf\) or \(4.80\) μK. These temperatures express energy units; no equilibrium ensemble was assumed. As a transfer test consider \(V=gx^4\). Rescaling \(x=(E/g)^{1/4}y\) makes the action proportional to \(E^{3/4}\). Quantization gives \(E_n\propto(n+1/2)^{4/3}\), so successive spacings grow with excitation. At sufficiently high level number the action changes slowly over a wavelength, making the predicted trend more trustworthy than a low-level numerical prefactor.

An independent check on the energy spacing follows by differentiating the action with respect to energy. The integrand vanishes at the turning points, so their moving endpoints add no first-order term. Inside the integral \(\partial p/\partial E=m/p=1/v\). Consequently the derivative of the one-way action is the time for the classical particle to travel from one turning point to the other. Adjacent quantum levels at high excitation then have separation approximately \(h\) divided by the full classical period. Measuring a variation of level spacing with excitation is therefore also probing the dependence of the classical orbit period on energy.
