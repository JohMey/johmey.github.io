A domain becomes part of the Hamiltonian

Constrain a nonrelativistic spinless particle of mass \(m\) to a flat region \(\Omega\) with perfectly impenetrable walls. There is no potential inside. The wavefunction vanishes on the boundary, and square-integrability fixes its overall norm. The stationary equation becomes \(-\nabla^2\psi=k^2\psi\) with \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)\). Thus the Laplacian, together with its domain and boundary condition, determines the spectrum. An acoustic pressure mode at a rigid reflecting wall generally has a different boundary condition and cannot be transferred unchanged.

For a rectangle with sides \(a,b\), write \(\psi=X(x)Y(y)\). Separation and the four zero-value conditions give \(X\propto\sin(n_x\pi x/a)\), \(Y\propto\sin(n_y\pi y/b)\) with positive integers. The energies are proportional to \(n_x^2/a^2+n_y^2/b^2\). If \(a=b\), exchanged index pairs give a degeneracy when unequal. Mildly stretching one side splits such a pair because nodes along the stretched direction cost less gradient energy.

Scaling and counting states

Under uniform dilation \(\mathbf r=s\mathbf r'\) the gradient becomes \(\nabla_{\mathbf r}=s^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf r'}\). All eigenvalues of \(-\nabla^2\) and all energies fall by \(s^2\) in the denominator while nodal patterns simply stretch with the boundary. This result holds for any shape and any level of the empty hard-wall well. A potential with a fixed external length scale would add another term whose scaling requires separate analysis.

For highly excited modes, count the allowed points inside a wavenumber disk. In a large rectangle the spacing is \(\pi/a\) by \(\pi/b\), and only the positive quadrant contributes. Dividing its area by one state’s area gives

\[N(k)\simeq\frac{ab\,k^2}{4\pi}=\frac{A k^2}{4\pi}.\]

The leading counting law extends to smooth or piecewise smooth domains, and the Dirichlet boundary gives a subleading term \(-Pk/(4\pi)\) for perimeter \(P\) under the usual asymptotic conditions. It predicts the general growth of the level count. At small \(k\), discreteness and geometry dominate and an individual ground level cannot be inferred from area alone.

Test an inference about shape

For an electron in a 2.00 nm square, \(\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.03810\) eV nm² and the ground energy is \(0.03810\times2\pi^2/(2.00)^2=0.188\) eV. Doubling just one side gives \(0.03810\pi^2(1/4+1/16)=0.118\) eV. Doubling both gives \(0.0470\) eV. These outcomes distinguish directional stretching from uniform dilation.

Now compare the two irregular wells in the Dirichlet eigenmode experiment. With a physical length unit of 1 nm, their first dimensionless eigenvalues are both about 2.538, giving electron ground energies about \(0.0967\) eV. Predict their energies with a 2 nm unit, then verify the lowest stored level drops to \(0.0242\) eV for the rescaled well while its dimensionless eigenvalue stays fixed. Agreement of a few computed levels within numerical error alone cannot establish exact isospectrality. Known noncongruent isospectral domains show why even a full list of spectral lines need not uniquely specify a boundary.

The mass scaling offers a separate diagnostic on the conversion from spatial eigenvalues. Replacing the electron by a hypothetical particle of double its mass halves every mapped energy at fixed shape and physical size. No spatial eigenproblem has to be solved again because the mass occurs only in the conversion factor.
