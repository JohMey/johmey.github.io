What information does a quantum state contain?

Suppose an electron is confined to a narrow region and a sequence of energy measurements shows distinct lines. To interpret them, distinguish a state from an outcome. In one spatial dimension a pure state has a complex amplitude \(\psi(x,t)\) normalised so that \(\int|\psi|^2dx=1\). The Born rule gives the probability in an interval by integrating \(|\psi|^2\) there. Relative phases affect interference between amplitudes.

An observable is represented by a self-adjoint operator with suitable boundary conditions. The Hamiltonian for a nonrelativistic particle of mass \(m\) in a scalar potential \(V\) is \(\hat H=-(\hbar^2/2m)\partial_x^2+V\). It both sets the possible energy outcomes and generates the time evolution \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\hat H\psi\).

Derive a spectrum from confinement

Take an infinite square well on \(0<x<L\). The potential vanishes inside and hard walls impose \(\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0\). Separating \(\psi(x,t)=\phi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\) reduces the equation to \(-\phi''=k^2\phi\), \(E=\hbar^2 k^2/(2m)\). The left wall selects a sine. The right wall fixes \(k_n=n\pi/L\). The integer starts at one because the zero solution cannot be normalised. Integrating \(\sin^2\) over the well fixes the normalisation

\[\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2mL^2}.\]

Take an electron and \(L=1.00\) nm. With \(\hbar=1.05457\times10^{-34}\) J·s, \(m_e=9.10938\times10^{-31}\) kg and 1 eV \(=1.60218\times10^{-19}\) J, the ground energy is 0.376 eV and the second energy is 1.504 eV. The ground state is lowest in energy because it has the gentlest curvature compatible with both walls.

Separate energy measurements and time evolution

Prepare \(\psi=(\phi_1+\phi_2)/\sqrt2\). Orthogonality makes \(|\langle\phi_n|\psi\rangle|^2=1/2\) for each of these two energy outcomes. An ideal projective measurement giving \(E_1\) leaves the state in \(\phi_1\); an immediate repetition gives \(E_1\) with probability one. Measurements on an ensemble of fresh preparations give mean energy \((E_1+E_2)/2=0.940\) eV.

Before any measurement the two components evolve with different phases. For these real eigenfunctions the density contains the cross term \(\phi_1\phi_2\cos[(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar]\). A position detector can therefore observe a period \(h/(E_2-E_1)=3.67\) fs for the 1 nm well. Each energy probability remains constant. This is the distinction between changing local probability and conserved energy in a time-independent Hamiltonian.

Transfer the measurement rule to spin

A spin-½ atom prepared in the upper output of a Stern–Gerlach analyser along \(z\) enters another analyser oriented along \(x\). In the two-dimensional spin space, \(|+z\rangle=(|+x\rangle+|-x\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The second analyser therefore sends half of a fresh ensemble into each output. Retain only the upper \(x\) beam and measure along \(z\) again. Since \(|+x\rangle=(|+z\rangle+|-z\rangle)/\sqrt2\), half of that selected beam now enters the lower \(z\) output, or one quarter of the original flux. With a second \(z\) analyser directly after the first, this lower output would have been empty. A change of measurement basis has observable consequences for the subsequent statistics.

How much geometry survives in a spectrum?

Inside a two-dimensional hard-wall region the same reasoning gives \(-\nabla^2\phi_n=k_n^2\phi_n\) with \(\phi_n=0\) on its boundary. A membrane fixed along its edge obeys the same spatial equation with \(k_n^2=\rho_A\omega_n^2/\mathcal T\), where \(\rho_A\) is mass per area and \(\mathcal T\) tension per unit length. This correspondence gives a numerical way to find the quantum energy levels of a freely moving particle confined within corresponding walls. Two distinct polygons can have identical lists of these eigenvalues. Mode shapes and local probabilities can still differ. Rigid-wall acoustic pressure modes illustrate how changing the boundary condition changes the spectrum. Standing waves in a closed room.

Exercises on measurement and scaling

1. Predict a local probability

At preparation time, what fraction of the equal, in-phase superposition in the well lies in its left half? What happens after half the beat period?

Integrate the interference term

Each individual eigenstate has probability \(1/2\) on the left. The cross-term integral is \(\int_0^{L/2}\phi_1\phi_2\,dx=4/(3\pi)\), using the difference of two cosines for the product of sines. Thus \(P_{\rm left}=1/2+4/(3\pi)\simeq0.924\) initially. Half a beat later the relative phase has changed by \(\pi\) and the probability is about 0.076. Both energy populations are still \(1/2\).

2. Identify the shifted spectral line

Double the width of the well while keeping the electron mass fixed. How do the lowest energy difference and the superposition’s beat period change?

Relate line spacing and time scale

Each energy scales as \(L^{-2}\), so the gap falls from 1.128 to 0.282 eV. The beat period scales as the inverse gap and grows from 3.67 to 14.7 fs. Scaling every coordinate of a two-dimensional hard-wall model by the same factor produces the same energy scaling.

Experiment · Obtain quantum energies from spatial modes

The isospectral drums notebook solves the Dirichlet eigenproblem on two polygons. It reports lam, the dimensionless squared wave number \(\lambda_n=k_n^2\ell^2\). Its length unit is 0.1 m for the drum. To interpret a geometrically similar quantum well, assign that unit a quantum length \(\ell\) in metres. Read the shapes and eigenvalues from the spatial solve; quantum time evolution and the measurement probabilities above follow analytically. Dirichlet spectra for two different outlines.

Run through the notebook’s results, then add this cell. It converts all ten computed eigenvalues into electron energies. sizes[-1] selects the supplied 0.005 m mesh.

from scipy.constants import hbar, electron_mass, electron_volt

def energies_eV(shape, length_unit_m):
    lam = runs[shape, sizes[-1]]["lam"]
    k_squared = lam / length_unit_m**2             # 1/m²
    return hbar**2 * k_squared / (2 * electron_mass * electron_volt)

print(energies_eV("drum A", 1e-9))
print(energies_eV("drum B", 1e-9))
print(energies_eV("drum A", 2e-9))
  1. Baseline. Assign 1 nm to the length unit in shape A. Check that the lowest dimensionless value agrees with 2.537944 within 0.01%, giving \(E_1\) near 0.09670 eV. The 1 nm unit sets the geometric scale of a polygon, not the width of the one-dimensional well above.
  2. Change the outline. Select shape B at the same scale. Compare the ground energies within 0.01% and inspect the first-mode maps. Equal eigenvalues can coexist with different spatial distributions.
  3. Change the confinement length. Keep shape A and set its quantum length unit to 2 nm. Check \(E_1(2\,\mathrm{nm})/E_1(1\,\mathrm{nm})=1/4\) and verify the ratio for an excited state. No new mesh is required for this exact uniform scaling.
