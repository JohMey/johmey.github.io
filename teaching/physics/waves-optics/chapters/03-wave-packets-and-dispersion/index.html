Wave packets and dispersion

Derive phase and group velocity from a pair of waves, then use a dispersion relation to predict how a finite packet moves and spreads.

Track a crest and an envelope separately

A burst contains many neighbouring frequencies. Individual crests can travel across its envelope as the burst moves past a detector. To see this with the simplest possible signal, superpose cosines with wave numbers \(k_0\pm\Delta k/2\) and angular frequencies \(\omega_0\pm\Delta\omega/2\). Applying the sum-to-product identity gives a rapid carrier \(\cos(k_0x-\omega_0t)\) multiplied by \(2\cos[(\Delta k\,x-\Delta\omega\,t)/2]\). A crest of the carrier moves at \(\omega_0/k_0\), while a maximum of the envelope moves at \(\Delta\omega/\Delta k\).

As the two wave numbers approach one another, the envelope velocity tends to \(v_g=d\omega/dk\). The phase velocity is \(v_p=\omega/k\). These coincide for a straight dispersion relation through the origin, as on the ideal nondispersive string. They can differ when restoring physics, geometry or material response introduces another length or time scale. Their distinction comes from which observable is followed.

Move a localised packet

Describe the packet as an integral of linear modes with complex amplitudes concentrated near \(k_0\). Write \(k=k_0+q\). The phase of each component is \(k_0x-\omega_0t+q(x-v_gt)-\omega''(k_0)q^2t/2\) to second order. The common first term is the carrier. Retaining only the linear term in \(q\) translates the original envelope without changing its shape, since all its Fourier components now depend on \(x-v_gt\).

The quadratic term changes the relative phases within the envelope. A packet spanning a wave-number width \(\Delta k\) samples a range of group velocities of order \(|\omega''|\Delta k\). After a time \(t\) the associated spatial separation is of order \(|\omega''|\Delta k\,t\). When this approaches the initial packet width, a single arrival time ceases to describe its full shape. This estimate concerns a narrow band and small enough bandwidth for the local expansion to describe the dispersion relation. Dissipation can also distort a pulse by attenuating frequencies unequally.

Apply the argument to surface gravity waves

For small-amplitude waves on deep water, neglect surface tension and viscosity. Incompressible, initially irrotational motion admits a velocity potential \(\Phi\) with \(\nabla^2\Phi=0\). A wave that decays rapidly with depth \(z<0\) has a potential proportional to \(e^{kz}e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\). At the surface the vertical velocity follows the elevation \(\eta\) through \(\eta_t=\Phi_z\), while the linearised pressure condition from Bernoulli gives \(\Phi_t+g\eta=0\). Differentiating the latter and eliminating \(\eta\) gives \(\Phi_{tt}+g\Phi_z=0\) at \(z=0\). This yields \(\omega^2=gk\).

Consequently \(v_p=\sqrt{g/k}\) and \(v_g=v_p/2\). The disturbance penetrates through a depth of order \(1/k\). The assumption of deep water means bottom depth much greater than this scale. A finite-depth bottom alters the dispersion and therefore the time predicted for the arrival of a burst.

Predict an arrival and a change of width

Take a packet with carrier wavelength 4.0 m on deep water. Its \(k_0=1.571\) m⁻¹ gives \(\omega_0=3.93\) rad/s, \(v_p=2.50\) m/s and \(v_g=1.25\) m/s. An envelope maximum takes approximately 16.0 s to cover 20 m. A crest travels twice as fast, so it crosses the packet rather than remaining attached to the envelope maximum. Timing successive crests at one point would measure the carrier frequency, while timing envelope peaks at two points measures the group velocity.

For a spread \(\Delta k=0.20\) m⁻¹ the magnitude of the curvature is \(|\omega''|=v_g/(2k_0)=0.398\) m²/s. Over 16 s the different group velocities produce about 1.3 m of relative separation in this estimate. Comparing that with the initial envelope width is essential. Dispersion that is small in a broad pulse can be a major correction when the initial pulse is sharply localised.

An envelope measurement requires an operational timing definition. For a nearly undistorted narrowband packet, its maximum and its energy centroid travel almost together. Strong reshaping can move the maximum relative to the centroid, so arrival times defined by a threshold crossing can depend on amplitude as well as propagation. Saving the full waveform at two stations allows cross-correlation delays and centroid delays to be compared before assigning a group velocity.
