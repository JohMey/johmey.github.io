Travelling waves and energy on a string

Derive wave speed from the local force balance and determine how much average power a transverse travelling wave carries.

Choose the displacement and the approximation

Strike one end of a stretched rope. A crest moves down its length, yet a marked point on the rope moves mainly up and down. Distinguishing the motion of a disturbance from the motion of the material is the starting point for predicting wave speed. Consider a uniform flexible string under tension \(F_T\) with mass per unit length \(\mu\). Its transverse displacement from a straight equilibrium line is \(y(x,t)\). Assume slopes \(|\partial_xy|\ll1\), negligible bending stiffness and negligible damping.

These assumptions have measurable consequences. To first order a segment of horizontal length \(dx\) retains that length and has mass \(\mu\,dx\). The tension magnitude can be treated as its equilibrium value, while the directions at the two ends vary with the local slope. For larger slopes the tension can change as the string stretches, and longitudinal motion need no longer decouple from transverse displacement.

Balance forces on one small segment

At its right end the upward tension component is approximately \(F_T y_x(x+dx,t)\) and at its left end it is \(-F_T y_x(x,t)\). Their sum is \(F_T y_{xx}dx\) to first order. Dividing Newton’s law by \(dx\) gives

\[\mu y_{tt}=F_T y_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{F_T/\mu}.\]

The equation predicts that a local curvature accelerates material towards straightening the string. Any twice-differentiable shape \(g(x-ct)\) travels right at speed \(c\) because its second time derivative is \(c^2g''\). A second independent shape \(h(x+ct)\) travels left. Their superposition solves the same linear equation, and initial displacement alone generally does not specify how the disturbance splits. An initial velocity field supplies the second piece of information.

For example, lifting a narrow region and releasing the whole string from rest produces opposite-going copies of half the initial displacement on a long uniform string. Launching the same shape exclusively to the right requires \(y_t=-cy_x\) at release. A sequence of still images can reveal the direction of motion, but a single displacement profile usually cannot.

Locate the energy being transmitted

Each segment stores kinetic energy \(\mu y_t^2dx/2\). Stretching it adds length \([\sqrt{1+y_x^2}-1]dx\simeq y_x^2dx/2\), costing potential energy \(F_Ty_x^2dx/2\) at the assumed constant tension. Defining their sum per length as \(u\), direct differentiation and substitution of the wave equation yield \(\partial_tu=\partial_x(F_Ty_ty_x)\). Thus the positive-direction power is \(P=-F_Ty_ty_x\) and \(\partial_tu+\partial_xP=0\).

For a right-moving wave \(y_t=-cy_x\), so the power is positive and equals \(cu\). A left-moving wave carries power in the opposite direction. The signs come from the mechanical work done across a cut. In a standing wave the two powers need not add constructively even where the displacement amplitude is large. An average amplitude alone therefore measures power only once the propagation conditions have been established.

Compare two launches

Let \(F_T=100\) N, \(\mu=0.010\) kg/m and frequency \(f=20.0\) Hz. The wave speed is 100 m/s and wavelength is 5.0 m. A sinusoidal right-moving displacement of peak amplitude \(A=3.0\) mm has peak transverse material speed \(2\pi f A=0.377\) m/s. The largest slope is \(2\pi A/\lambda=0.00377\), supporting the small-slope approximation.

The mean power is \(\mu c(2\pi f A)^2/2=0.0711\) W. Doubling the amplitude at the same frequency quadruples mean power while keeping the propagation speed fixed. If the tension is doubled at fixed amplitude and frequency, the speed and wavelength both increase by \(\sqrt2\) and the mean power increases by \(\sqrt2\). These predictions separate the energy required to maintain a sinusoidal drive from the arrival time of a signal along the string.

The two field measurements needed for instantaneous power are the transverse velocity and the local slope. Measuring displacement alone at one point gives velocity after differentiation, but not the spatial slope. A second nearby displacement probe can approximate that slope if its spacing is short compared with the wavelength. Standing waves illustrate why this matters: a point may move rapidly while the time-averaged transport across it is zero because counterpropagating components carry equal powers.
