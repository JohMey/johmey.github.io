Coherence and interferometry

Use a variable path delay to connect fringe visibility to a source spectrum and estimate how far two copies of a wave can remain correlated.

Compare a wave with its delayed copy

A Michelson interferometer divides one incoming beam into two arms, reflects the beams and recombines them. Moving one end mirror by \(d\) changes the round-trip optical path by \(2d\) in air. With equal arm intensities, identical polarisations and a monochromatic source, the recorded intensity at one output varies from dark to bright and back. One complete fringe passes a detector whenever the mirror moves by half a wavelength.

A real source contains several frequencies or a continuous band. Different frequencies then accumulate different phases for the same delay. A detector averages over many optical cycles and, under the usual stationary-source assumption, adds the intensity contributions of mutually uncorrelated spectral components. Bright fringes of one component can coincide with dark fringes of another. The visibility of the combined pattern can decay even when the beam paths remain perfectly aligned.

Write the measurement in terms of a correlation

Write \(E(t)\) for the complex field amplitude in one arm and \(E(t-\tau)\) for its delayed copy, with optical delay time \(\tau\). Expanding the detected intensity gives two arm intensities plus a cross term proportional to \(\operatorname{Re}\langle E^*(t)E(t-\tau)\rangle\). Normalise the correlation by its zero-delay value to define \(g^{(1)}(\tau)\). For equal arm intensities the contrast of fringes around a chosen delay is \(|g^{(1)}|\), provided the envelope varies slowly across one fringe.

The magnitude expresses temporal coherence over that delay. A fixed imbalance of arm intensities also reduces visibility. If one arm has four times the power of the other, perfect coherence gives contrast 0.8 even at zero delay. To interpret a decrease with delay as spectral information, measure both arm powers individually and check that they remain stable during the scan. Spatial misalignment and unequal polarisation can also reduce the cross term without broadening the source spectrum.

Compute the signature of two spectral lines

Let two narrow lines of equal power have frequencies \(\nu_0-\Delta\nu/2\) and \(\nu_0+\Delta\nu/2\). For each line, the interference term varies with \(\cos(2\pi\nu\tau)\). Their sum is proportional to \(\cos(2\pi\nu_0\tau)\cos(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)\). The fast oscillation gives ordinary optical fringes and the envelope gives visibility \(|\cos(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)|\). Its first zero occurs at \(\tau=1/(2\Delta\nu)\). Beyond that zero the fringes return with a phase reversal.

For a continuous spectral density \(S(\nu)\), the same sum becomes an integral. The normalised correlation is the Fourier transform of \(S\) divided by its integrated power, up to the chosen phase convention. A uniform spectrum across a band of width \(B\) produces a sinc envelope \(\sin(\pi B\tau)/(\pi B\tau)\). Its first zero occurs at \(1/B\). A coherence time therefore depends on the chosen definition of width and on the line shape. A single universal numerical factor would be misleading.

Read an interferogram in the laboratory

For a pair of lines separated by 100 GHz, the first visibility minimum is at 5.0 ps. The optical path difference in air is \(c\tau=1.50\) mm; the required mirror displacement from equal paths is 0.750 mm. The first visibility revival occurs after 1.50 mm of mirror travel. A mirror scan that ends before the first dip will not resolve the two lines reliably from their contrast modulation.

Suppose the average wavelength near the centre is 600 nm. During the first 0.750 mm mirror displacement roughly \(2(0.750\,\mathrm{mm})/(600\,\mathrm{nm})=2500\) fine fringes pass. Counting fine fringes calibrates the path scale, while locating the envelope minimum estimates spectral separation. The two observables use different parts of the same measurement record. This is an example of extracting information about a source through interference without physically separating its colours on a detector.

A drifting mirror can reduce the measured visibility by averaging different optical phases within one exposure. Shorter exposures or a separate stable reference fringe allow this instrumental loss to be distinguished from the coherence time of the source.
