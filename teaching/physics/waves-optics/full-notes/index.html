Chapter 1 · Travelling waves and energy on a string

Derive wave speed from the local force balance and determine how much average power a transverse travelling wave carries.

Choose the displacement and the approximation

Strike one end of a stretched rope. A crest moves down its length, yet a marked point on the rope moves mainly up and down. Distinguishing the motion of a disturbance from the motion of the material is the starting point for predicting wave speed. Consider a uniform flexible string under tension \(F_T\) with mass per unit length \(\mu\). Its transverse displacement from a straight equilibrium line is \(y(x,t)\). Assume slopes \(|\partial_xy|\ll1\), negligible bending stiffness and negligible damping.

These assumptions have measurable consequences. To first order a segment of horizontal length \(dx\) retains that length and has mass \(\mu\,dx\). The tension magnitude can be treated as its equilibrium value, while the directions at the two ends vary with the local slope. For larger slopes the tension can change as the string stretches, and longitudinal motion need no longer decouple from transverse displacement.

Balance forces on one small segment

At its right end the upward tension component is approximately \(F_T y_x(x+dx,t)\) and at its left end it is \(-F_T y_x(x,t)\). Their sum is \(F_T y_{xx}dx\) to first order. Dividing Newton’s law by \(dx\) gives

\[\mu y_{tt}=F_T y_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{F_T/\mu}.\]

The equation predicts that a local curvature accelerates material towards straightening the string. Any twice-differentiable shape \(g(x-ct)\) travels right at speed \(c\) because its second time derivative is \(c^2g''\). A second independent shape \(h(x+ct)\) travels left. Their superposition solves the same linear equation, and initial displacement alone generally does not specify how the disturbance splits. An initial velocity field supplies the second piece of information.

For example, lifting a narrow region and releasing the whole string from rest produces opposite-going copies of half the initial displacement on a long uniform string. Launching the same shape exclusively to the right requires \(y_t=-cy_x\) at release. A sequence of still images can reveal the direction of motion, but a single displacement profile usually cannot.

Locate the energy being transmitted

Each segment stores kinetic energy \(\mu y_t^2dx/2\). Stretching it adds length \([\sqrt{1+y_x^2}-1]dx\simeq y_x^2dx/2\), costing potential energy \(F_Ty_x^2dx/2\) at the assumed constant tension. Defining their sum per length as \(u\), direct differentiation and substitution of the wave equation yield \(\partial_tu=\partial_x(F_Ty_ty_x)\). Thus the positive-direction power is \(P=-F_Ty_ty_x\) and \(\partial_tu+\partial_xP=0\).

For a right-moving wave \(y_t=-cy_x\), so the power is positive and equals \(cu\). A left-moving wave carries power in the opposite direction. The signs come from the mechanical work done across a cut. In a standing wave the two powers need not add constructively even where the displacement amplitude is large. An average amplitude alone therefore measures power only once the propagation conditions have been established.

Compare two launches

Let \(F_T=100\) N, \(\mu=0.010\) kg/m and frequency \(f=20.0\) Hz. The wave speed is 100 m/s and wavelength is 5.0 m. A sinusoidal right-moving displacement of peak amplitude \(A=3.0\) mm has peak transverse material speed \(2\pi f A=0.377\) m/s. The largest slope is \(2\pi A/\lambda=0.00377\), supporting the small-slope approximation.

The mean power is \(\mu c(2\pi f A)^2/2=0.0711\) W. Doubling the amplitude at the same frequency quadruples mean power while keeping the propagation speed fixed. If the tension is doubled at fixed amplitude and frequency, the speed and wavelength both increase by \(\sqrt2\) and the mean power increases by \(\sqrt2\). These predictions separate the energy required to maintain a sinusoidal drive from the arrival time of a signal along the string.

The two field measurements needed for instantaneous power are the transverse velocity and the local slope. Measuring displacement alone at one point gives velocity after differentiation, but not the spatial slope. A second nearby displacement probe can approximate that slope if its spacing is short compared with the wavelength. Standing waves illustrate why this matters: a point may move rapidly while the time-averaged transport across it is zero because counterpropagating components carry equal powers.

Chapter 2 · Impedance, reflection and standing waves

Derive amplitude and energy reflection at a string junction and recover the resonance conditions of a finite resonator.

Make the joint part of the model

A travelling disturbance reaches the junction between a light string and a heavy one held at the same tension. The animation of the junction might show an inverted reflected crest. To tell which sign and amplitude are physically possible, isolate the joint. We assume it has negligible mass, that the strings are tied without slip and that it experiences no externally applied transverse force. The displacement must be continuous and the transverse components of tension must balance.

Place the junction at \(x=0\) and let positive \(x\) point into the transmitted string. Use a harmonic time convention \(e^{-i\omega t}\). In the first string write the complex displacement as \(A_i e^{ik_1x}+A_r e^{-ik_1x}\), and in the second as \(A_t e^{ik_2x}\). Since the joint experiences one oscillation frequency, wavelength changes with the respective speed. A change of wavelength alone says nothing about the reflected power.

Match motion and transverse force

At the joint the displacement condition gives \(A_i+A_r=A_t\). The force condition gives \(F_{T1}k_1(A_i-A_r)=F_{T2}k_2 A_t\). Defining the string impedance as \(Z_j=F_{Tj}/c_j=\sqrt{F_{Tj}\mu_j}\) converts the second equation into \(Z_1(A_i-A_r)=Z_2 A_t\). Under the equal static tensions assumed here, different impedances arise from different linear mass densities. Eliminating the transmitted amplitude gives

\[r=\frac{A_r}{A_i}=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\qquad t=\frac{A_t}{A_i}=\frac{2Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}.\]

The sign of \(r\) describes displacement relative to the incident displacement at the junction. For \(Z_2\gg Z_1\) the junction approaches a fixed end, \(r\to-1\). For \(Z_2\ll Z_1\) it approaches a free end, \(r\to+1\). These limiting experiments check the force signs independently.

Compare amplitude and power

The mean power of a sinusoidal travelling wave is \(Z\omega^2|A|^2/2\). Thus the reflected power fraction is \(|r|^2\) and the transmitted power fraction is \((Z_2/Z_1)|t|^2\). Adding the two fractions gives one for the ideal lossless junction. A transmitted displacement larger than the incoming displacement is consistent with energy conservation when the second medium has sufficiently small impedance.

Suppose both strings have tension 80 N, while \(\mu_1=0.0050\) and \(\mu_2=0.020\) kg/m. The ratio of impedances is two, so \(r=-1/3\) and \(t=2/3\). Of 0.90 W entering, 0.10 W is reflected and 0.80 W travels onwards. Reversing the incident direction makes \(r=+1/3\) and \(t=4/3\) while leaving the reflected fraction at one ninth. A sensor that records only displacement peaks could wrongly interpret the reverse experiment as amplification.

Build a resonator using two reflections

Put reflecting ends a distance \(L\) apart. After a round trip a monochromatic wave acquires propagation phase \(2kL\) and the phases of both reflection coefficients. A repeated wave pattern requires their sum to be an integer multiple of \(2\pi\). With two fixed ends, the two sign reversals add a complete cycle and \(k_n=n\pi/L\), \(n=1,2,\ldots\). With one fixed and one free end there is only one reversal, giving \(k_n=(2n-1)\pi/(2L)\). The boundary conditions, rather than the length alone, determine whether an antinode or a node sits at an end.

For a 0.60 m string with speed 120 m/s the fixed–fixed fundamental is 100 Hz. The fixed–free fundamental is 50 Hz. Replacing one ideal end by a partially transmitting attachment also leaks energy each round trip. One then observes a finite resonance width and a decaying signal after switching off the drive. A resonance frequency tests phase closure, whereas the decay time tests energy escape and internal loss.

A pulse experiment can also distinguish a fixed end from a free end without measuring force. At a fixed end the incoming and reflected displacements must cancel, so an upward pulse returns downward. At a free end the transverse force is zero, requiring slopes to cancel; the displacement returns with the original sign. In both cases ideal reflection conserves energy. A negative displacement reflection coefficient records a phase reversal, even though the reflected energy remains positive.

Problem sheet 1 · Pulses, junctions and resonances

Use the force and energy balances of Lectures 1 and 2 to interpret motion at a string junction.

Select the direction of a pulse

A string has tension 72 N and linear density 0.020 kg/m. At one instant its displacement is \(y=Ae^{-(x/w)^2}\) with \(A=2.0\) mm and \(w=0.10\) m. Specify the initial velocity field that sends the pulse only to the right. Find the material velocity at \(x=w\) and the time for its peak to travel 3.0 m.

Relate the initial velocity to the slope

The speed is \(c=\sqrt{72/0.020}=60\) m/s. A right-moving profile satisfies \(y_t=-cy_x=2cAx\,e^{-(x/w)^2}/w^2\). At \(x=w\), the positive material velocity is \(2(60)(0.0020)e^{-1}/0.10=0.883\) m/s. The crest travels 3.0 m in 0.050 s. Setting all velocities to zero instead produces two pulses in opposite directions, each with half the original displacement profile.

Match a measured reflection

A small junction connects strings under equal tension. A displacement pulse reflects with amplitude \(-0.20\) times the incident amplitude. Find the ratio of impedances, the ratio of linear mass densities and the transmitted power fraction for a long sinusoidal train. Does the reflected displacement sign change when the source is moved to the other side?

Invert the matching equations

Solving \(r=(1-z)/(1+z)\) with \(z=Z_2/Z_1\) gives \(z=(1-r)/(1+r)=1.50\). Equal tension implies \(\mu_2/\mu_1=z^2=2.25\). Four percent of the incident power reflects, so 96% transmits. Reversing incidence makes \(r=+0.20\) and leaves the reflected power fraction at 0.04. A sign reversal records phase; a power measurement alone cannot distinguish positive and negative \(r\).

Identify the boundary by spectroscopy

A string of length 0.75 m has speed 90 m/s. A vibration record contains adjacent peaks at 90 and 150 Hz. Compare the fixed–fixed and fixed–free spectra and predict one lower-frequency peak. Find how the peak spacing changes if the tension is increased by 44% without changing density.

Combine the mode conditions with the speed law

For fixed–fixed ends the frequencies are multiples of 60 Hz, inconsistent with both readings. A fixed–free string has odd multiples of \(c/(4L)=30\) Hz, producing 30, 90 and 150 Hz. The predicted lower peak is 30 Hz if the drive and probe couple to it. Increasing tension by a factor of 1.44 multiplies speed and all frequencies by 1.20. The spacing between adjacent allowed modes rises from 60 to 72 Hz.

Chapter 3 · Wave packets and dispersion

Derive phase and group velocity from a pair of waves, then use a dispersion relation to predict how a finite packet moves and spreads.

Track a crest and an envelope separately

A burst contains many neighbouring frequencies. Individual crests can travel across its envelope as the burst moves past a detector. To see this with the simplest possible signal, superpose cosines with wave numbers \(k_0\pm\Delta k/2\) and angular frequencies \(\omega_0\pm\Delta\omega/2\). Applying the sum-to-product identity gives a rapid carrier \(\cos(k_0x-\omega_0t)\) multiplied by \(2\cos[(\Delta k\,x-\Delta\omega\,t)/2]\). A crest of the carrier moves at \(\omega_0/k_0\), while a maximum of the envelope moves at \(\Delta\omega/\Delta k\).

As the two wave numbers approach one another, the envelope velocity tends to \(v_g=d\omega/dk\). The phase velocity is \(v_p=\omega/k\). These coincide for a straight dispersion relation through the origin, as on the ideal nondispersive string. They can differ when restoring physics, geometry or material response introduces another length or time scale. Their distinction comes from which observable is followed.

Move a localised packet

Describe the packet as an integral of linear modes with complex amplitudes concentrated near \(k_0\). Write \(k=k_0+q\). The phase of each component is \(k_0x-\omega_0t+q(x-v_gt)-\omega''(k_0)q^2t/2\) to second order. The common first term is the carrier. Retaining only the linear term in \(q\) translates the original envelope without changing its shape, since all its Fourier components now depend on \(x-v_gt\).

The quadratic term changes the relative phases within the envelope. A packet spanning a wave-number width \(\Delta k\) samples a range of group velocities of order \(|\omega''|\Delta k\). After a time \(t\) the associated spatial separation is of order \(|\omega''|\Delta k\,t\). When this approaches the initial packet width, a single arrival time ceases to describe its full shape. This estimate concerns a narrow band and small enough bandwidth for the local expansion to describe the dispersion relation. Dissipation can also distort a pulse by attenuating frequencies unequally.

Apply the argument to surface gravity waves

For small-amplitude waves on deep water, neglect surface tension and viscosity. Incompressible, initially irrotational motion admits a velocity potential \(\Phi\) with \(\nabla^2\Phi=0\). A wave that decays rapidly with depth \(z<0\) has a potential proportional to \(e^{kz}e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\). At the surface the vertical velocity follows the elevation \(\eta\) through \(\eta_t=\Phi_z\), while the linearised pressure condition from Bernoulli gives \(\Phi_t+g\eta=0\). Differentiating the latter and eliminating \(\eta\) gives \(\Phi_{tt}+g\Phi_z=0\) at \(z=0\). This yields \(\omega^2=gk\).

Consequently \(v_p=\sqrt{g/k}\) and \(v_g=v_p/2\). The disturbance penetrates through a depth of order \(1/k\). The assumption of deep water means bottom depth much greater than this scale. A finite-depth bottom alters the dispersion and therefore the time predicted for the arrival of a burst.

Predict an arrival and a change of width

Take a packet with carrier wavelength 4.0 m on deep water. Its \(k_0=1.571\) m⁻¹ gives \(\omega_0=3.93\) rad/s, \(v_p=2.50\) m/s and \(v_g=1.25\) m/s. An envelope maximum takes approximately 16.0 s to cover 20 m. A crest travels twice as fast, so it crosses the packet rather than remaining attached to the envelope maximum. Timing successive crests at one point would measure the carrier frequency, while timing envelope peaks at two points measures the group velocity.

For a spread \(\Delta k=0.20\) m⁻¹ the magnitude of the curvature is \(|\omega''|=v_g/(2k_0)=0.398\) m²/s. Over 16 s the different group velocities produce about 1.3 m of relative separation in this estimate. Comparing that with the initial envelope width is essential. Dispersion that is small in a broad pulse can be a major correction when the initial pulse is sharply localised.

An envelope measurement requires an operational timing definition. For a nearly undistorted narrowband packet, its maximum and its energy centroid travel almost together. Strong reshaping can move the maximum relative to the centroid, so arrival times defined by a threshold crossing can depend on amplitude as well as propagation. Saving the full waveform at two stations allows cross-correlation delays and centroid delays to be compared before assigning a group velocity.

Chapter 4 · Optical paths, lenses and images

Use stationary optical path length and the paraxial approximation to predict refraction, focal length and the image formed on a screen.

Travel time chooses a ray

A pulse travels from a point in air to a point in glass through a plane interface. A straight line minimises geometrical distance, but the pulse spends different times per metre in the two media. For a narrow-band wave in transparent, effectively nondispersive media, optical path length is \(\int n\,ds\). The ray connecting two fixed endpoints makes that integral stationary under small path variations. This description requires that the refractive index vary slowly on the scale of a wavelength within each material region and treats interfaces separately.

Let the crossing point slide horizontally by \(dx\). If the angles relative to the normal are \(\theta_1,\theta_2\), the length of the first segment changes by \(\sin\theta_1\,dx\) and that of the second by \(-\sin\theta_2\,dx\). Setting the first variation of optical path length to zero gives \(n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2\). The same condition follows from tangential phase matching. Stationarity gives the ray direction; the reflected and transmitted powers require the wave boundary conditions as well.

Approximate a spherical refracting surface

Take light travelling from left to right. Put the surface vertex at zero and its centre of curvature at distance \(R\) to the right for a convex interface. A near-axis ray reaching height \(y\) from a real on-axis object a distance \(s\) to the left makes a small angle \(y/s\) to the axis. The normal through the centre makes angle approximately \(-y/R\). For a refracted ray directed to an image \(s'\) to the right, its angle is approximately \(-y/s'\). Small-angle Snell refraction applied to the signed differences between ray and normal gives

\[\frac{n_1}{s}+\frac{n_2}{s'}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}.\]

This sign convention takes positive object distance on the incoming side and positive image distance on the outgoing side. A negative solution for \(s'\) means that rays diverge after refraction but their backwards extensions intersect on the incoming side. The algebra therefore distinguishes a real screen image from a virtual image.

Combine two surfaces

For a thin lens in air, treat the image from the first surface as the object for the second. Since their vertices are taken at the same axial position, these intermediate signed distances have opposite signs. Adding the two surface equations eliminates the intermediate image term, leaving

\[\frac1s+\frac1{s'}=\frac1f,\qquad \frac1f=(n-1)\left(\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}\right).\]

A symmetric biconvex glass lens has \(R_1>0\) and \(R_2<0\), hence positive focal length. Rays from a distant object then converge approximately one focal length beyond the lens. The formula assumes thin geometry and paraxial rays. Wide beams can generate spherical aberration, and index dispersion makes the focal length wavelength dependent. Even a perfect paraxial lens forms a diffraction spot when its pupil is finite.

Place a detector and interpret a virtual image

A lens has \(n=1.50\), \(R_1=+0.20\) m and \(R_2=-0.20\) m, giving \(f=0.20\) m. An object at \(s=0.60\) m gives \(1/s'=5.00-1.67=3.33\) m⁻¹, hence a real image at 0.30 m. The central undeviated ray gives transverse magnification \(-s'/s=-0.50\). A 12 mm tall object therefore produces a 6 mm inverted image. In practice a screen scan locates its sharpest plane, and the measured magnification independently checks the distance convention.

Move the object to 0.15 m. The same lens now gives \(s'=-0.60\) m and magnification \(+4.0\). The eye behind the lens sees rays diverging as if from an upright image on the object side, while a screen placed 0.60 m behind the lens finds no sharp real image. As the object passes through the focal plane, the sign changes through infinite image distance. This limiting case is readily checked using a collimated output beam.

A real image can be tested by moving a diffuse screen through its predicted location and recording the sharpness. For a virtual image, extend at least two emerging ray directions backwards to locate their apparent origin. An observer’s eye lens can still create a real retinal image from those diverging rays.

Problem sheet 2 · Packets and paraxial imaging

Use Lectures 3 and 4 to distinguish phase measurements, arrival-time measurements and image positions.

Extract two speeds from one dispersion curve

Near \(k_0=10\) m⁻¹ a guide has \(\omega(k)=120+8(k-k_0)+0.50(k-k_0)^2\) in consistent SI units. Find phase velocity, group velocity and the estimated spread of group velocity across a full wave-number band of 2 m⁻¹. Predict the carrier and envelope travel times across 24 m.

Use the value, slope and curvature

At the centre \(v_p=120/10=12\) m/s, while differentiating gives \(v_g=8\) m/s and \(\omega''=1.0\) m²/s. The group velocities at \(k_0\pm1\) are 7 and 9 m/s, spanning 2 m/s. The phase travel time is 2.0 s and the envelope-centre estimate is 3.0 s. Over that time velocities at the band edges build up a separation of about 6 m; an initially very narrow packet would be noticeably distorted.

Place an object and a screen

A 150 mm thin converging lens must project a 10 mm object as an inverted 30 mm image on a screen. Find both lens distances, then determine how far the lens itself can move to a second sharp position if the object and screen stay fixed.

Solve the conjugate distances

The magnification magnitude gives \(s'=3s\). Substitution in \(1/f=1/s+1/(3s)\) gives \(s=4f/3=200\) mm and \(s'=600\) mm. Object and screen are 800 mm apart. Interchanging the two distances is a second solution because the lens equation is symmetric, giving a lens motion of 400 mm. The new image magnification is \(-1/3\). A check of both sharpness and image size distinguishes which solution was reached.

Estimate a colour focus shift

A symmetric biconvex lens has \(R_1=+0.12\) m and \(R_2=-0.12\) m. Its red and blue refractive indices are 1.50 and 1.52. Locate both foci for a distant object and the axial shift, then explain why a ray calculation of two foci does not by itself give the size of a white-light spot.

Use dispersion in the lensmaker equation

For the symmetric lens \(f=R/[2(n-1)]\). Red focuses at 0.120 m and blue at 0.1154 m, about 4.62 mm closer to the lens. The refractive index is larger for blue, increasing optical power. To predict a spot on one fixed screen one also needs the illuminated pupil, spectral weighting, aberrations and diffraction. A focal shift is a longitudinal distance; the observed blur is transverse.

Chapter 5 · Polarisation and phase retardation

Predict the output of a polariser–retarder arrangement and distinguish changes of polarisation from loss of beam power.

Resolve the electric field into two components

A narrow-band plane wave propagating along \(z\) in a transparent medium has a transverse electric field. Write its physical value as the real part of \((A_x\hat{\mathbf x}+A_y\hat{\mathbf y})e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\). A common phase attached to both complex amplitudes shifts the time origin of the field oscillation. Their relative phase and amplitude ratio determine how the tip of the field vector moves within a cycle.

For amplitudes in phase the field traces a line. Equal magnitudes with relative phase \(\pi/2\) produce a circle, while intermediate combinations usually give an ellipse. A two-component Jones vector describes one fully polarised coherent beam. For randomly changing amplitudes, predictions require averaging intensities or field correlations over the exposure. A single Jones vector cannot represent every partially polarised state.

Project onto a measuring axis

An ideal linear polariser with transmission direction \(\hat{\mathbf a}=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\) passes the component \(\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf A\) and emits a field along \(\hat{\mathbf a}\). Its matrix is the outer product \(\hat{\mathbf a}\hat{\mathbf a}^{\mathsf T}\). A linearly polarised input at angle \(\beta\) therefore transmits a fraction \(\cos^2(\beta-\alpha)\) of the intensity. This is Malus’s law. It applies to the projection of the field before the detector takes a squared magnitude.

Two ideal polarisers at right angles transmit zero for an input aligned with the first. Inserting a third between them at 45° can increase transmitted power. After the middle polariser the direction of the transmitted wave has changed. The first projection passes half the original power and the second passes half of that half, so one quarter reaches the detector. The inserted component changes the state incident on the last analyser.

Transform a phase delay into an intensity reading

A lossless birefringent plate has two orthogonal principal axes with refractive indices \(n_f\) and \(n_s\). Through thickness \(d\), their phase difference is \(\delta=2\pi(n_s-n_f)d/\lambda_0\), where \(\lambda_0\) is vacuum wavelength. In the plate-axis basis its Jones matrix, apart from a common phase, is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\delta})\). The matrix preserves the sum of squared amplitudes. Only the relative phase changes.

Let the incident linear polarisation make an angle \(\beta\) to the fast axis. An analyser crossed with the original input has unit vector \((-\sin\beta,\cos\beta)\) in that basis. Projecting the output onto it gives amplitude proportional to \(\sin\beta\cos\beta(e^{i\delta}-1)\). Squaring yields \(I_\perp/I_{\rm in}=\sin^2(2\beta)\sin^2(\delta/2)\). Thus a dark crossed-analyser setting can indicate an axis-aligned input or an integer number of full-wave delays. One dark reading alone does not identify thickness or birefringence.

Distinguish a quarter-wave plate from a half-wave plate

Take \(\lambda_0=600\) nm and \(\Delta n=0.010\). The thinnest plate with \(\delta=\pi/2\) has \(d=\lambda_0/(4\Delta n)=15.0\) µm. At \(\beta=45°\), equal input amplitudes acquire a relative quarter-cycle delay, producing circular polarisation. A rotating ideal analyser then transmits half the intensity at every angle. With 4.0 mW arriving at the plate, the analyser passes 2.0 mW throughout the rotation.

Double the thickness. The delay becomes \(\pi\), so the two components have opposite signs and the output is linearly polarised along the original crossed analyser. It transmits the full 4.0 mW in that setting and zero in its orthogonal setting. Flat response under rotation does not by itself distinguish circularly polarised from unpolarised light. Adding a known quarter-wave plate before the analyser converts circular light to a linear state but leaves ideal unpolarised light unpolarised, giving an independent measurement.

Rotating the input polarization relative to a plate also checks the orientation of its principal axes. If the input is aligned with either axis, only one component is present and retardation has no effect on the polarization ellipse. The phase remains physically detectable only through interference with an independent reference. For comparable input components both axes contribute and an analyser becomes sensitive to their relative phase. This is why a small alignment error can suppress a retardance measurement even for a perfectly fabricated plate.

Chapter 6 · Coherence and interferometry

Use a variable path delay to connect fringe visibility to a source spectrum and estimate how far two copies of a wave can remain correlated.

Compare a wave with its delayed copy

A Michelson interferometer divides one incoming beam into two arms, reflects the beams and recombines them. Moving one end mirror by \(d\) changes the round-trip optical path by \(2d\) in air. With equal arm intensities, identical polarisations and a monochromatic source, the recorded intensity at one output varies from dark to bright and back. One complete fringe passes a detector whenever the mirror moves by half a wavelength.

A real source contains several frequencies or a continuous band. Different frequencies then accumulate different phases for the same delay. A detector averages over many optical cycles and, under the usual stationary-source assumption, adds the intensity contributions of mutually uncorrelated spectral components. Bright fringes of one component can coincide with dark fringes of another. The visibility of the combined pattern can decay even when the beam paths remain perfectly aligned.

Write the measurement in terms of a correlation

Write \(E(t)\) for the complex field amplitude in one arm and \(E(t-\tau)\) for its delayed copy, with optical delay time \(\tau\). Expanding the detected intensity gives two arm intensities plus a cross term proportional to \(\operatorname{Re}\langle E^*(t)E(t-\tau)\rangle\). Normalise the correlation by its zero-delay value to define \(g^{(1)}(\tau)\). For equal arm intensities the contrast of fringes around a chosen delay is \(|g^{(1)}|\), provided the envelope varies slowly across one fringe.

The magnitude expresses temporal coherence over that delay. A fixed imbalance of arm intensities also reduces visibility. If one arm has four times the power of the other, perfect coherence gives contrast 0.8 even at zero delay. To interpret a decrease with delay as spectral information, measure both arm powers individually and check that they remain stable during the scan. Spatial misalignment and unequal polarisation can also reduce the cross term without broadening the source spectrum.

Compute the signature of two spectral lines

Let two narrow lines of equal power have frequencies \(\nu_0-\Delta\nu/2\) and \(\nu_0+\Delta\nu/2\). For each line, the interference term varies with \(\cos(2\pi\nu\tau)\). Their sum is proportional to \(\cos(2\pi\nu_0\tau)\cos(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)\). The fast oscillation gives ordinary optical fringes and the envelope gives visibility \(|\cos(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)|\). Its first zero occurs at \(\tau=1/(2\Delta\nu)\). Beyond that zero the fringes return with a phase reversal.

For a continuous spectral density \(S(\nu)\), the same sum becomes an integral. The normalised correlation is the Fourier transform of \(S\) divided by its integrated power, up to the chosen phase convention. A uniform spectrum across a band of width \(B\) produces a sinc envelope \(\sin(\pi B\tau)/(\pi B\tau)\). Its first zero occurs at \(1/B\). A coherence time therefore depends on the chosen definition of width and on the line shape. A single universal numerical factor would be misleading.

Read an interferogram in the laboratory

For a pair of lines separated by 100 GHz, the first visibility minimum is at 5.0 ps. The optical path difference in air is \(c\tau=1.50\) mm; the required mirror displacement from equal paths is 0.750 mm. The first visibility revival occurs after 1.50 mm of mirror travel. A mirror scan that ends before the first dip will not resolve the two lines reliably from their contrast modulation.

Suppose the average wavelength near the centre is 600 nm. During the first 0.750 mm mirror displacement roughly \(2(0.750\,\mathrm{mm})/(600\,\mathrm{nm})=2500\) fine fringes pass. Counting fine fringes calibrates the path scale, while locating the envelope minimum estimates spectral separation. The two observables use different parts of the same measurement record. This is an example of extracting information about a source through interference without physically separating its colours on a detector.

A drifting mirror can reduce the measured visibility by averaging different optical phases within one exposure. Shorter exposures or a separate stable reference fringe allow this instrumental loss to be distinguished from the coherence time of the source.

Chapter 7 · Diffraction as a Fourier transform

Derive the far-field pattern from a finite aperture and identify which features control its scale and its sidelobes.

Where a geometrical shadow breaks down

Illuminate a thin screen at normal incidence with a monochromatic plane wave of vacuum wavelength \(\lambda\) in air. Light transmitted through different parts of an opening reaches a distant observation point along slightly different paths. Their phases matter even where geometrical rays would draw a hard shadow. Use scalar diffraction, suited to features much larger than the wavelength and modest angles. Represent the complex field just beyond the screen by an aperture function \(A(x,y)\), incorporating amplitude and phase.

For a far-away observation direction \(\mathbf n\), the propagation distance from aperture point \(\mathbf r'\) is \(r-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r'\) to first order. Replacing the inverse distance in the wave amplitude with \(1/r\) while retaining this linear phase yields

\[U(\theta_x,\theta_y)\propto\iint A(x,y)e^{-ik(x\sin\theta_x+y\sin\theta_y)}\,dx\,dy .\]

The angular field samples a spatial Fourier transform. The neglected quadratic path term produces a phase of order \(\pi a^2/(\lambda r)\) for aperture half-width \(a\). The free-space far-field approximation requires this quantity to be small. A lens supplies a quadratic phase that creates the Fourier pattern in its back focal plane even at a practical laboratory distance.

Add the contributions of a uniform slit

For a very tall slit of width \(w\), study the horizontal cross-section. Uniform amplitude across the width gives an integral of the exponential between \(-w/2\) and \(w/2\). The result is \(w\sin u/u\) with \(u=\pi w\sin\theta/\lambda\). Taking squared magnitude and normalizing at the origin yields

\[I(\theta)/I(0)=\left(\frac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2 .\]

The first zeros satisfy \(\sin\theta=\pm\lambda/w\). A broader opening makes the central spot narrower because a smaller angular change suffices to accumulate a full cycle across it. The oscillating tails arise from the abrupt edges. A gradual transmission taper reduces those sidelobes at the cost of a wider central maximum. Peak brightness additionally depends on the transmitted amplitude and collection geometry.

Measure width in a focal plane

Illuminate a \(120\) μm slit with 600 nm light and use a focal-length 200 mm lens. In the paraxial focal plane \(X\simeq f\sin\theta\). The first minima occur at \(X=\pm1.00\) mm, giving a central width of 2.00 mm. At half that distance \(u=\pi/2\), so the intensity is \(4/\pi^2=0.405\) of its central value. These two measurements separately test angular scale and profile shape.

If the slit is moved sideways, the Fourier shift theorem supplies only a phase factor in the far field, and the intensity pattern remains centred in this ideal arrangement. If it is illuminated at a small angle instead, the incident field carries a linear phase across the slit and the whole pattern moves. This distinction becomes useful when diagnosing whether a displaced focal-plane spot comes from a decentered opening or a tilted incoming beam.

A narrow receiving slit or detector pixel also integrates across part of the pattern. If it straddles a predicted zero, it collects light from either side and reports a positive count even for perfect diffraction. Reducing detector width tests this averaging interpretation.

Chapter 8 · Images through a finite pupil

Separate single-point diffraction, two-point resolution and spatial-frequency contrast for a circular imaging aperture.

A point becomes an extended image

A thin lens focuses a distant point into its back focal plane. An ideal geometrical ray diagram predicts a mathematical point. The finite entrance pupil limits the transverse field that can contribute. Define the point-spread function as the image intensity from a single on-axis point. Restrict attention to an aberration-free, circular, uniformly illuminated pupil, a narrow spectral band and an image region sufficiently small that shifting the source translates the same point-spread function.

The preceding aperture transform now runs over a disk of radius \(a\). For an observation angle \(\theta\) the transverse wavenumber is \(q=k\sin\theta\). Performing the angular integral of the plane-wave phase gives \(2\pi J_0(qr)\). The radial integral then uses \(d[zJ_1(z)]/dz=zJ_0(z)\), so the field normalized at the centre is \(2J_1(qa)/(qa)\). Squaring gives the Airy intensity pattern. Its first zero is at \(qa=3.8317\), hence

\[\sin\theta_1=1.22\lambda/D,\qquad D=2a .\]

Combine two point sources

For statistically independent emitters the fluctuating relative phase averages away. Their point-spread intensities add. The Rayleigh convention places the centre of one Airy spot on the first zero of its neighbour. It provides a useful separation scale for two comparable emitters, though achievable accuracy also depends on photon counts, background and whether the measurement estimates a known model. Two coherent emitters instead add complex fields before squaring. Their relative phase can brighten or extinguish the midpoint.

For an extended incoherent object each point contributes a shifted intensity pattern. Superposition gives convolution of object intensity with the intensity point-spread function. Taking a Fourier transform converts convolution to multiplication. The resulting optical transfer function is the autocorrelation of the pupil in spatial-frequency coordinates. Its support reaches twice the radius of the coherent field passband because displaced pupil copies overlap until their centres are separated by one diameter. In object space the incoherent cutoff in cycles per metre is \(2\,\mathrm{NA}/\lambda_0\), where \(\mathrm{NA}=n\sin\alpha\) and \(\lambda_0\) is vacuum wavelength.

Choose the object-side sampling

Use an air objective of numerical aperture 0.40 at 520 nm. The Rayleigh scale is \(0.61\lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}=0.793\) μm. The incoherent cutoff is \(1.54\) cycles/μm, corresponding to a limiting sinusoidal period of \(0.650\) μm with contrast vanishing at the cutoff. These describe different target geometries. A grating near the cutoff can have weak contrast even though its nominal period exceeds the reciprocal cutoff.

To sample the full image band without aliasing, the object-side pixel pitch must be at most \(1/(2f_{\rm cut})=0.325\) μm. A detector with 6.5 μm pixels therefore needs at least \(20\times\) lateral magnification. A still higher magnification gives more samples of the same optical image. Recovering finer physical structure would require a larger numerical aperture, a shorter wavelength, or additional measurement information.

A separate point-source image is a practical calibration target. Its recorded radial profile can be convolved with a proposed object model to predict the measured image, including overlapping spots, before attempting an inversion of noisy data.

Chapter 9 · Multiple-beam interference in a cavity

Sum the returning fields in a plane-parallel optical cavity and turn measured line spacing and width into a mirror diagnostic.

Many transmitted copies

A transparent gap of length \(L\) and refractive index \(n\) sits between two identical plane mirrors. Illuminate it at normal incidence with a coherent, monochromatic plane wave. Assume the mirrors are lossless, their intensity reflectivity \(R\) is constant over the band, and the outgoing beams overlap in space. Each trip between mirrors adds phase. Each completed round trip also reflects the field twice. Because one mirror reflection has amplitude magnitude \(\sqrt R\), the field magnitude after both is multiplied by \(R\).

Choose a phase convention absorbing constant reflection phases into the resonance condition. A single transmitted pass has amplitude magnitude \((1-R)\) times the incident amplitude. The pass emerging after one additional round trip acquires \(R e^{i\delta}\) with \(\delta=4\pi nL/\lambda_0\). Summing the geometric series and squaring the magnitude gives

\[\mathcal T=\frac{(1-R)^2}{|1-Re^{i\delta}|^2}=\frac{1}{1+F\sin^2(\delta/2)},\qquad F=\frac{4R}{(1-R)^2}.\]

Resolving the peaks

Transmission reaches unity at \(\delta=2\pi m\) in this symmetric lossless model. Coherent addition of multiply reflected fields simultaneously cancels the returning field at the input. With unequal mirrors or intracavity loss, peak transmission is generally lower. The resonance frequencies are \(\nu_m=mc/(2nL)\) when dispersion is negligible, giving a free spectral range \(\mathrm{FSR}=c/(2nL)\). If \(n\) varies appreciably, differentiation of round-trip phase replaces it with the group index.

The half-power points satisfy \(|\sin(\delta/2)|=1/\sqrt F\) near resonance. Their full separation in phase is \(4\arcsin(1/\sqrt F)\) when \(F\ge1\). Dividing the \(2\pi\) phase interval by that width yields finesse, \(\mathcal F=\pi/[2\arcsin(1/\sqrt F)]\). For large \(F\) this reduces to \(\pi\sqrt R/(1-R)\). Finesse counts how many linewidths fit between adjacent orders. It is distinct from resolving power \(\nu/\Delta\nu\), which also depends on the order.

Inspect a scanned cavity

An air gap of 15.0 mm with \(R=0.900\) has a free spectral range of 9.99 GHz. The exact finesse is about 29.8, so adjacent half-power frequencies across one peak differ by 335 MHz. At a 600 nm carrier that width corresponds to about \(0.00040\) nm. Two lines one free spectral range apart nevertheless reach resonance together. Very narrow peaks and unambiguous identification across a broad input spectrum are different instrument requirements.

Mirror tilt makes different parts of a beam see different round-trip phases. Finite source divergence has a similar effect, since oblique rays accumulate a different axial phase. If raising the nominal mirror reflectivity no longer narrows a measured peak as predicted, compare the line shape at two illuminated spot sizes or angular apertures. Such a test separates instrumental phase averaging from a change in the source spectrum.

Measuring a frequency scan in both directions supplies a useful consistency test. Thermal expansion of the spacer or actuator hysteresis can change the calibration between scan position and optical frequency. An independently known reference line or sideband establishes that calibration without relying on the actuator displacement alone.

Chapter 10 · How a grating separates wavelengths

Derive the angular dispersion and resolving power of a finite grating and diagnose an order overlap.

Interference across equally spaced openings

Illuminate \(N\) identical narrow slits separated by distance \(d\) with normally incident monochromatic light. In the far field every slit produces the same single-slit envelope. At observation angle \(\theta\) adjacent contributions differ in optical path by \(d\sin\theta\), so their phases differ by \(\phi=2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda\). The array field equals the single-slit field multiplied by a sum of \(N\) unit phasors. Taking its geometric sum and squared magnitude gives the array intensity factor

\[G(\phi)=\left[\frac{\sin(N\phi/2)}{\sin(\phi/2)}\right]^2 .\]

At \(\phi=2\pi m\) the expression has a removable limit \(N^2\). Those directions satisfy the grating equation \(d\sin\theta_m=m\lambda\). Each peak collects contributions from all illuminated slits in phase. Some orders can nevertheless be faint or missing if the single-slit envelope is small or zero there. Transmission efficiency into a selected order thus depends on the groove or opening profile in addition to the grating period.

Dispersion and finite width

At fixed order different wavelengths peak at different angles. Differentiate the grating equation to obtain \(d\theta_m/d\lambda=m/(d\cos\theta_m)\). A large angular dispersion aids spatial separation at the detector. To assess resolution, also compute the width of each peak. The adjacent zeros of the numerator lie one phase increment \(2\pi/N\) either side of the maximum. Converting the increment back to angle gives a one-sided width \(\lambda/(Nd\cos\theta_m)\).

Choose the conventional criterion that one wavelength's peak falls on the neighbour's first zero. Equate the angular separation due to \(\Delta\lambda\) to this one-sided width and obtain \(\lambda/\Delta\lambda=|m|N\). Broad illumination of a grating therefore improves the ideal resolving power even with unchanged dispersion. The derivation assumes uniform coherent illumination, collimation, adequate detector sampling, and an entrance slit narrow enough to preserve this diffraction-limited width.

A small spectrometer

A 600-lines/mm grating has \(d=1.667\) μm. A collimated 10.0 mm beam illuminates 6000 lines. At \(\lambda=600\) nm first order occurs at \(\arcsin(0.360)=21.1^\circ\). The ideal smallest separation is \(600/6000=0.100\) nm. The angular dispersion is \(6.43\times10^{-4}\) rad/nm. A camera of focal length 200 mm centred on that order maps a 0.100 nm change to about \(12.9\) μm near its axis. Detector pixels that are large compared with this separation reduce the measurable resolution.

At that same angle, 300 nm light in second order has the same value of \(m\lambda\). Increasing the beam width narrows both peaks without separating their centres. An order-selecting filter can restrict the allowed band before measurement. A second grating or prism dispersing perpendicular to the first can instead separate overlapping orders into different traces. This illustrates why calibration with only one visible line cannot certify the identity of every line in a wide-band spectrum.

Test the finite-array argument with the two-slit acoustic field. For two equally excited slits the array factor has its second positive-angle zero at \(d\sin\theta=3\lambda/2\). With \(d/\lambda=3\), predict \(30.0^\circ\) and compare with the sampled \(30.50^\circ\) at receiver radius 0.90 m. At \(d/\lambda=2\) predict \(48.59^\circ\). Compare the measured angle at both receiver radii to see how the finite observation distance affects the far-field limit.

Chapter 11 · Gaussian beams and focusing

Relate beam waist, wavefront curvature and divergence, and estimate the focus of a collimated laser beam.

Diffraction during propagation

A narrow parallel-looking laser beam eventually spreads. Use a scalar monochromatic field \(U=u(x,y,z)e^{ikz}\), with \(u\) changing slowly on the scale of a wavelength. Substitution into the homogeneous Helmholtz equation cancels the largest longitudinal terms. Dropping \(\partial_z^2u\) relative to \(2ik\partial_zu\) gives the paraxial equation

\[2ik\,\partial_z u+\nabla_\perp^2u=0 .\]

This approximation requires a small angular spectrum about the optical axis. The transverse Fourier component with wavevector \(\mathbf q\) acquires phase \(\exp[-iq^2z/(2k)]\). A Gaussian waist field \(u(r,0)=u_0e^{-r^2/w_0^2}\) has a Gaussian angular spectrum proportional to \(\exp(-w_0^2q^2/4)\). On propagation, multiplication by the quadratic phase keeps that spectrum Gaussian with a complex width.

Invert the Gaussian transform

Collect its exponent as \(-q^2(w_0^2/4+iz/2k)\) and invert the transverse Fourier transform. The reciprocal complex width determines both the real amplitude decay in \(r\) and the phase curvature. Separating the real and imaginary parts gives

\[w(z)=w_0\sqrt{1+(z/z_R)^2},\qquad R(z)=z[1+(z_R/z)^2],\qquad z_R=\pi w_0^2/\lambda .\]

The convention here defines \(w\) at \(1/e\) of field amplitude and \(1/e^2\) of on-axis intensity. The on-axis intensity falls as \(w_0^2/w^2\) so the transverse integral of intensity stays constant. At a Rayleigh range from the waist the area doubles and the axial intensity halves. At large distance the half-angle divergence approaches \(\theta=\lambda/(\pi w_0)\). Waist size and angular spread therefore cannot be chosen independently for this fundamental mode.

A focused spot with finite depth

A collimated ideal Gaussian at 633 nm has \(1/e^2\) intensity radius \(w_{\rm in}=1.00\) mm at a lens of focal length 100 mm. Assume negligible clipping and a nearly flat incident wavefront. The lens converts input radius into convergence angle \(w_{\rm in}/f=0.0100\) rad. Equating this angle to the far-field Gaussian divergence gives \(w_0\simeq\lambda f/(\pi w_{\rm in})=20.1\) μm. The focused Rayleigh range is \(2.01\) mm. At axial displacement 4.02 mm the spot radius grows to about \(45.0\) μm and the axial intensity drops to a fifth.

Increasing the input radius by a factor of two halves the waist while reducing Rayleigh range by four. This trade matters when illuminating a sample extending several millimetres along the beam. A hard aperture that clips substantial power reshapes the angular spectrum, so this simple product no longer predicts the entire spot. A measured waist and divergence with product exceeding \(\lambda/\pi\) can also signal imperfect mode quality. Radius measurements must use the same intensity convention before making that comparison.

Two radius measurements equidistant from a proposed waist should give the same result for an ideal beam. If they do not, first refine the estimate of waist position. Multiple axial samples can be fitted to \(w^2(z)=w_0^2[1+(z-z_0)^2/z_R^2]\) to infer the waist plane and divergence simultaneously. Power falling through an aperture, however, also depends on the beam centre. A beam walking sideways past a fixed detector can imitate a growing radius unless beam position is measured independently. Recording transverse profiles instead of only total collected power makes this distinction observable.

Chapter 12 · Measuring a star with interference

Infer angular diameter or binary separation from the spatial coherence of light at two separated collectors.

When two copies cease to interfere

A narrow-band point source sends essentially the same waveform to two distant collectors after the proper path delay. Two extended sources can light both collectors equally while producing different fringe patterns. The measurable quantity is the complex degree of coherence \(\gamma\), normalized by the geometric mean of the two intensities. If the two collected beams have equal intensities, controlled recombination with variable phase produces fringe visibility \(|\gamma|\). Unequal intensities reduce visibility by an additional known balance factor.

Consider collectors separated by projected baseline \(B\) transverse to their line of sight. A source element at a small angular coordinate \(\theta\) along the baseline has geometric delay \(B\theta/c\) and phase \(2\pi B\theta/\lambda\). Different surface elements of a thermal source radiate independently on average, so cross terms between distinct elements disappear. Each element still interferes with itself through the two collectors. Adding their complex interference contributions, weighted by angular intensity \(S\), gives

\[\gamma(B)=\frac{\int S(\theta)e^{2\pi iB\theta/\lambda}\,d\theta}{\int S(\theta)\,d\theta}.\]

Here \(S\) is angular brightness integrated perpendicular to the baseline. Successive baselines sample different Fourier components of the projected brightness profile. The narrow-band, far-field conditions let one use a single wavelength and a planar angular phase across the entire source.

Two stars and a disk

For equally bright unresolved points at angles \(\pm s/2\), the normalized sum is \(\gamma=\cos(\pi Bs/\lambda)\). Its first zero occurs at \(B=\lambda/(2s)\). Rotating the baseline changes the projected separation and can restore visibility. If one point is brighter, the unequal phasor lengths cannot cancel completely. For a uniform circular disk of angular diameter \(\Theta\) the two-dimensional brightness integral instead gives \(2J_1(x)/x\) with \(x=\pi B\Theta/\lambda\). The first zero occurs at \(B=1.22\lambda/\Theta\). The same Bessel function appeared for a filled circular aperture, now describing coherence as a function of collector separation.

Interpret a visibility measurement

At 550 nm a uniform disk first loses its fringes at projected baseline 50.0 m. The inferred diameter is \(1.22(550\times10^{-9})/50.0=1.34\times10^{-8}\) rad or 2.77 milliarcseconds. At distance 10.0 parsecs its linear diameter would be \(0.0277\) astronomical units, about \(4.14\times10^9\) m. If independent spectroscopy suggested a binary made of two unresolved equals instead, the same null would correspond to projected separation 1.13 milliarcseconds. One zero by itself does not determine which brightness distribution produced it.

Measurements at several baseline lengths distinguish the cosine from the disk transform, and observations after rotation examine orientation. A broad optical passband averages different \(\lambda\) values and can fill a sharp null. In addition, uncontrolled fluctuations of the optical path during an exposure reduce contrast even for a point source. Observing a nearby unresolved calibrator at the same instrumental setting separates that loss from the target’s physical angular extent.

The measured baseline is a projection perpendicular to the line of sight. Earth’s rotation changes both its effective length and orientation for a fixed pair of collectors, providing additional Fourier samples over a night.

Applied study

Predict the spacing and visibility of interference fringes from phase, wavelength and aperture size.

Compare the experimental runs

What determines the dark fringes behind two slits?

Light reaches a point on a screen from two open slits. Closing one slit can make that point brighter. To predict the result, start with a wave field rather than the power arriving from each slit separately. In a homogeneous lossless medium a scalar component \(u\) obeys \(\nabla^2u=v^{-2}\partial_{tt}u\). A monochromatic solution can be written \(u=\operatorname{Re}\{A(\mathbf x)e^{-i\omega t}\}\). The phase of \(A\) advances by \(kr\) along a path of length \(r\), with \(k=2\pi/\lambda\) and \(v=\omega/k\).

The wave equation is linear, so complex amplitudes add. A detector averaging over many cycles records an intensity proportional to the squared magnitude. For two waves of the same frequency and polarisation, \(I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\Delta\phi\). This last term records the relative phase. Random changes of relative phase over a measurement wash it out.

Go from path difference to angular position

Let two identical narrow slits be separated centre to centre by \(d\) and illuminated normally and in phase. In the far field the paths at angle \(\theta\) to the screen normal differ by \(d\sin\theta\). Write the two equal amplitudes as \(A_0e^{\pm ikd\sin\theta/2}\). Their sum is \(2A_0\cos(kd\sin\theta/2)\), apart from a common phase. Destructive interference therefore occurs at \(d\sin\theta=(m+1/2)\lambda\), with integer \(m\) when the angle exists.

A real slit has width \(w\). Every point \(x\) across it contributes phase \(kx\sin\theta\). Integrating \(e^{ikx\sin\theta}\) from \(-w/2\) to \(w/2\) gives \(w\sin\beta/\beta\), \(\beta=\pi w\sin\theta/\lambda\). Uniformly illuminated rectangular slits then give the normalised intensity

\[\frac{I(\theta)}{I(0)}=\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2 \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right).\]

The first factor sets the envelope. The second makes the fine fringes. The expression assumes equal coherent illumination, a common polarisation and negligible change of amplitude across each slit. For an observation distance \(z\) much larger than \((d+w)^2/\lambda\), the quadratic variation of propagation phase across this aperture can be neglected.

Predict a fringe spacing and a missing order

Use red light of wavelength 600 nm, \(d=0.20\) mm, \(w=0.040\) mm and a screen 2.0 m away. Here \((d+w)^2/\lambda=0.096\) m, well below the screen distance. At small angles the screen position is \(y\simeq z\sin\theta\). The first dark fringe is at \(z\lambda/(2d)=3.0\) mm. Adjacent nominal bright orders are 6.0 mm apart.

The first single-slit zero lies at \(z\lambda/w=30\) mm. It coincides with the fifth positive interference order, so that order is missing even with perfect coherence. Changing slit width can remove an order without changing the underlying spacing of the interference fringes.

What sets the resolving power of a lens?

Apply the aperture integral to a different observation. Two distant point sources viewed through a lens each produce a diffraction pattern in the focal plane. For a rectangular pupil of width \(W\), a single source produces a first zero at angle \(\lambda/W\) in the direction across that width. Translating a second source through an angle \(\Delta\theta\) shifts its entire pattern by \(f\Delta\theta\) for focal length \(f\).

When a second maximum falls at the first zero of the first source, the angular separation is about \(\lambda/W\) (the Rayleigh criterion for this pupil). With \(W=5.0\) mm, \(\lambda=600\) nm and \(f=100\) mm, this is \(1.2\times10^{-4}\) rad or 12 µm between image peaks. For mutually incoherent sources the intensities of the two patterns add. In the slit experiment the coherent amplitudes added. This distinction determines whether overlapping light shows interference fringes. At an interface the relative strength of each optical polarisation can change. Polarisation and reflection at glass.

Exercises on visibility and wavelength

1. Distinguish imbalance from lost coherence

At the observation point one slit alone gives intensity \(4I_0\), the other \(I_0\). The phase can be varied. What are the extremes and fringe visibility for perfectly coherent, parallel-polarised illumination? What would completely random relative phase do?

Calculate the extremes

The individual amplitudes have ratio 2:1. In phase, \(I_{\max}=(2+1)^2I_0=9I_0\). Out of phase, \(I_{\min}=(2-1)^2I_0=I_0\). The visibility \((I_{\max}-I_{\min})/(I_{\max}+I_{\min})=0.80\). A nonzero minimum can therefore coexist with perfect coherence. With a uniformly random relative phase the time-averaged intensity is \(5I_0\) throughout the phase scan and visibility vanishes.

2. Change the light source

Replace 600 nm light by 450 nm light in the worked arrangement. Find the first dark position, the first envelope zero and the index of the first missing bright order.

Compare the two length scales

Both screen distances scale with wavelength at small angle, giving \(3.0(450/600)=2.25\) mm and \(30(450/600)=22.5\) mm. The condition for coincidence of a bright order and the first envelope zero gives \(m=d/w=5\), independent of wavelength.

Experiment · Compare measured angles with the far-field prediction

The numerical example drives two acoustic slits in phase. Sound pressure obeys the scalar Helmholtz equation, so the path-difference and aperture calculations transfer directly for the same \(d/\lambda\) and \(w/\lambda\). This model uses rigid acoustic walls and has no optical polarisation. Its receivers lie at a finite distance of 0.90 m. Two-slit pressure field and angular probes.

Run the notebook with its supplied ratios = [2, 3, 4] sweep, \(d=0.30\) m, \(w=0.050\) m and sound speed 343 m/s. Each frequency is a separate solve on the same mesh. Read the first minimum on the positive-angle side of the pressure-amplitude profile. Squaring the pressure magnitude gives a mean-square pressure pattern with the same minima. In a locally travelling plane wave, mean-square pressure divided by acoustic impedance gives the mean intensity.
