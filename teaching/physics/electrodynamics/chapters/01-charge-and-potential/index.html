Enclose the source before integrating the field

Imagine an insulating sphere charged uniformly throughout its volume. A probe outside it measures the field of a point charge, yet that representation fails at its centre. Our first objective is to see how a local charge distribution determines which measurements can distinguish the two sources. Use electrostatics in vacuum, with a sphere of radius \(R\) and total free charge \(Q\). A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium would distribute its excess charge differently.

Gauss’s law states that the electric flux through any closed surface is the enclosed charge divided by \(\epsilon_0\). Flux is the integral of the component normal to the surface. Pulling the field magnitude outside that integral requires symmetry in addition to the law itself. For this sphere rotational symmetry forces the field to be radial and of equal magnitude everywhere on a concentric spherical surface. The test surface can pass through the charged material, since it is a geometrical construction and does not insert a conductor.

Match the interior and exterior solutions

The uniform density is \(\rho=3Q/(4\pi R^3)\). At a radius \(r<R\) the enclosed charge is \(Q(r/R)^3\). At \(r>R\) the surface encloses all of \(Q\). Substitution in \(4\pi r^2 E_r=Q_{\rm encl}/\epsilon_0\) gives an interior field \(E_r=Qr/(4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3)\) and an exterior field \(E_r=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)\). The field vanishes at the centre and is continuous at the surface. A finite change in volume density changes the slope there. A true surface sheet of charge could instead create a finite jump in the normal field.

Since a static field has zero curl, a scalar potential can be chosen such that \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\). Take \(V(\infty)=0\) and integrate the radial field inward. The exterior potential equals \(Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)\). To reach an interior point, continue the integral from the surface with the interior field. This gives

\[V(r)=\frac{Q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left(3-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right),\qquad 0\le r\le R.\]

Matching at the surface supplies the additive constant which an interior integration alone cannot fix. A zero electric field at the centre means that the slope of the potential vanishes there. Its value need not vanish.

Assemble the sphere and account for the work

Build the charge by adding successive spherical shells while holding the final density fixed. When the assembly reaches radius \(r\), its charge is \(q(r)=Q(r/R)^3\). Bringing a small additional shell \(dq=3Qr^2dr/R^3\) from infinity costs \(dW=q(r)dq/(4\pi\epsilon_0 r)\). Integration gives \(W=3Q^2/(20\pi\epsilon_0 R)\). This positive work is stored in the electrostatic configuration. The shell’s interaction with itself is higher order in the infinitesimal added charge.

A complementary description uses the energy density \(\epsilon_0 E^2/2\). Integration over the exterior alone gives \(Q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0R)\), which is five sixths of the total. The remainder resides inside the charge distribution. A thin spherical conducting shell of equal radius and charge produces the same outside field, yet it has no interior field in its empty cavity. Its total energy is therefore lower.

Interpret three probe readings

For \(R=0.10\) m and \(Q=2.0\) nC, the surface field is \(1.80\times10^3\) V/m. At half the radius the field halves to about 899 V/m. At twice the radius it is roughly 449 V/m. The surface potential is 180 V, while the centre is near 270 V. Their difference predicts the work per unit charge required to carry a small positive test charge from surface to centre. A sufficiently large test charge would disturb the source distribution and violate the assumed measurement model.

The assembly energy is only \(2.16\times10^{-7}\) J. Doubling \(Q\) multiplies the field and potential by two but multiplies the stored energy by four. A comparison of fields outside two spherical samples probes their total charge. A measurement of assembly work at fixed radius can reveal how that charge was distributed inside them.

If the charge is reduced gradually while its spatial shape is preserved, the surface voltage is proportional to total charge. The incremental work needed to assemble the next element therefore grows with charge already present. Integrating that growth explains the familiar factor of one half in electrostatic self-energy expressions.
