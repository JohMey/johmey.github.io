Fields constrained by metal walls

Can a hollow metal tube carry an arbitrarily slowly varying electromagnetic wave? Take perfectly conducting walls, a uniform vacuum interior, width \(a\) along \(x\), height \(b\) along \(y\) and translation symmetry along \(z\). Tangential electric field must vanish on each wall. In free space a plane wave can have uniform transverse fields. Inside a hollow simply connected guide a nonzero transverse electrostatic potential difference between separate conductors cannot be maintained. The lowest guided pattern consequently has transverse variation.

For the simplest transverse-electric mode set \(E_y=E_m\sin(\pi x/a)\exp[i(\beta z-\omega t)]\), taking real parts for physical fields. At the side walls \(E_y\) is tangential and the sine vanishes. At the top and bottom the electric field is normal, as allowed by surface charge. Its zero divergence and accompanying magnetic field follow from Maxwell’s equations. In particular the curl includes an \(x\) derivative, giving a longitudinal magnetic component and the conventional label TE\(_{10}\).

Separate transverse and longitudinal wavenumbers

Substitute the field into the vacuum wave equation. The two spatial derivatives contribute \(-(\pi/a)^2\) and \(-\beta^2\), while the time derivative contributes \(-\omega^2/c^2\). Hence

\[\beta^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^2,\qquad f_c=\frac{c}{2a}.\]

Below cutoff the longitudinal dependence is exponential. In a long uniform section the bounded solution decays along the guide. A finite closed-off section can also have a growing exponential determined by reflections at its far end. Above cutoff the phase velocity is \(\omega/\beta\), while differentiating the dispersion relation gives the narrowband envelope velocity \(d\omega/d\beta=c^2\beta/\omega\). Their product is \(c^2\). Phase fronts move faster than \(c\) because the fixed transverse field variation contributes to total wavenumber. A modulation envelope in this lossless guide propagates more slowly than \(c\).

Choose a usable frequency band

For a guide with \(a=22.86\) mm and \(b=10.16\) mm, the first cutoff is \(6.56\) GHz. Higher-order patterns have transverse wavenumber squared \((m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2\). The next candidate cutoffs are \(13.11\) GHz for TE\(_{20}\) and \(14.75\) GHz for TE\(_{01}\). Consequently a 10.0 GHz drive supports one propagating mode in this ideal guide.

At 10.0 GHz the group velocity is \(c\sqrt{1-(6.56/10)^2}=0.755c\). A 2.00 m section delays a narrow envelope by \(8.84\) ns. Its guided wavelength is \(c/[f\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}]=39.7\) mm, exceeding the 30.0 mm free-space wavelength. Near cutoff the group velocity varies rapidly across a finite bandwidth, so quoting one envelope delay can conceal pulse distortion. Separately, real metal walls introduce attenuation and bends can excite evanescent modes even in the quoted single-mode band.

A practical way to measure the guided wavelength is to create a partial reflection at the termination. The forward and backward fields interfere and their stationary intensity maxima are separated by half the guided wavelength. At the example frequency the spacing should be about 19.9 mm. That measurement tests the propagation constant at one frequency. To test the group velocity, launch a short modulated burst or measure how transmitted phase changes between two nearby frequencies.
