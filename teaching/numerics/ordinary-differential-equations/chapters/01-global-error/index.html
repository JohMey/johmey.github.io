Initial values and propagated error

Derive an error bound for forward Euler and distinguish a poor approximation of a well-posed trajectory from uncertainty in the starting state.

Specify what the trajectory represents

In an initial-value problem \(y'=f(t,y)\), \(y(t_0)=y_0\), the time history follows from both the law and the initial state. A measurement made up to an absolute uncertainty gives a family of possible trajectories even before selecting a method. A numerical calculation adds further deviations each time it replaces the continuous flow over a short interval by an algebraic update. To estimate the final error, track how deviations arising early in a run are transported through later steps.

If \(f\) is continuous in time and locally Lipschitz in the state, the initial-value problem has a unique local solution. Lipschitz continuity is more than a technical detail. For \(y'=2\sqrt{|y|}\) with \(y(0)=0\), solutions may remain zero up to any chosen nonnegative time \(c\) and then follow \((t-c)^2\). Continuity alone gives no unique departure time. A code that keeps returning zero is computing one admissible trajectory, but there is no single exact departure trajectory against which to grade it without further modelling information.

Accumulate one-step errors

Write the exact integral from \(t_n\) to \(t_n+h\). Approximating the integrand by its value at the start gives \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n)\). Inserting an exact smooth solution in this update produces a one-step defect bounded by \(Mh^2/2\), where \(M\) bounds \(|y''|\) over the interval. This defect starts a step from the exact state. Actual steps generally start from an already perturbed state.

Let \(e_n=|y(t_n)-y_n|\), with state Lipschitz bound \(L\). Adding and subtracting a step started at the exact value gives \(e_{n+1}\leq(1+hL)e_n+Mh^2/2\). Iterate this inequality as a geometric series. For \(L>0\) and elapsed time \(T=nh\), use \((1+hL)^n\leq e^{LT}\) to conclude

\[e_n\leq e^{LT}e_0+\frac{Mh}{2L}(e^{LT}-1).\]

For \(L=0\) the second term becomes \(MTh/2\). A local error of order two gives order one over fixed time, while the initial uncertainty contributes separately. The bound treats every perturbation as if amplified. A dissipative system may damp them strongly, so a more problem-specific calculation can be much sharper.

Test the prediction against exact exponential growth

Take \(y'=y,\ y(0)=1\) over one time unit. Forward Euler with two steps gives \(1.5^2=2.25\); with four it gives \(1.25^4=2.44141\). The exact result is \(e=2.71828\). The errors 0.46828 and 0.27688 decrease by a factor about 1.69. The limiting factor of two requires a finer asymptotic regime. Expanding \(N\log(1+1/N)=1-1/(2N)+O(N^{-2})\) predicts \(e-(1+1/N)^N=e/(2N)+O(N^{-2})\).

If the initial value is actually 1.01, its exact trajectory at time one differs from the nominal one by \(0.01e\). Mesh refinement of the nominal solver cannot decide which initial reading is correct. To evaluate an integration scheme experimentally, fix the model and initial data, compare at a common endpoint and record the norm used. Resampling a coarse trajectory at extra plot positions changes the reported curve but does not alter the integration error accumulated at its original nodes.

Use a manufactured trajectory to test a solver

Instead of relying on a familiar unforced exponential, choose an exact function and derive the source that produces it. For \(y'+2y=s(t)\), selecting \(y_\star=1+\sin t\) requires \(s=\cos t+2(1+\sin t)\) and \(y(0)=1\). The forcing and damping are both active. A solver that silently evaluates the source at the wrong stage time can pass constant-source tests but fail this one.

Over one Euler step of 0.2 the initial derivative is one, so \(y_1=1.2\) versus the exact \(1+\sin(0.2)=1.19867\). This small first-step discrepancy is unusually favourable because \(y_\star''(0)=0\). It does not imply the global method has become second order. Report an error over a fixed time interval with multiple nontrivial phases of the forcing. A single specially located sample can suppress the leading contribution and give a misleading estimate.
