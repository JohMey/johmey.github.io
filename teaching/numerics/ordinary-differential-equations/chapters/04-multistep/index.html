Multistep formulas and parasitic modes

Reuse information from earlier steps

A Runge–Kutta method constructs within-step stage values and can usually start from a single known initial state. A multistep method instead keeps a short history of states or slopes. Once the history exists, it can attain higher order with fewer new function evaluations per step. Startup, changes of step size and discontinuities need special care because the coefficients embody assumptions about where past data lie.

For a uniform step \(h\), approximate \(f\) on \([t_n,t_{n+1}]\) by the line through \(f_{n-1}\) and \(f_n\). In the forward interval the line is \(f_n+s(f_n-f_{n-1})/h\), with \(s=t-t_n\). Integrating from zero to \(h\) gives \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h(3f_n-f_{n-1})/2\), the two-step Adams–Bashforth method. The negative weight results from extrapolating a straight line beyond its data. The formula integrates a linearly varying right-hand side exactly if the previous states and slopes are exact.

Differentiate a polynomial at the new point

A backward differentiation formula instead interpolates the recent state values. Differentiating the quadratic through \(y_{n-1},y_n,y_{n+1}\) at \(t_{n+1}\) gives \((3y_{n+1}-4y_n+y_{n-1})/(2h)\). Equating this to \(f(t_{n+1},y_{n+1})\) defines BDF2. To check the coefficients without constructing the full polynomial, substitute the constants, linear and quadratic monomials centred at \(t_{n+1}\). Their derivatives at the evaluation point are \(0,1,0\), respectively, which gives three linear conditions for the three weights.

For \(y'=-y\) with step 0.2 and exact starts \(y_0=1,y_1=e^{-0.2}=0.81873\), BDF2 gives \(y_2=(4y_1-y_0)/(3+0.4)=0.66910\). Adams–Bashforth gives \(y_2=y_1+0.1(-3y_1+1)=0.67311\). The exact value at 0.4 is 0.67032. Exact starting data isolate the behaviour of the multistep formula. In a practical code, a single-step method of adequate order creates the first value. The starting error becomes an input disturbance for the recurrence.

Inspect zero stability before trusting the order

For \(y'=0\) with no new forcing, BDF2 obeys \(3y_{n+1}-4y_n+y_{n-1}=0\). Try a sequence \(y_n=\zeta^n\); the characteristic roots are \(1,1/3\). One root represents the constant physical solution, and the other is a decaying parasitic component. In a general constant-step linear multistep formula all characteristic roots must satisfy \(|\zeta|\leq1\) and those on the unit circle must be simple for zero stability. A repeated root at one would admit a term proportional to \(n\) and hence unbounded amplification of a fixed initial mismatch as \(h\) decreases at fixed physical time.

Zero stability controls propagation over many small steps near \(h=0\). Absolute stability addresses a given finite \(h\lambda\) for a dynamical test mode. A formula may satisfy the first and still require a restrictive explicit step for the second. With consistency and a Lipschitz right-hand side, zero stability is the condition that converts small local defects and sufficiently accurate starts into convergence on fixed intervals. After a state reset or a discontinuity, reconstruct the history from the new side rather than extrapolating slopes belonging to a different evolution law.

Change the step without silently changing the order

If the preceding step had length \(h_p\) and the proposed step has length \(h\), the slope of the line through the two old right-hand sides is \((f_n-f_{n-1})/h_p\). Integrating that line over the proposed interval gives \(h f_n+h^2(f_n-f_{n-1})/(2h_p)\). The constant-step coefficients \(3/2,-1/2\) are recovered only when \(h=h_p\). For \(h=h_p/2\), the new weights multiplying \(h\) are \(5/4,-1/4\).

As a numerical check, integrate \(y'=t\) starting at \(t_n=1\) with \(h_p=0.2,\ h=0.1\). Slopes are 1 and 0.8, so the variable-step formula predicts an increment \(0.1(1.25-0.25\cdot0.8)=0.105\), exactly \(\int_1^{1.1}t\,dt\). Reusing constant-step coefficients would give 0.11. Adjusting only the time interval in the integrator while leaving coefficients fixed can therefore introduce a measurable error even on linear forcing.

After an abrupt change of forcing

Suppose an input is switched from zero to one exactly at a recorded time. A two-step formula mixing a pre-switch derivative with the new value is extrapolating across a discontinuity. On the first step wholly after the switch, the true integral of the input is exactly \(h\), whereas the old and new samples in Adams–Bashforth give \(3h/2\). Restarting with a suitable one-step update reconstructs a history belonging to the new smooth segment.
