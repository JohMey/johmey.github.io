Variation of constants and exponential steps

Integrate a stiff linear decay exactly while approximating only its slowly changing forcing term.

Preserve the linear response

For \(y'=Ay+g(t,y)\) with constant \(A\), multiply by \(e^{-At}\). The derivative of the product is \(e^{-At}g(t,y(t))\), because the two terms containing \(A\) cancel. Integrating between \(t_n\) and \(t_n+h\), then multiplying back, gives the exact variation-of-constants formula

\[y(t_n+h)=e^{hA}y(t_n)+\int_0^he^{(h-s)A}g(t_n+s,y(t_n+s))\,ds.\]

It separates the free propagation of the initial state from the accumulated, propagated forcing. Freezing \(g\) at the beginning of the step gives exponential Euler. Its forcing weight is \(h\varphi_1(hA)\), where \(\varphi_1(z)=(e^z-1)/z\) has the continuous value 1 at zero. The defining integral or power series applies to a singular matrix without computing an inverse.

Derive the leading defect

Assume along the solution that the change in \(g\) over \(s\) is bounded by \(Ls\), and that the linear propagator has norm at most \(M\) during the step. Subtract the frozen approximation from the exact integral. Its norm is bounded by \(ML\int_0^h s\,ds=MLh^2/2\). This is a local error bound. To obtain a global first-order estimate one must also bound propagation of earlier state errors, using the stability of the linear propagator and the Lipschitz dependence of the remaining forcing on the state. A stiff term hidden inside \(g\) can still impose a severe practical restriction.

Heat loss with a steady input

Let \(y'=-100y+20\) with \(y(0)=1\) and time in seconds. The equilibrium is 0.2. At \(h=0.1\), exponential Euler gives \(y_1=e^{-10}+0.2(1-e^{-10})\approx0.20003632\), exactly the analytical solution at that time. Forward Euler would give \(-7\), whereas backward Euler gives \(3/11\approx0.27273\). Both the exponential step and backward Euler damp the fast mode here, but the computed amount of transient that survives differs substantially. A large step that accurately reproduces its endpoint can still leave an output trace too sparse to measure the early decay time.

Change the input to \(20+10t\). During the first step exponential Euler ignores its linear growth. The missing term is \(10\int_0^h e^{-100(h-s)}s\,ds=10[h/100-(1-e^{-100h})/10000]\). At 0.1 s it is approximately 0.00900005. Including a linear approximation to the forcing would recover this term exactly. This calculation isolates the effect of freezing a changing input while the stiff decay remains exact.

Evaluating the weights

For small \(|z|\), direct subtraction of one from \(e^z\) loses significant digits. A dedicated exponential-minus-one function or the series \(1+z/2+z^2/6+\cdots\) yields a reliable \(\varphi_1\). Large sparse systems often require only the action of an exponential on a vector. Computing this action introduces another error budget separate from time approximation; a constant-forcing test with a known equilibrium checks both parts together.

Constant states as an implementation test

If \(Ay_\ast+g_\ast=0\), an exponential step starting at \(y_\ast\) must reproduce the same state for every step length. The series identity \(h\varphi_1(hA)A=e^{hA}-I\) makes the cancellation exact mathematically. Test this condition with a matrix containing both a zero eigenvalue and a strongly negative eigenvalue: the zero mode should accumulate a constant source linearly, while the decaying mode approaches its forced equilibrium. This test distinguishes a faulty treatment of the zero limit from ordinary temporal error caused by a varying input.
