A state has fewer independent coordinates than entries

A semi-explicit system has \(y'=f(t,y,z)\) and \(0=g(t,y,z)\). The algebraic equation supplies a condition at every instant; choosing every initial coordinate independently can violate it before time integration starts. If \(g_z\) is square and nonsingular locally, the implicit function theorem determines \(z=\psi(t,y)\) and substitution gives an ordinary differential equation for \(y\). Differentiating the constraint gives \(g_z z'=-g_t-g_y f\), so one differentiation determines the missing derivative. This is the usual semi-explicit index-one situation.

For \(y'=-y+z,\quad0=y+2z-t\), one has \(z=(t-y)/2\) and \(y'=(t-3y)/2\). If \(y(0)=2\), consistency forces \(z(0)=-1\) and \(y'(0)=-3\). Differentiating the constraint gives \(z'(0)=(1-y'(0))/2=2\). Entering both initial coordinates as zero and two in the wrong order would describe a different trajectory even if a numerical solver silently repaired their algebraic inconsistency.

Discretise both conditions at the same time

A backward Euler step at \(h=0.2\) enforces \(y_1-2=0.2(-y_1+z_1)\) and \(y_1+2z_1=0.2\). Eliminating \(z_1\) gives \(1.3y_1=2.02\), so \(y_1\approx1.553846\) and \(z_1\approx-0.676923\). Computing the second variable from the new time matters. Using \(z_0\) throughout the update and reporting it as the new algebraic variable would violate the current constraint. Report its residual together with the local or global accuracy estimate for \(y\).

Hidden constraints and the force multiplier

For a unit-mass pendulum with Cartesian position \(q\), velocity \(v\), rod length \(L\) and downward gravity vector \(g\), take \(q'=v,\ v'=g-\lambda q\) and \(q^Tq=L^2\). Differentiating the rod constraint once imposes \(q^Tv=0\); velocity must be tangent to the circle. Differentiating again gives \(v^Tv+q^Tg-\lambda L^2=0\), determining the multiplier \(\lambda=(v^Tv+q^Tg)/L^2\). The original position constraint contains no direct multiplier, so the index-one elimination used above does not apply.

At the bottom of a 1 m pendulum take \(q=(0,-1)\) m, \(v=(2,0)\) m/s and \(g=(0,-9.81)\) m/s². The multiplier is \(13.81\) s⁻² and acceleration is \((0,4)\) m/s², the required inward centripetal acceleration. A velocity with a nonzero vertical component would already violate tangency at this position. Advancing differentiated equations alone can let position and tangent constraints drift because rounding and discretisation accumulate. Check both \(q^Tq-L^2\) and \(q^Tv\) over time; projecting position alone may leave a velocity pointing slightly through the rod constraint.

An algebraic residual can hide an inaccurate state

For the linear example, change a consistent state at fixed time by \(\delta y=0.02,\ \delta z=-0.01\). Its constraint residual stays exactly zero, since those changes cancel in \(y+2z\). Its differential right-hand side changes by \(-0.03\). The perturbation lies along the constraint manifold and is invisible to any diagnostic that checks only the algebraic equation. Conversely a perturbation normal to the manifold can be easy to see in the residual even if \(y\) is accurate. Estimate time-discretisation error by a separate comparison, such as refining the time step at a common physical output time.
