Eliminate an unknown without changing the solution

Gaussian elimination replaces one equation by itself minus a multiple of another. In exact arithmetic this is reversible: add the same multiple back. In the first column choose a nonzero pivot \(a_{11}\) and form multipliers \(l_{i1}=a_{i1}/a_{11}\) for the rows below it. Subtract \(l_{i1}\) times row one from row \(i\). All lower entries in column one vanish, and the remaining unknowns satisfy a smaller system. Applying the same operations in the same order to the load vector is essential.

Repeating the argument produces an upper triangular matrix \(U\). The inverse row additions can be collected into a unit lower triangular matrix \(L\) containing the multipliers. If rows have been permuted, the relation is \(PA=LU\), where \(P\) records those exchanges. To solve for a new right-hand side, first solve \(Ly=Pb\) by forward substitution, then \(Ux=y\) by backward substitution. The factorisation depends on the matrix alone. This is valuable in static analysis when the material and supports stay fixed and only the applied loads change.

Factor once and load twice

Consider \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1&0\\1&3&1\\0&1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Subtract half the first row from the second to get \((0,2.5,1)\). Subtract 0.4 times the new second row from the third to get \((0,0,1.6)\). Thus \(U\) has diagonal \(2,2.5,1.6\) with ones immediately above, and \(L\) has multipliers 0.5 and 0.4 immediately below its unit diagonal. This matrix needs no pivot exchange. Multiplying the two factors recovers the original entries and gives a quick arithmetic check.

For the load \(b=(1,2,3)^T\), forward substitution yields \(y=(1,1.5,2.4)^T\). Back substitution starts with \(x_3=2.4/1.6=1.5\), then gives \(x_2=(1.5-1.5)/2.5=0\) and \(x_1=(1-0)/2=0.5\). A second load \(b=(0,1,0)^T\) gives \(y=(0,1,-0.4)^T\) and \(x=(-0.25,0.5,-0.25)^T\). Both calculations are short once the triangular factors are available.

Why exchange rows?

Consider \(10^{-5}x+y=1\) and \(x+y=2\). The exact solution is \(x=1/(1-10^{-5})\), \(y=2-x\). Eliminating with the first coefficient gives multiplier \(10^5\). The reduced equation contains the difference between numbers of size \(10^5\). In arithmetic rounded to three significant digits, both the new coefficient and right-hand side round to \(-1.00\times10^5\). Back substitution gives \(y=1\), then \(x=0\). Substitution in the second original equation exposes a residual of one.

Swap the rows first. The multiplier is now \(10^{-5}\), and the reduced equation rounds to \(y=1.00\). Back substitution in the first pivot equation gives \(x=1.00\), close to the exact answer. Partial pivoting chooses the largest absolute available coefficient in the pivot column, bounding the magnitudes of the multipliers by one. It usually limits numerical growth, though it cannot remove sensitivity already present in the original matrix.

For a dense matrix of size \(n\), the triangular factorisation takes order \(n^3\) arithmetic operations and substitutions take order \(n^2\) per load. Sparse discretisations can be much cheaper, but eliminating an unknown may create new nonzero entries between its neighbours. This fill depends on ordering. Ordering for small fill and pivoting for accuracy answer different questions, and production solvers may need both.

Use symmetry when the physics supplies it

For a symmetric positive definite stiffness matrix, Cholesky factorisation represents \(A=CC^T\) with a lower triangular \(C\) and positive real diagonal. The first diagonal entry is \(\sqrt{a_{11}}\). Entries below it are fixed by the first column of the product, and subtracting that column’s outer product leaves a smaller positive definite Schur complement. Positivity follows by minimising the full quadratic energy over the eliminated variable for every fixed nonzero remaining vector.

For \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&2\\2&5\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), the first column of \(C\) is \((2,1)^T\). The remaining diagonal is \(\sqrt{5-1^2}=2\). A zero or negative candidate under the square root signals that the positive definite assumption or numerical precision needs inspection. Unrestrained mechanical assemblies can have rigid modes of zero energy. Supplying appropriate physical constraints addresses that singularity directly instead of treating it as a pivot-ordering problem.
