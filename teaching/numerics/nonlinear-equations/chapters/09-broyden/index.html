Secant information for systems

What one trajectory identifies

After a step \(s=x_{k+1}-x_k\), two measured residuals provide \(y=F(x_{k+1})-F(x_k)\). Integrating the Jacobian along the connecting segment yields \(y=\int_0^1 J(x_k+ts)s\,dt\). Thus \(y\) measures the action of an averaged Jacobian on just one direction. Rebuilding an unrestricted \(n\times n\) Jacobian from these \(n\) numbers is underdetermined. Impose the secant equation \(B_{\rm new}s=y\) and prefer the smallest possible change to the current approximate Jacobian \(B\) in Frobenius norm.

Set \(w=y-Bs\). Each row of the correction must have specified dot product with \(s\). The shortest such row is a multiple of \(s^T\); any added component orthogonal to \(s\) increases its squared length. Solving the scalar normalisation in each row gives

\[B_{\rm new}=B+\frac{(y-Bs)s^T}{s^Ts}.\]

The new matrix is exact on \(s\) for the observed change and leaves every vector perpendicular to \(s\) unchanged. An earlier secant equation can be disturbed if its direction has a component along the latest step. This update approximates the Jacobian; updating an inverse directly requires its own formula and conditions.

Observe two independent directions

Let \(F(x)=Ax-b\), \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(B_0=I\). A displacement \(s_1=(1,0)^T\) gives \(y_1=(2,1)^T\). The first correction changes column one, giving \(B_1=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). An independent orthogonal displacement \(s_2=(0,1)^T\) gives \(y_2=(1,3)^T\) and recovers the second column exactly. After these two measurements the whole linear operator is known. Such orthogonal probe steps illustrate identifiability; an actual solver selects directions from its approximate Newton equations, so its steps need not be orthogonal.

Instead repeat the first step scaled by two. The predicted and observed residual changes now agree on that direction and the update vanishes, even though \(B_1\) is still wrong on \(e_2\). Small secant corrections by themselves therefore do not demonstrate that all directions of the linearisation are reliable.

When to request a new Jacobian

Given \(B_k\), solve \(B_ks=-F(x_k)\), test the step and update with the displacement actually accepted. Reusing the proposed full displacement after a line search violates the secant equation for the measurements taken. If function data contain errors of size \(\epsilon\), then dividing a change in their difference by a tiny step can corrupt the learned derivative by order \(\epsilon/\|s\|\). Compare achieved reduction with the predicted reduction, monitor the size of the residual itself and occasionally recompute a Jacobian if the model becomes unreliable. These diagnostics matter most when evaluating the underlying residual is substantially cheaper than assembling its derivatives.

Judge a secant across a bend

For the scalar residual \(f=x^2-1\), moving from one to two supplies secant slope three, the average of endpoint slopes two and four. Reusing it near two underestimates the current slope by one. Over a step from 1.9 to two the secant slope is instead 3.9. This simple example isolates two types of Jacobian discrepancy: even perfectly accurate data constrain only the sampled direction, and nonlinear variation makes that directional measurement an average over a finite segment. Shorter accepted steps reduce the latter discrepancy until residual evaluation noise becomes important.
