More equations than unknowns

Repeated measurements generally leave a residual even at the best parameters. For \(r(x)\in\mathbb R^m\), minimise \(\Phi(x)=\|r(x)\|^2/2\). Differentiating gives \(\nabla\Phi=J^Tr\). At a stationary point the residual is perpendicular to every first-order change accessible by varying the parameters. It may remain large if the model cannot reproduce the observations. Different physical units require weights or a clear rescaling before squaring coordinates; otherwise a change of units silently changes what solution is optimal.

Approximate \(r(x+s)\) by \(r+Js\) and minimise its squared norm. The least-squares condition is \(J^TJs=-J^Tr\). In practice a QR or singular-value factorisation of \(J\) avoids squaring its condition number. The exact Hessian is \(J^TJ+\sum_i r_i\nabla^2r_i\). Thus the neglected term vanishes at a zero-residual solution, but can matter for strongly curved residuals with a poor fit. A descent direction follows with full column rank whenever the gradient is nonzero: multiplying the normal equations by \(s^T\) gives \(s^T\nabla\Phi=-\|Js\|^2<0\).

Fit two incompatible observations

A parameter \(x\) predicts \(x\) for one sensor and \(x^2\) for another. Suppose their dimensionless observations are 1 and 2. Then \(r=(x-1,x^2-2)^T\). At \(x=1\) the residual is \((0,-1)^T\) and \(J=(1,2)^T\). The linear model chooses \(s=2/5=0.4\), moving to 1.4. The objective drops from 0.5 to \((0.4^2+0.04^2)/2=0.0808\). An exact fit is impossible because \(x=1\) would require the other observation to be one.

At the original point the exact second derivative is \(5-2=3\), so a full scalar Newton step on the objective would be \(2/3\), quite different from the Gauss–Newton step. At 1.4 the gradient is \(0.4+2.8(-0.04)=0.288\). Another Gauss–Newton step is \(-0.288/(1+2.8^2)\approx-0.03258\). The iteration reverses direction as the two sensor discrepancies begin to balance. Monitoring only the second sensor would favour a different parameter.

Regularise the step model

Minimising \(\|r+Js\|^2+\lambda\|s\|^2\) gives \((J^TJ+\lambda I)s=-J^Tr\). A positive \(\lambda\) controls overly large steps along weakly observed directions. With \(\lambda=5\) at the original point the step becomes 0.2 and the true objective at 1.2 is 0.1768. This is a smaller first improvement but may be more reliable elsewhere. Compare the actual decrease with that predicted by the local model to adjust damping. This temporary penalty on the step differs physically from assuming in advance that the final parameter itself must be small.

Changing one sensor's reliability changes the fit

Suppose the squared residual of the second sensor receives weight one quarter because its standard deviation is twice that of the first. In the worked example at \(x=1\), the scalar normal-equation numerator becomes \(0+(1/4)\,2(-1)=-0.5\); its denominator becomes \(1+(1/4)\,2^2=2\). The step is now 0.25. The model has moved less far from the exactly matched first sensor because the second sensor's discrepancy now carries less statistical weight. Repeated observations with correlated errors would require a full covariance model to interpret this weighting as a statistical fit.
