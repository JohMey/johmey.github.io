Chapter 1 · Piecewise interpolation and error bounds

Construct a continuous profile from sensor values, prove a local interpolation bound and distinguish sampling error from measurement uncertainty.

Reconstruct from the information available

A line of thermometers supplies values \(y_i\) at increasing coordinates \(x_i\). It does not determine a unique continuous function: any function vanishing at every sensor can be added to a candidate profile. Choosing piecewise straight segments asserts that the unresolved curvature between consecutive readings is small enough for the intended measurement. This assumption may be excellent for predicting a mean temperature and poor for estimating a local heat flux, since differentiation increases the influence of measurement noise.

For \(x\in[x_i,x_{i+1}]\), let \(s=(x-x_i)/h_i\), where \(h_i=x_{i+1}-x_i>0\). The unique affine function matching both readings is \(I_hy=(1-s)y_i+sy_{i+1}\). It follows by writing an unknown intercept and slope, imposing the two endpoint values and eliminating both unknown coefficients. The weights are nonnegative and sum to one. The interpolated value lies between adjacent readings, constants are reproduced, and bounded additive errors of size \(\epsilon\) at the two sensors change the interpolant by at most \(\epsilon\). These properties can fail when extrapolating a considerable distance outside the measured interval.

Why the error is quadratic

Suppose \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable on a panel. Fix an interior point \(t\), and choose a coefficient \(c\) so that \(g(x)=f(x)-I_h f(x)-c(x-x_i)(x-x_{i+1})\) also vanishes at \(t\). It already vanishes at both panel endpoints. Rolle’s theorem applied twice gives a point \(\xi\) where \(g''(\xi)=0\). The interpolant has zero second derivative there, so \(c=f''(\xi)/2\). Evaluate the identity at \(t\) to obtain

\[f(t)-I_h f(t)=\frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(t-x_i)(t-x_{i+1}).\]

The product is nonpositive within the interval and has greatest magnitude \(h_i^2/4\) at its midpoint. Thus the uniform error is at most \(h_i^2\max|f''|/8\). If every panel width is halved, an upper bound based on the same curvature falls by four. When curvature is positive, the segment overestimates the function. Knowledge of the curvature sign supplies information that an absolute error bound would discard.

Turn the bound into a sampling decision

On the unit interval consider a temperature \(T=290+8x^2\) K, with \(x\) given numerically in metres. On the first panel of length 0.25 m the endpoint readings are 290 and 290.5 K. Their average is 290.25 K. The actual midpoint is 290.125 K, an overestimate of 0.125 K. The bound \(16(0.25)^2/8=0.125\) K is attained because the curvature is constant. A second panel of the same width has the same maximum interpolation error even though its temperature change is different.

Now allow an uncertainty of 0.03 K at every source node. A conservative total bound is \(2h^2+0.03\) K for \(h\) in metres. Achieving 0.05 K requires \(h\leq0.10\) m, or at least 11 measurements including both ends. No amount of interpolation onto a denser display grid changes this source requirement. For the slope, two readings with opposite errors can change the estimated derivative by \(2\epsilon/h\), which grows when \(h\) shrinks. Choosing sensor spacing therefore depends on the quantity to be estimated. A spacing suitable for accurate point values may require additional smoothing before slopes inferred from noisy data are useful.

Interpret a finite sensor footprint

Sometimes the observation is already a mean over a finite sensor footprint. For a symmetric footprint of width \(w\) centred at \(x\), the mean of a smooth field is \(f(x)+f''(x)w^2/24+O(w^4)\), by Taylor expansion and symmetry. For the quadratic temperature above this correction is exactly \(16w^2/24\) K when \(w\) is measured in metres. With a 0.10 m footprint it is about 0.0067 K above the point value. Interpreting a cell mean as a point reading therefore adds another positive bias before the chord interpolation even begins. This distinction becomes important when comparing a temperature probe with simulation values stored as cell averages.

Chapter 2 · Polynomial interpolation and node placement

Derive the Lagrange interpolant, diagnose amplification of sample noise and use cosine-distributed nodes to control a polynomial error factor.

One polynomial through all measurements

Given \(n+1\) distinct nodes \(x_j\), seek one polynomial \(p\) of degree at most \(n\) matching \(y_j\) at each node. Define \(\ell_j(x)=\prod_{i\ne j}(x-x_i)/(x_j-x_i)\). This basis has value one at its own node and zero at all the others, so \(p=\sum_j y_j\ell_j\) satisfies the observations. If another such polynomial existed, the difference would have \(n+1\) separate zeros despite degree at most \(n\). It must vanish identically. Uniqueness concerns exact arithmetic and says little by itself about sensitivity.

For the nodes \(-1,0,1\), the three basis functions are \(x(x-1)/2,\ 1-x^2,\ x(x+1)/2\). At \(x=1/2\) their values are \(-1/8,3/4,3/8\). They sum to one but are not all positive. Consider samples \(2,1,2\): the predicted value is \(1.25\), consistent with the quadratic \(1+x^2\). If the three readings instead carry independent signed perturbations bounded by \(\epsilon\), the possible prediction error at this point reaches \((1/8+3/4+3/8)\epsilon=1.25\epsilon\). The worst perturbation takes signs matching those of the basis values.

Separate noise and approximation

The maximum of \(\sum_j|\ell_j(x)|\) over the interval, the Lebesgue constant, bounds the operator’s amplification of data errors. A different quantity controls the error of interpolation from exact samples. For sufficiently smooth \(f\), repeating the Rolle argument with \(n+2\) zeros gives \(f(x)-p(x)=f^{(n+1)}(\xi)\prod_j(x-x_j)/(n+1)!\). This formula compares the exact function with the selected polynomial. It contains both a geometric factor controlled by the nodes and a higher derivative that may grow quickly with degree. A small geometric factor cannot by itself establish convergence for an arbitrary function.

On \([-1,1]\) let \(T_m(x)=\cos(m\arccos x)\). From the cosine addition formula, \(T_{m+1}=2xT_m-T_{m-1}\) with \(T_0=1,T_1=x\). Thus \(T_m\) is a polynomial of degree \(m\) with leading coefficient \(2^{m-1}\) for \(m\geq1\). Its \(m\) zeros lie at \(\cos[(2k+1)\pi/(2m)]\). Choosing them as interpolation nodes makes the monic geometric factor \(2^{1-m}T_m(x)\), whose maximum magnitude is exactly \(2^{1-m}\).

Why the alternating extrema matter

At the \(m+1\) points \(\cos(k\pi/m)\), this monic factor alternates between its positive and negative extreme. Suppose another monic degree-\(m\) polynomial had a strictly smaller maximum magnitude. Subtract it from the Chebyshev factor. The leading powers cancel, leaving degree at most \(m-1\). At the successive extrema the difference must alternate in sign, forcing at least \(m\) distinct zeros between them. That contradicts its degree. This minimax argument concerns the node polynomial, and explains the deliberate concentration of nodes near the endpoints.

For \(m=3\) the recommended points are \(0,\pm\sqrt3/2\); their monic factor is \(x^3-3x/4\) with magnitude at most 0.25. The equally spaced alternative \(-1,0,1\) has factor \(x^3-x\), whose largest magnitude is \(2/(3\sqrt3)=0.385\). If data already exist at fixed locations, moving them is seldom an option. Subdividing the interval and fitting lower-degree polynomials locally instead limits long-range coupling. For evaluation at many points, barycentric interpolation can reuse stored node weights while avoiding explicit construction of a large monomial coefficient vector.

Evaluate without forming monomial coefficients

Define barycentric weights \(w_j=1/\prod_{i\ne j}(x_j-x_i)\). Dividing the Lagrange interpolation formula and the identity \(\sum\ell_j=1\) by the common nodal polynomial gives the evaluation ratio \(\sum_j w_j y_j/(x-x_j)\) divided by \(\sum_j w_j/(x-x_j)\). At an exact interpolation node, return its stored value directly. Multiplying all weights by the same constant has no effect, which is useful for numerical scaling.

For nodes \(-1,0,1\) the weights are proportional to \(1,-2,1\). At \(x=1/2\) with data \(2,1,2\), the weighted numerator is \(4/3-4-4=-20/3\), and the denominator is \(2/3-4-2=-16/3\). Their ratio gives 1.25. This evaluates the very same polynomial as the basis construction. Better arithmetic for evaluation cannot remove an intrinsically large Lebesgue constant associated with a poor set of nodes.

Problem sheet 1 · Nodes and interpolation

Analyse local interpolation errors, demonstrate noise amplification under extrapolation and compare two sets of global interpolation nodes.

Estimate a probe requirement

A smooth pressure profile on a 2 m pipe satisfies \(|p''|\leq24\) kPa/m². Each probe has absolute uncertainty 0.01 kPa. Bound the total error of piecewise linear interpolation. Find the largest equal spacing consistent with 0.04 kPa total error and the smallest number of probes including the two endpoints.

Worked solution

Between measured nodes the interpolation error of exact samples is bounded by \(24h^2/8=3h^2\) kPa. The two sample errors enter with nonnegative weights summing to one, adding at most 0.01 kPa. The requirement \(3h^2+0.01\leq0.04\) gives \(h\leq0.10\) m. Twenty intervals on a 2 m pipe require 21 probes.

This calculation controls worst-case pressure values and assumes the curvature estimate holds on every panel. A single unnoticed discontinuity would invalidate its use on that panel.

Predict beyond the sensors

Two sensors at positions 0 and 1 m each have error at most 0.5 K. Compute the interpolation weights at 0.4 m and the extrapolation weights at 1.5 m. Find the largest change to each predicted temperature and provide a sign pattern attaining each bound.

Worked solution

At 0.4 the weights are 0.6 and 0.4. Equal positive errors produce the worst-case rise of 0.5 K. At 1.5 the weights are \(-0.5\) and \(1.5\). Taking the first error negative and the second positive yields \(0.25+0.75=1.0\) K.

Even for a perfectly affine true profile the second estimate is more sensitive to data noise. If the true profile is curved, extrapolation adds an entirely separate approximation error.

Construct a minimax nodal factor

Choose two symmetric nodes \(\pm c\) on \([-1,1]\). Find the maximum magnitude of their monic nodal polynomial and minimise it over \(0\leq c\leq1\). Use the result to bound the interpolation error when \(|f''|\leq4\).

Worked solution

The nodal polynomial is \(x^2-c^2\); its negative extreme is \(-c^2\) at zero and positive extremes occur at the endpoints with value \(1-c^2\). Thus its norm is \(\max(c^2,1-c^2)\), minimised at \(c^2=1/2\). The nodes are \(\pm1/\sqrt2\) and the best monic norm is \(1/2\).

The Lagrange remainder yields maximum error at most \(4(1/2)/2=1\). For endpoints as nodes the same reasoning gives a bound of 2. The change improves this worst-case bound even though the endpoint readings themselves are no longer sampled.

Chapter 3 · Hermite pieces and cubic splines

Build a cubic from endpoint values and slopes, then determine missing curvature information by joining adjacent pieces smoothly.

Four conditions on a single panel

Line segments reproduce values but change slope abruptly at a sensor. A trajectory planner may require a smooth velocity as well as an accurate position. On a panel of width \(h\) let \(s=(x-x_i)/h\). A cubic polynomial has four coefficients. Specifying \(y_i,y_{i+1}\) and slopes \(d_i,d_{i+1}\) determines it uniquely. A convenient basis writes the interpolant as

\[H=(2s^3-3s^2+1)y_i+(-2s^3+3s^2)y_{i+1}+h(s^3-2s^2+s)d_i+h(s^3-s^2)d_{i+1}.\]

To reconstruct these terms, seek a cubic multiplying \(y_i\) with value one at \(s=0\), zero at \(s=1\), and zero slope at both ends. The four linear conditions give \(2s^3-3s^2+1\). The other basis functions follow by imposing a unit on their own datum and zero on the remaining three. The factors of \(h\) convert derivatives with respect to physical \(x\) into derivatives with respect to dimensionless \(s\). Dropping these factors would make the interpolant change shape when switching between metres and centimetres.

For positions \(y_0=0,y_1=1\) m over one second with both endpoint velocities zero, \(H=3s^2-2s^3\) m. At half time the position is 0.5 m but the speed is 1.5 m/s, greater than the mean speed of 1 m/s. Imposing rest at both ends forces the intervening speed to exceed the mean somewhere. The piece alone leaves endpoint accelerations \(+6\) and \(-6\) m/s². A smooth join to a subsequent segment therefore needs more than identical endpoint positions.

Recover slopes when only values are measured

A cubic spline fits one cubic on every interval, requiring continuity of both first and second derivatives at interior nodes. Let \(M_i=S''(x_i)\). Since a cubic has a linear second derivative, on panel \(i\) interpolate \(M_i\) and \(M_{i+1}\) linearly and integrate twice. Use the two measured endpoint values to fix the integration constants. On equal panels the resulting right-hand slope at the left endpoint is \((y_{i+1}-y_i)/h-h(2M_i+M_{i+1})/6\). The left-hand slope at the right endpoint is \((y_{i+1}-y_i)/h+h(M_i+2M_{i+1})/6\).

Equating these expressions at an interior join gives \(M_{i-1}+4M_i+M_{i+1}=6(y_{i-1}-2y_i+y_{i+1})/h^2\). This tridiagonal system relates each undetermined curvature to the neighbouring data. Two additional end conditions are needed. A natural spline imposes zero curvature at both endpoints. A clamped spline uses given endpoint slopes instead. Choose those conditions from what the experiment actually controls.

Examine the influence of endpoint assumptions

Fit a natural spline at \(x=0,1,2\) to \(y=0,1,0\). The end curvatures are zero and the interior equation gives \(4M_1=6(0-2+0)=-12\), hence \(M_1=-3\). On the first panel \(S''=-3x\). Two integrations give \(S=-x^3/2+ax+b\). The endpoint values set \(b=0,a=1.5\), yielding \(S(0.5)=0.6875\) compared with 0.5 from the straight chord. Symmetry gives zero slope at the summit and a reflected cubic on the second interval.

Smoothness alone cannot establish the true midpoint value. A sensor measuring a sharp corner would support a piecewise linear description, and monotone data can require a slope limiter to avoid artificial extrema in a cubic fit. Likewise, setting endpoint curvature to zero in a mechanical fit asserts a moment condition only under the appropriate beam model. Report the end conditions with the fitted curve, especially when derivatives near the ends are used as physical observables.

Use independent endpoint information as a check

Suppose the three displacement values in the example come with an independent measurement of the initial slope, \(S'(0)=1\) rather than 1.5. Imposing a natural left endpoint as well as this slope would overdetermine that end. One reasonable choice is a mixed spline, clamped on the left and natural on the right. For \(h=1\), the slope formula at the left reads \(1-(2M_0+M_1)/6=1\), giving \(2M_0+M_1=0\). The interior equation gives \(M_0+4M_1=-12\), since \(M_2=0\).

Solving yields \(M_1=-24/7\) and \(M_0=12/7\). A measured slope at one end has changed curvatures inside the interval. That coupling across segments distinguishes a global spline fit from interpolating each segment independently with prescribed slopes at every node.

Chapter 4 · Composite quadrature and cancellation

Derive midpoint, trapezoidal and Simpson formulas, and use the sign of curvature to interpret a pair of numerical integral estimates.

Measure an integral with finite samples

The energy delivered by a time-varying power input is the area under its history. If readings are available only at a fixed interval, integration must proceed from those samples or from additional physical assumptions. On one panel \([a,a+h]\), the midpoint rule uses \(hf(a+h/2)\), while integration of the chord between the endpoints gives \(h[f(a)+f(a+h)]/2\). Both are exact for affine \(f\). They may nonetheless give opposite errors on the same curved signal, which makes their comparison informative.

Expand about the midpoint \(m\), using \(s=x-m\). Odd powers of \(s\) integrate to zero on \([-h/2,h/2]\). The leading correction to \(hf(m)\) is \(f''(m)h^3/24\). Averaging the endpoint expansions instead gives \(hf(m)+f''(m)h^3/8+O(h^5)\). Subtracting the integral, the leading trapezoidal overestimate is \(+f''(m)h^3/12\), while the midpoint estimate has leading error \(-f''(m)h^3/24\). This expansion requires a smooth integrand. At a known abrupt switch in power, align a panel edge with the switch before applying it.

Increase the order by cancelling a term

Cover a fixed interval with equal panels. There are \(O(1/h)\) local errors of order \(h^3\), giving global second-order accuracy for both rules. Now combine two parts midpoint and one part trapezoid. Their leading second-derivative terms cancel. On a panel of width \(H\), the result is \(H[f(a)+4f(a+H/2)+f(a+H)]/6\), Simpson’s rule. It integrates all cubics exactly: the quadratic error cancels and odd terms about the midpoint cancel by symmetry. For sufficiently smooth data the panel error is \(O(H^5)\) and the composite error is \(O(H^4)\).

Simpson’s formula needs midpoint readings. With an even number of equal subintervals, join consecutive pairs and obtain the familiar alternating weights \(1,4,2,4,\dots,4,1\). Applying those integer weights to unequally spaced observations loses the derivation. In that setting use correctly derived nonuniform weights or the trapezoidal rule panel by panel.

Check the signs with a quartic pulse

Let \(P(t)=t^4\) W with \(t\) measured numerically in seconds over \([0,1]\). The exact energy is \(1/5\) J. With one whole panel, the midpoint estimate is \(1/16=0.0625\) J and the trapezoidal estimate is 0.5 J. Convexity explains why the true integral falls between them. Simpson gives \((0+4/16+1)/6=5/24=0.2083333\) J. Its positive error of \(1/120\) J is much smaller, though the quartic term prevents exactness.

Now split into two Simpson panels. The fourth derivative is constant, so halving panel width reduces the global error by exactly 16. The new estimate is \(0.2+(1/120)/16=0.20052083\) J. In a real measurement, noise can dominate such a small discretisation improvement. A common positive bias of 0.01 W integrates to an extra 0.01 J regardless of panel count. Distinguish uncertainty on the measurements from the discretisation of a known smooth curve. The rod experiment provides a direct check of the trapezoidal error sign and its reduction on mesh refinement. Measure the area under a sampled profile.

Handle a jump with one-sided values

Consider a heater that supplies 4 W until \(t=0.3\) s and 1 W from then to one second. The energy is \(4(0.3)+1(0.7)=1.9\) J. A two-panel trapezoidal rule reading at 0, 0.5 and 1 s sees the values 4, 1 and 1. It predicts \(0.5(4+1)/2+0.5(1+1)/2=1.75\) J. The piecewise linear representation invents a sloping transition lasting half a second.

If the switch time is known, split there and use the left and right limiting powers separately. Each constant segment then integrates exactly, including the correct jump without treating an isolated endpoint as carrying energy. If the switch time itself has uncertainty 0.01 s, the resulting energy uncertainty is 0.03 J because the power level changes by 3 W. Refining a numerical integration grid cannot resolve that timing uncertainty by itself.

Problem sheet 2 · Smooth pieces and integrated loads

Build a constrained trajectory, infer a spline curvature, and check quadrature by energy conservation over a measured interval.

Connect two resting positions

A carriage moves from 0 to 4 m in two seconds, starting and finishing at rest. Find the unique cubic trajectory satisfying these four endpoint data. Determine midpoint speed and the two endpoint accelerations. Can this cubic join a stationary segment with continuous acceleration?

Worked solution

With \(s=t/2\) the trajectory is \(4(3s^2-2s^3)\). Differentiating with respect to time gives \(v=12s(1-s)\) m/s, so \(v(1\ {\rm s})=3\) m/s. Acceleration is \(6(1-2s)\) m/s², giving \(+6\) and \(-6\) at the endpoints.

The velocity can join rest continuously. The stationary segment has zero acceleration, so this particular cubic cannot also give a continuous acceleration there. Enforcing both endpoint accelerations adds two constraints, motivating a higher-degree segment.

Identify what a natural endpoint buys

At \(x=0,1,2\) a rod has displacements \(0,2,0\). Construct the first segment of the natural cubic spline and find its slope at 0 and its value at 0.5. Compare with the quadratic through all three data.

Worked solution

The natural curvatures are \(M_0=M_2=0\). The interior equation gives \(4M_1=6(0-4+0)=-24\), so \(M_1=-6\). Integrate \(S''=-6x\) and impose the first two values to get \(S=3x-x^3\). The initial slope is 3 and midpoint value 1.375.

The global quadratic is \(4x-2x^2\), with initial slope 4 and midpoint value 1.5. Both match every measured value. The difference is detectable in unmeasured derivatives and in the interior prediction because the quadratic has nonzero end curvature.

Recover a missing quadratic load

On a unit-width panel a power signal is quadratic. Its endpoint values are 2 W and 6 W; its measured energy over one second is 3 W·s. Infer its midpoint value, compare the midpoint and trapezoidal estimates, and determine the sign of curvature.

Worked solution

Simpson integrates the quadratic exactly, so \(3=(2+4P_m+6)/6\), giving \(P_m=2.5\) W. The midpoint estimate is 2.5 J and the trapezoidal estimate is 4 J. Their weighted combination \((2\cdot2.5+4)/3=3\) J reproduces the measured energy.

The signal midpoint lies below the endpoint chord value 4 W, so the curvature is positive. These conclusions use the stipulated quadratic law; the same three observations cannot uniquely determine an arbitrary power history.

Chapter 5 · Gaussian quadrature and exactness

Choose quadrature nodes from moment conditions, prove the polynomial exactness of Gauss rules and map a reference rule to a finite element.

Spend samples where they are most useful

When the integrand is a callable function, quadrature nodes need not coincide with pre-existing sensors. A rule with \(n\) distinct nodes and \(n\) weights has \(2n\) scalar parameters. Can these integrate every polynomial through degree \(2n-1\)? Merely counting parameters does not prove existence. The Gauss construction supplies nodes for a positive weight function on an interval and establishes the exactness by orthogonality.

Start on \([-1,1]\) with a symmetric two-node rule of the form \(w[f(-c)+f(c)]\). Testing the constant function gives \(2w=2\), hence \(w=1\). Symmetry automatically integrates the odd monomials \(x,x^3\). Testing \(x^2\) gives \(2c^2=2/3\), or \(c=1/\sqrt3\). These four independent moment conditions establish exactness on every cubic by linearity. Testing \(x^4\) produces \(2/9\), whereas its integral is \(2/5\). The error appears at degree four exactly as the parameter count suggested.

Prove the general degree claim

Let \(P_n\) be the degree-\(n\) orthogonal polynomial for the prescribed positive integration weight \(w(x)\). Use its \(n\) distinct interior zeros as nodes. Define the weights by integrating the Lagrange basis on these nodes, so every polynomial of degree at most \(n-1\) is already integrated exactly. To treat any polynomial \(f\) of degree at most \(2n-1\), divide it by \(P_n\), giving \(f=P_n q+r\) with \(\deg q,\deg r\leq n-1\).

The integral of \(P_nq\) vanishes by orthogonality, and every quadrature evaluation of that product vanishes because the nodes are zeros of \(P_n\). Both the integral and the rule therefore reduce to the same value for \(r\). This proves exactness. There is a sharp obstruction to one extra degree: \(P_n^2\) is nonnegative, positive except at finitely many points, and all its sampled values vanish. Its positive integral cannot be reproduced by this rule. Positivity of the individual Gauss weights follows by applying exactness to the square of each Lagrange basis polynomial.

Transfer the rule into a physical element

An element \([a,b]\) is obtained by the affine change \(x=m+\ell \xi\), with midpoint \(m=(a+b)/2\), half-length \(\ell=(b-a)/2\), and \(dx=\ell\,d\xi\). Both the node locations and their weights change. For \([2,4]\), the two physical nodes are \(3\pm1/\sqrt3\), with unit physical weights. The integral of \(x^3\) is \((4^4-2^4)/4=60\). Summing the nodal cubes gives \(2\cdot3^3+6\cdot3(1/3)=60\). A missing Jacobian would escape detection on this particular element where \(\ell=1\); test a second element of a different length.

In a linear finite element with constant density, each consistent mass entry integrates a product of two linear basis functions, hence a quadratic. Two Gauss points integrate it exactly. On a unit element the true mass matrix has diagonal \(1/3\) and off-diagonal \(1/6\). Using just the midpoint makes all four entries \(1/4\). The alternating nodal vector \((1,-1)^T\) then has zero discrete mass although its interpolated field has a positive squared integral. This loss of a mode is a practical consequence of insufficient quadrature. With curved mappings or varying coefficients, determine the degree and smoothness of the entire transformed integrand before reusing a quadrature order.

Distinguish a load integral from a stiffness integral

On the same unit element, a constant axial stiffness integrand made from two derivatives of linear shape functions is itself constant. A single midpoint therefore reproduces the stiffness exactly, despite failing for the consistent mass. If \(EA(x)=1+x^2\), the same midpoint now approximates its integral by 1.25, whereas the exact result is \(4/3\). The difference is \(1/12\) in the chosen units. A rule sufficient for one element contribution may thus be insufficient for another in the same analysis.

To select a quadrature order systematically, multiply out the shape functions or their derivatives, coefficient field and mapping Jacobian in the reference coordinate. For discontinuous coefficients, a single smooth polynomial degree may not exist on the entire element. Splitting at the interface, or integrating separate material subcells, then addresses the modelling geometry more directly than increasing the polynomial exactness of one unbroken-panel rule.

Chapter 6 · Adaptive integration and error estimates

Construct a local error estimate from nested Simpson panels, allocate an absolute error budget and recognise signals that defeat sampling-based adaptivity.

Use two resolutions to estimate a missing integral

Suppose a composite rule on a smooth integrand has \(Q_h=I+Ch^p+O(h^{p+2})\). Halving the uniform spacing gives \(Q_{h/2}=I+Ch^p/2^p+\cdots\). Subtract the two approximations to eliminate \(I\). The fine-grid error satisfies \(I-Q_{h/2}\simeq(Q_{h/2}-Q_h)/(2^p-1)\). The sign of this estimate matters if correcting the numerical answer. Its magnitude supplies an error indicator. The relation assumes the same leading power operates on both grids and that the error exceeds the noise in the data.

For Simpson’s rule \(p=4\). On a parent interval compare one Simpson panel \(S\) with two half-panels whose sum is \(S_2\). The fine estimate of the local integral error is \(|S_2-S|/15\), and an extrapolated value is \(S_2+(S_2-S)/15\). The comparison needs only two new function evaluations because the endpoints and original midpoint are reused. If the indicator is too large, split the interval and apply the same test to each child separately.

Build a global tolerance from local decisions

Let the total absolute tolerance be \(\epsilon\). Begin with the whole interval assigned that budget. When a panel splits, a simple allocation gives half its budget to each child. By induction the sum of all final panel budgets equals the original tolerance. If each local actual error lies within its budget, the triangle inequality bounds the global error by \(\epsilon\). In practice the indicators estimate those actual errors, so this last statement is conditional. Around a discontinuity the fourth-order expansion can fail, and tiny intervals can run into evaluation noise or floating-point spacing.

For signed integrals with strong cancellation, relative error alone is hard to interpret. A large positive area and nearly equal negative area may give a tiny sum that changes sign under rounding. Use an absolute tolerance meaningful for the physical output, possibly combined with a relative term based on a stable scale. Reusing shared endpoint values helps both reproducibility and cost, especially when each evaluation invokes a constitutive model or a simulation.

Inspect one accepted panel and one failure

On \([0,1]\) use \(f(x)=x^4\). One Simpson panel gives \(S=0.20833333\), and two panels give \(S_2=0.20052083\). Their difference divided by 15 is \(-0.00052083\). The corrected result is 0.2, exactly the known integral up to the printed rounding. Here the fourth derivative is constant and there are no higher powers in the integrand, so cancellation removes the entire leading error. An estimated error of \(5.21\times10^{-4}\) would pass a local tolerance of \(10^{-3}\) while \(10^{-4}\) would trigger another split.

Now consider a narrow triangular pulse entirely between 0.10 and 0.11 of the interval, with height one. Its exact area is 0.005. The five points initially used by adaptive Simpson are \(0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\). Every sampled ordinate is zero. The algorithm would accept zero with a zero indicator, even with a very small tolerance. This failure follows from lack of information, and it applies to any finite deterministic sample set when arbitrary narrow features are permitted. If event times or material interfaces are known, partition at them before sampling. If smoothness bounds are available, they supply an independent scale for an initial mesh. A small estimated local error should be interpreted together with those physical and mathematical assumptions.

Choose an independent comparison

A practical confirmation is to integrate the same signal using a different initial split and a different set of quadrature nodes. If a narrow pulse missed on one mesh is intersected on the other, their discrepancy reveals that the earlier indicator was misleading. Agreement between both estimates improves confidence but still cannot rule out an arbitrarily narrow feature absent from both sample sets.

When a physical bound \(|f'|\leq B\) is known, it supplies complementary information. Across an unsampled gap of width \(h\) whose endpoint values are both zero, a possible positive pulse is bounded by two straight ramps of slopes \(+B\) and \(-B\). Integrating the triangular envelope gives a maximum hidden area \(Bh^2/4\). A credible slope bound therefore turns a statement about unsampled behaviour into a quantitative reason to reduce the largest gap.

Problem sheet 3 · Accuracy with few evaluations

Derive mapped Gaussian weights, expose an underintegrated element and design a tolerance check for a sharply localised signal.

Map a rule and check its units

Use the two-node Gauss rule to integrate \(x^2\) over \([1,4]\). Give physical nodes and weights. Compare with the exact antiderivative, then explain what happens if the reference weights are used without the mapping factor.

Worked solution

The midpoint is 2.5 and half-length is 1.5, so nodes are \(2.5\pm1.5/\sqrt3\) and weights are both 1.5. The sum of squared nodal values is \(2[2.5^2+(1.5/\sqrt3)^2]=14\), giving integral 21. The antiderivative gives \((64-1)/3=21\).

Using weights equal to one would report 14. When \(x\) is measured in metres, the weights carry metres and the integral carries m³. Ignoring the Jacobian is a scale error as well as a dimensional inconsistency.

Measure mass of an alternating displacement

On \(0\leq x\leq1\), linearly interpolate nodal values \((1,-1)\). Compute the exact squared norm, the one-point midpoint approximation and the two-point Gauss result. Explain which field escapes the one-point sampling.

Worked solution

Linear interpolation gives \(u=1-2x\), so \(\int_0^1 u^2dx=1-2+4/3=1/3\). The midpoint value is zero, giving zero numerical norm with one point. The two Gauss nodes are \(1/2\pm1/(2\sqrt3)\), where \(u=\mp1/\sqrt3\). Weights \(1/2\) give \(1/3\).

The field changes sign inside the element and its node lies exactly at the one-point sample. Treating zero discrete norm as proof of zero field would introduce a spurious unconstrained mode.

Interpret an adaptive estimate

For a smooth interval, one Simpson panel gives 1.2000 and two half-panels give 1.1850. Estimate the signed correction, the corrected integral and whether an assigned tolerance of 0.0005 is met. Separately, a narrow pulse is known to lie between two sample positions. How would you modify the initial partition?

The correction to the fine value is \((1.1850-1.2000)/15=-0.0010\). The extrapolated estimate is 1.1840. The local error indicator 0.0010 exceeds the budget, so refine both halves and reuse the existing nodal evaluations.

Chapter 7 · Divided differences and derivative data

Build an interpolating polynomial one measurement at a time and incorporate a measured slope at a repeated node.

Keep the previous fit

A new measurement arrives after a polynomial has been fitted to \(x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1}\). To preserve those measurements, add a multiple of \(\omega_k(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(x-x_j)\). At each old node the correction vanishes. At a new distinct node \(x_k\) its coefficient is fixed by \(c_k=(f(x_k)-p_{k-1}(x_k))/\omega_k(x_k)\). This gives a triangular construction. Once a coefficient has been determined it remains in every subsequent polynomial provided the original order is retained.

The coefficients can also be obtained using a table. Put \(f[x_i]=f(x_i)\) and subtract adjacent entries of the previous order before dividing by the distance between the two outer nodes. The subtraction cancels shared lower-order information. Algebraically the leading coefficient of the polynomial through \(x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k}\) is

\[f[x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k}]=\frac{f[x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{i+k}]-f[x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k-1}]}{x_{i+k}-x_i}.\]

This follows by combining the two lower-degree interpolants with factors \(x-x_i\) and \(x-x_{i+k}\). Their difference divided by the outer spacing agrees with every tabulated value and has the required leading coefficient.

Three temperatures and a test point

Suppose a temperature sensor reports 2, 5 and 17 degrees at 0, 1 and 3 hours. First divided differences are 3 and 6 degrees per hour. The second difference is \((6-3)/3=1\) degree per hour squared. Hence \(p(x)=2+3x+x(x-1)\). At hour 2 this gives 10 degrees; the original straight line would give 8. The correction shows how strongly the additional observation changes that prediction. Neither interpolant by itself certifies that the temperature really did reach its predicted value between readings.

Nested evaluation uses \(2+x[3+(x-1)]\) here. Each new layer adds one multiplication and one addition. Reordering the nodes changes the intermediate table, but uniqueness ensures the same final polynomial in exact arithmetic. Strongly unequal spacings can magnify noise in higher differences and make numerical results sensitive to the order.

Let two nodes coalesce

If a second node approaches the first, the corresponding difference quotient tends to \(f'(x_0)\) for differentiable \(f\). An independently available derivative therefore supplies the diagonal entry directly. For a value 1 and slope 2 at zero and a value 4 at one, the required quadratic is \(1+2x+cx^2\); matching the final value gives \(c=1\). Its prediction at one half is 2.25. Perturb the initial slope by 0.1 while keeping endpoints fixed. The correction becomes \(0.1x(1-x)\), attaining 0.025 at the midpoint. Endpoint agreement alone would miss that uncertainty.

Rescale before comparing coefficients

The coefficients have different units at successive orders. In the temperature example, expressing time in minutes divides the first divided differences by 60 and the second difference by \(60^2\). Their smaller printed magnitudes do not mean the curvature has become less significant. Evaluating the second term at the same physical instant multiplies by two correspondingly larger node differences and recovers exactly the original contribution. Scaling the node interval near unit size can make intermediate arithmetic more convenient; interpret a coefficient together with the product of distances it multiplies.

Chapter 8 · Uniform approximation and alternation

Certify a best uniform polynomial by finding alternating errors and compare its guarantee with interpolation at fixed nodes.

An error budget that applies everywhere

A calibration curve may have a specification requiring its largest absolute deviation anywhere in an interval to stay below a threshold. Assign a polynomial \(p\) of degree at most \(m\) and consider \(E(p)=\max_{a\leq x\leq b}|f(x)-p(x)|\) for a continuous target. Interpolating selected measurement locations fixes the polynomial there. It need not allocate the remaining error efficiently. Shifting an interpolant by a constant sometimes halves its largest deviation at once.

Suppose \(m+2\) ordered locations attain the same error magnitude with alternating signs. Any polynomial with strictly smaller maximum error must move up at each positive extreme and down at each negative extreme. Its difference from the candidate then has at least one zero between each consecutive pair. This requires \(m+1\) zeros in a polynomial of degree at most \(m\), a contradiction. Those extreme points thus certify optimality globally once the candidate's error is bounded over the entire interval.

Find the best line by hand

Approximate \(x^2\) on \([-1,1]\) by \(a+bx\). Averaging that line with its reflection gives the constant \(a\). For each \(x\), the averaged error is the mean of the errors at \(x\) and \(-x\), so the uniform norm cannot increase. For constants the target covers the whole range from zero to one. The midpoint \(a=1/2\) balances its two extreme distances. At \(-1,0,1\), the errors are \(+1/2,-1/2,+1/2\). These three points certify the optimum among all lines, including nonsymmetric ones.

The endpoint interpolant is the constant 1. Its error is zero at both ends and reaches magnitude 1 at zero. Lowering the line sacrifices exact agreement with the endpoints in exchange for a factor of two in the worst case. If the endpoint values encode compulsory boundary conditions, such a shift is inadmissible and the optimisation problem must include those constraints.

Exchange trial extrema

For an unknown target, guess \(m+2\) extreme locations and solve the linear system \(f(x_i)-p(x_i)=(-1)^iE\) for the coefficients and \(E\). Search the full interval, including its boundaries, for the largest deviation. When it exceeds the trial level, exchange a support point while retaining sign alternation and repeat. For smooth targets, the zeros of \(f'-p'\) supply candidate interior extrema. A coarse plotting grid alone cannot certify the resulting maximum.

For a constant approximation to \(e^x\) on \([0,1]\), monotonicity identifies the endpoints immediately. Equalising \(a-1=e-a\) gives \(a=(1+e)/2\) and error \((e-1)/2\), approximately 0.85914. Replacing the upper endpoint by a larger value raises both quantities. The constant optimum depends on the range, providing a simple independent check on an implementation of the exchange procedure.

A lower bound from three measurements

Suppose measurements at \(-1,0,1\) give the exact values \(1,0,1\) but the interior shape is otherwise unknown. Any line still has maximum error at least one half on those three measurements: averaging its two endpoint predictions recovers its value at zero, so it cannot be within less than one half of both endpoint targets and the central target. The lower bound survives with sparse data. The matching upper bound requires information between the measurements, supplied in the earlier example by the analytic formula \(x^2\).

Problem sheet 4 · Data and uniform error

Update measurements in Newton form and certify a uniform approximation by following the signs of its error.

Add a fourth observation

At times 0, 1 and 2 a displacement takes values 1, 2 and 5. Construct its Newton polynomial. Append a value of 13 at time 3 and evaluate the revised prediction at 1.5.

Worked solution

The first differences are 1 and 3; the second is 1. The quadratic is \(1+x+x(x-1)=1+x^2\). At 3 its value is 10.

The new coefficient is \((13-10)/(3\cdot2\cdot1)=1/2\). The correction at 1.5 is \(0.5(1.5)(0.5)(-0.5)=-0.1875\). The revised value is \(3.25-0.1875=3.0625\). All three original nodes annul the added term.

Combine a slope with two values

Find a quadratic with \(p(1)=4\), \(p'(1)=-1\) and \(p(3)=6\). If the last measurement changes by 0.2, find the change of the midpoint prediction while preserving the first two conditions.

Worked solution

Write \(p=4-(x-1)+c(x-1)^2\). At 3 this gives \(2+4c=6\) and hence \(c=1\). The midpoint prediction at 2 is 4.

The revised curvature coefficient rises by \(0.2/4=0.05\), so \(p(2)\) rises by 0.05. The effect at the repeated node and its derivative both vanish, as the original conditions demand.

Certify a line

Approximate \(x^2\) on \([0,2]\) by a line parallel to its endpoint interpolant. Choose the vertical shift minimising the uniform error. Show that allowing a different slope cannot improve the result.

Worked solution

The interpolant is \(2x\). Its error \(x^2-2x=(x-1)^2-1\) lies between \(-1\) and 0. Dropping the line by 0.5 centres the error range, giving \(p=2x-0.5\).

At 0, 1 and 2 the errors are \(+0.5,-0.5,+0.5\). Any strictly better line would differ from this one by a polynomial changing sign twice. A nonzero linear polynomial cannot have those two distinct roots. Thus 0.5 is globally optimal.

Chapter 9 · Fourier projection and sampled frequencies

Recover periodic modes by projection and identify signals which become indistinguishable on a uniform sample grid.

Orthogonality on a period

For a square-integrable \(2\pi\)-periodic signal define \(\langle f,g\rangle=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx\). The exponentials \(e^{ikx}\) are orthonormal for integer \(k\). Integrating their product gives one when the frequencies coincide and zero otherwise because a nonconstant exponential completes an integer number of cycles. Minimising the squared mean discrepancy over modes \(|k|\leq m\) therefore gives \(c_k=\langle f,e^{ikx}\rangle\). The residual is orthogonal to every retained mode.

Orthogonality also splits energy. The squared norm of the original signal equals the sum of the retained squared coefficient magnitudes plus the remaining squared error. This identity gives the truncation error exactly for a finite trigonometric series. For a general square-integrable periodic signal the projections converge in the mean-square norm. That statement concerns an integral; a narrow region of large pointwise error can contribute little to it.

A resolvable frequency and an invisible one

Take \(f(x)=2+\cos(2x)+0.4\sin(5x)\). Projecting onto frequencies through three retains the constant and cosine. The remaining mean-square error is \(0.4^2/2=0.08\), so its root mean square is about 0.28284. Now sample at \(x_j=2\pi j/8\). At those points \(5x_j\) differs from \(-3x_j\) by \(2\pi j\). Thus the data are equally compatible with \(2+\cos(2x)-0.4\sin(3x)\). A discrete fit through frequency three can have zero sample residual yet disagree between nodes.

At \(x=\pi/8\), the two sine contributions are \(0.4\sin(5\pi/8)\) and \(-0.4\sin(3\pi/8)\). Their difference is about 0.7391. This unsampled point exposes the false low-frequency reconstruction. More generally the finite sum of \(e^{2\pi i(k-l)j/N}\) vanishes unless \(k-l\) is divisible by \(N\). This geometric-series argument explains frequency equivalence on the grid and also supplies the discrete orthogonality behind a Fourier transform.

Smoothness and endpoint matching

Integrating a Fourier coefficient by parts divides by its frequency. The boundary term vanishes for a smooth periodic continuation. Repeating the operation shows faster coefficient decay when successive derivatives also match at the endpoints and remain sufficiently regular. A mismatch when a finite record is periodically repeated creates a jump. Near a jump partial sums develop oscillations, and adding modes confines them to a narrower region while a peak overshoot persists.

Start a numerical study by refining the sample grid with a fixed retained bandwidth. Then hold that refined grid and enlarge the bandwidth. The first experiment probes whether the coefficient estimates are polluted by unresolved modes. The second probes how much physical detail a deliberately truncated representation omits. A single refinement that changes both quantities makes these explanations difficult to distinguish.

The special frequency at half the sample rate

With eight equally spaced samples the mode \(\cos(4x)\) gives \(1,-1,1,-1,\ldots\), whereas \(\sin(4x)\) gives zero at every point. A phase shift of a sinusoid at precisely this frequency therefore changes its observed amplitude and can hide the entire oscillation. This explains the special treatment of the highest coefficient in an even-length real Fourier transform. Ten samples over the same period distinguish both sine and cosine components at frequency four because their sample values are now linearly independent.

Chapter 10 · Weighted projections and Legendre polynomials

Derive a best mean-square polynomial and determine how a change of weight changes what that polynomial predicts.

Distance depends on the observation

A large error over a small part of an interval may be acceptable when an instrument averages over the interval. With a positive integrable weight \(w\), minimise \(J(p)=\int_a^b w(x)|f(x)-p(x)|^2\,dx\) over polynomials of degree at most \(m\). The first variation in an arbitrary allowed direction \(q\) gives \(\int w(f-p)q=0\). Conversely this orthogonality implies \(J(p+q)=J(p)+\int wq^2\). This Pythagorean identity proves uniqueness and explains why a residual with large local extrema can still be optimal in the chosen metric.

In a monomial basis these conditions couple every coefficient to every other. Choose polynomials \(\phi_j\) orthogonal for the same weight instead. Then \(p=\sum c_j\phi_j\) with \(c_j=\int wf\phi_j/\int w\phi_j^2\). Increasing the degree preserves every coefficient already computed. Using quadrature to estimate these integrals introduces another approximation. A rule that samples too sparsely can make two distinct basis vectors look identical under the discrete inner product.

Construct a basis on a symmetric interval

For unit weight on \([-1,1]\), start with \(1,x,x^2\). Odd integrands integrate to zero, so the first two are orthogonal already. Subtract the projection of \(x^2\) onto the constant, namely \(1/3\), and retain \(x^2-1/3\). Scaling this polynomial to take value one at the right endpoint gives \(P_2=(3x^2-1)/2\), the second Legendre polynomial. Its squared integral is \(2/5\), obtained by integrating its expanded fourth-degree square.

Approximating \(x^2\) by a line retains just \(1/3\). Squaring the residual and integrating gives \(2/5-4/9+2/9=8/45\). The mean-square error is half that number and its square root is about 0.29814. The worst local error occurs at the endpoints and equals \(2/3\). The constant \(1/2\) has smaller uniform error but a larger squared integral of \(7/30\). The objective function explains the difference without appealing to how a fitted curve looks.

Move the weight toward one endpoint

On \([0,1]\) choose density \(w(x)=2x\), normalised to integrate to one. Fitting a constant to \(f(x)=x\) now gives its weighted mean \(2\int_0^1 x^2\,dx=2/3\). The weighted mean-square error is \(2\int x^3\,dx-(2/3)^2=1/18\). Equal weighting would instead place the constant at \(1/2\). Selecting a weight thus amounts to specifying the region over which a loss should be economical. When interpreting coefficients state the interval, basis normalisation and weight together; otherwise two reported coefficients can be different descriptions of the same fitted polynomial.

A discrete inner product can give a different optimum

If only the endpoints of \([-1,1]\) are sampled with equal weight, then \(x^2\) takes value one at every observation and the best discrete constant is one. Adding the midpoint with the same weight changes it to \(2/3\). Simpson weights on those three samples instead give \((1+4\cdot0+1)/6=1/3\), the exact continuous coefficient, because that particular integral is quadratic. A mismatch between a discrete fit and a continuous projection can thus arise entirely from the observation design, even with error-free measurements.

Problem sheet 5 · Spectral measures of error

Distinguish mean-square projections on continuous and sampled intervals and interpret the residual energy.

Recover the invisible frequency

A recorder samples \(\sin(7x)\) at \(x_j=2\pi j/10\). Identify its low-frequency alias. Give a point where the two functions disagree, and find the mean-square energy of their difference over a period.

Worked solution

Since 7 is congruent to −3 modulo 10, the sampled signal agrees with \(-\sin(3x)\). At \(x=\pi/10\) their difference is \(2\sin(3\pi/10)\approx1.618\).

On the continuous period the two sine modes are orthogonal. Each has mean square \(1/2\), so their difference has mean square 1. A vanishing discrepancy at every sample therefore coexists with an order-one continuous error.

Which modes deserve space?

Let \(f(x)=1+0.6\sin x+0.8\cos(4x)\). Compute its mean square, the root-mean-square error after retaining frequencies at most one, and the best additional single real basis function among all higher-frequency sine and cosine modes.

Pairwise orthogonality gives \(1+0.36/2+0.64/2=1.5\). Omitting the fourth cosine leaves root-mean-square error \(\sqrt{0.32}\approx0.566\).

The only omitted nonzero coefficient multiplies \(\cos(4x)\). Adding this mode removes the entire remaining mean-square error. Choosing an intermediate frequency for its lower index would make no change for this specific signal.

Find the best linear mean-square approximation to \(x^3\) on \([-1,1]\) with unit weight. Compute its squared integrated error and compare the optimum slope with endpoint interpolation.

Symmetry forces zero constant. Orthogonality to \(x\) requires \(a=\int x^4/\int x^2=(2/5)/(2/3)=3/5\).

Chapter 11 · Quadrature near singularities and oscillations

Transform integrable endpoint singularities and use phase information to diagnose a misleading quadrature result.

When derivative bounds fail

The usual trapezoid error bound needs a bounded second derivative. The integral of \(x^{-1/2}\) from zero to one is finite, yet the integrand itself is undefined at the first endpoint. Calling a closed quadrature formula on its original samples is already impossible. Simply omitting that endpoint also omits part of a finite area, and its size scales as the square root of the omitted interval. A substitution can encode the known local behaviour exactly.

Set \(x=t^q\). A factor \(x^\alpha dx\) becomes \(qt^{q(\alpha+1)-1}\,dt\). For \(\alpha>-1\), choosing \(q=1/(\alpha+1)\) removes the leading power. Any additional factor \(g(t^q)\) still determines the transformed smoothness. For \(\alpha=-1/2\), \(q=2\) and \(g(x)=1+x\), the integral becomes \(\int_0^1 2(1+t^2)\,dt=8/3\). Composite Simpson quadrature on just two equal panels is exact on this quadratic. Assign the endpoint of the transformed expression by its limit to avoid evaluating zero times infinity.

A deceptively constant sample

Now consider \(\int_0^1\cos(16\pi x)\,dx\). The exact value is zero, because eight periods fit the interval. At the nine nodes of an eight-panel uniform trapezoid rule each value is one. The computed area is one, a full unit of error for an analytic function. A finer grid of sixteen panels samples alternating signs and returns zero. Repeating the first calculation on four panels also returns one. Agreement between the four- and eight-panel estimates therefore gives a false impression of convergence.

The aliasing follows from summing a geometric progression of sampled complex exponentials. When the phase step is an integer multiple of \(2\pi\), neighbouring samples coincide. Changing the offset slightly can expose the problem even before changing the number of nodes. A bound involving derivatives recognises the difficulty because the second derivative grows quadratically with the phase frequency.

Use cancellation as part of the method

When the oscillatory factor is known analytically, integrate it rather than forcing the grid to resolve every period. For a differentiable amplitude \(g\), integration by parts gives

\[\int_a^b g(x)\cos(\omega x)\,dx=\left[\frac{g(x)\sin(\omega x)}{\omega}\right]_a^b-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_a^b g'(x)\sin(\omega x)\,dx.\]

This identity shows how smooth amplitudes can produce small integrals even when pointwise values are large. For \(g=x\) and \(\omega=\pi\) on \([0,1]\), the boundary term vanishes. The remaining sine integrates to \(2/\pi\), giving \(-2/\pi^2\). A quadrature scheme that approximates the slowly varying amplitude by lines and integrates each line against the phase reproduces this result exactly for this example. If the phase itself is uncertain, that modelling error must be assessed separately from quadrature error.

Test integrability before applying the change

For \(f(x)=1/x\) on \((0,1]\) the substitution \(x=t^q\) leaves \(q/t\), still nonintegrable for every positive exponent. A numerical rule excluding zero may nevertheless return an ordinary finite number. The integral from a cutoff \(\delta\) to one is \(-\log\delta\), so reducing the cutoff by a factor of ten adds \(\log10\approx2.3026\) every time. That persistent change signals a divergent problem definition. It calls for a physically justified finite cutoff or a different mathematical quantity before refining a quadrature rule.

Chapter 12 · Monte Carlo integration and stratification

Estimate an integral from random samples, attach a sampling uncertainty and derive the gain from stratification in a simple case.

Turn area into an expectation

For an integrable \(f\) on \([a,b]\), let \(U\) be uniform on the interval. Then \(I=(b-a)\mathbb E[f(U)]\). Independent draws give an unbiased estimator by averaging their function values and multiplying by the length. With finite variance \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(f(U))\), independence eliminates cross-covariances and gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat I)=(b-a)^2\sigma^2/n\). The typical size of the sampling error decreases with the square root of the sample count. Multiplying the budget by four halves the standard error.

In a numerical run replace the unknown variance by the sample variance with denominator \(n-1\). For a sufficiently large sample with a suitable distribution, a normal approximation provides an approximate confidence interval. A particular interval need not contain the exact answer. Heavy tails, correlated draws or a nearly unsampled small peak can make the estimated uncertainty unreliable. Recording the random seed reproduces the same sequence of sample values; independent seeds are needed for independent replications.

Four draws and the uncertainty they reveal

For \(f(x)=x\) on \([0,1]\), four illustrative draws at 0.1, 0.2, 0.7 and 0.8 have mean 0.45. Their squared deviations sum to 0.37, yielding sample variance \(0.37/3\) and estimated standard error \(\sqrt{0.37/12}\approx0.176\). The exact integral is 0.5, so the observed error is 0.05. Because the uniform variance is exactly \(1/12\), the true standard deviation for repeated sets of four draws is \(\sqrt{1/48}\approx0.144\). These three numbers answer different questions about this one run and its possible replications.

Guarantee some coverage before sampling

Divide the interval into \(n\) equal strata and draw one independent point uniformly inside each. Sum each sample value times its stratum width. The estimator remains unbiased since each local expectation equals the mean over its stratum. For \(f(x)=x\) every local variance is \(1/(12n^2)\). Multiplying by the squared width and summing \(n\) independent contributions gives a total variance of \(1/(12n^3)\). For four samples the standard deviation drops to about 0.0361. The gain comes from fixing the amount of sampling effort in each region.

A single sample per stratum cannot estimate its own local variance. Independent repetitions of the complete stratified procedure or more samples per stratum permit empirical uncertainty estimates. In high dimension a full Cartesian partition requires rapidly growing numbers of cells. Simple Monte Carlo retains the same square-root rate under finite variance, but the variance constant and the cost of sampling can still depend strongly on dimension. Identify both the estimator and the method used to measure its uncertainty when reporting a numerical integral.

Use a known integral to reduce variability

For \(f(x)=x+x^2\), the integral of the first term is known to be one half. Sampling only \(x^2\) and adding one half leaves the expectation unchanged. The original sample variance per uniform draw is \(1/3+1/2+1/5-(5/6)^2=61/180\). The residual sample variance per draw is \(1/5-(1/3)^2=4/45\), less than one third as large. This control-variate construction uses a known mean and a correlation with the original integrand. An arbitrary replacement by a superficially similar function without restoring its known mean would bias the estimate.

Problem sheet 6 · Choosing where to sample

Exploit endpoint structure, test aliasing and design a stratified random estimate with a known variance.

Remove a singular factor

Evaluate \(\int_0^1 x^{-2/3}(1+x)\,dx\) using \(x=t^q\). Select \(q\), derive the transformed integrand, and decide whether one Simpson panel pair gives the exact integral.

Worked solution

To neutralise the power require \(q(1-2/3)-1=0\), giving \(q=3\). Then \(x^{-2/3}dx=t^{-2}3t^2dt=3dt\) and the finite endpoint value is 3.

Integrating \(3(1+t^3)\) gives \(3+3/4=3.75\). Simpson is exact on cubics; its three samples are \(3,3.375,6\), and their weighted sum divided by six is 3.75.

Expose a false agreement

For \(f(x)=\cos(12\pi x)\) on \([0,1]\), find the trapezoid results with 3 and 6 panels. Test 12 panels and explain why the initial equal answers cannot justify a zero error estimate.

Worked solution

With three panels the phase advances \(4\pi\) at each sample; with six it advances \(2\pi\). Both return the constant sample average 1.

With twelve panels samples alternate between 1 and −1. The half-weight endpoints together count as one positive value, so all contributions cancel. The exact integral is zero. Correlated aliasing on the first two grids invalidates a smooth-error extrapolation.

Two strata with different variability

A response has variance 1 on the first half of \([0,1]\) and 4 on the other half. Estimate the integral from twelve independent evaluations. Compare the variances for allocations (6,6) and (4,8). Derive the continuous optimum under the same fixed budget.

Worked solution

Each stratum mean has variance \(\sigma_i^2/n_i\) and weight \(1/2\), so total variance is \((1/n_1+4/n_2)/4\). Equal allocation gives \(5/24\); (4,8) gives \(3/16\), slightly smaller.

Differentiating \(1/n_1+4/(12-n_1)\) gives \(-1/n_1^2+4/(12-n_1)^2\). Its zero requires \(12-n_1=2n_1\). Positive second derivative yields the optimum (4,8), allocating in proportion to each stratum's standard deviation.

Applied study

Reconstruct a field from samples and estimate how interpolation changes its integral.

Compare the experimental runs

What lies between two measurements?

A probe records temperatures at equally spaced positions along a rod. To estimate the mean temperature, first decide what profile those samples represent. For neighbouring points \(x_j,x_{j+1}\) at distance \(h\), write \(x=x_j+sh\) with \(0\leq s\leq1\). The straight line matching both readings is

\[(I_h f)(x)=(1-s)f(x_j)+sf(x_{j+1}).\]

The weights are nonnegative and sum to one. This reproduces every affine function, keeps interpolated values between their neighbours, and amplifies bounded sample errors by at most a factor of one. A finer display grid can draw this line more densely. Recovering new curvature requires new source information.

Use curvature to predict the sign and size of error

For twice continuously differentiable \(f\), the interpolation remainder on one panel is \(f(x)-I_h f(x)=\tfrac12 f''(\xi)(x-x_j)(x-x_{j+1})\) for some \(\xi\) on that panel. The product is nonpositive and reaches magnitude \(h^2/4\) at the midpoint. Thus the maximum error is bounded by \(h^2\max|f''|/8\). For positive curvature the chord lies above the curve.

Integrating the chord gives \(h[f(x_j)+f(x_{j+1})]/2\), the trapezoidal rule. Integrating the bound with the quadratic factor retained gives a panel integral error at most \(h^3\max|f''|/12\). On a fixed interval of length \(L\) there are \(L/h\) panels, so the composite error is bounded by \(Lh^2\max|f''|/12\). The pointwise and total error both decrease quadratically, for different reasons.

Integrate a curved temperature profile

Use the exact profile \(T(x)=300+20(x/L)^2\) K with \(L=1\) m. Four equal panels have \(h=0.25\) m. On the first panel the endpoints are 300 and 301.25 K. Their interpolated midpoint is 300.625 K, while the exact midpoint is 300.3125 K. The overestimate of 0.3125 K is the same on every panel because the curvature is constant.

The exact integral is \(300+20/3=306.6667\) K·m on this unit-length rod. Here \(T''=40\) K/m² and the trapezoidal overestimate equals \(Lh^2 T''/12=0.20833\) K·m. Dividing the integral by length gives mean temperature. To obtain thermal energy above a reference temperature, additional material properties and cross-sectional area are needed.

Problem class

Choose the sensor spacing

For the quadratic rod, select the minimum number of equal intervals so the midpoint error never exceeds 0.05 K. How many measurements are needed including both endpoints?

Show the calculation

With \(N\) intervals on a metre, the bound is \(40/(8N^2)=5/N^2\) K. Requiring \(5/N^2\leq0.05\) gives \(N\geq10\), hence 11 measurements. At constant curvature this bound is attained at the midpoints.

Infer an integral from two estimates

Four and eight panels give 306.875 and 306.71875 K·m. Assume \(Q_h=Q+c h^2\). Eliminate \(c\) and estimate \(Q\). Would adding sensors only to the display grid make this correction possible?

Show the calculation

Multiply \(Q_{h/2}=Q+c h^2/4\) by four and subtract \(Q_h\). This gives \(Q=(4Q_{h/2}-Q_h)/3=306.66667\) K·m. For the quadratic profile the error law is exact. Subdividing the same source chords on screen preserves their total area and supplies no second source approximation.
