Monte Carlo integration and stratification

Estimate an integral from random samples, attach a sampling uncertainty and derive the gain from stratification in a simple case.

Turn area into an expectation

For an integrable \(f\) on \([a,b]\), let \(U\) be uniform on the interval. Then \(I=(b-a)\mathbb E[f(U)]\). Independent draws give an unbiased estimator by averaging their function values and multiplying by the length. With finite variance \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(f(U))\), independence eliminates cross-covariances and gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat I)=(b-a)^2\sigma^2/n\). The typical size of the sampling error decreases with the square root of the sample count. Multiplying the budget by four halves the standard error.

In a numerical run replace the unknown variance by the sample variance with denominator \(n-1\). For a sufficiently large sample with a suitable distribution, a normal approximation provides an approximate confidence interval. A particular interval need not contain the exact answer. Heavy tails, correlated draws or a nearly unsampled small peak can make the estimated uncertainty unreliable. Recording the random seed reproduces the same sequence of sample values; independent seeds are needed for independent replications.

Four draws and the uncertainty they reveal

For \(f(x)=x\) on \([0,1]\), four illustrative draws at 0.1, 0.2, 0.7 and 0.8 have mean 0.45. Their squared deviations sum to 0.37, yielding sample variance \(0.37/3\) and estimated standard error \(\sqrt{0.37/12}\approx0.176\). The exact integral is 0.5, so the observed error is 0.05. Because the uniform variance is exactly \(1/12\), the true standard deviation for repeated sets of four draws is \(\sqrt{1/48}\approx0.144\). These three numbers answer different questions about this one run and its possible replications.

Guarantee some coverage before sampling

Divide the interval into \(n\) equal strata and draw one independent point uniformly inside each. Sum each sample value times its stratum width. The estimator remains unbiased since each local expectation equals the mean over its stratum. For \(f(x)=x\) every local variance is \(1/(12n^2)\). Multiplying by the squared width and summing \(n\) independent contributions gives a total variance of \(1/(12n^3)\). For four samples the standard deviation drops to about 0.0361. The gain comes from fixing the amount of sampling effort in each region.

A single sample per stratum cannot estimate its own local variance. Independent repetitions of the complete stratified procedure or more samples per stratum permit empirical uncertainty estimates. In high dimension a full Cartesian partition requires rapidly growing numbers of cells. Simple Monte Carlo retains the same square-root rate under finite variance, but the variance constant and the cost of sampling can still depend strongly on dimension. Identify both the estimator and the method used to measure its uncertainty when reporting a numerical integral.

Use a known integral to reduce variability

For \(f(x)=x+x^2\), the integral of the first term is known to be one half. Sampling only \(x^2\) and adding one half leaves the expectation unchanged. The original sample variance per uniform draw is \(1/3+1/2+1/5-(5/6)^2=61/180\). The residual sample variance per draw is \(1/5-(1/3)^2=4/45\), less than one third as large. This control-variate construction uses a known mean and a correlation with the original integrand. An arbitrary replacement by a superficially similar function without restoring its known mean would bias the estimate.
