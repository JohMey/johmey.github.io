When derivative bounds fail

The usual trapezoid error bound needs a bounded second derivative. The integral of \(x^{-1/2}\) from zero to one is finite, yet the integrand itself is undefined at the first endpoint. Calling a closed quadrature formula on its original samples is already impossible. Simply omitting that endpoint also omits part of a finite area, and its size scales as the square root of the omitted interval. A substitution can encode the known local behaviour exactly.

Set \(x=t^q\). A factor \(x^\alpha dx\) becomes \(qt^{q(\alpha+1)-1}\,dt\). For \(\alpha>-1\), choosing \(q=1/(\alpha+1)\) removes the leading power. Any additional factor \(g(t^q)\) still determines the transformed smoothness. For \(\alpha=-1/2\), \(q=2\) and \(g(x)=1+x\), the integral becomes \(\int_0^1 2(1+t^2)\,dt=8/3\). Composite Simpson quadrature on just two equal panels is exact on this quadratic. Assign the endpoint of the transformed expression by its limit to avoid evaluating zero times infinity.

A deceptively constant sample

Now consider \(\int_0^1\cos(16\pi x)\,dx\). The exact value is zero, because eight periods fit the interval. At the nine nodes of an eight-panel uniform trapezoid rule each value is one. The computed area is one, a full unit of error for an analytic function. A finer grid of sixteen panels samples alternating signs and returns zero. Repeating the first calculation on four panels also returns one. Agreement between the four- and eight-panel estimates therefore gives a false impression of convergence.

The aliasing follows from summing a geometric progression of sampled complex exponentials. When the phase step is an integer multiple of \(2\pi\), neighbouring samples coincide. Changing the offset slightly can expose the problem even before changing the number of nodes. A bound involving derivatives recognises the difficulty because the second derivative grows quadratically with the phase frequency.

Use cancellation as part of the method

When the oscillatory factor is known analytically, integrate it rather than forcing the grid to resolve every period. For a differentiable amplitude \(g\), integration by parts gives

\[\int_a^b g(x)\cos(\omega x)\,dx=\left[\frac{g(x)\sin(\omega x)}{\omega}\right]_a^b-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_a^b g'(x)\sin(\omega x)\,dx.\]

This identity shows how smooth amplitudes can produce small integrals even when pointwise values are large. For \(g=x\) and \(\omega=\pi\) on \([0,1]\), the boundary term vanishes. The remaining sine integrates to \(2/\pi\), giving \(-2/\pi^2\). A quadrature scheme that approximates the slowly varying amplitude by lines and integrates each line against the phase reproduces this result exactly for this example. If the phase itself is uncertain, that modelling error must be assessed separately from quadrature error.

Test integrability before applying the change

For \(f(x)=1/x\) on \((0,1]\) the substitution \(x=t^q\) leaves \(q/t\), still nonintegrable for every positive exponent. A numerical rule excluding zero may nevertheless return an ordinary finite number. The integral from a cutoff \(\delta\) to one is \(-\log\delta\), so reducing the cutoff by a factor of ten adds \(\log10\approx2.3026\) every time. That persistent change signals a divergent problem definition. It calls for a physically justified finite cutoff or a different mathematical quantity before refining a quadrature rule.
