Polynomial interpolation and node placement

One polynomial through all measurements

Given \(n+1\) distinct nodes \(x_j\), seek one polynomial \(p\) of degree at most \(n\) matching \(y_j\) at each node. Define \(\ell_j(x)=\prod_{i\ne j}(x-x_i)/(x_j-x_i)\). This basis has value one at its own node and zero at all the others, so \(p=\sum_j y_j\ell_j\) satisfies the observations. If another such polynomial existed, the difference would have \(n+1\) separate zeros despite degree at most \(n\). It must vanish identically. Uniqueness concerns exact arithmetic and says little by itself about sensitivity.

For the nodes \(-1,0,1\), the three basis functions are \(x(x-1)/2,\ 1-x^2,\ x(x+1)/2\). At \(x=1/2\) their values are \(-1/8,3/4,3/8\). They sum to one but are not all positive. Consider samples \(2,1,2\): the predicted value is \(1.25\), consistent with the quadratic \(1+x^2\). If the three readings instead carry independent signed perturbations bounded by \(\epsilon\), the possible prediction error at this point reaches \((1/8+3/4+3/8)\epsilon=1.25\epsilon\). The worst perturbation takes signs matching those of the basis values.

Separate noise and approximation

The maximum of \(\sum_j|\ell_j(x)|\) over the interval, the Lebesgue constant, bounds the operator’s amplification of data errors. A different quantity controls the error of interpolation from exact samples. For sufficiently smooth \(f\), repeating the Rolle argument with \(n+2\) zeros gives \(f(x)-p(x)=f^{(n+1)}(\xi)\prod_j(x-x_j)/(n+1)!\). This formula compares the exact function with the selected polynomial. It contains both a geometric factor controlled by the nodes and a higher derivative that may grow quickly with degree. A small geometric factor cannot by itself establish convergence for an arbitrary function.

On \([-1,1]\) let \(T_m(x)=\cos(m\arccos x)\). From the cosine addition formula, \(T_{m+1}=2xT_m-T_{m-1}\) with \(T_0=1,T_1=x\). Thus \(T_m\) is a polynomial of degree \(m\) with leading coefficient \(2^{m-1}\) for \(m\geq1\). Its \(m\) zeros lie at \(\cos[(2k+1)\pi/(2m)]\). Choosing them as interpolation nodes makes the monic geometric factor \(2^{1-m}T_m(x)\), whose maximum magnitude is exactly \(2^{1-m}\).

Why the alternating extrema matter

At the \(m+1\) points \(\cos(k\pi/m)\), this monic factor alternates between its positive and negative extreme. Suppose another monic degree-\(m\) polynomial had a strictly smaller maximum magnitude. Subtract it from the Chebyshev factor. The leading powers cancel, leaving degree at most \(m-1\). At the successive extrema the difference must alternate in sign, forcing at least \(m\) distinct zeros between them. That contradicts its degree. This minimax argument concerns the node polynomial, and explains the deliberate concentration of nodes near the endpoints.

For \(m=3\) the recommended points are \(0,\pm\sqrt3/2\); their monic factor is \(x^3-3x/4\) with magnitude at most 0.25. The equally spaced alternative \(-1,0,1\) has factor \(x^3-x\), whose largest magnitude is \(2/(3\sqrt3)=0.385\). If data already exist at fixed locations, moving them is seldom an option. Subdividing the interval and fitting lower-degree polynomials locally instead limits long-range coupling. For evaluation at many points, barycentric interpolation can reuse stored node weights while avoiding explicit construction of a large monomial coefficient vector.

Evaluate without forming monomial coefficients

Define barycentric weights \(w_j=1/\prod_{i\ne j}(x_j-x_i)\). Dividing the Lagrange interpolation formula and the identity \(\sum\ell_j=1\) by the common nodal polynomial gives the evaluation ratio \(\sum_j w_j y_j/(x-x_j)\) divided by \(\sum_j w_j/(x-x_j)\). At an exact interpolation node, return its stored value directly. Multiplying all weights by the same constant has no effect, which is useful for numerical scaling.

For nodes \(-1,0,1\) the weights are proportional to \(1,-2,1\). At \(x=1/2\) with data \(2,1,2\), the weighted numerator is \(4/3-4-4=-20/3\), and the denominator is \(2/3-4-2=-16/3\). Their ratio gives 1.25. This evaluates the very same polynomial as the basis construction. Better arithmetic for evaluation cannot remove an intrinsically large Lebesgue constant associated with a poor set of nodes.
