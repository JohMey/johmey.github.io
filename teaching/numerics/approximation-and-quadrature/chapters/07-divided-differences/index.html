Keep the previous fit

A new measurement arrives after a polynomial has been fitted to \(x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1}\). To preserve those measurements, add a multiple of \(\omega_k(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(x-x_j)\). At each old node the correction vanishes. At a new distinct node \(x_k\) its coefficient is fixed by \(c_k=(f(x_k)-p_{k-1}(x_k))/\omega_k(x_k)\). This gives a triangular construction. Once a coefficient has been determined it remains in every subsequent polynomial provided the original order is retained.

The coefficients can also be obtained using a table. Put \(f[x_i]=f(x_i)\) and subtract adjacent entries of the previous order before dividing by the distance between the two outer nodes. The subtraction cancels shared lower-order information. Algebraically the leading coefficient of the polynomial through \(x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k}\) is

\[f[x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k}]=\frac{f[x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{i+k}]-f[x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k-1}]}{x_{i+k}-x_i}.\]

This follows by combining the two lower-degree interpolants with factors \(x-x_i\) and \(x-x_{i+k}\). Their difference divided by the outer spacing agrees with every tabulated value and has the required leading coefficient.

Three temperatures and a test point

Suppose a temperature sensor reports 2, 5 and 17 degrees at 0, 1 and 3 hours. First divided differences are 3 and 6 degrees per hour. The second difference is \((6-3)/3=1\) degree per hour squared. Hence \(p(x)=2+3x+x(x-1)\). At hour 2 this gives 10 degrees; the original straight line would give 8. The correction shows how strongly the additional observation changes that prediction. Neither interpolant by itself certifies that the temperature really did reach its predicted value between readings.

Nested evaluation uses \(2+x[3+(x-1)]\) here. Each new layer adds one multiplication and one addition. Reordering the nodes changes the intermediate table, but uniqueness ensures the same final polynomial in exact arithmetic. Strongly unequal spacings can magnify noise in higher differences and make numerical results sensitive to the order.

Let two nodes coalesce

If a second node approaches the first, the corresponding difference quotient tends to \(f'(x_0)\) for differentiable \(f\). An independently available derivative therefore supplies the diagonal entry directly. For a value 1 and slope 2 at zero and a value 4 at one, the required quadratic is \(1+2x+cx^2\); matching the final value gives \(c=1\). Its prediction at one half is 2.25. Perturb the initial slope by 0.1 while keeping endpoints fixed. The correction becomes \(0.1x(1-x)\), attaining 0.025 at the midpoint. Endpoint agreement alone would miss that uncertainty.

Rescale before comparing coefficients

The coefficients have different units at successive orders. In the temperature example, expressing time in minutes divides the first divided differences by 60 and the second difference by \(60^2\). Their smaller printed magnitudes do not mean the curvature has become less significant. Evaluating the second term at the same physical instant multiplies by two correspondingly larger node differences and recovers exactly the original contribution. Scaling the node interval near unit size can make intermediate arithmetic more convenient; interpret a coefficient together with the product of distances it multiplies.
