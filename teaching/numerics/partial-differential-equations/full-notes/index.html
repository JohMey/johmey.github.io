Chapter 1 · Flux differences for an elliptic balance

Assemble a conservative boundary-value problem from face fluxes, trace the effect of boundary conditions and establish a discrete energy identity.

Place fluxes where conservation uses them

Consider steady conduction on \(0<x<L\) with heat flux \(q=-k(x)T'\) and volumetric generation \(f\). Taking unit cross-sectional area, steady energy balance gives \(q'=f\). On uniform nodes separated by \(h\), integrate the balance over a control segment centred on \(x_i\): \(q_{i+1/2}-q_{i-1/2}\simeq h f_i\). A shared face must have the same numerical flux in both adjacent equations, otherwise summing local equations can create an artificial internal source or sink.

Use \(q_{i+1/2}=-k_{i+1/2}(T_{i+1}-T_i)/h\). Then row \(i\) of the discrete operator has diagonal \((k_{i-1/2}+k_{i+1/2})/h^2\) and off-diagonals \(-k_{i\pm1/2}/h^2\). For positive \(k\) the off-diagonals are negative and the diagonal balances them. With zero source, an unconstrained interior value is a weighted average of its neighbours. This relation explains why a source-free discrete conductor with fixed end temperatures cannot develop an interior temperature larger than both ends.

Compute the face law across an interface

For smooth \(k\) an arithmetic average of neighbouring nodal coefficients provides a second-order face approximation on a uniform grid. A sharp material interface calls for a different physical calculation. Consider two half-segments in series with constants \(k_L,k_R\) and no interface storage or contact resistance. The same flux crosses both halves. Adding their temperature drops gives \(T_L-T_R=q[h/(2k_L)+h/(2k_R)]\), hence \(k_{\rm eff}=2/(1/k_L+1/k_R)\). This is a harmonic average for equal half-widths.

With \(k_L=1,\ k_R=9\) W/(m·K) and a 10 K drop over a 0.1 m pair, the effective conductivity is 1.8 W/(m·K) and the flux is 180 W/m². Using the arithmetic mean would give 500 W/m², severely underestimating the resistance of the poorer conductor. In the limit \(k_L\to0^+\), the physical flux tends to zero while that arithmetic estimate remains finite. For unequal layer thicknesses use the actual layer resistances rather than either unweighted average.

Examine uniqueness and the role of the boundary

For homogeneous fixed temperatures and an interior vector \(u\) extended by zero at both ends, grouping adjacent row contributions gives

\[h\sum_i u_i(A_hu)_i=\sum_{\rm faces}\frac{k_{i+1/2}}h(u_{i+1}-u_i)^2.\]

Each term is nonnegative. Vanishing energy requires a constant vector, and the fixed ends force that constant to be zero. Thus the interior matrix is symmetric positive definite. This reasoning justifies CG and gives a uniqueness argument that does not depend on inspection of a particular numerical matrix. With only prescribed heat fluxes a spatial constant instead remains undetermined. A solution then requires overall heat compatibility and one temperature reference or a mean constraint.

As a check, take constant \(k=1\), \(L=1\), zero end temperatures and generation 2. The exact profile \(T=x(1-x)\) has central value 0.25. Central differences recover its second derivative exactly, so all uniform grid nodes satisfy the balance without truncation error. This successful check verifies signs, boundary handling and the quadratic patch. To measure a convergence order use a smooth field with a nonzero leading truncation term, such as a sine profile, and compare at common physical positions.

Eliminate boundary values with the correct sign

With four intervals on a unit rod, fixed left temperature 10, fixed right temperature 20 and zero source, multiply each row by \(h^2/k\). The three unknowns then satisfy a tridiagonal system with diagonal two, off-diagonal minus one and load \((10,0,20)^T\). Moving a fixed value to the right changes its sign, producing positive boundary contributions here. Solving gives the linearly spaced interior temperatures 12.5, 15 and 17.5. Every discrete face reports the same flux for constant \(k\).

This deliberately simple case isolates boundary elimination and sign conventions. Reversing only one load sign can generate a plausible-looking smooth curve with entirely wrong end-to-end transport. After checking the values, also sum all interior balances and verify cancellation of the internal face contributions. The resulting global statement includes only boundary fluxes and any source integrated over the interior control volumes.

Chapter 2 · Weak forms and finite elements

Derive a variational statement by integration by parts, assemble element matrices for a one-dimensional rod and explain what the Galerkin solution minimises.

Reduce the differentiability required by equilibrium

A rod is fixed at \(x=0\), pushed by a distributed load \(f\) and by an end force \(t\) at \(x=L\). Axial displacement \(u\) satisfies \(-(EAu')'=f\), \(u(0)=0\) and \(EAu'(L)=t\). The product \(EA\) is positive. A trial profile assembled from linear pieces has jumps in its derivative, so differentiating it twice pointwise is unsuitable. Instead multiply the strong equation by a test displacement \(v\) vanishing at the fixed end and integrate by parts once.

The resulting equation is \(\int_0^L EA\,u'v'\,dx=\int_0^L fv\,dx+t v(L)\). Only first derivatives are required. The fixed displacement is imposed directly on trial and test spaces; the end traction appears in the load functional. This clarifies why prescribing both force and displacement independently at the same end can overconstrain a second-order problem. The test functions represent allowable virtual displacements, and the integral identity expresses equality of internal and external virtual work.

Assemble one element and connect it to its neighbour

On an element from \(x_i\) to \(x_{i+1}\) of length \(h\), use shape functions \(N_1=1-s,\ N_2=s\) with \(s=(x-x_i)/h\). Their physical derivatives are \(-1/h,\ 1/h\). For constant \(EA\), integrating products \(EA N'_aN'_b\) gives the matrix \((EA/h)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A constant distributed load contributes \(fh(1,1)^T/2\). Summing element contributions for a shared global node enforces balance there without prescribing the internal traction manually.

Take \(L=1,\ EA=1,\ f=2,\ t=0\) in consistent units and use two equal elements. After eliminating the fixed displacement, the stiffness matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&-2\\-2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and the load is \((1,0.5)^T\). Solving gives \(u(0.5)=0.75,\ u(1)=1\). The exact solution is \(2x-x^2\) and agrees at both nodes. Inside the first element at \(x=0.25\), the linear interpolant gives 0.375, whereas the exact value is 0.4375. Nodal agreement can coexist with a substantial interior error.

Identify the energy that is best approximated

Let \(a(u,v)=\int EA\,u'v'\) and denote the external work by \(\ell(v)\). The discrete solution \(u_h\) satisfies \(a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)\) for every test function from its finite-dimensional space. The continuous solution satisfies the same equation for every such test, so \(a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\). This Galerkin orthogonality means the error is perpendicular to the trial space in the energy inner product. For any other admissible discrete \(w_h\), write \(u-w_h=(u-u_h)+(u_h-w_h)\) and expand its squared energy norm. The cross term vanishes and \(\|u-w_h\|_a^2=\|u-u_h\|_a^2+\|u_h-w_h\|_a^2\).

Thus the conforming Galerkin solution is the best energy approximation in its space for this symmetric linear problem and exact integration. The statement concerns a specific norm and model. It does not guarantee pointwise optimal stresses or carry unchanged into nonsymmetric transport problems. In the rod example the computed strain is constant on each element, replacing a linearly varying exact strain. Refinement improves this derivative field even when a table of nodal displacements already looks exact.

Recover a support reaction

The reaction at a prescribed-displacement node is hidden if one inspects only the reduced interior system. Assemble all element internal forces \(Ku_h\) before discarding constrained rows, then subtract the full external load vector. The entries at free degrees of freedom should be near zero; the entry at a prescribed degree of freedom gives the reaction in the chosen sign convention.

In the uniformly loaded rod, the first element strain is \((0.75-0)/0.5=1.5\). Its internal nodal force at the left is \(-1.5\), while the first node receives an external distributed-load share of \(+0.5\). Their difference \(-2\) is the support reaction. It balances the total applied load \(2\cdot1=2\). Looking only at the derivative in the first element would have reported magnitude 1.5, missing that load share. A reaction from assembled balance and a stress recovered by pointwise differentiation are related but distinct quantities on a finite mesh.

Problem sheet 1 · Flux balances and virtual work

Choose the correct conductance at an interface, test pure-flux compatibility and assemble a rod with unequal stiffnesses.

Preserve heat flow across unequal layers

A wall has two layers, 0.02 m at \(k=2\) W/(m·K) and 0.06 m at \(k=12\) W/(m·K). The outer faces are held at 350 K and 320 K, respectively. Find the common flux, the interface temperature and the effective conductivity over the full thickness.

Worked solution

The resistances per unit area are \(0.02/2=0.01\) and \(0.06/12=0.005\) m²·K/W. Total resistance is 0.015, so the flux magnitude is \(30/0.015=2000\) W/m² from hot to cold. The first drop is \(2000(0.01)=20\) K, giving an interface temperature of 330 K.

The effective conductivity is \(0.08/0.015=5.333\) W/(m·K). Equal-width averaging would ignore the different thicknesses. Substituting this conductivity across the full wall reproduces the same flux.

Check a pure-flux problem

On \(0\leq x\leq2\), the steady flux obeys \(q'=3\) in consistent units. The prescribed endpoint fluxes, both positive along increasing \(x\), are \(q(0)=2\) and \(q(2)=7\). Is a steady state possible? Correct the right flux. For \(k=1\), find a compatible temperature with mean temperature zero.

Worked solution

Integrating requires \(q(2)-q(0)=\int_0^2 3\,dx=6\). The supplied difference is 5, so no steady solution satisfies both data. Take \(q(2)=8\), giving \(q=2+3x\).

Because \(q=-T'\), \(T=C-2x-1.5x^2\). Its average over the interval is \(C-2-2=C-4\), hence \(C=4\). The mean fixes the otherwise arbitrary temperature offset while leaving flux unchanged.

Assemble two rods in series

Two equal elements of unit length have \(EA=2\) and \(EA=6\) in consistent units. Fix the left node, apply a unit tensile load on the right and omit distributed load. Assemble the reduced matrix, solve for displacements and verify the axial forces and strain energy.

Worked solution

Assembly gives \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}8&-6\\-6&6\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(b=(0,1)^T\). The second row gives \(u_2-u_1=1/6\). The first then gives \(2u_1=1\), hence \(u_1=1/2,\ u_2=2/3\).

The forces are \(2(1/2)=1\) and \(6(1/6)=1\). The stored energies sum to \(2(1/2)^2/2+6(1/6)^2/2=1/3\), also \(u^Tb/2=(2/3)/2\). This verifies both continuity of axial force and consistency of the assembled work.

Chapter 3 · Finite volumes and moving fronts

Derive an upwind transport update by conservation, interpret the Courant number as a fraction of cell contents and determine what conservation says about front speed.

Store quantities over cells

Consider a passive concentration satisfying \(u_t+(au)_x=0\) with positive constant speed \(a\) on a periodic line. Let \(\bar u_i=h^{-1}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}u\,dx\) be its cell mean. Integrating the conservation law in space and time shows that every change in cell inventory comes from fluxes through its two faces. If neighbouring cells use the same face flux, summing all updates cancels every internal transfer exactly. On a periodic line the total stays fixed, even for a discontinuous concentration.

For positive speed, material reaching the right face during a short step comes from the cell to its left. Approximate the outgoing state by that cell’s mean, giving \(F_{i+1/2}=a\bar u_i\). Forward Euler in time yields \(\bar u_i^{n+1}=(1-C)\bar u_i^n+C\bar u_{i-1}^n\), where \(C=a\Delta t/h\). For \(0\leq C\leq1\) this is a convex average, preserving the range of neighbouring values. At \(C=1\) the entire array shifts exactly by one cell per step. If \(a<0\), information comes from the other side of the face and the upwind direction must reverse.

Identify the source of artificial spreading

Start with one cell mean equal to one and zeros everywhere else. With \(C=1/2\), one step gives adjacent means \(1/2,1/2\). A second step gives \(1/4,1/2,1/4\), while the total remains one. Exact advection over two such steps shifts the original pulse by one cell with no change in shape. The scheme has conserved mass yet broadened the pulse. Peak height or variance therefore tests information not supplied by a global balance.

For smooth \(u\), Taylor-expand the upwind update in space and time. Replace the leading \(u_{tt}\) using the exact transport equation, \(u_{tt}=a^2u_{xx}\). The leading modified equation is \(u_t+a u_x=[ah(1-C)/2]u_{xx}+\cdots\). The bracket acts as numerical diffusivity. It is positive for the monotone range and vanishes at \(C=1\). This local smooth expansion helps interpret observed spreading, but the Taylor expansion itself does not hold across a jump.

Choose a flux when speed depends on the state

For a nonlinear conservation law \(u_t+f(u)_x=0\), characteristics can intersect and create a discontinuity. Enclose a moving jump in a thin control volume. The net flux imbalance fixes its speed \(s=(f(u_R)-f(u_L))/(u_R-u_L)\). For Burgers flux \(f=u^2/2\) with left state 2 and right state 0, this gives \(s=1\). The faster characteristics on the left overtake the slower ones to form a shock. Reversing these states produces a spreading fan under the entropy condition even though the algebraic jump formula alone would still supply a speed.

A numerical face flux must be consistent when the two cells agree and must incorporate information from both sides when a nonlinear wave can travel either way. A conservative method that converges can reproduce the weak jump balance, while selection of a physically admissible solution also requires an entropy criterion. For practical assessment, monitor total inventory, motion of the front, overshoot and the number of cells over which it is smeared. The four measurements address distinct properties, and reducing one defect need not reduce all of them.

Move the control surface instead of the grid

Across a front moving with speed \(s\), flux in the front’s own frame is \(f(u)-su\). The jump relation enforces equality of this quantity on both sides. For the Burgers jump from 2 to 0 with speed one, the left flux in the moving frame is \(2-1\cdot2=0\), and the right flux is also zero. The laboratory flux differs across the front because a moving interface is sweeping conserved material through stationary space.

This distinction helps when checking a simulation by counting material in a region that tracks a front. Differentiating an integral over moving endpoints adds boundary-motion terms. In a periodic fixed window the update should conserve total amount without those extra terms. In a moving measurement window, physical transport through the window and motion of the window both influence the recorded inventory, even under a perfectly conservative scheme.

Chapter 4 · Time-dependent diffusion

Derive the diffusion time-step restriction from two independent arguments and compare explicit and implicit decay of spatial modes.

Discretise space and time separately

A heat pulse in a thin insulated rod smooths over time according to \(u_t=D u_{xx}\) for constant positive diffusivity \(D\). Use a uniform grid of spacing \(h\), and initially examine interior points or periodic boundaries so the stencil is unchanged everywhere. Replacing the second derivative by a centred difference gives a system of ordinary differential equations for the nodal temperatures. The time method still has to resolve the fastest rate that this spatial operator can generate.

An explicit Euler step yields \(u_i^{n+1}=r u_{i-1}^n+(1-2r)u_i^n+r u_{i+1}^n\) with \(r=D\Delta t/h^2\). If \(0\leq r\leq1/2\), the update is a convex combination of three temperatures. The maximum cannot rise and the minimum cannot fall in a source-free interior step. Starting with one hot node at 1 and neighbours at zero, \(r=0.4\) gives a centre value 0.2 and neighbours 0.4 after a single step. At \(r=0.6\) the centre would become negative although the continuum heat equation preserves nonnegativity.

Identify the spatial modes that set the limit

Insert \(u_j^n=g^n e^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}\), where \(\mathrm{i}\) is the imaginary unit and \(j\) indexes the grid. The two neighbouring values contribute factors \(e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\theta}\), yielding \(g=1-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Long waves have \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\simeq\theta^2/4\), so their decay approaches the continuum rate as the mesh is refined. The alternating grid mode \(\theta=\pi\) has the most negative amplification and enforces \(r\leq1/2\) for bounded magnitude.

At the limiting value \(r=1/2\) the alternating mode flips sign without losing amplitude. A strict margin damps it. For \(D=10^{-4}\) m²/s and \(h=2\) mm, the maximum explicit step is \(h^2/(2D)=0.020\) s. Refining to 1 mm reduces that limit to 0.005 s. The mesh gives a sharper temperature profile yet can cost four times more steps over the same physical interval, besides the increased number of spatial degrees of freedom.

Compare backward Euler and Crank–Nicolson

Writing the semidiscrete system as \(u'=-A_hu\), backward Euler solves \((I+\Delta t A_h)u^{n+1}=u^n\). Crank–Nicolson averages the old and new diffusive fluxes, giving \((I+\Delta t A_h/2)u^{n+1}=(I-\Delta t A_h/2)u^n\). For a Fourier mode put \(s=4r\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Their factors are \(1/(1+s)\) and \((1-s/2)/(1+s/2)\), respectively. Both remain bounded at arbitrarily large \(s\), but Crank–Nicolson may alternate for under-resolved sharp features.

With \(s=20\), backward Euler retains only \(1/21\) per step. Crank–Nicolson yields \(-9/11\), retaining much more of the fast component and reversing its sign. For a smooth low-frequency mode Crank–Nicolson generally gives more accurate temporal decay because it is second order. Decide whether early sharp transients or later smooth behaviour is the relevant physical quantity. To identify spatial and temporal error separately, refine \(\Delta t\) first on a fixed mesh, then refine space with time error kept well below spatial error. Merely halving both at once cannot reveal which term dominated the original discrepancy.

Apply a prescribed heat input without confusing it with temperature

An insulated periodic rod with uniform heating rate \(s\) measured in K/s has an average temperature that must rise by \(s\Delta t\) each step. Sum the centred second differences over the periodic indices: each nodal value appears with coefficients \(1,-2,1\), which cancel. The source alone changes the mean. For \(s=3\) K/s and a step of 0.02 s the numerical average must increase by 0.06 K, independently of the diffusivity and initial profile.

A code can pass this global test while still mishandling short-wave damping, since an alternating error has zero spatial mean. Pair a mean-temperature test with a nonconstant mode-decay test. Use a homogeneous periodic run for isolates of diffusion, and a uniform temperature with prescribed source for the storage term. A failure in one test then identifies a different part of the mathematical balance than a failure in the other.

Problem sheet 2 · Fronts and diffusive modes

Track conservation on a periodic mesh, distinguish shocks from expanding fans and choose a time step for an explicit diffusion solver.

Advect a marked volume

On a periodic ring of four cells take means \((1,0,0,0)\), positive speed and Courant number \(C=1/4\). Compute two first-order upwind updates, verify inventory and compare the maximum with that from an exact translation of a square pulse by half a cell.

Worked solution

The first update is \((3/4,1/4,0,0)\). Applying the same convex average gives \((9/16,6/16,1/16,0)\). Both arrays sum to one, so the total conserved amount is one cell width times this sum.

After an exact shift of half a cell, the two overlapping cell averages are \(1/2,1/2\) and the other two vanish, although the pointwise pulse height is still one. The numerical maximum of \(9/16\) is larger than the exact cell-average peak at this instant, even as the numerical support is more spread out. A peak comparison must specify whether it uses point values or cell means.

Identify a shock and a fan

For \(f(u)=u^2/2\), consider left and right states 3 and 1. Compute the discontinuity speed, compare it with the two characteristic speeds and locate its position after 0.4 s from the origin. Reverse the states and identify the edges of the entropy-admissible solution.

Worked solution

Fluxes are 4.5 and 0.5, giving \(s=(0.5-4.5)/(1-3)=2\). Since the characteristic speeds \(f'(u)\) are 3 on the left and 1 on the right, both enter the discontinuity in its moving frame. Its position is \(0.4(2)=0.8\).

With left 1 and right 3 the characteristics move apart and an expansion fan fills the region between \(x=0.4\) and \(x=1.2\). A single jump satisfying the algebraic jump balance would have the wrong entropy behaviour for these data.

Allocate a time budget on a refined grid

On a periodic rod \(D=2\times10^{-5}\) m²/s and \(h=1\) mm. Find the explicit diffusion step limit. To reach 1 s with \(r=0.4\), how many steps are needed? Repeat after halving spatial spacing and compare the total number of nodal updates on a fixed length.

Worked solution

The stability limit is \(h^2/(2D)=0.025\) s. For \(r=0.4\), use \(0.4h^2/D=0.020\) s, or 50 steps. Halving \(h\) quarters the step to 0.005 s, giving 200 steps.

There are about twice as many spatial nodes on the refined line. The combination of twice as many nodes and four times as many steps requires about eight times as many local updates, neglecting boundary and setup costs.

Chapter 5 · Wave speeds and numerical dispersion

Derive the leapfrog dispersion relation, use the Courant condition to distinguish boundedness from accurate travel time and initialise a standing-wave calculation consistently.

Distinguish transport of phase from transport of energy

A taut string with small transverse displacements and fixed tension obeys \(u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}\). The physical speed \(c\) is independent of wavelength in this ideal one-dimensional model. A discrete solver can transport long waves at nearly the correct speed while short waves arrive late. In a pulse comprising many wavelengths, this artificial dependence on frequency distorts its shape even in a stable computation. A phase comparison at a probe provides information beyond monitoring maximum amplitude.

Use centred second differences in both time and space, with \(C=c\Delta t/h\). Solving explicitly for the new level gives \(u_i^{n+1}=2u_i^n-u_i^{n-1}+C^2(u_{i-1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i+1}^n)\). To initialise, expand \(u(\Delta t)=u(0)+\Delta t\,v(0)+(\Delta t)^2 c^2u_{xx}(0)/2+\cdots\). Replacing \(u_{xx}(0)\) by the same spatial difference gives a first level consistent with the initial velocity and acceleration. Simply copying the starting displacement to level one can introduce a spurious launch velocity.

Derive the discrete dispersion relation

Take a travelling mode \(u_i^n=\exp[\mathrm{i}(i\theta-n\omega_h\Delta t)]\) with spatial phase \(\theta=kh\). Inserting it makes the temporal second difference proportional to \(-4\sin^2(\omega_h\Delta t/2)\) and the spatial second difference proportional to \(-4\sin^2(\theta/2)\). Thus on the positive-frequency branch

\[\sin(\omega_h\Delta t/2)=C\sin(kh/2).\]

A real \(\omega_h\) exists for all resolvable real spatial phases only when \(C\leq1\). If \(C>1\), the shortest wave makes the right-hand side exceed one; a temporal amplification root then lies outside the unit circle. Even when stable, the ratio of numerical to physical phase speed is \(2\arcsin[C\sin(\theta/2)]/(C\theta)\). A fine spatial sample makes \(\theta\) small and the ratio tends to one. At \(C=1\) the ideal uniform one-dimensional interior stencil reproduces the wave phase on the resolved branch. Boundaries, material transitions and multiple dimensions require separate analysis.

Estimate a travel-time error

Take \(C=0.5\) and four points per wavelength, so \(\theta=\pi/2\). Then \(\omega_h\Delta t=2\arcsin[0.5\sin(\pi/4)]=0.72273\) rad. The physical advance is \(C\theta=\pi/4=0.78540\) rad. The numerical phase speed is about 0.920 of the true speed. After ten physical periods, its accumulated phase is about 9.20 cycles. A stable solution can therefore be nearly one oscillation behind while retaining a visually plausible amplitude.

Group velocity is \(d\omega_h/dk\), obtained by differentiating the dispersion relation. On this branch \(v_g/c=\cos(\theta/2)/\sqrt{1-C^2\sin^2(\theta/2)}\), about 0.756 in the four-point example. A narrow-band packet’s envelope and an individual crest then propagate at different numerical speeds. When interpreting a probe trace, say whether the reported arrival corresponds to a crest, an envelope or a threshold. Repeat the calculation at additional points per wavelength using the same Courant number to isolate spatial-phase improvement from changes in the time integration.

Interpret the grid’s travelling domain of dependence

In one explicit wave step, a node uses only itself and its two nearest neighbours. After \(n\) steps its numerical domain of dependence extends at most \(n\) grid cells to either side. The continuous wave at the same time can depend on initial data a distance \(cn\Delta t\) away. For convergence that physical domain must fit inside the numerical one, giving \(c\Delta t\leq h\). This reproduces the Courant condition from geometry without inserting a Fourier mode. It is a necessary information-speed test; by itself it does not establish stability or accuracy of an arbitrary scheme.

As an example, with \(c=340\) m/s and \(h=0.01\) m, the step bound is about 29.4 microseconds. A 20 microsecond step satisfies it but need not give an accurate answer for a mode represented by only three or four points per wavelength. Time-of-flight information and wavelength resolution are related to different discretisation choices. Document both when comparing two wave calculations.

Chapter 6 · Verification, refinement and local error

Use a manufactured solution to verify an implementation, estimate observed convergence and identify when adaptivity is needed for the physical quantity of interest.

Build a test with a known answer

Consider \(-\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)+\kappa u=f\) on the unit square. Select the exact field \(u_\star=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\) and prescribe its value at the boundary. Now choose \(D=1+x\), \(\kappa=2\), using coordinates normalised to a metre. Differentiation gives \(\Delta u_\star=-2\pi^2u_\star\) and \(u_{\star,x}=\pi\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\). The required source is therefore

\[f=[2(1+x)\pi^2+2]u_\star-\pi\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).\]

The derivative of \(D\) is essential: inserting only the local value of \(D\) times the Laplacian specifies a different differential equation. Manufacturing a source tests whether the code solves the stated model and boundary conditions. Experimental validation asks a separate question about fidelity to physical observations. A polished plot resembling the exact field does not substitute for a numerical error measure with defined sample positions and units.

Read a three-grid study

On successively halved mesh spacings suppose the error in one norm follows \(E_h=C h^p+o(h^p)\). Taking ratios gives \(p\simeq\log_2(E_h/E_{h/2})\). Three grids provide two such estimates, indicating whether a consistent asymptotic trend is present. A single ratio may result from cancellation between terms of different order. State the norm, grid location, geometry representation, time step if applicable, and the residual tolerance. Comparing a cell average on one grid with a nodal peak on another can produce a meaningless rate.

For the variable-diffusion square, maximum nodal errors at 24, 48 and 96 intervals per axis are approximately \(1.33345\times10^{-3},3.33763\times10^{-4},8.34283\times10^{-5}\). The observed orders are approximately 1.998 and 2.000. The finest-grid algebraic residual is already far below the field error in its respective normalisation. Tightening the iteration tolerance alone will not remove the leading spatial defect. Raising the sine mode while holding the mesh fixed increases derivatives of the exact solution and tests a different resolution regime. Inspect the grid and mode experiment.

Decide where further resolution matters

A manufactured sine is smooth everywhere. Actual domains may contain a re-entrant corner, a material jump or a narrow source. Derivative bounds used in theoretical convergence estimates can fail locally there. Uniformly halving a two-dimensional mesh roughly quadruples the cell count while spending most new cells away from a localised feature. Local refinement attempts to concentrate new degrees of freedom where they matter to a chosen output. Which cells merit refinement depends on the equation, boundary data and the output being measured.

For a conforming finite-element approximation, one class of error indicators combines an element residual with jumps in normal flux across interior faces. The residual tests whether each element satisfies the differential balance, while the jumps measure disagreements between neighbouring reconstructed fluxes. In a constant-coefficient Poisson example, a piecewise linear solution can have zero elementwise Laplacian even when it bends globally; the slope changes then appear at interelement faces. An indicator using only the interior derivative would miss this information. Mesh-length factors and coefficient weights are necessary to combine the terms in a specific norm.

Finish a refinement study by tracking the physical observable itself. If the objective is integrated heat flow, a changing maximum pointwise gradient near one corner may have little influence on that integral. Conversely, apparently stable domain averages can hide an unresolved peak. Report observed change with resolution and an independent balance or analytic check, specifying exactly which quantity has been verified.

Check refinement at an identical location

Suppose a displacement is reported at the centre of a square. On an even grid that point may coincide with a node, whereas on an odd grid it can lie at the centre of a cell. Taking the maximum over mesh nodes produces a different sample on each grid. A refinement curve can then combine field discretisation error with the movement of the evaluation point. Choose a fixed physical point and evaluate each solution there with its own consistent interpolation, or compare a well-defined integral over the same physical region.

Similarly, compare gradients on compatible supports. Nodal averages of element stresses can look smoother than the individual element values, yet smoothing can alter extrema and convergence rates. Record the native support and any recovery operator. This makes an apparent improvement reproducible and gives students enough information to explain a discrepancy between two otherwise credible simulation reports.

Problem sheet 3 · Wave resolution and verification

Prescribe the first time level of a vibrating string, measure a convergence order and decide what a residual reveals under grid refinement.

Release a mode from rest

For a unit string with fixed ends take initial shape \(u_i^0=\sin(\pi i/N)\) and initial velocity zero. Derive the factor relating \(u_i^1\) to \(u_i^0\) for the centred wave scheme with Courant number \(C\). Evaluate it for \(N=4,\ C=0.5\).

Worked solution

The central second difference of the sine is \(-4\sin^2(\pi/(2N))u_i^0\) before dividing by \(h^2\). The first-step Taylor formula therefore gives \(u_i^1=[1-2C^2\sin^2(\pi/(2N))]u_i^0\).

At \(N=4,C=0.5\), the factor is \(1-0.5\sin^2(\pi/8)=0.92678\). Copying the initial displacement instead would omit the physical acceleration produced by the curved shape.

Quantify a grid-dependent observable

A computed outflow on meshes \(h,h/2,h/4\) is 2.080, 2.020 and 2.005 W. Assuming an asymptotic \(Q_h=Q+c h^p\), estimate \(p\), the extrapolated flux and the fine-grid error. Can those figures alone demonstrate conservation to the same tolerance?

Worked solution

The successive differences are 0.060 and 0.015 W, whose ratio is 4. Thus \(2^p=4,\ p=2\). Extrapolate \(Q=2.005+(2.005-2.020)/3=2.000\) W, giving estimated fine-grid error +0.005 W.

A separate conservation check must sum inflows, outflows, sources and storage. A sequence of outflow values can converge cleanly even under a method that systematically loses heat elsewhere.

Relate flux jumps to a residual indicator

A unit rod is approximated by two equal linear elements with nodal values \((0,1,1)\) and \(k=1\). Compute each element’s flux, the flux jump at their interface, and the elementwise second derivative. In a source-free steady model, explain what a check based only on that second derivative would miss.

Worked solution

The slopes are 2 and 0, giving fluxes −2 and 0 in the positive coordinate direction. The right-element flux minus the left-element flux is therefore 2. Second derivatives vanish inside both linear pieces.

In a source-free steady conductor the same flux must cross the interface from either element. An interior derivative check would report zero inside each element while ignoring the change at the joining node. An indicator including interface flux mismatch detects this loss of balance.

Chapter 7 · Interfaces, contact resistance and Robin data

Derive a face flux that transports the same heat through two different materials and through a convective boundary.

One flux through resistances in series

Let \(q=-kT'\) be heat flux in the positive coordinate direction, with unit cross-sectional area and no internal source. Between two cell centres separated by an interface, let the left and right centre-to-interface distances be \(d_L,d_R\). Piecewise constant conductivities \(k_L,k_R>0\) make the equilibrium temperature linear in each segment. If the interface has perfect contact, its temperature \(T_f\) is continuous. Integrating Fourier's law on each side gives \(T_L-T_f=q\,d_L/k_L\) and \(T_f-T_R=q\,d_R/k_R\). Adding eliminates the unknown interface temperature.

\[q=\frac{T_L-T_R}{d_L/k_L+d_R/k_R}.\]

For equal distances this amounts to using the harmonic mean conductivity over the full centre spacing. Inserting the same signed \(q\) as the outflow of the left cell and the inflow of the right cell produces cancellation when their balances are added. If a material interface stores energy or supplies heat independently, a flux jump belongs in the balance and this steady series calculation must be modified.

Measure the temperature drop

Take \(d_L=d_R=0.01\) m, \(k_L=2,\ k_R=10\) W/(m K) and \(T_L=30,\ T_R=18\) °C. The series resistance per unit area is \(0.005+0.001=0.006\) m²K/W. The flux is \(2000\) W/m² and the interface temperature is \(30-2000(0.005)=20\) °C. The lower conductivity carries the larger temperature drop despite carrying the same flux. Using the arithmetic mean 6 with the centre difference over 0.02 m instead predicts \(3600\) W/m².

Introduce a contact resistance \(R_c=0.002\) m²K/W. Its temperature jump is \(qR_c\); the total resistance rises to 0.008, reducing the heat flux to 1500 W/m². The two sides of the contact now have temperatures 22.5 and 19.5 °C. Imposing a single interface temperature on this model would remove the physically measured 3 K jump.

Apply a convective boundary

At an exterior face the outward heat flux obeys \(q=h_c(T_s-T_\infty)\), a Robin condition. Eliminate the unknown surface temperature as above to obtain \(q=(T_P-T_\infty)/(d/k+1/h_c)\). If \(h_c\) tends to zero, the boundary becomes insulating. With very large \(h_c\), the surface approaches ambient temperature while the finite centre-to-face resistance remains. For \(d=0.01\) m, \(k=2\), \(h_c=100\) W/(m² K), \(T_P-T_\infty=15\) K, the predicted flux is 1000 W/m² and the surface is 10 K above ambient. Multiplying by face area converts this flux to the power used in a cell energy balance. Mixing up these two quantities makes the result depend incorrectly on mesh area.

A jump located inside a cell

If the materials change at a known position inside a segment, the fractions of that segment matter. Moving the interface so that 75 percent of the length has conductivity 2 and 25 percent has conductivity 10 produces effective conductivity \(1/(0.75/2+0.25/10)=2.5\) W/(m K). Giving each material half the segment instead would give \(10/3\) W/(m K). The harmonic rule depends on actual layer thicknesses. A mesh-refinement study that snaps the interface to different positions at every refinement can mix geometric representation error with flux-discretisation error.

Chapter 8 · Anisotropic diffusion and mesh alignment

Relate a diffusion tensor to principal directions and test whether a face-flux approximation can represent a rotated material.

Begin with an energy and a constitutive law

In a conducting sheet with directional properties, heat flux is \(\boldsymbol q=-K\nabla T\), where \(K\) is symmetric positive definite. With constant volumetric capacity, conservation gives a diffusion operator proportional to \(\nabla\cdot(K\nabla T)\). For insulating boundaries and no sources, multiplying by \(T\) and integrating by parts gives a decay term \(-\int\nabla T^TK\nabla T\). It is nonpositive because the eigenvalues of \(K\) are positive. This energy test checks the sign of a discrete spatial operator even when flux and gradient do not point along the same line.

Let the principal conductivities be 4 and 1. Rotating the principal axes through 45 degrees gives \(K=R\operatorname{diag}(4,1)R^T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2.5&1.5\\1.5&2.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Along the diagonal direction \((1,1)/\sqrt2\), multiplying by \(K\) scales the gradient fourfold; along the other diagonal the scale is one. Along the Cartesian \(x\) direction a transverse flux component appears. The off-diagonal entries express a real constitutive response and cannot be dropped by refining the same incorrect stencil.

A patch test for a face flux

Use the exact affine temperature \(T(x,y)=y\), whose gradient is \((0,1)^T\). The flux is \((-1.5,-2.5)^T\). A vertical face with outward normal \(e_x\) therefore carries \(-1.5\) units of flux per unit length. A two-point formula based only on temperatures at the left and right cell centres sees identical temperatures and returns zero. Both fields have zero divergence in the interior because each flux is constant, so checking interior residuals alone would miss the incorrect boundary transport. Compare individual face fluxes as well as cell residuals in a linear patch test.

To recover the normal flux one can reconstruct the full gradient using neighbouring data, or choose a weak form integrating \((\nabla v)^TK\nabla T\) over each element. On a triangle with affine basis functions the gradients are constant, so constant \(K\) yields an element matrix \(A_{ij}=|E|\nabla\phi_i^TK\nabla\phi_j\). Symmetry is immediate and the row sums vanish because the basis functions sum to one.

Distinguish length scales from transport axes

For constant \(K\), a Fourier mode \(e^{i\xi\cdot x}\) decays with factor \(e^{-t\xi^TK\xi}\) when capacity is one. At unit wave number, a mode along the fast principal axis falls to \(e^{-4}\approx0.0183\) after one unit of time; a perpendicular one retains \(e^{-1}\approx0.368\). This test excites the two conductivities separately. On an elongated mesh the mesh dimensions also affect the fastest discrete time scale, so the same physical tensor can require different explicit step sizes on two grids.

Rotate the temperature, keeping the material fixed

The directional heat transport along a unit vector \(n\) is controlled by \(n^TKn\) when the imposed temperature gradient is parallel to \(n\). For the worked tensor and \(n=(1,0)^T\), this effective conductivity is 2.5 even though the full flux has a transverse component. If the specimen instead has insulated long sides, then its transverse gradient can adjust until transverse flux vanishes. Solving \(1.5T_x+2.5T_y=0\) gives \(T_y=-0.6T_x\) and effective longitudinal flux \(-1.6T_x\). The same material therefore gives different one-dimensional reductions under different boundary constraints.

Problem sheet 4 · Material interfaces and anisotropy

Recover face temperatures and fluxes, and use a linear temperature field to test a rotated conductivity tensor.

Add an interface contact

Two cell centres at 45 and 15 °C each lie 0.02 m from a contact. The conductivities are 4 and 2 W/(m K), and the contact resistance is 0.005 m²K/W. Find flux, both surface temperatures and the conducted power over 0.03 m².

Worked solution

The resistances are \(0.02/4=0.005,\ 0.005,\ 0.02/2=0.010\) m²K/W in series. Total 0.020 gives \(q=30/0.020=1500\) W/m² toward the colder cell.

The hot-side face is \(45-1500(0.005)=37.5\) °C. The cold-side face is \(37.5-7.5=30\) °C, and the last drop is 15 K. Power is \(1500(0.03)=45\) W.

Identify a convective resistance

The centre temperature is 50 °C, ambient is 20 °C and outward flux is 1200 W/m². The half-cell resistance is 0.01 m²K/W. Determine surface temperature and boundary heat-transfer coefficient. Test the prediction after halving the centre-to-face distance while keeping centre and ambient values fixed.

The surface is \(50-1200(0.01)=38\) °C. Boundary resistance is \((38-20)/1200=0.015\) m²K/W, giving \(h_c=66.67\) W/(m²K).

Test a rotated flux

Take \(K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&-1\\-1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(T=2x+y\). Find both flux components and eigenvalues. Which diagonal plane-wave direction decays faster, and what normal flux should an east-facing face record?

\(K\nabla T=(5,1)^T\), so the flux is \((-5,-1)^T\). The principal conductivities are 2 along \((1,1)\) and 4 along \((1,-1)\).

Chapter 9 · Cell Péclet number and monotonicity

Derive the sign restriction for centred transport, identify a numerical boundary layer and quantify the diffusion introduced by upwinding.

Which length sets the balance?

Model a steady scalar on \([0,L]\) with \(a u'-\kappa u''=0\), constant speed \(a>0\) and diffusivity \(\kappa>0\). Advection and diffusion balance over length \(\ell=\kappa/a\). On a mesh with spacing \(h\), the cell Péclet number \(P=ah/\kappa\) measures how many layer lengths fit into one cell. This definition uses the full cell spacing; some conventions divide it by two. Stating the convention matters when quoting a threshold.

Write the centred interior equation in flux form. Its coefficients multiplying \(u_{j-1},u_j,u_{j+1}\) are \(-\kappa/h^2-a/(2h),2\kappa/h^2,-\kappa/h^2+a/(2h)\). For \(P\le2\), the neighbour coefficients are nonpositive and the row sum is zero, so each interior value is a weighted average of its neighbours. For \(P>2\), one weight changes sign. The discrete maximum principle can fail even though the centred derivative is formally second-order accurate on smooth resolved fields.

Watch a node cross below the boundary range

Take \(L=1,a=1,\kappa=0.1\), \(u(0)=0,u(1)=1\) and two equal cells. Then \(h=0.5,P=5\). At the only interior node the coefficients are \(-1.4,0.8,0.6\). The numerical value is \(-0.75\), outside the prescribed boundary range. The exact solution is \((e^{ax/\kappa}-1)/(e^{aL/\kappa}-1)\), increasing and close to zero except near the outlet. At \(x=0.5\) it is \(1/(e^5+1)\approx0.00669\). The coarse grid does not resolve a physical layer of width 0.1.

Using the upstream difference \((u_j-u_{j-1})/h\) changes the coefficients to \(-2.4,2.8,-0.4\); the computed midpoint is \(1/7\). The value lies in range but the layer is excessively broad. A monotone method can replace an oscillation with a large smoothing error. Plot the magnitude of the profile at fixed physical locations while refining to distinguish those outcomes.

Explain the extra diffusion

Expand \(u_{j-1}=u_j-hu'_j+h^2u''_j/2+\cdots\). The upstream derivative is \(u'_j-hu''_j/2+O(h^2)\). The leading modified equation therefore contains effective diffusivity \(\kappa+ah/2\). In the example the artificial addition is 0.25, larger than the physical diffusivity 0.1. Reducing \(h\) below 0.2 restores the coefficient-sign requirement for the centred formula, while resolving the layer shape accurately generally needs several cells across \(\ell\). A convergence study should report both bounds compliance and the actual layer width or flux.

Fit the face flux to a constant-coefficient cell

For smooth constant coefficients the total flux \(J=au-\kappa u'\) is constant in a source-free cell. Solve this first-order equation between endpoints separated by \(h\). The exact relation is \(u_R=e^P u_L+(J/a)(1-e^P)\), hence \(J=a(e^P u_L-u_R)/(e^P-1)\). At large positive \(P\), the flux approaches \(a u_L\), the value carried from upstream. As \(P\) tends to zero, expansion gives a diffusive difference \(-\kappa(u_R-u_L)/h\) plus the centred advective contribution \(a(u_L+u_R)/2\). This exponential fitting uses additional local knowledge of the differential equation. It reproduces the constant-coefficient cell relation exactly, but variable material properties and sources require new local approximations.

Chapter 10 · Incompressibility and discrete pressure modes

Derive the velocity–pressure block system and use a periodic mesh to detect a pressure pattern that exerts no discrete force.

Pressure enforces a constraint

For a steady incompressible viscous fluid, one simplified model is \(-\mu\Delta u+\nabla p=f\), \(\nabla\cdot u=0\), with prescribed wall velocity. Multiplying momentum by a velocity test function vanishing at the wall and integrating by parts gives \(\mu\int\nabla v:\nabla u-\int p\,\nabla\cdot v=\int f\cdot v\). Testing incompressibility with a pressure test function completes a coupled weak formulation. Adding a constant to pressure leaves its gradient unchanged, so use a reference pressure or impose zero mean in a closed domain.

Choose discrete velocity and pressure spaces, and define \(B\) as the negative weak divergence. After eliminating prescribed velocities, the block equations are \(Au+B^Tp=f,\ Bu=0\), with \(A\) positive definite under these boundary conditions. Eliminating velocity yields the pressure operator \(BA^{-1}B^T\). Its quadratic form is \((B^Tp)^TA^{-1}(B^Tp)\). A nonconstant pressure whose discrete gradient vanishes therefore survives as a spurious null mode, even if the velocity block is exceptionally well conditioned.

Test a checkerboard

On an even periodic one-dimensional grid use the collocated centred gradient \((p_{j+1}-p_{j-1})/(2h)\). Choose \(p_j=(-1)^j\). The two neighbouring pressures match, so the gradient is zero at every velocity point. The pressure has zero mean and amplitude one, making it independent of the constant pressure ambiguity. For values \(1,-1,1,-1\) and \(h=0.25\), the result is still the identically zero vector. A pressure solver applied to such a discretisation can retain arbitrary amounts of this alternating pattern without changing the velocity residual.

Placing velocities on the faces instead leads to a face gradient \((p_{j+1}-p_j)/h\). On the same four-cell grid its magnitudes are all 8 with alternating signs. This layout couples the previously invisible pressure variation to the velocity unknowns. The one-dimensional example diagnoses an algebraic pairing issue. In higher dimensions the geometry, boundaries and full selection of discrete spaces also affect stability.

A uniform coupling requirement

A family of stable mixed spaces requires a lower bound, independent of mesh size, on the largest discrete divergence pairing a normalised pressure can obtain with a normalised velocity test. This is the discrete inf-sup condition, with pressures measured after removing their constant ambiguity. It excludes both exact checkerboards and nearly invisible modes that become worse as the mesh is refined. A standard finite-element pairing on suitable triangulations combines continuous quadratic velocities with continuous linear pressures. Other pairings need a separate stability argument or stabilising terms.

Check discrete divergence and momentum, then inspect pressure on grid-scale patterns. A smooth velocity, or even a small residual in both block equations, provides very little information about the amplitude of a pressure component on which those equations have no sensitivity.

Give pressure a reference without changing forces

Replacing a four-cell periodic pressure vector \((2,4,6,8)^T\) by its zero-mean representative \((-3,-1,1,3)^T\) leaves every face difference unchanged. The mean removal fixes one arbitrary pressure level. Adding the checkerboard \((1,-1,1,-1)^T\) also preserves its mean, yet changes the individual face differences. This distinction makes a practical diagnostic: verify a constant shift has no physical effect, then test whether a nonconstant alternating shift produces a physically nonzero force in the chosen velocity space. Passing the former test alone cannot reveal uncoupled pressure coordinates.

Problem sheet 5 · Transport weights and pressure spaces

Locate the onset of a sign error and test which discrete pressure differences actually drive a velocity.

Resolve a layer

Let \(a=3\) m/s, \(\kappa=0.06\) m²/s on a 1 m interval. Find the minimum number of equal cells for the centred operator's nonpositive neighbour coefficients, and the number giving five cells per physical layer length. Estimate the upwind artificial diffusivity at each resolution.

Worked solution

The layer length is \(\ell=\kappa/a=0.02\) m. The sign test requires \(h\le2\ell=0.04\) m, hence at least 25 cells. Five cells across \(\ell\) require \(h\le0.004\) m or 250 cells.

The upwind addition \(ah/2\) is 0.06 m²/s at 25 cells and 0.006 at 250. The monotonicity threshold and satisfactory physical resolution describe quite different demands.

Change transport direction

On an equally spaced grid take \(a<0\). Derive the upstream discrete derivative, its leading artificial diffusivity in \(a u'-\kappa u''\), and the boundary near which a thin outflow layer forms for fixed unequal Dirichlet values.

Worked solution

With flow toward decreasing \(x\), upstream information lies to the right, giving \((u_{j+1}-u_j)/h=u'_j+hu''_j/2+O(h^2)\).

Multiplying by a negative speed gives the modified coefficient \(-(\kappa+|a|h/2)u''\). On \([0,L]\) inflow is at \(L\) and outflow at zero. The term \(e^{ax/\kappa}\) decays away from zero, locating a possible outflow boundary layer there.

Find the pressure nullspace

For an even periodic grid with six pressure nodes, solve \(p_{j+1}-p_{j-1}=0\) for all \(j\). Remove the constant ambiguity and give the dimension of the remaining nullspace. Repeat the argument for five nodes.

Worked solution

The relation is \(p_{j+2}=p_j\). Six nodes split into independent even and odd cycles, giving two arbitrary amplitudes. Removing the constant leaves one alternating mode.

With five nodes, increments of two cycle through every index, so all entries agree. Only the constant remains and zero mean removes it. This finite odd-grid observation by itself does not establish a mesh-independent mixed stability bound.

Chapter 11 · Multigrid and coarse-grid correction

Combine smoothing with a coarse residual equation and compute which components a two-grid correction can remove exactly.

Two kinds of persistent error

For a uniform Dirichlet grid let \(A_h\) be the positive three-point approximation to \(-u''\). A smooth algebraic error changes slowly between adjacent nodes. Its local residual can be modest even when its amplitude is appreciable. High-frequency errors have large second differences and respond rapidly to a suitable relaxation sweep. Weighted Jacobi uses \(u\leftarrow u+\omega D^{-1}(f-A_hu)\), with diagonal \(D=2I/h^2\). On a sine mode of grid angle \(\theta\) its error multiplier is \(1-\omega(1-\cos\theta)\).

For \(\omega=2/3\), this multiplier lies between \(-1/3\) and \(1/3\) for angles between \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\). Low angles have multipliers near one, so repeated sweeps alone cost increasingly many iterations as the grid is refined. Those surviving smooth errors can often be represented on a coarser mesh. The purpose of restricting a residual is to solve an error equation for that representation.

Derive the correction

Let \(P\) interpolate coarse values to the fine grid and \(R\) restrict a fine vector. Choose the Galerkin coarse operator \(A_H=RA_hP\). Given current residual \(r=f-A_hu\), solve \(A_He_H=Rr\) and add \(Pe_H\). If the true error is precisely \(Pv\), the right-hand side equals \(A_Hv\). An exact coarse solve recovers \(v\) and removes the entire error in one correction. For general errors the new residual satisfies \(Rr_{\rm new}=0\); high-frequency components may remain on the fine grid.

Compute one correction by hand

Take \(h=1/4\) with three interior fine nodes and one interior coarse node at the midpoint. Linear interpolation is \(P=(1/2,1,1/2)^T\), and full weighting gives \(R=P^T/2\). The fine operator is 16 times the tridiagonal matrix with diagonal 2 and off-diagonal −1. Multiplication gives \(A_hP=(0,16,0)^T\) and \(A_H=8\). Suppose \(u=0\) and the exact fine solution is \(P\), so the load is \((0,16,0)^T\). The restricted residual is 8. The coarse correction returns the coefficient one and removes the error exactly.

For error \(e=(1,0,-1)^T\), the fine residual is \(32e\), whose restriction is zero. The coarse grid cannot correct this component. Its grid angle is \(\pi/2\), so one weighted Jacobi sweep leaves \(e/3\). These two vectors demonstrate why combining the two operations matters. Repeating the idea recursively gives a V-cycle. With a fixed number of smoothing steps per level, geometrically shrinking grids have total work proportional to the finest number of unknowns, provided coarse solvers and transfers also have linear cost. Good convergence rates still depend on smoothing and approximation properties for the actual operator.

Coarse linear solves do not have to be exact at every level

In the three-node example with fine error \(P\), suppose an approximate coarse solve returns 0.9 instead of one. The correction leaves \(0.1P\), so the restricted residual falls from 8 to 0.8. A recursive V-cycle effectively uses one approximate correction of this kind on intermediate levels; only the smallest system is usually solved directly. Measure the residual reduction per cycle across a family of grids. A roughly constant work per unknown is valuable only if the number of cycles required for a fixed reduction also remains controlled.

Chapter 12 · Adjoints and errors in a chosen output

Weight equation residuals by the response of a chosen observation and distinguish algebraic error from mesh error.

A residual has a direction

Let \(Au=b\) be an invertible linear discrete model and let \(Q(u)=c^Tu\) report a scalar output, for example a weighted mean temperature. For a computed approximation \(\tilde u\), set \(r=b-A\tilde u\). The error is \(A^{-1}r\), and hence \(Q(u)-Q(\tilde u)=c^TA^{-1}r\). Define the adjoint or dual solution by \(A^Tz=c\). Then the exact output identity is

\[Q(u)-Q(\tilde u)=z^Tr.\]

A single adjoint gives the output effect of many possible residuals. A large residual where the observation has low sensitivity can contribute less than a small residual elsewhere. Opposite contributions can cancel in this particular output, even though they represent substantial state errors.

Compute the weight of each row

Take \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(b=(1,0)^T\), and observe the second unknown with \(c=(0,1)^T\). Then \(u=(2/3,1/3)^T\) and \(z=(1/3,2/3)^T\). For \(\tilde u=(0.6,0.3)^T\), the residual is \((0.1,0)^T\). Its weighted sum is \(1/30\), exactly the missing part \(1/3-0.3\). If a solve produced residual \((0,0.1)^T\) instead, the same residual norm would double the output error. This output cares twice as much about the second row as about the first.

For residual \((0.2,-0.1)^T\), the signed estimate is exactly zero. The sum of absolute contributions is \(2/15\), an upper bound insensitive to cancellation. An analyst interested only in the selected output can accept a calculation whose state vector another observer would reject. Specify the observation before choosing stopping and refinement criteria.

Move from an algebraic error to a mesh error

An accurately solved finite-element system has almost zero algebraic residual in its own discrete test space. It can still differ from the continuum solution. Let \(a(u,v)=\ell(v)\) be the continuum weak equation and define \(R(v)=\ell(v)-a(u_h,v)\). For a linear observation the continuum dual satisfies \(a(v,z)=Q(v)\), giving \(Q(u)-Q(u_h)=R(z)\). Galerkin orthogonality means \(R(z_h)=0\) for every test function on the same mesh, hence \(R(z)=R(z-z_h)\).

Estimating this term requires a dual approximation richer than the test space used for the primal solve, or an independent reconstruction. Integrating element residuals by parts can expose both cell defects and jumps in normal flux. Weight these local contributions by the unresolved dual variation to discover which regions can affect the output strongly. Check an estimated correction against actual output changes on a refined mesh; a poor dual approximation can misidentify important regions even when the primal residuals have been evaluated exactly.

Compare a point observation with an average

In the two-unknown example, replace the second-node observation by the mean \(c=(1/2,1/2)^T\). The constant vector is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue one, so the adjoint becomes \(z=c\). A purely antisymmetric residual \((0.1,-0.1)^T\) makes the mean output error zero. For the earlier second-node output the same residual gives \(-1/30\). A mesh designed to resolve an average can therefore allocate effort differently from one designed for a single sensor. Whenever a reported error changes markedly between observables, inspect the dual weights before attributing the change to a flaw in the physical simulation.

Problem sheet 6 · Transfers and observation error

Track which error can survive a grid transfer and compute an exact adjoint correction for a nonsymmetric system.

A smooth mode survives smoothing

Weighted Jacobi for a Dirichlet Laplacian uses \(\omega=2/3\). Compute the multiplier at grid angles \(\pi/3\) and \(2\pi/3\), and the error left by two sweeps. Explain how this affects the choice of a coarse-grid space.

Worked solution

For \(\pi/3\) the multiplier is \(1-(2/3)(1-1/2)=2/3\), leaving \(4/9\) after two sweeps. At \(2\pi/3\) it is \(1-(2/3)(1+1/2)=0\), so the error vanishes in one sweep.

Smoother errors can persist while oscillatory components decay. A coarse interpolation space should approximate those persistent smooth components. A coarse solve alone cannot remove arbitrary fine-grid error.

Test exact coarse correction

Use \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(P=(1,1)^T\), \(R=P^T/2\). Find the coarse operator and the coarse correction for true error \((3,1)^T\). Verify the restricted residual afterward.

Worked solution

\(AP=(1,1)^T\) and \(RAP=1\). The initial residual is \(A(3,1)^T=(5,-1)^T\). Its restriction is 2, giving coarse correction \(P\,2=(2,2)^T\).

The remaining error is \((1,-1)^T\), with residual \((3,-3)^T\). Restricting gives zero even though the fine error remains. An exact coarse solve eliminated only the represented component.

Use the transpose

Let \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(c=(1,0)^T\), and suppose a computed state's residual is \(r=(0,0.3)^T\). Compute the observation error with an adjoint, verify it by solving \(Ae=r\), and explain the sign.

Worked solution

Solving \(A^Tz=c\) gives \(2z_1=1,\ z_1+3z_2=0\), so \(z=(1/2,-1/6)^T\). Its dot product with \(r\) is −0.05.

The error equation gives \(e_2=0.1,\ 2e_1+0.1=0\), hence \(c^Te=-0.05\). A positive second-row defect forces a negative first-component change through the positive off-diagonal coupling. Using \(A\) instead of its transpose for the dual would miss that effect.

Replace a continuum balance by nodal equations

Suppose a scalar diffuses along a unit interval, with a fixed value at each end. The steady balance is \(-u''=f\) for constant unit diffusivity. At equally spaced interior nodes \(x_i=ih\), approximate the derivative at each face by the difference between its neighbouring values divided by \(h\). Subtracting the outgoing and incoming diffusive fluxes and dividing by cell width gives

\[\frac{2u_i-u_{i-1}-u_{i+1}}{h^2}=f(x_i).\]

The endpoints are known, leaving \(N-1\) interior unknowns for \(N\) intervals. Each interior flux appears once with each sign in neighbouring balances. Summing balances therefore leaves the net boundary flux and the total source. With variable diffusivity \(D\), multiply each face gradient by its face value of \(D\) before taking the difference.

Find the error made by a single stencil

Insert the exact values \(u(x_i-h)\) and \(u(x_i+h)\), expand both in Taylor series and add them. Odd terms cancel. The central second difference satisfies \(\delta_{xx}u=u''+h^2u^{(4)}/12+O(h^4)\) for sufficiently smooth \(u\). This local consistency calculation predicts the scale of the defect when the exact solution is inserted in the discrete equations.

Write the discrete problem \(A_hu_h=f_h\) and define \(\tau_h=A_hu_\star-f_h\) using exact nodal values \(u_\star\). Then \(A_h(u_\star-u_h)=\tau_h\). A stability bound on \(A_h^{-1}\), uniform in mesh size in the chosen norm, turns second-order consistency into second-order solution error. A tight iterative solve reduces the residual of \(A_hu_h=f_h\), but leaves \(\tau_h\) in place.

Use an exact sine mode to predict the discrete amplitude

Prescribe \(u(0)=u(1)=0\) and \(f(x)=\pi^2\sin(\pi x)\). The exact solution is \(u_\star=\sin(\pi x)\). For its grid samples \(s_i=\sin(\pi ih)\), the sine addition formula yields \(A_hs=\lambda_hs\), with \(\lambda_h=4\sin^2(\pi h/2)/h^2\). The right-hand side is \(\pi^2s\), so the discrete solution has the same nodal shape and amplitude \(\pi^2/\lambda_h\).

With four intervals, \(\lambda_h=64\sin^2(\pi/8)=9.3726\) and the numerical midspan value is 1.0530. The exact value is 1. The sign makes sense: the discrete stiffness of this mode is below \(\pi^2\), so the same load produces a larger amplitude. Expansion gives \(\pi^2/\lambda_h=1+\pi^2h^2/12+O(h^4)\), predicting a fourfold error reduction on refinement.

Test a polynomial before trusting the mesh

Keep the homogeneous end values and choose \(u_\star=x(1-x)\). Derive \(f\), then predict the nodal error for any uniform mesh in exact arithmetic. Would this make a good test for measuring convergence order?

Show the calculation

Since \(u_\star''=-2\), use \(f=2\). The centred second difference of any quadratic equals its exact second derivative. The nodal vector therefore solves the discrete equations exactly. It is a useful stencil consistency check, but its zero leading error prevents a meaningful order measurement from a ratio of errors.

Manufacture a source for variable diffusion

On the unit square let \(D(x)=1+x\), \(u_\star=\sin(ax)\sin(ay)\), and \(a=m\pi\) for positive integer \(m\), using metre coordinates. What forcing makes \(-\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)+2u=f\) hold with zero boundary values?

Show the calculation

Apply the product rule, \(-\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)=-D\Delta u-D'(x)u_x\). Here \(\Delta u_\star=-2a^2u_\star\) and \(D'=1\). Thus \(f=[2(1+x)a^2+2]u_\star-a\cos(ax)\sin(ay)\). Each boundary has a zero sine factor. Omitting \(D'u_x\) would produce a continuous modelling error that persists even when the mesh is refined.
