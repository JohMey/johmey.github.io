Why does a freely spinning handle flip?

Use angular momentum to distinguish stable and unstable rotation about the principal axes of a rigid body.

Prerequisites Angular momentum and linear second-order differential equations.

A constant angular momentum in a rotating frame

Toss a rigid handle with a slight wobble. A spin about one of its axes can lead to a dramatic tumble even after it leaves your hand. Where does the change in rotation come from when there is no torque about the centre of mass?

Describe the spin using the body's three principal axes through its centre of mass. On those axes, angular momentum has components \(L_i=I_i\omega_i\). In an inertial frame \(\mathbf L\) is constant. A body-fixed observer sees the axes rotate, so the derivative rule gives \((d\mathbf L/dt)_{\rm body}=-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L\). Its first component and the two cyclic permutations are Euler’s equations

\[I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\quad I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\quad I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2.\]

Assume a rigid body with three distinct moments \(I_1<I_2<I_3\), negligible drag and no applied torque. Exact spin about any single principal axis is a solution. Stability asks what happens after a small error in the launch.

Perturb the middle-axis spin

Start near \((0,\Omega,0)\). Write \(\omega_1=a\), \(\omega_3=b\). The change in \(\omega_2\) is proportional to the product \(ab\), so to first order \(\omega_2=\Omega\). The other equations then read \(\dot a=-(I_3-I_2)\Omega b/I_1\) and \(\dot b=-(I_2-I_1)\Omega a/I_3\). Differentiating the first gives

\[\ddot a=\lambda^2 a,\qquad \lambda^2=\Omega^2\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1 I_3}.\]

The positive coefficient permits a growing exponential. A generic launch error contains this growing component. The approximation describes the onset of tumbling. Once the transverse components are comparable with \(\Omega\), the full equations determine the flip.

Estimate how quickly a wobble grows

Consider a rotor with principal moments \(2,3,4\) kg·m² spinning at \(6\) rad/s about axis 2. Its growth rate is \(6\sqrt{(4-3)(3-2)/(2\times4)}=2.12\) s⁻¹. A disturbance in the growing mode takes \(\ln 2/\lambda=0.327\) s to double from 0.03 to 0.06 rad/s. Both are small compared with the spin rate. This doubling time measures instability during the initial motion.

Tutorial on spin stability

1. Launch the rotor about its largest moment

Keep the same rotor and spin rate, but give axis 3 the large spin component. Find the equation for a small \(\omega_1\) and predict what a body-mounted sensor would see.

Solution to the stability calculation

With \(\omega_3=\Omega\), the first two Euler equations give \(\dot\omega_1=-(I_3-I_2)\Omega\omega_2/I_1\) and \(\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\Omega\omega_1/I_2\). Now \(\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega^2(I_3-I_2)(I_3-I_1)\omega_1/(I_1 I_2)\). The sensor sees a bounded oscillation of angular frequency \(6\sqrt{2/6}=3.46\) rad/s. One sign change in this feedback accounts for the difference in stability.

2. Can a flip consume rotational energy?

Multiply each Euler equation by its own \(\omega_i\) and add. Decide whether a measured decrease in one angular velocity component is evidence that energy has been dissipated.

Solution to the energy balance

The left-hand side is the derivative of \(E=\tfrac12\sum_i I_i\omega_i^2\). On the right, the coefficient of \(\omega_1\omega_2\omega_3\) is \((I_2-I_3)+(I_3-I_1)+(I_1-I_2)=0\). Energy is constant. Its contributions along the three moving axes can change during the flip, and a single component can even reverse sign.
