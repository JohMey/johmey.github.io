Chapter 1 · Free bodies, moments and support reactions

Replace a distributed load by its force and moment, then determine which reactions a support can supply.

Choose the body before writing equations

A platform scale under a shelf can read more than the weight placed directly above it. The measurement depends on all the other supports and the locations of all the applied forces. To predict it, imagine removing the shelf from its surroundings. Replace each lost contact by the force or couple that the surroundings could exert. Internal forces between parts of the isolated body cancel in pairs. The choice of boundary determines which forces are internal.

In a planar model a smooth roller on a horizontal track supplies one vertical reaction. A pin supplies two force components and admits rotation. A clamp supplies both force components and a reaction moment. A cable pulls along its length. A roller simply resting on a track can push, but a negative calculated upward reaction would require a hold-down or a change of contact. These restrictions are part of the physical model, even before a single number is inserted.

Sum forces and moments

A rigid body at rest satisfies \(\sum F_x=\sum F_y=0\) and \(\sum M_O=0\) about any point \(O\). In two dimensions, a force applied at \((x,y)\) contributes \(xF_y-yF_x\) to the moment about the origin. Adopt positive upward force and counterclockwise moment throughout. Taking moments about a pin eliminates its two unknown forces at once. A specified couple has the same signed moment about every origin and contributes zero resultant force.

For a vertical load distributed with downward intensity \(q(x)\) in N/m, a strip of width \(dx\) supplies \(q(x)\,dx\). Adding the strips gives the force magnitude \(W=\int q(x)\,dx\). Its clockwise moment magnitude about the left end is \(\int xq(x)\,dx\). The equivalent line of action therefore passes through \(\bar x=\int xq(x)\,dx/W\). This replacement preserves the external reactions. When finding an internal force at a cut, integrate only the load actually lying on the chosen side.

Weigh a platform with a triangular load

A 4.0 m platform is pinned at its left end A and supported by a roller at B at its right end. Bulk material forms a triangular pile, loading the platform with intensity rising linearly from zero at A to 3.0 kN/m at B. A 2.0 kN crate sits 1.0 m from A. Platform self-weight has already been tared off. Write \(q(x)=q_Bx/L\). Integration gives \(W=q_BL/2=6.0\) kN and \(\bar x=(q_BL^2/3)/W=2L/3=2.667\) m. The resultant is closer to the heavily loaded end.

\[4B_y-6(2.667)-2(1)=0,\qquad A_y+B_y=8.0\ {\rm kN}.\]

The right reaction is 4.50 kN and the left is 3.50 kN. There is no applied horizontal force, so \(A_x=0\). Check independently about B: the clockwise effect of the left reaction is \(3.50(4)=14.0\) kN·m and the two weights give the opposite moment \(6(1.333)+2(3)=14.0\) kN·m. Replacing the triangle by a uniform load of equal total weight would pass a force check and fail this moment check.

Predict the next scale reading

Move the crate 0.50 m to the right. Its force is unchanged, but its moment about A increases by 1.0 kN·m. The right reaction therefore rises by \(1.0/4.0=0.25\) kN and the left falls by the same amount. In practice this relation lets two load cells measure both weight and the position of a moving load. It assumes a stable support arrangement with known moment arms. Three vertical supports under a rigid platform introduce an extra unknown. Their individual readings then require information about deflections and support stiffness, which equilibrium alone cannot supply.

Find the load position from the reactions

An inverse problem uses the same equilibrium equations. A 2.0 m simply supported rail carries a single unknown load and sensors read 900 N at the left and 300 N at the right after taring the empty rail. The total applied downward load is 1200 N. Taking moments about the left support gives its position as \(300(2.0)/1200=0.50\,\mathrm m\). Repeating this reasoning for a distributed load gives the position of its resultant but cannot reconstruct its detailed distribution.

Chapter 2 · Axial stress, strain and compatibility

Determine how much a stepped member stretches and why a restrained temperature change produces force.

Find the force carried through a section

Two shafts that support the same suspended mass can stretch by very different amounts. A load cell measures force, while a clearance specification usually limits displacement. Connect them by taking a cut normal to a straight member and replacing the removed material by its axial force \(N\). When the load is centred, changes slowly and is far from grips or abrupt changes in section, the nominal normal stress is \(\sigma=N/A\). Adopt tension as positive. Near holes or grips the mean value remains useful for equilibrium, while local stresses can differ appreciably.

If a small material line of original length \(dx\) gains length \(du\), its engineering strain is \(du/dx\). Assume small strain, a uniform temperature across each section and a linear elastic material. Elastic strain is \(\sigma/E\), where Young’s modulus \(E\) has units of stress. Free thermal expansion adds the strain \(\alpha\Delta T\). Thus an element can change length even if its axial force is zero.

Reassemble the pieces

Multiply the strain by \(dx\) and add along the member. The displacement difference between its ends is

\[u(L)-u(0)=\int_0^L\frac{N(x)}{E(x)A(x)}\,dx+\int_0^L\alpha(x)\Delta T(x)\,dx.\]

Every term has units of length. Bars joined end to end carry the same force in a segment without an intervening axial load. Their extensions add, so their compliances \(L/(EA)\), measured in m/N, add. Bars between the same two rigid plates instead share the same extension. Their forces add, and their stiffnesses \(EA/L\), measured in N/m, add. Draw the connections before deciding which quantities are equal. The material with the larger modulus does not always carry the larger load because area and length also matter.

Stretch a two-material tie

A steel section is 0.60 m long with area 120 mm² and \(E_s=200\) GPa. An aluminium section continues another 0.40 m, has area 200 mm² and \(E_a=70\) GPa. Pull on the joined tie with 12 kN at constant temperature. Equilibrium gives 12 kN in both pieces. The nominal stresses are 100 MPa and 60 MPa respectively. Their elongations are \(12\,000(0.60)/(200\times10^9\,120\times10^{-6})=0.300\) mm and \(12\,000(0.40)/(70\times10^9\,200\times10^{-6})=0.343\) mm. The end displacement is 0.643 mm. The aluminium extends more although its nominal stress is smaller, because its compliance is larger.

Dividing total force by total displacement gives a tie stiffness of 18.7 MN/m. This is smaller than the stiffness of either isolated piece. Checking this limit catches an accidental parallel-spring calculation. The stress calculation still treats each piece separately. Averaging their areas first would conceal the location of the larger nominal stress.

Install the tie between rigid walls

Now let the same tie fit exactly between rigid walls with zero force at its installation temperature. Raise the uniform temperature by 30 K. Take \(\alpha_s=12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) and \(\alpha_a=23\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). If free, the pieces would extend by 0.216 and 0.276 mm. The walls prescribe zero total displacement, so an elastic shortening of 0.492 mm must develop. Multiplying this mismatch by the combined stiffness gives \(N=-9.18\) kN. The negative sign denotes compression.

A force balance on the entire tie merely says the two wall reactions are opposite. Their magnitude came from the displacement condition. Real supports deflect, giving part of the expansion back to the tie and reducing the force. If the tie is long and slender, check whether it can remain straight under this force before interpreting the compressive stress.

Test the difference between member force and extension

In a statically determinate pin-jointed truss the axial forces follow from joint equilibrium before the bar areas enter the displacement calculation. Predict both outputs when every bar area is doubled at unchanged geometry and load. If the compressive top chord initially carries 75 kN and the monitored joint moves down 3.219 mm, which reading changes, and by what factor? Check the two runs and explain the final rounding using \(\delta_i=N_iL_i/(E_iA_i)\). Open the truss experiment.

Problem sheet 1 · Equilibrium and axial compatibility

Draw each isolated body, state the sign convention and identify the compatibility condition before substituting numbers.

1. Find a load from two readings

A 5 m platform with a pin at the left end and a roller at the right weighs 6 kN uniformly. An added crate makes the reactions 4.2 kN and 5.8 kN respectively. (a) Determine the crate weight. (b) Find its position measured from the left. (c) Slide it 0.50 m left. Predict both new readings and show how their sum and difference respond.

Work the two balances

The total vertical load is \(4.2+5.8=10.0\) kN, so the crate weighs 4.0 kN. Taking moments about the left support gives \(5.8(5)=6(2.5)+4x\); hence \(x=3.50\) m. Moving left removes \(4(0.50)=2.0\) kN·m from the clockwise load moment. The right reading falls by \(2.0/5=0.40\) kN to 5.4 kN, while the left rises to 4.6 kN. Total weight remains 10 kN. The right-minus-left difference decreases from 1.6 to 0.8 kN because one reading rises while the other falls.

2. Let two bars share one load

Rigid parallel plates are joined by a 0.40 m long steel bar (\(E=200\) GPa, \(A=100\) mm²) and an equal-length aluminium bar (\(E=70\) GPa, \(A=200\) mm²). The plates are guided to remain parallel. A centred 17 kN tensile load separates them. (a) Find each stiffness. (b) Determine the extension and forces. (c) Compare the two strains and two stresses.

Work the common-displacement condition

The stiffnesses \(EA/L\) are 50 MN/m and 35 MN/m. The common extension is \(17000/(85\times10^6)=0.200\) mm. Steel takes 10.0 kN and aluminium 7.0 kN, summing to 17 kN. Both strains are \(0.0002/0.40=0.00050\), but the stresses are 100 and 35 MPa. Equal length and equal displacement enforce equal strain. Different moduli turn equal strain into different stress, and different areas convert those stresses to forces.

3. Close a gap before loading a support

A stress-free 1.20 m steel bar points toward a rigid stop with a 0.18 mm gap. Its other end is fixed. Use \(A=150\) mm², \(E=200\) GPa and \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). (a) How much warming closes the gap? (b) Find the compressive force and stress after a uniform 30 K rise. (c) Interpret a calculation that uses all 30 K as a restrained rise.

Separate free and restrained stages

Free expansion closes the gap at \(0.00018/(1.20\alpha)=12.5\) K. The subsequent 17.5 K would add another 0.252 mm of expansion, which the stop prevents. Compatibility now requires elastic strain \(-\alpha(17.5)=-0.000210\). The resulting stress is −42.0 MPa and force −6.30 kN. Treating all 30 K as restrained would give −72 MPa and would incorrectly predict a reaction before contact. This piecewise solution assumes the bar remains straight and the stop carries compression.

Chapter 3 · Shear force and bending moment

Construct the internal-force diagrams of a simply supported beam and locate the section with the largest nominal bending stress.

Read a section cut as an equilibrium problem

A transfer rail carries a trolley and the distributed weight of its own structure. Its supports may be strong enough while the rail still bends excessively at an interior section. Start with reactions for the entire beam. Next isolate the part from its left end to a cut at coordinate \(x\). The portion discarded from the drawing exerts an axial force, a shear force and a bending moment on the cut. For a horizontal beam with vertical loading the axial force is zero.

Use downward distributed load \(q(x)>0\), take \(M>0\) for sagging and define positive shear \(V\) by the resultant upward external load on the left-hand part. Thus a positive \(V\) corresponds to a downward internal shear on its right cut face. Choose the signs once and test a cut just to the right of the left support. With a positive left reaction the first value of the shear diagram must be positive.

Build a diagram from the loading

Take an element between \(x\) and \(x+dx\). Its external load increases the downward resultant by \(q\,dx\), so \(V(x+dx)-V(x)=-q\,dx\). Moment balance retains the term \(V\,dx\); the distributed load times its half-element arm is second order in \(dx\). Passing to the limit gives

\[\frac{dV}{dx}=-q(x),\qquad \frac{dM}{dx}=V(x).\]

A point load produces a finite jump in \(V\) and a change of slope in \(M\). The bending moment is continuous there unless an applied concentrated couple acts. On an unloaded interval shear is constant and moment is a straight line. Under uniform loading the moment is parabolic. At a simple end support without an applied end couple the bending moment is zero. These four facts are often enough to find a sign mistake before calculating stress.

Combine a point load and distributed weight

Let a simply supported 6.0 m rail carry uniform weight 2.0 kN/m over its full span and a 6.0 kN trolley 2.0 m from the left end. The total downward load is 18 kN. Moment balance about the left gives \(6R_B=12(3)+6(2)=48\) kN·m, so \(R_B=8\) kN and \(R_A=10\) kN. To the left of the trolley, \(V=10-2x\) kN and \(M=10x-x^2\) kN·m for \(x\) in metres. At \(x=2\), \(M=16\) kN·m. Shear falls from 6 kN to zero on crossing the trolley.

To the right of the trolley, \(V=4-2x\) kN and \(M=10x-x^2-6(x-2)\) kN·m. Shear is negative throughout \(2<x<6\); the moment therefore falls to zero at the right support. The largest bending moment is 16 kN·m under the trolley. At the right end \(V(6^-)=-8\) kN. The upward reaction returns it to zero, another check on the construction.

Translate the moment into a fibre stress

For a symmetric homogeneous section undergoing small elastic curvature, strain varies linearly through depth. Integrating the moments of the Hookean normal stresses about the neutral axis gives \(M=EI\kappa\). A fibre a distance \(c\) from that axis has stress magnitude \(Mc/I=M/Z\), where \(Z=I/c\) is the section modulus. Take a rectangular section 80 mm wide and 160 mm deep. Then \(Z=bh^2/6=3.41\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}\), giving a nominal bending stress of 46.9 MPa under the trolley. At a positive sagging moment the bottom is in tension. A local wheel-contact stress or weld stress requires its own length scale and model.

What jumps under an applied couple?

A concentrated transverse force produces a jump in shear. A concentrated moment produces a jump in the bending-moment diagram. For example, apply only a clockwise couple of 4 kN m midway along a 2 m simply supported beam. Global moment equilibrium gives a right reaction of 2 kN upwards and a left reaction of 2 kN downwards. The shear stays at −2 kN between supports. The moment thus falls from zero to −2 kN m on the left half, jumps to +2 kN m at the couple and falls to zero at the right support. The two zero end moments provide a check on the jump direction.

Chapter 4 · Elastic curves and virtual work

Predict a specified displacement from the bending-moment field and distinguish a stiffness limit from a stress limit.

When a beam is strong enough but too flexible

A tool rail may stay below its elastic limit while its cutting head moves outside a position tolerance. The maximum bending stress depends on the largest moment at one section. Deflection accumulates the effect of curvature over a length. A displacement sensor near an unloaded end can therefore move even if the nearby bending moment is zero. To predict it, identify both the point and direction of the required displacement.

Consider a straight, slender Euler–Bernoulli beam. Cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the deformed centreline. Take \(w\) positive upward and \(M\) positive in sagging. For small slopes the signed curvature is \(w''\), with \(\kappa=M/(EI)\). Integrate once to find the slope and once more to find the displacement. Each constant of integration has physical meaning. A clamp fixes displacement and rotation. A simple support fixes its vertical displacement while permitting rotation. At an ordinary join without a hinge, both displacement and slope are continuous.

Work through a tip reading

Clamp a beam at \(x=0\). Apply a downward point load \(P\) at \(x=a\) on a beam of total length \(L>a\). The signed moment in \(0<x<a\) is \(-P(a-x)\). For \(x>a\) the free segment has no external force and no moment. Taking constant stiffness and using the clamp conditions gives

\[EI w'(x)=-P(ax-x^2/2),\qquad EI w(x)=-P(ax^2/2-x^3/6),\quad 0\le x\le a.\]

At the load point the displacement is \(-Pa^3/(3EI)\) and the slope is \(-Pa^2/(2EI)\). The free extension beyond it is straight but tilted. Use those values as initial conditions for the unloaded segment: \(w(L)=w(a)+(L-a)w'(a)=-Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI)\). This expression correctly vanishes when the force is applied at the clamp and reduces to the standard end-load expression when \(a=L\).

Calculate both position and slope

Let \(EI=8400\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}\), \(L=1.20\) m, \(a=0.80\) m and \(P=100\) N. Under the load the downward displacement is 2.03 mm and the rotation magnitude is \(100(0.80)^2/[2(8400)]=0.00381\) rad. The 0.40 m overhang contributes another 1.52 mm at the tip, for a total downward reading of 3.56 mm. The slope is small compared with a radian, consistent with the linear curvature assumption. A gauge at the load and one at the tip would give different stiffness estimates if their positions were omitted.

Obtain one displacement directly

There is another way to compute a single sensor reading. Introduce a fictitious force \(Q\) at the sensor in its desired positive direction. The moment becomes \(M_P+Qm\), where \(m\) is the moment per unit sensor force. The bending energy is \(U=\int(M_P+Qm)^2/(2EI)\,dx\). Differentiating with respect to \(Q\) gives the displacement conjugate to that force. Set \(Q=0\) afterwards to recover the original beam.

\[\delta=\int_0^L\frac{M_P(x)m(x)}{E(x)I(x)}\,dx.\]

For a downward unit force at the free tip, \(m=-(L-x)\). Only the portion up to \(a\) contributes because \(M_P=0\) further along. The integral gives \(Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI)\), the positive downward reading obtained above. This form is especially useful for stepped sections: split the integral where either stiffness or loading changes. Its positive self-flexibility for a load and a sensor pointing the same way is a useful sign check.

Exchange the load and the gauge

The unit-load formula supplies a test that can be made on the same beam without knowing its exact stiffness distribution. Put the same 100 N force at the tip and move the displacement sensor to \(x=0.80\) m. The new integral has exactly the previous pair of moment fields with their roles exchanged. Since their product is unchanged, the new reading is also 3.56 mm downwards. This Maxwell–Betti reciprocity applies to these work-conjugate forces and displacements in a linear elastic structure with unchanged restraints. A slipping clamp, a support that lifts off or motion large enough to change the load geometry can cause discrepancies between reciprocal tests. Swapping load and sensor positions is therefore also a practical check of the assumed support behaviour.

Problem sheet 2 · Beam actions and deformations

Follow each load from the support diagram to the internal moment and the sensor reading.

1. Move a wheel along a beam

A single 8 kN wheel loads a simply supported 4.0 m span 1.0 m from A. (a) Find the reactions and largest moment. (b) Place the wheel 2.0 m from A and repeat. (c) A section has \(Z=160\,000\) mm³. Compare the largest nominal stresses.

Work from reactions to stress

The first moment balance about A gives \(R_B=8(1)/4=2\) kN and \(R_A=6\) kN. Shear changes sign at the wheel, which is where \(M_{\max}=6(1)=6\) kN·m. At midspan the reactions are both 4 kN and \(M_{\max}=8\) kN·m. Convert moments to N·mm and divide by \(Z\). The extreme stresses are 37.5 and 50.0 MPa. Symmetry maximises the moment from one mobile wheel at midspan for this support arrangement.

2. Locate the maximum moment under a tapered load

A 3.0 m simple span carries downward load rising from zero at A to 6.0 kN/m at B. (a) Determine the reactions. (b) Obtain \(V(x)\) and \(M(x)\) on the open span. (c) Find the maximum and check the other end condition.

Integrate the triangular load

The resultant is 9 kN at 2 m from A, so \(R_B=6\) kN and \(R_A=3\) kN. In metres the intensity is \(2x\) kN/m. Integrating \(V'=-2x\) gives \(V=3-x^2\) kN. With \(M(0)=0\), \(M=3x-x^3/3\) kN·m. The positive shear vanishes at \(x=\sqrt3=1.732\) m, where \(M=2\sqrt3=3.46\) kN·m. At B, \(M(3)=9-9=0\) and the 6 kN support reaction exactly cancels the remaining negative shear.

3. Compare two displacements on a cantilever

A 0.90 m cantilever with \(EI=6000\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}\) carries a 240 N downward tip force. (a) Derive the end deflection by the unit-load method. (b) Compute the deflection at the halfway station using a fictitious load there. (c) Explain why linear interpolation from the tip is inaccurate.

Work the two virtual-load diagrams

For the tip reading multiply \(M=-P(L-x)\) by \(m=-(L-x)\) and integrate from 0 to \(L\). The displacement is \(PL^3/(3EI)=9.72\) mm. For a unit load at \(a=L/2\), \(m=-(a-x)\) up to \(a\) and zero beyond. Thus \(\delta(a)=P\int_0^a(L-x)(a-x)\,dx/(EI)=Pa^2(3L-a)/(6EI)=3.04\) mm. Half the tip reading would be 4.86 mm. The zero slope at the clamp and bending curvature throughout the beam give a nonlinear deflection shape.

Chapter 5 · Stress transformation and combined loading

Find principal stresses and elastic yield measures at a shaft surface carrying bending and torsion.

A section resultant does not specify a failure plane

The free-body diagram of a shaft gives bending moments and torque. A potential crack, however, can cut the shaft on any plane. To compare the local stress with a material test, first evaluate the stress components at the same material point. At the outer fibre of a circular shaft the bending normal stress may be large. At the neutral axis the bending stress vanishes. Values from different points cannot be combined into one stress state.

For a circular shaft of diameter \(d\) bending moment \(M\) gives surface stress \(32M/(\pi d^3)\), and torque \(T\) gives surface shear \(16T/(\pi d^3)\). The torsion relation uses \(J=\pi d^4/32\). The combination assumes elastic small deformation in a smooth prismatic portion, sufficiently far from shoulders, load introduction and keyways. At a traction-free outer surface the radial traction is zero. Use a local basis with longitudinal and circumferential axes for the remaining plane-stress components.

Change the orientation of a cut

In two dimensions write the stress tensor with normal components \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y\) and shear \(\tau_{xy}\). The traction vector on a cut with unit normal \(\mathbf n\) is \(\mathbf t=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\). Project that vector along and perpendicular to \(\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). The normal stress becomes a constant average plus a sinusoidal term; the shear varies with twice the same angle:

\[\sigma_n=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta.\]

The largest and smallest in-plane normal stresses differ from the mean by \(R=\sqrt{[(\sigma_x-\sigma_y)/2]^2+\tau_{xy}^2}\). The shear on those principal planes vanishes. Taking the third principal stress as zero allows a three-dimensional maximum-shear check: evaluate half the difference of the largest and smallest of all three principal stresses. In a biaxial tensile state, ignoring the zero third stress can underestimate this difference.

Compare principal stresses on a machine shaft

Let a 40 mm diameter steel shaft carry \(M=300\) N·m and \(T=200\) N·m. At its tensile bending surface, \(\sigma_x=47.75\) MPa, \(\sigma_y=0\) and \(\tau_{xy}=15.92\) MPa. The in-plane mean is 23.87 MPa and the radius is 28.69 MPa. The principal stresses are about 52.57 and −4.82 MPa. The plane of greatest tension is rotated through half of \(\operatorname{atan2}(2\tau_{xy},\sigma_x-\sigma_y)\), here 16.8° from the original plane normal. The maximum shear magnitude is 28.7 MPa.

A useful check is the trace: the principal stresses sum to 47.75 MPa, the same sum as the original normal components. A second check is the determinant: the two principal values have opposite signs since the determinant of this particular matrix is \(-\tau_{xy}^2\). A picture with the two directions marked prevents confusing the angle of a plane normal with that of the line drawn for the plane itself.

Interpret an equivalent stress

For isotropic ductile materials a common onset-of-yield model uses von Mises equivalent stress. Under plane stress it is \(\sqrt{\sigma_x^2-\sigma_x\sigma_y+\sigma_y^2+3\tau_{xy}^2}\), giving 55.1 MPa here. Compare this scalar with the uniaxial yield strength from a material test under a comparable temperature and loading rate. A given yield strength of 220 MPa corresponds to a nominal static factor of about 4.0. That number answers a defined static question. A shaft rotating under a stationary transverse force cycles the tensile and compressive stress of a given material point once per revolution. Evaluating its fatigue resistance requires the stress range and a separate material model.

Change the mean stress without changing the distortion

Apply a uniform external hydrostatic pressure of 50 MPa to a material point whose original principal stresses are 100, 0 and 0 MPa. The new principal stresses are 50, −50 and −50 MPa, since pressure acts on every plane. Their pairwise differences remain 100, 0 and −100 MPa, so the von Mises stress remains 100 MPa. This illustrates why a distortion-based yield condition must include all three normal directions under confinement. Applying a plane-stress formula after adding pressure only to the in-plane directions would represent a different three-dimensional loading.

Chapter 6 · Elastic stability of compression members

Derive the ideal buckling load, check its governing axis and explain how an imperfection changes the measured response.

Equilibrium can persist while stability changes

Press gently on the ends of a thin steel ruler. The straight configuration satisfies force equilibrium over a range of compressive loads. Eventually a small lateral disturbance produces a large deflection. The issue concerns the restoring response to a disturbance, even when the nominal compressive stress is well within the material’s elastic range.

Model a slender, initially straight, uniform elastic column with ideal pins at both ends. Apply a conservative compressive load \(P\) along the initial end-to-end line, with small transverse slope and negligible shear deformation. A lateral excursion \(w(x)\) creates a bending moment of magnitude \(P|w|\). Combining its restoring sign with the small-curvature law gives \(EI w''+P w=0\). The difference from a laterally loaded beam is that the load itself creates a moment through the unknown displacement.

Apply two pin conditions

Write \(k^2=P/(EI)\) and integrate: \(w=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)\). The first pin imposes \(w(0)=0\), so \(B=0\). The second imposes \(A\sin(kL)=0\). The zero-amplitude straight solution always exists. A nonzero amplitude is possible within this linearised model only when \(kL=n\pi\), for positive integer \(n\). The smallest compressive load admitting a bent mode is

\[P_{\mathrm{cr}}=\frac{\pi^2EI}{L^2},\qquad w_1(x)\propto\sin(\pi x/L).\]

The proportionality leaves the amplitude undetermined. It locates a loss of stiffness, not a post-buckled deflection. The same result follows from energy: for \(w=A\sin(\pi x/L)\) the quadratic elastic energy is \(EI\pi^4A^2/(4L^3)\), while end shortening releases \(P\pi^2A^2/(4L)\). Their difference changes sign at the critical load.

Find which direction buckles first

A rectangular steel strut is 1.50 m long with section 40 mm by 12 mm and \(E=200\) GPa. Bending across the 12 mm dimension gives \(I_{\min}=40(12)^3/12=5760\,\mathrm{mm^4}\). Across the 40 mm dimension, \(I=12(40)^3/12=64\,000\,\mathrm{mm^4}\). Free to buckle either way, the column chooses the smaller bending stiffness. Converting \(5760\,\mathrm{mm^4}\) to \(5.76\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) gives \(P_{\rm cr}=5.05\) kN.

The corresponding mean stress is only \(5050/480=10.5\) MPa. The radius of gyration about the weak axis is \(r_g=\sqrt{I/A}=3.46\) mm. Writing \(P_{\rm cr}/A=\pi^2E/(L/r_g)^2\) makes the sensitivity to slenderness explicit. If a real brace prevents weak-axis deflection at midspan, a pin-like brace divides that buckling length by two. Check other unbraced axes and the brace stiffness before crediting the fourfold ideal increase.

Interpret a pre-curved strut

Suppose the unloaded column already bows with amplitude \(w_0\) in the first sine shape. Elastic bending resists the curvature change from that initial shape. Writing its total amplitude as \(w\), the mode equation now gives \(P_{\rm cr}(w-w_0)=Pw\), hence \(w=w_0/(1-P/P_{\rm cr})\). If \(w_0=1.0\) mm, the predicted amplitude is 2.0 mm at half the ideal load and 5.0 mm at 80%. The steep increase can show up in a test well before a distinct peak load.

This model assumes small slopes and a material still in its elastic range. Large deflection changes the geometry, and local yielding can reduce the tangent stiffness. End restraint also changes the eigenvalue. The often-used effective-length notation replaces \(L\) by the pin-ended length giving the same ideal load. It must reflect the actual translations and rotations permitted by the supports.

Use two readings to estimate an elastic critical load

For an imperfect elastic column dominated by its first buckling shape, write total offset \(e=e_0/(1-P/P_{\rm cr})\). Let \(y=e-e_0\) be the additional deflection measured from its unloaded shape. Rearrangement gives \(y/P=(y+e_0)/P_{\rm cr}\). A graph of \(y/P\) against \(y\) should therefore be linear with slope \(1/P_{\rm cr}\). As a consistency test, measured pairs \(P=20\) kN, \(y=0.50\) mm and \(P=30\) kN, \(y=1.00\) mm give slope \((1/30-0.5/20)/(1.0-0.5)=1/60\,\mathrm{kN^{-1}}\). The implied critical load is 60 kN and the intercept implies a 1 mm initial offset. Clearance or nonlinear material response can spoil this inference before the theoretical critical load is reached.

Combine bending and torsion

At 1200 rpm the angular speed is \(40\pi\) rad/s and \(T=6000/(40\pi)=47.75\) N·m. At the tensile surface \(\sigma=32(80)/(\pi d^3)=30.18\) MPa and \(\tau=16(47.75)/(\pi d^3)=9.01\) MPa. Thus \(\sigma_{\rm vm}=\sqrt{\sigma^2+3\tau^2}=33.98\) MPa. Halving the speed doubles the torque and the shear to 18.01 MPa while bending stays fixed. The equivalent stress becomes about 43.4 MPa. Keeping power constant does not keep the torque demand constant.

A straight pin-ended column is 2.0 m long with \(E=200\) GPa, \(I_y=8.0\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) and \(I_z=2.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^4}\). (a) Calculate both mode loads. Chapter 7 · Torsional stiffness of shafts and closed sections

Derive torque–twist relations and explain the large difference in stiffness between an intact tube and an open strip.

Rotate two adjacent sections

A torque meter measures twist between two stations on a shaft. To infer torque, first ask how material between the stations deforms. A straight circular shaft in uniform, small-deformation torsion has cross-sections that rotate as rigid discs. Two discs separated by \(dx\) rotate relative to one another by \(d\theta\). A fibre at radius \(r\) moves sideways by \(r\,d\theta\), giving shear strain \(\gamma=r\,d\theta/dx\). With elastic shear modulus \(G\), the shear stress increases linearly from the centre to the surface.

Each area element transmits a torque \(r\tau\,dA\). Integrating produces the polar second moment of area \(J\), whose dimensions are length to the fourth power. With constant torque and cross-section, integration along the shaft then converts twist per length into total relative angle.

\[T=\int_A r\tau\,dA=G\theta'\int_A r^2dA,\qquad J=\frac{\pi}{2}(r_o^4-r_i^4),\qquad \theta=\frac{TL}{GJ}.\]

Follow a torque through a stepped shaft

A 0.75 m aluminium tube of outer radius 15 mm and inner radius 12 mm transmits 45 N m. For \(G=26\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(J=4.695\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^4}\). Its twist is \(45(0.75)/(26\times10^9J)=0.02765\,\mathrm{rad}=1.58^\circ\). Surface shear is \(45(0.015)/J=14.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\). The inside surface still carries 80% of this stress. Drilling a central hole removes the least highly stressed material first.

Twist can also identify an unknown joint compliance. If an instrument measures 2.00° across this tube plus its end couplings, and all parts carry the same torque, the couplings contribute about 0.42°. Their combined torsional compliance is approximately \(0.42\pi/[180(45)]=1.6\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/(N\,m)}\). Such a comparison assumes that the gauges do not include a second shaft segment and that the torque is transmitted without slip.

Carry torque around a single closed cell

For a long, thin-walled closed tube with a single cell, use the shear flow \(q=\tau t\), the force per unit length of wall. Longitudinal equilibrium makes this flow approximately constant around the perimeter if no intermediate longitudinal force is introduced. A length \(ds\) carries tangential force \(q\,ds\). Its moment about an arbitrary origin is \(q\) times twice the triangular area swept by the radius vector. Around the median wall contour this gives \(T=2A_mq\).

For a rectangular cell with median dimensions 80 by 40 mm, a torque of 320 N m gives \(q=320000/[2(80)(40)]=50\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). A 2 mm wall carries 25 MPa while a 4 mm wall carries 12.5 MPa. A slender longitudinal slit interrupts the closed path. The constant circulating flow solution then ceases to apply. A seam that reconnects the two lips must transmit the longitudinal shear flow if the component is to recover its closed-section behaviour.

Distinguish two section properties

For a noncircular closed tube, the polar area moment and torsion constant generally differ. Compatibility around a single thin-walled cell gives \(\theta'=T\oint ds/(Gt)/(4A_m^2)\). Making only a short wall segment very thin substantially increases this integral. A wall segment can therefore control angle of twist even when equilibrium fixes the same shear flow everywhere around the cell.

Chapter 8 · Transverse shear and connections in built-up beams

Derive shear flow from changing bending stress and determine the force transferred between parts of a built-up section.

Why bending generates an interface force

Stack two boards and support them as a beam. Under a transverse load their neighbouring surfaces tend to slide. A bond or row of fasteners connects the boards so they can act as one deeper section. To calculate the demand on that connection, isolate only the material above the interface over a short beam length. Even when the external load points vertically, horizontal equilibrium of this isolated piece requires longitudinal shear at its lower face.

Under elastic bending about a centroidal principal axis, longitudinal stress is \(\sigma_x=My/I\) up to the chosen sign. Its resultant on the isolated partial area \(A'\) has magnitude \(N=M Q/I\), where \(Q=\int_{A'} y\,dA\) is its first moment about the neutral axis of the complete section. For constant cross-section, the difference between adjacent resultants is \(dN=(Q/I)\,dM\). The balancing interface force is \(q\,dx\).

\[q=\frac{dN}{dx}=\frac{VQ}{I},\qquad \tau=\frac{q}{b_i}.\]

Here \(b_i\) is the local contacting width, and \(dM/dx=V\) for the sign convention used. Shear flow has units N/mm and stress has units N/mm². Evaluate \(Q\) with the portion on one side of the cut and \(I\) with the complete section. Using the second moment of an individual board defeats the assumption that the connection makes them share a curvature.

Select fastener spacing for an assembled beam

Two boards each 60 mm wide and 40 mm deep form an 80 mm deep rectangular beam. At a section carrying \(V=2.4\,\mathrm{kN}\), the complete centroidal second moment is \(60(80)^3/12=2.56\times10^6\,\mathrm{mm^4}\). The upper board has area 2400 mm² and its centroid lies 20 mm above the interface, which coincides with the full neutral axis. Hence \(Q=48000\,\mathrm{mm^3}\).

The required flow is \(2400(48000)/(2.56\times10^6)=45\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). An adhesive over the full contact width would carry an average longitudinal shear stress of \(45/60=0.75\,\mathrm{MPa}\). A single row of connectors rated for 900 N each in the idealised shear mode would require a spacing no greater than \(900/45=20\,\mathrm{mm}\). For a load varying significantly over a connector spacing, integrate the flow over its tributary length instead.

Recover the shear profile in a rectangle

At any elevation \(y\) in that rectangle the partial area extends from \(y\) to \(h/2\). Its first moment is \(Q=b[(h/2)^2-y^2]/2\). Substituting \(I=bh^3/12\) gives \(\tau(y)=[3V/(2bh)](1-4y^2/h^2)\). The stress vanishes at the top and bottom free surfaces, reaching one and a half times the section-average transverse shear at mid-depth. The elastic bending stress, in contrast, vanishes at that mid-depth. A joint placed near the neutral axis can therefore carry substantial shear even when its local normal bending stress is small.

Where should fasteners become more closely spaced?

Under a uniform transverse load a simply supported beam has largest shear magnitude near the supports and zero shear at its symmetric midspan. With the same cross-section along the span, the connector force per unit length follows that shear diagram. Midspan may govern bending strength while the regions near the supports govern connector spacing.

Chapter 9 · Compatibility and redundant supports

Use elastic work to determine a support reaction that cannot be obtained from force and moment balance alone.

Choose a redundant force

A cantilever with a vertical prop at its far end has one more reaction than the independent planar equilibrium equations can determine. A change of support elevation changes those reactions even though the applied loads stay the same. This sensitivity provides the additional equation: the displacement at the prop equals its specified settlement. Remove the prop temporarily and replace it by an unknown upward force \(R\). The released cantilever must be stable, and its displacement at that location must be calculable.

For small elastic deformation the effects of a known load and of the unknown force may be superposed. Write downward displacements as positive. Under a distributed downward load the released tip displaces by \(\delta_w\). An upward reaction displaces it by \(-fR\), where \(f\) is tip flexibility measured in metres per newton. If the prop settles downwards by \(s\), compatibility becomes \(\delta_w-fR=s\).

Find the coefficients by virtual work

For constant \(EI\), impose a unit downward load at the free tip. Its unit-load bending moment has magnitude \(m(x)=L-x\), measured from the fixed end. With a uniformly distributed load of intensity \(w\), the moment magnitude is \(M_w=w(L-x)^2/2\). Multiplying the actual and virtual moments and integrating gives

\[\delta_w=\int_0^L\frac{M_wm}{EI}\,dx=\frac{wL^4}{8EI},\qquad f=\int_0^L\frac{m^2}{EI}\,dx=\frac{L^3}{3EI}.\]

For a rigid prop at its initial elevation \(s=0\), the reaction becomes \(R=3wL/8\). The remaining vertical force at the fixed end is \(5wL/8\). The external support moment there has magnitude \(wL^2/2-RL=wL^2/8\). Recovering equilibrium at the end provides an independent check after using flexibility to close the calculation.

Predict the effect of a small support movement

Take \(L=3.0\,\mathrm m\), \(w=4.0\,\mathrm{kN/m}\) and \(EI=1.8\,\mathrm{MN\,m^2}\). Without the prop, \(\delta_w=0.0225\,\mathrm m\), and \(f=5.00\,\mathrm{mm/kN}\). Without settlement the prop takes 4.50 kN. With a measured downward settlement of 6 mm, it takes \((22.5-6)/5=3.30\,\mathrm{kN}\). The fixed-end vertical reaction rises to 8.70 kN and its moment magnitude rises from 4.50 to \(18.0-3.3(3)=8.10\,\mathrm{kN\,m}\).

A unilateral prop can push upwards but cannot pull the beam down. At settlements exceeding 22.5 mm the linear compatibility solution would give a negative prop reaction. In that case the beam hangs clear of the prop and behaves as the released cantilever. A bolted hold-down could sustain the negative reaction and would require a different contact model. Replacing the rigid prop by a spring gives another direct extension: its reaction moves its top by \(R/k\), so use \(\delta_w-fR=s+R/k\) and obtain \(R=(\delta_w-s)/(f+1/k)\). An increasingly compliant prop takes an increasingly small fraction of the load.

Check a reaction against a stiffness change

If all beam cross-sections gain twice their original bending stiffness, both the load-induced tip deflection and the tip flexibility halve. For a rigid prop with zero settlement their ratio stays constant, so reactions are unchanged. For a prescribed settlement, however, the term \(s/f\) doubles. A stiff structure can therefore develop large additional reactions from a small imposed support movement.

Chapter 10 · From a strain rosette to principal stresses

Recover a complete plane-strain measurement at a surface from three gauges and infer the corresponding plane-stress state.

What a bonded gauge actually measures

A resistance gauge aligned with a line on a free surface records extension along that line. A single gauge cannot distinguish uniaxial tension from combined stretching and shear arranged to give the same directional extension. Bond three gauges at different angles near enough together that the local strain is approximately uniform. A rectangular rosette uses directions of zero, 45 and 90 degrees, all referred to the same positive surface axis.

Under infinitesimal deformation, a unit direction vector \(n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) experiences normal strain \(n^T\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}n\). Engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{xy}\) is twice the off-diagonal strain tensor component. Expanding the directional expression yields

\[\varepsilon_\theta=\varepsilon_x\cos^2\theta+\varepsilon_y\sin^2\theta+\gamma_{xy}\sin\theta\cos\theta .\]

The zero and 90° readings give \(\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y\) directly. The middle reading gives \(\gamma_{xy}=2\varepsilon_{45}-\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y\). A gauge at minus 45° measures the opposite shear contribution. A sketch of the physical gauge labels avoids reversing the inferred shear sign during data reduction.

Introduce the local stress assumption

At an unloaded outer surface the normal and shear tractions on that surface vanish. With \(z\) normal to it, this gives local plane stress. For isotropic linear elasticity and mechanical strains obtained after temperature correction, invert the plane-stress compliance equations and use the shear modulus for the remaining component.

\[\sigma_x=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}(\varepsilon_x+\nu\varepsilon_y),\qquad \sigma_y=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}(\varepsilon_y+\nu\varepsilon_x),\qquad \tau_{xy}=\frac{E\gamma_{xy}}{2(1+\nu)} .\]

A strain rosette measures only the in-plane quantities. A nonzero through-thickness strain is consistent with plane stress because the in-plane stresses cause Poisson contraction. Beneath a contact patch the local traction condition changes, so the same stress recovery would need additional information.

Interpret the readings on a plate

Suppose a steel surface gives \(400,\ 250,\ -100\) microstrain at zero, 45 and 90 degrees. The recovered shear strain is \(500-400+100=200\) microstrain. With \(E=210\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and \(\nu=0.30\), the stresses are \(85.38,\ 4.62,\ 16.15\,\mathrm{MPa}\) for \(x,y,xy\), respectively. The shear is positive under the stated gauge orientation. Converting microstrain to \(10^{-6}\) before multiplying by MPa is essential.

The principal stresses are the eigenvalues of this symmetric matrix. Its mean normal stress is 45.0 MPa, and its Mohr-circle radius is \(\sqrt{[(85.38-4.62)/2]^2+16.15^2}=43.50\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Thus the two in-plane principal stresses are approximately 88.5 and 1.5 MPa. The major direction is \(\tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}(2\tau,\sigma_x-\sigma_y)=10.9^\circ\) from the zero gauge. A strain reading of −100 microstrain along \(y\) therefore does not imply compressive normal stress along that axis: Poisson contraction from the dominant \(x\) load accounts for much of that reading.

Resolve a uniform thermal signal

Uniform heating of an unconstrained isotropic plate adds the same free thermal strain \(\alpha\Delta T\) to each gauge reading, after allowing for the gauge instrumentation itself. It cancels from the inferred engineering shear because \(2-1-1=0\), but it persists in both recovered normal components. For a 20 K temperature rise and linear expansion \(12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), subtract 240 microstrain from each reading before applying the mechanical plane-stress relation.

Chapter 11 · Yield fronts and plastic bending capacity

Track the spread of yielding through a rectangular section and distinguish the first-yield moment from the fully plastic moment.

First yield occurs at a surface

A beam can retain moment capacity after its first surface fibres yield. Consider a straight rectangular bar of width \(b\), total depth \(2c\), equal tensile and compressive yield stresses \(\sigma_Y\), and an elastic, perfectly plastic law. Assume pure bending about its centroid, small strains and a compact section that avoids local buckling. Plane sections remain plane, so longitudinal strain varies as \(\kappa y\). Symmetry keeps the neutral axis at mid-depth for this material and section.

While the whole section is elastic, \(\sigma=E\kappa y\). As curvature increases, the first yield occurs when \(E\kappa_Y c=\sigma_Y\). Integrating stress times moment arm gives the elastic first-yield moment \(M_Y=EI\kappa_Y=\sigma_Y I/c\). A section with a narrow outer flange or unequal tensile and compressive material behaviour requires a fresh stress distribution and force equilibrium.

Integrate the elastic core and yielded layers

At a greater curvature let \(a=\sigma_Y/(E\kappa)\) be the half-depth of the remaining elastic core. Stress in the core grows linearly as \(\sigma_Y y/a\). Outside the core it remains at the yield magnitude. Each half of the section contributes the same bending moment. Splitting the integral at the moving yield front gives

\[M=2b\left[\int_0^a\frac{\sigma_Yy^2}{a}\,dy+\int_a^c\sigma_Yy\,dy\right]=b\sigma_Y(c^2-a^2/3).\]

At \(a=c\) this reproduces \(M_Y=(2/3)b\sigma_Yc^2\). As \(a\) approaches zero, \(M\) tends to \(M_p=b\sigma_Y c^2\), which is 1.5 times first yield for this solid rectangle. The yield fronts advance towards the centre while progressively larger curvature increments produce smaller changes of moment. The bending stiffness at first loading is therefore history dependent after yielding begins.

Quantify the onset and extent of plastic deformation

A 20 mm wide bar is 40 mm deep, with \(E=200\,\mathrm{GPa}\) and \(\sigma_Y=250\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Its first-yield curvature is \(250/(200000\times20)=6.25\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}\), or 0.0625 m⁻¹. First yield occurs at 1.333 kN m and the limiting plastic moment is 2.000 kN m. At twice the yield curvature the elastic core half-depth has fallen to 10 mm. Substitution gives \(M=20(250)[400-100/3]=1.833\,\mathrm{kN\,m}\).

Upon unloading, assume the stress changes elastically without reverse yield. The immediate moment–curvature slope is then \(EI\), so the recovered curvature is \(M/(EI)\), where \(I=20(40)^3/12=106667\,\mathrm{mm^4}\). It is \(8.59\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}\). The peak curvature was \(1.25\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}\), leaving about 0.0391 m⁻¹ residual curvature. A permanent bend can therefore coexist with zero applied bending moment. The residual internal stresses balance their own force and moment across the section.

Compare equal areas in different locations

Redistributing material towards the outer fibres changes the reserve between first yield and full plasticity. In the limiting idealisation of two very thin equal flanges of area \(A_f\) at \(y=\pm c\), essentially all material reaches yield together. The first-yield and plastic moments both approach \(2A_f\sigma_Y c\). A shape factor of 1.5 is specific to the solid rectangle and cannot be carried over to this ideal flange pair.

Chapter 12 · Crack driving force and fracture assessment

Use an energy balance and a stress-intensity model to distinguish crack-controlled fracture from yielding of the gross section.

A sharp crack changes the question

An uncracked tensile coupon can often be assessed by comparing nominal stress with a yield stress. A sharp pre-existing crack concentrates the stress into a small region near its tip. Within linear elastic fracture mechanics the leading opening-mode field has magnitude proportional to \(K_I/\sqrt{2\pi r}\) at distance \(r\) ahead of the tip. The unbounded mathematical stress signals the limits of the elastic tip description. A material toughness measured under comparable conditions instead sets a threshold for crack extension.

A common representation is \(K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\), where \(a\) is the specified crack dimension, \(\sigma\) is a stated remote stress, and \(Y\) describes the geometry and loading. For a central crack in a sufficiently wide plate \(a\) is the half-length and \(Y\) approaches one. For a short edge crack in a wide plate \(a\) is its depth and a common approximation is \(Y\approx1.12\). Finite width, bending or a different crack shape changes the factor.

Relate compliance to an energy release rate

In a linear test specimen let load \(P\), opening displacement \(u\), and compliance \(C(a)=u/P\) refer to the same load point. At fixed load the stored elastic energy is \(P^2C/2\). The external load does work \(P\,du=P^2\,dC\) as the crack extends. The released potential energy is the difference, \(P^2 dC/2\). For a through crack with one advancing front and specimen thickness \(B\), its projected area increases by \(B\,da\), giving

\[G=\frac{P^2}{2B}\frac{dC}{da}=\frac{K_I^2}{E'},\qquad E'=E\ {\rm (plane\ stress)},\quad E'=E/(1-\nu^2)\ {\rm (plane\ strain)}.\]

The compliance argument explains how a distant load–displacement measurement can identify energy available at a crack. Define the crack area consistently: a symmetric specimen in which two tips advance together changes projected area twice as quickly for the same increment of half-length.

Infer a critical flaw size

Take a wide plate with an edge crack of depth 3.0 mm, tensile remote stress 160 MPa and illustrative applicable toughness \(K_c=35\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}\). The estimate \(K_I=1.12(160)\sqrt{\pi(0.003)}=17.4\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}\) uses metres for crack depth. Holding geometry factor and stress fixed, the critical depth is \(a_c=[35/(1.12\times160)]^2/\pi=0.0121\,\mathrm m\), about 12.1 mm.

At twice the stress this depth falls by a factor of four, to approximately 3.0 mm. Before using that extrapolation, check remaining net section, applicability of the wide-plate geometry and whether plasticity near the crack is small compared with crack and ligament dimensions. Toughness depends on temperature, constraint and loading rate. An inspection interval would additionally require a crack-growth law, a load history and a detection limit. The calculated critical depth describes one load state and cannot by itself predict the number of cycles remaining.

Interpret a crack-length uncertainty

At fixed remote stress and geometry factor a small relative error in crack depth gives approximately half that relative error in stress intensity, because \(dK/K=da/(2a)\). A 0.3 mm uncertainty on a 3 mm measurement therefore changes this estimated intensity by about five percent.

Problem sheet 6 · Plastic deformation and crack driving force

How much of the cross-section has yielded?

A rectangular strip 12 mm wide and 30 mm deep has elastic–perfectly plastic yield stress 200 MPa. Determine the first-yield and plastic moments. At a curvature three times the yield curvature, find the depth of the elastic core and resulting moment.

Reveal the worked solution

With \(c=15\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(M_p=b\sigma_Yc^2=540\,\mathrm{N\,m}\) and \(M_Y=2M_p/3=360\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). The elastic half-depth is \(a=c/3=5\,\mathrm{mm}\), giving a 10 mm deep core. The moment is \(12(200)(15^2-5^2/3)=520\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Yield has spread far inward for a comparatively small increase over the first-yield moment.

Changing the inspection threshold

A wide plate with a central through crack has applicable toughness 45 MPa√m at the service temperature. With remote tension 180 MPa and \(Y=1\), calculate critical half-length and full length. If inspection finds a full crack length of 16 mm, what remote stress gives the critical intensity?

Reveal the worked solution

The critical half-length is \(a_c=(45/180)^2/\pi=0.0199\,\mathrm m\), or 19.9 mm; the full length is 39.8 mm. The measured half-length is 8 mm, so \(\sigma_c=45/\sqrt{\pi(0.008)}=284\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Both predictions assume small-scale yielding and adequate plate width. Using full length inside the square root would underpredict the critical stress.

Infer release rate from two readings

A specimen with one advancing crack front has thickness 10 mm. Its measured compliance increases from 0.0250 to 0.0270 mm/kN as crack length rises by 1.0 mm. Under a fixed load of 4 kN estimate release rate. For plane stress with \(E=70\,\mathrm{GPa}\), find the corresponding intensity.

Reveal the worked solution

The slope is \(0.0020\,\mathrm{kN^{-1}}=2.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N^{-1}}\). Thus \(G=4000^2(2.0\times10^{-6})/[2(0.010)]=1600\,\mathrm{J/m^2}\). Then \(K=\sqrt{EG}=10.58\,\mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}\). This uses a local finite-difference slope, so significant curvature of the compliance data would call for measurements at closer crack lengths.

Where does a joint load go?

A lifting frame spans 6 m between a pin and a roller. A machine hanging from the middle applies a downward 100 kN load. The frame must carry that load without yielding, buckling or deflecting too far. Each check asks for a different property of the structure.

For the first calculation, idealise the joints as frictionless pins, the members as straight elastic bars and the load as a joint force. Neglect self-weight and out-of-plane motion. Each bar is then a two-force member. Its end forces lie along its axis. Let positive member force mean tension.

Place bottom joints A, B and C at \(x=0,3,6\) m and \(z=0\). Top joints D and E sit at \(x=1.5,4.5\) m and \(z=2\) m. Join AB, BC and DE horizontally, then connect the zigzag A–D–B–E–C. Support A is pinned, C is a horizontal roller, and the load acts at B. Horizontal and vertical force balance and moments about A give \(A_x=0\) and \(A_z=C_z=F/2=50\) kN.

Use a joint free-body diagram

Each diagonal rises 2 m over 1.5 m, so its length is 2.5 m and \(\sin\theta=0.8,\ \cos\theta=0.6\). Assume that every unknown force pulls away from its joint. Vertical balance at A then gives \(50+0.8N_{AD}=0\), hence \(N_{AD}=-62.5\) kN. Horizontal balance gives \(N_{AB}=-0.6N_{AD}=37.5\) kN. The minus sign identifies a compressed diagonal.

At B the two inner diagonals share the downward load, each supplying \(F/2\) vertically. Each therefore carries 62.5 kN in tension. At D the horizontal components of AD and DB both point to the right and equal 37.5 kN. Member DE carries 75 kN in compression to close the joint balance. Reflection supplies the remaining forces. The horizontal support reactions vanish, yet the chords carry substantial horizontal forces inside the frame.

From member strain to a displacement

With \(E=200\) GPa and area \(A=1000\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) throughout, the top chord carries a nominal compressive stress of 75 MPa. To find the movement of B, store the elastic strain energy of all seven bars. A bar of length \(\ell_i\) extends by \(N_i\ell_i/(EA)\) and stores \(N_i^2\ell_i/(2EA)\). Ramp the load slowly from zero. The external work is \(F\delta/2\) because the displacement grows linearly with load. Equating the two gives

\[\delta=\frac{1}{F}\sum_i\frac{N_i^2\ell_i}{EA}.\]

Use two 3 m bottom chords at 37.5 kN, one 3 m top chord at 75 kN and four 2.5 m diagonals at 62.5 kN. These groups contribute 0.422 mm, 0.844 mm and 1.953 mm respectively. Joint B moves down by 3.219 mm. Compression stores positive energy just as tension does. The largest individual force and the largest group contribution to displacement occur in different members.

For a member that carries a transverse load between joints, the bending moment also contributes to strain energy. Study bending in a clamped beam.

Transfer problem · A shaft that carries torque

A drive shaft transmits 5.0 kW at 600 rpm to a lifting drum. Its internal load is a torque, even when its axial force is zero. In a uniform solid circular shaft of radius \(r_o\), cross-sections rotate relative to their neighbours. A fibre at radius \(r\) acquires shear strain \(\gamma=r\,d\phi/dx\). Using \(\tau=G\gamma\) and taking moments of the shear stresses gives \(T=GJ\,d\phi/dx\), with \(J=\int_A r^2dA=\pi r_o^4/2\).

Take \(r_o=15\) mm, length 0.80 m and \(G=80\) GPa. The angular speed is \(600(2\pi/60)=62.8\) rad/s, so the transmitted torque is \(T=P/\omega=79.6\) N·m. Here \(J=7.95\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^4}\). The surface stress is \(Tr_o/J=15.0\) MPa, and the twist angle is \(TL/(GJ)=0.0100\) rad or 0.57°. Doubling the radius at the same power and speed reduces stress eightfold and twist sixteenfold. An area check alone would capture neither dependence.
