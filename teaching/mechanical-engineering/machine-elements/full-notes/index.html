Chapter 1 · From machine duty to a design load

Translate a machine’s operating cycle into member forces, stress amplitudes and a life estimate for a repeated duty block.

Follow the force through the machine

A machine specification may give lifting capacity in kilograms or motor power in kilowatts. Neither quantity by itself gives the tension in an individual link. Begin with the moving assembly and its connections. Isolate one body, mark every contact and decide which reactions the connection can transmit. A pin carries two force components in a planar model. A flexible cable carries tension along its length. A pair of guides may transmit a couple even though each individual contact carries only a normal force. These decisions determine which part experiences each load.

Consider a carriage raised by a vertical cable. Its cable attachment transfers tension through a flat link of net area \(A\). If upward is positive, the carriage balance reads \(F-mg=ma\), hence \(F=m(g+a)\). This also applies while the carriage is moving downwards and slowing down. Its upward acceleration increases tension. A speed record alone therefore conceals an important load case. Draw both the velocity and its slope before assigning signs. A cable solution with negative tension means that the cable would slacken and the assumed constraint has failed.

Separate a steady bias from an alternating load

Cut the link across its smallest section. With the load centred and bending negligible, integration of an approximately uniform axial stress gives \(F=\sigma A\). The stress at the two turning points defines the mean stress and alternating amplitude for that cycle. An amplitude is measured from the mean, so a recorded peak-to-peak range must be divided by two.

\[\sigma_{\max,\min}=F_{\max,\min}/A,\qquad \sigma_m=(\sigma_{\max}+\sigma_{\min})/2,\qquad \sigma_a=(\sigma_{\max}-\sigma_{\min})/2.\]

A part loaded from zero to a maximum tensile stress has equal positive mean and alternating components. A fully reversed load with the same peak tensile stress has zero mean and twice that alternating amplitude. To assess fatigue, a material curve must describe the applicable mean stress, surface condition, stress concentration and environment. For yielding, first compare the largest local stress with the yield strength. Those two calculations use different features of the same history.

A lift with repeated starts

A carriage and its payload have a combined mass of 800 kg. In each trip the drive accelerates upwards at 2.0 m/s² and later decelerates while travelling upwards at 3.0 m/s². The cable passes without mechanical advantage to a link with net section 80 mm². Gravity is 9.81 m/s². Assume the cable remains taut, and treat the measured accelerations as representing the load on the carriage.

The maximum tension is \(800(9.81+2.0)=9448\,\mathrm N\) and the minimum during the stated loaded cycle is \(800(9.81-3.0)=5448\,\mathrm N\). Dividing by area gives 118.1 and 68.1 MPa. Their mean is 93.1 MPa and their amplitude is 25.0 MPa. A stopped, suspended carriage still loads the link at 98.1 MPa. Unloading the carriage fully between trips would introduce another turning point. The original two-level description would no longer define every cycle experienced by the link.

Convert a repeated block into a fatigue estimate

Suppose tests at the applicable stress ratios give lives of \(8.0\times10^5\) cycles for ordinary loaded trips and \(1.2\times10^5\) for heavier trips. One representative block contains 200 ordinary and 30 heavy cycles. Palmgren–Miner averaging assigns the used fraction \(n_i/N_i\) to each level. If a block is repeated \(B\) times, its predicted accumulated damage is

\[D=B\sum_i n_i/N_i=B(200/800000+30/120000).\]

The conventional estimate \(D=1\) gives 2000 blocks. The smaller count of heavy cycles contributes half of the estimated damage. This calculation can help decide which trips to instrument. It assumes that sequence effects and interactions between levels can be neglected. Changing the order of the same cycles would leave this prediction unchanged. An overload that yields the link, changes its residual stress or damages a connection calls for evidence beyond this linear rule. Record the peak event separately when preparing the input for a component calculation.

Chapter 2 · Shaft reactions, twist and rotating bending

Trace power and belt forces into shaft reactions, then check the shaft section for yielding, twist and cyclic bending.

Rotation determines the torque, contacts determine the bending

A shaft transmits a given power through torque at a specified speed. Mechanical work in a small rotation is \(dW=T\,d\theta\), so power is \(T\omega\), with angular speed in radians per second. Belt tensions act at opposite sides of a pulley. Their difference gives the net driving torque. For two nearly parallel belt spans pulling in the same transverse direction their sum loads the shaft sideways. A belt with greater pretension can increase bearing loads although the transmitted power remains constant.

\[\omega=2\pi n/60,\qquad T=P/\omega=(F_1-F_2)r,\qquad F_\perp\simeq F_1+F_2 .\]

Choose bearing centres as ideal simple supports and identify where each torque enters or leaves the shaft. A section between an input coupling and an output pulley carries the transmitted torque. Beyond the output pulley it may carry transverse shear while carrying virtually zero torque. Mark the bending and torque diagrams separately so the combination at a critical cross-section represents loads that actually coexist there.

Determine the stress components from section geometry

For a solid circular shaft the bending second moment is \(I=\pi d^4/64\), and its polar moment is \(J=2I\). The elastic bending stress at the outer fibre is \(M(d/2)/I\); the torsional shear stress is \(T(d/2)/J\). Under proportional elastic loading, the plane-stress von Mises expression then gives a static yield check for a ductile material.

\[\sigma_b=32M/(\pi d^3),\qquad \tau_t=16T/(\pi d^3),\qquad \sigma_v=\sqrt{\sigma_b^2+3\tau_t^2}.\]

The cubic diameter dependence makes a shoulder important even before its local stress concentration is considered. These equations describe a smooth prismatic section and nominal stresses. At a fillet, the maximum stresses may be estimated from geometric concentration factors appropriate to bending and torsion. For cyclic design a fatigue concentration factor may differ from the elastic geometric factor because sensitivity to the notch depends on material and radius.

Size the loading at the pulley seat

A 120 mm radius pulley sits at the middle of a 0.40 m span and draws 6.0 kW at 600 rpm. The two belt forces are parallel and their sum is 1800 N. The driving coupling lies immediately outside the left bearing. The shaft is 32 mm in diameter. From \(\omega=62.83\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), the transmitted torque is 95.5 N m and the tension difference is \(95.5/0.120=795.8\,\mathrm N\). Combining difference and sum gives 1298 and 502 N.

Each bearing carries 900 N. Taking moments about a cut just to the left of the pulley gives \(M=900(0.20)=180\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). There the shaft still transmits full torque. Substitution gives 56.0 MPa bending and 14.8 MPa torsional shear. Thus the nominal von Mises stress is 61.6 MPa, giving a nominal yield safety factor of 4.06 for steel with 250 MPa yield strength. This calculation has not yet accounted for a keyway or the pulley seat fillet.

Follow one material point through a revolution

A fixed vertical belt pull produces a steady bending moment in the laboratory. Mark a point on the rotating shaft surface. It passes through the tensile and compressive bending fibres once each per turn, so that its bending stress is fully reversed with amplitude 56.0 MPa. Steady driving torque supplies mean shear. A fatigue test at zero mean axial load describes only one part of this combined loading history. A shaft that spends a long time at constant speed can accumulate many stress cycles while the torque meter shows a level trace.

The twist angle is a further service requirement. Integrate \(d\theta/dx=T/(GJ)\) along the torque-carrying length. With \(G=80\,\mathrm{GPa}\), \(J=1.029\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^4}\), and a 0.20 m torque-carrying span, the relative twist is 0.00232 rad, or 0.133°. Bending stress varies as the inverse cube of diameter and twist at fixed torque as the inverse fourth power. A diameter selected from a strength limit can still give an unacceptable positioning error at the driven component.

Problem sheet 1 · Duty blocks and shaft sections

Resolve a duty record into stresses and compare shaft bending, torsion and twist using the actual load paths.

An accelerating counterweight

A 550 kg counterweight hangs from a single vertical link of area 60 mm². Its upward acceleration ranges from −1.8 to +2.2 m/s² during each loaded cycle. Find the force extremes and the stress mean and amplitude. During commissioning the same link is fully unloaded once per trip instead. Which stress quantity changes for a simple zero-to-peak cycle?

Resolve the accelerations

The link force is \(m(g+a)\), giving 4406 and 6606 N for the stated endpoints. The corresponding stresses are 73.43 and 110.09 MPa. The mean is 91.76 MPa and the amplitude 18.33 MPa. For a single excursion from zero to the same peak, mean and amplitude are both 55.05 MPa. The peak is unchanged although the amplitude triples. Multiple turning points in an actual trip require separate cycle counting.

Use a measured duty block

A pump link sees 800 cycles per shift at a stress level with test life 2.0 million cycles, and 60 cycles at a level with test life 150,000 cycles. Find the expected shift count at unit damage under linear accumulation. What is the effect of halving the number of heavy events only?

Weight each level by its tested life

The two contributions are \(800/2000000=0.0004\) and \(60/150000=0.0004\) per shift. Their sum gives 1250 shifts. With only 30 heavy events per shift, the total becomes 0.0006 and the estimate rises to 1667 shifts. The predicted improvement is one third, despite the heavy-event count falling by half.

Bending at an off-centre pulley

A smooth 30 mm shaft spans 0.45 m between bearings. A pulley 0.15 m from the left support imposes a 1200 N downward load. The shaft receives 60 N m torque at its left end and delivers it at the pulley. Find both reactions, the nominal stresses just left of the pulley and the twist between left bearing and pulley for \(G=80\) GPa.

Follow the section cut

Moment balance about the left gives \(R_B=1200(0.15)/0.45=400\,\mathrm N\), hence \(R_A=800\,\mathrm N\) and \(M=120\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Using millimetres and N mm, \(\sigma_b=32(120000)/(\pi30^3)=45.3\,\mathrm{MPa}\) and \(\tau=16(60000)/(\pi30^3)=11.3\,\mathrm{MPa}\). With \(J=\pi(0.030)^4/32\), \(\theta=60(0.15)/(80\times10^9J)=0.00141\,\mathrm{rad}\), or 0.081°. The section to the right carries bending but no drive torque.

Chapter 3 · Preload, joint stiffness and separation

Compute how a tensile service load changes bolt force and clamp force, and determine when a closed-joint calculation ceases to apply.

Establish the state before the external load arrives

A tensioned bolt in an assembled flange has already elongated. The material clamped between head and nut has already shortened. In the ideal concentric joint these initial forces have equal magnitude \(F_i\), as a free-body diagram of either flange shows. Tightening stores elastic energy in both parts. Calling a joint unloaded usually means that the external service load is zero. The thread root can still carry a significant tensile stress.

Represent the effective bolt grip by a tensile spring \(k_b\) and the compressed members by a spring \(k_m\). For a uniform segment the axial stiffness is \(EA/L\); threaded and plain segments in series have added compliances. The member stiffness depends on the path through which compressive force spreads from the head and washer. A thin flexible flange may also bend. For this derivation consider one bolt, an axial load introduced at the outside faces and no eccentricity or prying. Let clamp force denote a positive magnitude in compression.

Apply compatibility to the load increment

An external separating force \(P\) allows the bolt to extend a little while the clamped members recover some of their initial compression. As long as the faces remain in contact, the extension and recovery have the same magnitude \(x\). The bolt-force increase is \(k_bx\) and the clamp-force decrease is \(k_mx\). Force equilibrium across the joint gives \(P=F_b-F_m=(k_b+k_m)x\). Eliminate \(x\) and introduce the load fraction \(C\).

\[C=\frac{k_b}{k_b+k_m},\quad F_b=F_i+CP,\quad F_m=F_i-(1-C)P.\]

The slopes of bolt force and clamp-force magnitude have opposite signs. The joint opens in this ideal model when \(F_m\) reaches zero, at \(P_{\rm sep}=F_i/(1-C)\). Continuing the same line beyond that point would predict tension between unbonded faces. Once open, the bolt must carry the full external tensile force and the load slope changes to unity. Both expressions give the same bolt force at the instant of separation.

Check a service cycle and a separate overload

Take \(k_b=180\,\mathrm{kN/mm}\), \(k_m=720\,\mathrm{kN/mm}\), \(F_i=25\,\mathrm{kN}\) and bolt tensile stress area \(A_t=58.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}\). A pressure pulsation creates a separating force cycling between 4 and 16 kN. Here \(C=0.20\), hence bolt force ranges from 25.8 to 28.2 kN. Clamp force falls from 21.8 to 12.2 kN and remains positive. The bolt’s alternating tensile stress is the force range divided by twice its tensile stress area.

\[\sigma_a=(28.2-25.8)10^3/(2\times58.0)=20.7\,\mathrm{MPa}.\]

At the maximum ordinary load the calculated tensile stress is 486 MPa. If the specified proof stress is 600 MPa, the axial proof-load benchmark is \(600(58.0)=34.8\,\mathrm{kN}\). The estimated separation threshold is \(25/0.8=31.25\,\mathrm{kN}\). A singular tensile overload of 34 kN opens the interface and brings the bolt to 34 kN, just below the supplied axial proof benchmark. It can nevertheless break the seal at the flange face. Different requirements can control the same event.

Use attainable preload, including its scatter

Increasing preload by 3 kN raises both initial forces and delays separation by \(3/(1-C)=3.75\,\mathrm{kN}\). It also raises the peak bolt stress by \(3000/58=51.7\,\mathrm{MPa}\) throughout the closed regime. Reducing bolt stiffness relative to the members can lower the alternating force. For example a longer effective grip can increase bolt compliance. Recheck the residual clamp and separation conditions whenever changing stiffness.

If tightening produces a range of preloads, assess separation with the low end and proof stress with the high end. An assembly tolerance of ±3 kN around the nominal value gives a minimum separation threshold of \(22/0.8=27.5\,\mathrm{kN}\). The largest ordinary bolt force would be \(28+0.2(16)=31.2\,\mathrm{kN}\). The preload scatter enters these checks even though the external service force is perfectly known. In a real flange, surface settling, differential thermal expansion and off-axis loading can change the available clamp force after assembly.

Chapter 4 · Bearing arrangements and rating life

Select the reactions carried by each bearing and convert a changing bearing load into an equivalent load and a basic rating life.

Constrain motion while allowing expansion

A radial bearing supports the shaft perpendicular to its axis. Some bearing types can also transmit an axial reaction in one or both directions. A common two-bearing shaft arrangement uses one locating bearing or bearing pair to establish axial position. A second support permits axial expansion while still providing radial support. Depending on construction, that relative movement occurs within the bearing or between a ring and its seat. Drawing a free-body diagram with two fully fixed axial supports can hide an unwanted thermal force.

For example, a 0.60 m steel shaft with thermal expansion coefficient \(12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) lengthens by 0.288 mm when it warms by 40 K relative to a housing of unchanged length. If the support arrangement can accommodate that travel, it need not create a significant thermal axial reaction. A locating pair set with insufficient operating clearance can behave very differently. First identify radial reactions in each transverse plane and how the axial thrust returns to the housing. Only then assign loads to individual bearings.

Express a survival criterion in revolutions

A loaded rolling contact subjects the ring material to repeated subsurface stresses. The basic dynamic load rating \(C\) is associated with a basic rating life of one million revolutions at a constant equivalent dynamic load equal to \(C\). The basic rating life \(L_{10}\) describes a population under the rating assumptions. Ninety percent are expected to reach or exceed this life with respect to rolling-contact fatigue. For ball bearings use exponent \(p=3\); for roller bearings use \(p=10/3\). The rating model is empirical.

\[L_{10}=(C/P)^p \ \text{million revolutions},\qquad L_{10h}=\frac{10^6}{60n}(C/P)^p .\]

Here \(P\) is the equivalent dynamic bearing load and \(n\) the speed in revolutions per minute. A purely radial load on a suitable radial ball bearing can be used directly. When radial and axial components both act, obtain the appropriate equivalent-load coefficients and applicability thresholds for that bearing. Their directions relative to the shaft matter. The hypotenuse of axial and radial loads generally fails to represent the internal rolling contact load distribution.

Weight changing loads by revolutions

Suppose interval \(i\) occupies fraction \(f_i\) of the revolutions. Under the usual linear accumulation approximation, the life fraction consumed per revolution scales with \(P_i^p/C^p\). Equating the weighted consumption to that of a constant load gives

\[P_{\rm eq}^p=\sum_i f_iP_i^p,\qquad f_i=\frac{n_it_i}{\sum_jn_jt_j}.\]

Consider a ball bearing rated at \(C=18\,\mathrm{kN}\). It operates for 30 minutes at 1000 rpm under 2 kN of pure radial load, followed by 10 minutes at 1500 rpm under 4 kN. The intervals contain 30,000 and 15,000 revolutions. Thus \(P_{\rm eq}^3=(2/3)2^3+(1/3)4^3=26.667\,\mathrm{kN^3}\), giving 2.988 kN. The rating life is \(18^3/26.667=218.7\) million revolutions. The cycle mean speed is \(45000/40=1125\) rpm, giving about 3240 operating hours. The heavier interval contributes four fifths of the calculated damage even though it occupies only one quarter of the operating time.

Treat slow overloads as a separate question

While the shaft is stationary there are no accumulated revolutions in the preceding fatigue sum. A large stationary force can still leave permanent indentations at the rolling contacts. A static check compares the bearing-specific static equivalent load \(P_0\) with its basic static rating \(C_0\). For an example catalogue entry \(C_0=10\,\mathrm{kN}\) and a stationary equivalent load of 6 kN, the ratio \(s_0=C_0/P_0=1.67\). Compare this value with the application requirement, taking account of shock and required smoothness.

A selected rolling bearing also requires sufficient lubrication and compatible fits and operating clearances. The basic life calculation quantifies one fatigue mechanism. It is especially useful for comparing changes to a known duty cycle because of the strongly nonlinear dependence on load. When a measured bearing fails well before its estimated rating life, inspect mounting marks, lubrication and contamination evidence before changing the equivalent load to fit the failure time.

The manufacturer explains the bearing ratings and exponents in its SKF rolling bearing catalogue.

Distinguish displacement control from a rating test

Contact between a rolling element and a race concentrates elastic load over a small area. An ideal sphere on an elastic half-space gives \(a\propto\sqrt{R\delta}\) and \(F\propto E^*\sqrt{R}\,\delta^{3/2}\) for imposed approach \(\delta\). Predict the radius and force ratios when approach falls from 25 to 12.5 micrometres. In a second test halve both Young’s moduli while keeping the original approach. Which output should remain almost constant? Compare both predictions on the same mesh. These static contact outputs describe the stress-producing contact mechanism; the bearing rating life also requires repeated loading and bearing-specific rating data. Inspect the sphere-contact experiment.

Problem sheet 2 · Clamped interfaces and rolling contact

Evaluate the least favourable preload and bearing duty, then distinguish thermal expansion from externally applied loading.

A tolerance band on preload

A joint has stiffnesses \(k_b=200\) and \(k_m=600\) kN/mm and is preloaded to \(18\pm2\) kN. The external separating force cycles from 2 to 12 kN. Find the minimum residual clamp, maximum bolt force and alternating stress for a stress area of 36 mm². Find the smallest separation load.

Use the appropriate end of the tolerance interval

The bolt load fraction is 0.25. The least residual clamp is \(16-0.75(12)=7\,\mathrm{kN}\). The largest bolt force is \(20+0.25(12)=23\,\mathrm{kN}\). For any fixed preload within the band, the stress amplitude is \(0.25(12-2)1000/(2\times36)=34.7\,\mathrm{MPa}\). The lowest preload separates first, at \(16/0.75=21.33\,\mathrm{kN}\), above the ordinary maximum.

Convert a load block into bearing hours

A ball bearing turns for 15 min at 800 rpm under radial load 3 kN, then 10 min at 1200 rpm under 5 kN. Find the equivalent radial load, the basic life in hours for \(C=22\,\mathrm{kN}\), and the static ratio for a separate stationary load \(P_0=8\,\mathrm{kN}\) and \(C_0=12\,\mathrm{kN}\).

Count revolutions before averaging

Each interval contains 12,000 revolutions. Thus \(P_{\rm eq}^3=(3^3+5^3)/2=76\,\mathrm{kN^3}\), hence \(P_{\rm eq}=4.236\,\mathrm{kN}\). Life is \(22^3/76=140.1\) million revolutions at cycle-average speed \(24000/25=960\,\mathrm{rpm}\), or about 2432 h. For the separate stationary event, \(C_0/P_0=1.50\). A stationary overload adds no revolutions but can indent the races.

Two bearing seats warm by different amounts

A 0.50 m steel shaft warms by 55 K while its aluminium housing between the support planes warms by 20 K. Use linear expansions 12 and 23 micrometres per metre per kelvin. Determine relative growth. Does 0.08 mm axial travel accommodate it? Estimate the force after that travel is exhausted if the equivalent axial stiffness is 110 kN/mm.

Compare travel with differential growth

The shaft lengthens by 0.330 mm and the housing by 0.230 mm. Thus one end must accommodate 0.100 mm relative to the locating end. The available travel leaves 0.020 mm incompatible. In the stated linear idealisation the extra axial force magnitude is \(110(0.020)=2.2\,\mathrm{kN}\). Changing the locating scheme or the operating clearances changes this reaction.

Chapter 5 · Spur gear kinematics and tooth loads

Use pitch-line compatibility and power balance to find gear speed, tooth forces and the bending demand at a tooth root.

Replace the mesh by two rolling pitch circles

Two external spur gears have straight teeth parallel to their axes. The common normal to a pair of involute flanks passes through a fixed pitch point on the line joining the centres. At that point the pitch circles roll together at equal tangential speed. The actual contacting flanks slide relative to one another away from the pitch point. Distinguishing pitch-circle motion from local sliding explains why the simple velocity ratio can hold while the tooth surfaces still require lubrication.

For unshifted standard gears of the same module \(m\), pitch diameter equals module times tooth count, and the design centre distance equals half the sum of the pitch diameters. With a common angular sign convention on both parallel axes, the two external gears turn in opposite directions. Writing equality of pitch speeds in magnitude and then inserting direction gives

\[d_i=mz_i,\qquad a=(d_1+d_2)/2,\qquad \frac{\omega_2}{\omega_1}=-\frac{d_1}{d_2}=-\frac{z_1}{z_2}.\]

An idler changes the direction of the last gear in a simple train without changing the magnitude of the first-to-last ratio. A compound shaft carrying two distinct gears is different. Those two gears share angular speed, so the ratios of successive meshes multiply. Draw the shafts before multiplying any tooth counts.

Resolve the force along the common normal

Neglect tooth friction in the force construction. The resultant \(F_n\) acts normal to the contacting flanks. Its projection along the pitch tangent, \(F_t=F_n\cos\alpha\), produces torque on each shaft. Its radial projection, \(F_r=F_t\tan\alpha\), pushes the two gear centres apart. A straight spur mesh with aligned shafts produces no ideal axial component. The bearings must return both transverse force components to the housing.

\[T_1=F_t d_1/2,\qquad T_2=F_t d_2/2,\qquad P=F_t v,\quad v=\pi d_1 n_1/60 .\]

The transmitted tooth forces have equal magnitudes on both gears. The larger gear develops more torque because that force acts through a larger radius. Multiplying output torque by the correspondingly lower output speed recovers the input power in an ideal mesh. When estimating useful output power for a real stage, multiply input power by the specified efficiency and state which shaft receives that output.

A low-speed conveyor stage

A motor drives a 20-tooth pinion at 1200 rpm. It meshes with a 60-tooth gear of module 3 mm and pressure angle 20°. The input power is 4.0 kW. Pitch diameters are 60 and 180 mm, the centre distance is 120 mm, and the output turns at 400 rpm in the opposite direction. Angular input speed is 125.66 rad/s, giving pinion torque 31.83 N m. The pitch-line speed is 3.770 m/s.

Tangential force follows from \(4000/3.770=1061\,\mathrm N\). The radial separating force is \(1061\tan20^\circ=386\,\mathrm N\). The normal force is \(1061/\cos20^\circ=1129\,\mathrm N\). The ideal output torque is 95.5 N m. With a measured stage efficiency of 0.96, useful output is 3.84 kW and output torque is 91.7 N m. A design based on shaft torque alone omits the transverse force that bends the shaft and loads the bearings.

Build a first tooth-root estimate

For a preliminary bending model treat the loaded tooth as a short cantilever of face width \(b\), effective thickness \(s\) and moment arm \(h\). The rectangular section modulus is \(bs^2/6\); the bending moment is approximately \(F_t h\). Hence root stress scales as \(6F_t h/(bs^2)\). For geometrically similar teeth write \(Y=s^2/(6hm)\), a dimensionless form factor in the module convention used here. Then \(\sigma_b\simeq F_t/(bmY)\).

Taking \(b=24\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(m=3\,\mathrm{mm}\) and an illustrative \(Y=0.32\) gives \(1061/(24\times3\times0.32)=46.1\,\mathrm{MPa}\). This estimates nominal bending in the simplified geometry. The real tooth also sees local root concentration and surface contact stress. Uneven load across the face, dynamic tooth loads and changing load sharing during mesh can change the result. The shaft experiences a transverse force throughout steady running; an individual tooth is loaded only as it enters each mesh. That distinction changes its number of fatigue cycles.

Chapter 6 · Tolerances, interference and torque transmission

Turn shaft and hub limits into a range of contact pressures and check whether a cylindrical interference fit can transmit torque.

Convert manufacturing limits into assembly conditions

A shaft and a hub can share a nominal diameter while their manufactured diameters differ by several tens of micrometres. Name the diameters explicitly before subtracting them. For the present joint use positive diametral interference \(I=d_s-d_h\), where \(d_s\) is the free shaft diameter and \(d_h\) the free bore. Assemble the smallest shaft with the largest hole to obtain the minimum interference. The opposite pair gives the maximum. Worst-case limits do not require a probability distribution for the production process.

Consider a 40 mm connection with shaft limits 40.030 to 40.045 mm and bore limits 40.000 to 40.015 mm. The minimum difference is 0.015 mm and the maximum 0.045 mm. Every permitted pair interferes. The radial mismatch is half the diametral interference because the radius changes on both sides of a diameter. Confusing these conventions introduces a factor of two in the inferred pressure and torque.

Relate geometric mismatch to elastic deformation

When the parts return to their common assembly temperature, contact pressure compresses the shaft and expands the bore. Assume cylindrical surfaces, a short open-ended elastic hub with negligible axial stress, a solid elastic shaft and approximately uniform pressure away from the ends. Define the radial compliance \(c_r\) as the sum of the inward shaft and outward hub displacements per unit interface pressure. Compatibility across the common radius then reads

\[\delta_r=I/2=|u_s|+u_h=p\,c_r,\qquad p=\frac{I}{2c_r}.\]

The combined compliance can be found from an elastic cylinder calculation or a verified test of geometrically identical parts. Providing it separately permits a torque calculation without assuming an infinitely stiff hub. A thinner hub generally expands further at the same pressure. Its larger compliance reduces the pressure developed by a given interference. Surface roughness that flattens during assembly can also reduce the effective interference relative to a measurement over the original asperities.

Integrate friction over the contact surface

A narrow surface patch of area \(dA\) can support a tangential friction force up to \(\mu p\,dA\). All such forces act at radius \(r=d/2\) and oppose impending rotation. Under uniform contact pressure and fully mobilised Coulomb friction, integrating around the circumference and along the overlap length \(L\) gives the limiting torque. For a purely axial pull the same friction forces act along the axis and their direct sum gives the limiting pull-out force.

\[T_{\lim}=\int r\mu p\,dA=\frac{\pi}{2}\mu pLd^2,\qquad F_{\lim}=\mu p\pi dL .\]

Take \(L=35\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\mu=0.12\), and \(c_r=0.50\,\mathrm{\mu m/MPa}\) in the 40 mm joint. The radial mismatches range from 7.5 to 22.5 micrometres, so calculated pressures range from 15 to 45 MPa. At the lower limit the tangential force capacity is \(0.12(15)\pi(40)(35)=7917\,\mathrm N\), and the torque capacity is \(7917(0.020)=158\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). At the upper limit pressure and capacity are three times larger. Use the lowest pressure to test transmission and the highest to evaluate hub stress.

Provide thermal clearance and account for combined service loads

Suppose only the hub is heated, so its bore grows approximately by \(\alpha d\Delta T\). To slip the worst-case pair together with a further 0.020 mm of diametral assembly clearance, the bore must grow by \(0.045+0.020=0.065\,\mathrm{mm}\). With \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) a uniform temperature rise of \(0.065/[40(12\times10^{-6})]=135\,\mathrm K\) is required in this ideal expansion calculation. Heat loss during transfer changes the actual temperature at insertion.

A shaft may also carry a sustained axial pull while delivering torque. Under the uniform-traction model the axial and circumferential components of the surface friction force share one bound. At the lower interference with a 4.0 kN pull the remaining circumferential capacity is \(\sqrt{7917^2-4000^2}=6832\,\mathrm N\), or about 137 N m. Checking the separate pure-load capacities would miss this interaction. If repeated microslip, an uncertain friction coefficient or stiffness near the hub edges is important, measurements of the actual assembly can determine which assumptions need closer investigation.

Chapter 7 · Helical springs, wire stress and stored energy

Derive the rate of a close-coiled compression spring and check its working stress and available travel.

An axial spring force twists the wire

A helical spring appears to shorten along its axis under a compressive force. Follow one turn of its wire, however, and the main elastic deformation is torsion. For a close-coiled spring the helix angle is small and the force line along the spring axis lies a mean radius \(D/2\) from the wire centre. A cut through a wire turn therefore transmits a torque \(T=FD/2\) as well as direct shear. Use mean coil diameter \(D\), wire diameter \(d\) and \(N_a\) active turns. End turns that rest against a seat may add solid height without contributing equally to the spring rate.

Approximate wire length as \(\pi D N_a\) and wire polar moment as \(\pi d^4/32\). Under small elastic deformation the torsional strain energy is \(U=T^2\ell/(2GJ)\). Differentiating this energy with respect to axial load gives axial deflection, since an incremental load is work-conjugate to that displacement.

\[U=\frac{4F^2D^3N_a}{Gd^4},\qquad \delta=\frac{\partial U}{\partial F}=\frac{8FD^3N_a}{Gd^4},\qquad k=F/\delta=\frac{Gd^4}{8D^3N_a}.\]

Doubling the number of active turns halves the rate at fixed wire and coil diameter. Increasing wire diameter has a much stronger fourth-power effect. Changing wire diameter at fixed outside diameter also changes the mean coil diameter, so both terms must be recomputed.

Account for the curvature of the wire

Nominal torsional surface stress in straight wire is \(16T/(\pi d^3)=8FD/(\pi d^3)\). Curvature and direct shear raise the maximum stress on the inside of a real coil. One common elastic estimate for round wire is the Wahl factor \(K_w=(4C-1)/(4C-4)+0.615/C\), with spring index \(C=D/d\). Here it multiplies the nominal torsional expression to estimate peak shear. A small spring index makes the curvature contribution more important. Use a consistent stress convention with the material or fatigue data used for assessment.

Compare working travel with available space

A fixture spring uses 4 mm steel wire, 28 mm mean diameter, six active turns and \(G=79\,\mathrm{GPa}\). Its rate is \(79000(4^4)/[8(28^3)(6)]=19.2\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). At 120 N it compresses 6.25 mm and stores \(F\delta/2=0.375\,\mathrm J\). The spring index is seven, giving \(K_w=1.213\) and corrected shear \(1.213[8(120)(28)/(\pi4^3)]=162\,\mathrm{MPa}\).

With eight total turns and an ideal closed and ground construction, an approximate solid height is \(8(4)=32\,\mathrm{mm}\). A 44 mm free length leaves about 12 mm before full contact. At the calculated working deflection the remaining travel is 5.75 mm. Travel to solid height sets a geometric limit even if a stress calculation permits a larger load. When coils start contacting sequentially, the active turn count falls and the spring stiffens; the preceding constant-rate relation then no longer describes its full travel.

Check the active turn count

Two measured loads of 40 and 100 N at compressions of 2 and 5 mm give an incremental rate of \(60/3=20\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). Using a difference between readings removes a constant force offset. A change of slope near full compression can indicate that additional coils have made contact.

Chapter 8 · Belt traction, wrap and power transmission

Derive the tension-ratio bound for a flat belt and determine the pretension and bearing load needed to transmit a specified power.

Resolve the forces on a small arc

A flat belt transmits torque through friction distributed over the part of the pulley it touches. Cut out a short belt element subtending \(d\theta\). Let tension entering the element be \(T\) and tension leaving be \(T+dT\). For a flexible belt in quasistatic normal equilibrium the two tension vectors have a small inward radial resultant \(T\,d\theta\). The pulley supplies an outward normal force of the same magnitude. Neglect belt bending stiffness and centrifugal tension in the following low-speed model.

At impending gross slip, available tangential friction is \(\mu\,dN\). Tangential equilibrium gives \(dT=\mu T\,d\theta\) along the direction of increasing belt tension. Integrate from the slack to the tight end of a contact arc of angle \(\beta\) in radians. Below impending slip the resulting ratio is an inequality.

\[\ln(T_1/T_2)\le\mu\beta,\qquad T_1/T_2\le e^{\mu\beta},\qquad P=(T_1-T_2)v.\]

The final power expression comes from transmitted torque \((T_1-T_2)r\) times angular velocity \(v/r\). It is the tension difference that drives the machine. Both tensions contribute to radial loading on the shafts. In an open belt drive the smaller pulley often has the smaller contact angle and controls traction first.

Compute the least operating tensions

A flat belt must transmit 2.0 kW at 8.0 m/s. It wraps through 160° on the limiting pulley, with effective friction coefficient 0.30. The required tension difference is 250 N. Converting 160° to 2.793 rad gives a maximum ratio \(r_T=e^{0.30(2.793)}=2.31\). At the limiting ratio \(T_2=250/(r_T-1)=191\,\mathrm N\) and \(T_1=441\,\mathrm N\). Lower slack-side tension would require a ratio beyond the assumed friction limit.

For negligible centrifugal effect and approximately equal span compliances, an initial pretension estimate is \(T_0=(T_1+T_2)/2=316\,\mathrm N\) per span. If the two straight spans pull nearly parallel, the resultant transverse shaft force is approximately \(T_1+T_2=632\,\mathrm N\). If their directions differ appreciably, add the two tension vectors rather than their magnitudes. A higher pretension improves the available traction margin while increasing load on bearings and belt cords.

Decide what a loss of friction does

If oil contamination reduces the friction coefficient to 0.20 at the same wrap, the limiting tension ratio falls to \(e^{0.20(2.793)}=1.75\). If the tension sum remains 632 N, the largest transferable difference is \(632(r_T-1)/(r_T+1)\simeq172\,\mathrm N\), giving at most 1.38 kW at 8 m/s. The original power demand now exceeds available traction. A larger contact angle, different belt design or altered surface condition may address the shortfall; increasing motor power alone does not change the traction bound. A toothed timing belt has a different transmission mechanism and requires its tooth engagement and cord loading to be evaluated separately.

Interpret belt speed

The pitch-line speed in the no-slip approximation is \(\pi d n/60\). A 200 mm pulley carrying the original 8 m/s belt turns at approximately 764 rpm. Speed should be measured on both shafts if slipping contact is suspected.

Chapter 9 · Friction clutches, braking torque and heat

Integrate annular contact traction and connect the torque needed to synchronise rotating bodies with their frictional heat release.

Each ring acts through a different lever arm

A single-plate clutch uses an axial force to transmit torque across annular friction interfaces. Assume axisymmetric pressure \(p(r)\) with inner radius \(r_i\), outer radius \(r_o\) and friction coefficient \(\mu\). A narrow ring has normal force \(dN=p(r)2\pi r\,dr\). When slipping with fully mobilised friction, it transmits torque \(dT=\mu r\,dN\). The normal force on each active interface is the axial clamp force \(W\) in an ordinary axial stack; the total torque capacity adds over the number \(z\) of active interfaces.

For uniform pressure integrate the normal force and torque separately, then eliminate pressure. For the idealised uniform-wear law the wear rate scales with \(pv\). Since sliding speed is proportional to \(r\), equal wear implies \(p(r)r\) constant. Integration gives two different effective radii.

\[T=z\mu W r_e,\qquad r_{e,p}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{r_o^3-r_i^3}{r_o^2-r_i^2},\qquad r_{e,w}=(r_o+r_i)/2 .\]

Uniform pressure is a useful model for a new ideal face; uniform wear models a different radial distribution. Both depend on alignment and the ability of the backing plates to distribute load. The distinction is greater for a wide annulus than for a narrow ring.

Determine the relative speed decay

During engagement two rotors initially have angular speeds \(\omega_1,\omega_2\) and inertias \(I_1,I_2\). If external torques are negligible during a short engagement, angular momentum conservation gives the common final speed. Friction does equal and opposite angular work on the two rotors, but at different instantaneous speeds while they slip. Their net mechanical energy decreases. Eliminating the final speed from the energy difference gives

\[\omega_f=\frac{I_1\omega_1+I_2\omega_2}{I_1+I_2},\qquad E_{\rm heat}=\tfrac12 I_{\rm red}(\omega_1-\omega_2)^2,\quad I_{\rm red}=\frac{I_1I_2}{I_1+I_2}.\]

For a constant slipping torque \(T\), relative speed decreases at \(T/I_{\rm red}\) until synchronisation. Increasing clamp force shortens the engagement but does not reduce this energy at the same initial speeds in the isolated model. It increases the heat generation rate and can alter peak surface temperature.

Check one start and repeated operation

Let \(I_1=0.20,\ I_2=0.30\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and starting speeds be 100 and 0 rad/s. Then \(\omega_f=40\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(I_{\rm red}=0.12\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and heat release is 600 J. For \(r_i=40,\ r_o=80\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(z=2\), \(\mu=0.30\), \(W=1000\,\mathrm N\) and uniform wear, the torque is \(2(0.30)(1000)(0.060)=36\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). Synchronisation takes \(0.12(100)/36=0.333\,\mathrm s\). The mean heat generation over that interval is 1.80 kW. Six identical starts per minute give an average of 60 W over a complete operating cycle. These two rates govern different aspects of cooling and surface temperature; an application with an actively driven motor during engagement requires its external work to be included.

Check the pressure at the inner radius

Under uniform wear \(pr=C\) and \(W=2\pi C(r_o-r_i)\). At the stated load and radii the maximum pressure occurs at the inner edge and equals \(1000/[2\pi(0.080-0.040)(0.040)]=99.5\,\mathrm{kPa}\). An average face pressure alone would hide this radial concentration.

Chapter 10 · Hydrodynamic lubrication and sliding bearings

Derive the flow balance in a converging lubricant film and determine why the sliding surface can support a load without solid contact.

Where lubricant pressure comes from

A shaft turning inside a plain bearing drags lubricant around with its surface. A load displaces the journal from the bore centre, creating a film whose gap narrows in one direction. Wall motion carries oil into this wedge, and a pressure gradient redistributes the flow so the same fluid rate passes successive sections. Pressure integrated over the bearing can then support a load. Surface motion alone in a uniform gap between surfaces with equal end pressures cannot produce this load in the ideal steady model.

Consider a broad slider bearing rather than the full journal geometry. Let a lower wall move at speed \(U\); a fixed upper wall forms a slowly varying small gap \(h(x)\). Assume incompressible Newtonian lubricant, constant viscosity, negligible inertia and negligible leakage through the sides. Pressure is nearly uniform across the thickness. The axial momentum equation reduces to \(\mu d^2u/dy^2=dp/dx\), and no slip gives \(u(0)=U,\ u(h)=0\).

\[u=U(1-y/h)-\frac{1}{2\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}\,y(h-y),\qquad q=\frac{Uh}{2}-\frac{h^3}{12\mu}\frac{dp}{dx}.\]

Mass conservation makes \(q\) per unit width constant along the bearing. Where the local dragged flow \(Uh/2\) exceeds \(q\), pressure rises downstream. Where it falls short, pressure falls and supplies a forward pressure-driven contribution. The transition locates a pressure maximum.

Solve a two-depth ideal bearing

Consider a stepped pad with two equal 10 mm long regions, gaps \(h_1=0.20\) and \(h_2=0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) in the sliding direction. Both ends have ambient pressure, \(U=1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\), and \(\mu=0.05\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\). Ignore the local loss at the abrupt step and treat each region as fully developed. Integration of \(dp/dx=12\mu(Uh/2-q)/h^3\) across both regions gives zero net pressure change. With equal lengths this fixes

\[q=\frac U2\frac{h_1^{-2}+h_2^{-2}}{h_1^{-3}+h_2^{-3}}=5.56\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}.\]

In the first region \(Uh_1/2=1.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\). The positive pressure gradient is \(3.33\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa/m}\), giving an interface gauge pressure of 33.3 kPa. The second region has an equal opposite gradient. The resulting triangular pressure distribution integrates to a force per unit width of \(0.5(0.020)(33333)=333\,\mathrm{N/m}\). A nominal 50 mm width would thus support approximately 16.7 N under the negligible-side-leakage assumption.

Understand failure at low speed

With zero sliding speed, equal end pressures imply zero steady gauge pressure everywhere in this model, so the load capacity vanishes. Start-up and shutdown therefore differ from established hydrodynamic running. A separately pressurised hydrostatic bearing can carry load at zero shaft speed. If a solution predicts large negative absolute pressures in a diverging region, actual oil may cavitate and the full-film boundary conditions must change. Viscous heating can also lower the local viscosity. Film thickness, thermal balance and supply conditions all matter when transferring the simple slider result to a journal in service.

Predict a viscosity change

For the same slider geometry and speed with equal prescribed end pressures, the calculated flux is independent of viscosity but all gauge pressure gradients are proportional to viscosity. Halving viscosity in the ideal stepped example therefore halves its load capacity at unchanged film thickness.

Chapter 11 · Eccentrically loaded weld groups

Resolve an eccentric in-plane force into direct shear and torsional shear and find the combined nominal throat demand.

Reduce a weld to a carrying area

A fillet weld joins two plates along a line, but its specified leg length is not the effective area transmitting force. For an ideal equal-leg 45° fillet, the shortest distance from the root to the theoretical weld face is the throat \(t=s/\sqrt{2}\), where \(s\) is leg length. Effective throat area is \(t\) times effective weld length under the simplified model. End details and local weld defects require separate assessment.

Consider a bracket carrying an in-plane force whose line of action misses the weld group centroid. Move the force to that centroid and retain its moment \(M_z=Fe\). The direct force gives a uniform elastic shear flow vector \(q_d=F/\sum L\) under the line-weld approximation. The moment demands another shear flow, tangent about the centroid. For a rigid attachment and uniform throat and elastic response, assume its magnitude is proportional to radius \(r\), as expected from a small relative rotation.

\[J_L=\int_{\rm welds}r^2\,ds,\qquad q_m(r)=\frac{M_z r}{J_L},\qquad \boldsymbol{\tau}=(\mathbf q_d+\mathbf q_m)/t .\]

The line property \(J_L\) has units of length cubed, since weld length rather than throat area is integrated. For an unequal throat one must weight the centroid and polar property with the appropriate area or stiffness. Add direct and moment contributions as vectors at each point before locating maximum stress.

Calculate a corner stress

Take two vertical parallel welds of length 80 mm each, centred at \(x=\pm30\,\mathrm{mm}\). Their endpoints lie at \(y=\pm40\,\mathrm{mm}\). An upward force \(F=4.0\,\mathrm{kN}\) acts at \(x=+100\,\mathrm{mm}\). Its equivalent counterclockwise moment is 400,000 N mm. The total line length is 160 mm, so the direct flow is 25 N/mm upwards. The group line polar property is

\[J_L=2[80(30)^2+80^3/12]=229333\,\mathrm{mm^3}.\]

At the upper right corner the moment-induced flow vector is \(M_z(-y,x)/J_L=(-69.8,52.3)\,\mathrm{N/mm}\). Adding the direct contribution gives \((-69.8,77.3)\,\mathrm{N/mm}\), magnitude 104.2 N/mm. By symmetry the lower right corner has the same magnitude; the left endpoints experience partial cancellation of the vertical components. For an effective throat 4 mm, the maximum nominal combined shear is 26.0 MPa.

Decide what changes the joint demand

If the force passes through the centroid the moment vanishes and the throat stress is only \(25/4=6.25\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Increasing throat lowers nominal stress but leaves the line shear flow required by equilibrium unchanged. Moving the force closer to the attachment or spreading the welds farther apart can reduce the moment-induced flow. A transverse load normal to the plate introduces bending or prying and would require a different weld-group analysis. Fatigue at the weld toe depends on local geometry and the applicable welded-detail category, so the present nominal elastic calculation alone cannot predict a fatigue life.

Recover a leg dimension

A 4 mm ideal throat corresponds to a theoretical equal leg of \(4\sqrt2=5.66\,\mathrm{mm}\). A drawing that specifies a 4 mm leg instead leaves only a 2.83 mm theoretical throat, raising the calculated nominal maximum stress from 26.0 to about 36.8 MPa.

Chapter 12 · Tolerance stacks and reliability of assemblies

Determine functional clearance by worst-case and statistical stacking, and connect component life probabilities to assembly reliability.

Begin with a physical closing dimension

A drawing is useful only if the manufactured pieces can meet in the intended order and provide the required clearance. Choose a positive direction along the assembly and write the closing dimension from a single datum. For a spacer fitted between two shoulders with a washer, suppose the available nominal length is \(H\), the spacer length is \(S\) and the washer thickness is \(W\). The clearance is \(g=H-S-W\). Housing expansion or coating thickness can be included as additional signed terms if they occur in the operating condition being assessed.

With specified independent admissible intervals, the worst-case minimum is \(H_{\min}-S_{\max}-W_{\max}\). Independence is unnecessary for this conservative bound, although correlated dimensions may prevent its most extreme combination from occurring. A statistical treatment asks a different question: given a supported production distribution, what fraction is likely to achieve a required clearance? Manufacturing limits alone do not determine such a distribution.

Propagate measured spreads

Write each dimension as its mean plus a deviation. The mean clearance is the same signed sum of means. For independent dimensions the variance is the sum of their variances because squaring the coefficients removes their signs. More generally, for a dimension vector with covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) and coefficient vector \(a\), \(\sigma_g^2=a^T\Sigma a\). A common machining datum can introduce correlations that either reduce or increase clearance variation.

Assume \(H=50.00\pm0.15,\ S=48.00\pm0.12,\ W=1.70\pm0.09\,\mathrm{mm}\). The nominal clearance is 0.30 mm and limiting clearance interval is \([0.30-0.36,0.30+0.36]=[-0.06,0.66]\,\mathrm{mm}\). Thus the stated tolerances do not guarantee a positive gap. Suppose separately that production measurements support independent, centred normal models with standard deviations 0.050, 0.040 and 0.030 mm, without further sorting. The resulting standard deviation is

\[\sigma_g=\sqrt{0.050^2+0.040^2+0.030^2}=0.0707\,\mathrm{mm}.\]

Interference requires clearance below zero, a deviation of \(0.30/0.0707=4.24\) standard deviations. The normal model estimates about \(1.1\times10^{-5}\) of pairs would interfere, roughly eleven per million. This small estimated fraction and the possibility in a worst-case limit calculation address different production questions. An unmeasured mean shift or sorting parts to their tolerance limits can substantially change the probability.

Combine component survival probabilities

Define reliability at an explicit mission duration as the probability that a component performs its required function through that duration. If a machine requires both of two independent components to survive, its reliability is the product. Two bearings each assessed at 0.98 mission survival give \(0.98^2=0.9604\) for both. If two independent drives are genuinely redundant and either can carry the full demand, failure requires both to fail, giving \(1-(1-0.98)^2=0.9996\).

A shared lubricating supply or housing defect can defeat independence in either calculation. Similarly, a clearance that is satisfactory on the day of assembly need not remain satisfactory after thermal growth or wear. Carry the measured operating temperature range into the same dimension chain, and carry the relevant mission duration and shared causes into the reliability calculation. Both methods depend as strongly on the physical definition of success as on the arithmetic.

Applied study

Determine how load and curvature set the contact area, pressure and stresses between components.

Compare the experimental runs

How much area really carries the load?

A ball presses against a bearing race. The unloaded bodies touch at a point, which would imply infinite pressure for any finite force. Both bodies deform elastically under load, opening a contact patch whose size is part of the solution. A pin in a clevis carries a transverse load through a different combination of contact and shear. The relevant area depends on which failure mechanism is being examined.

Start with a sphere of radius \(R\) on a flat body. Assume a smooth nonadhesive interface, negligible tangential traction, isotropic linear elasticity and a small contact patch relative to curvature and body dimensions. Hertz’s local solution then treats each solid as an elastic half-space. Their normal compliances add through the effective modulus \(1/E^*=(1-\nu_1^2)/E_1+(1-\nu_2^2)/E_2\). A grooved race with appreciable curvature can require different principal radii and an elliptical patch.

From contact pressure to the normal force

In a circular contact of radius \(a\), the elastic solution gives \(p(r)=p_0\sqrt{1-r^2/a^2}\), with the greatest pressure at the centre. Equilibrium of a loaded body connects this local field to a measurable force. Integrating over rings of area \(2\pi r\,dr\) gives \(F=2\pi p_0a^2/3\), so the peak pressure is 1.5 times the patch mean.

Matching the curved gap to the elastic deflections gives \(a^2=R\delta\), with \(\delta\) the mutual approach of the remote bodies, and \(F=(4/3)E^*\sqrt{R}\,\delta^{3/2}\). Eliminating the approach yields

\[a=\left(\frac{3FR}{4E^*}\right)^{1/3},\qquad p_0=\frac{3F}{2\pi a^2},\qquad k_n=\frac{dF}{d\delta}=2E^*a.\]

The tangent stiffness \(k_n\) increases as load enlarges the patch. A single constant contact stiffness can describe small oscillations around a chosen preload. Following the surface during rolling subjects material below the track to a changing stress state. For Poisson ratio 0.30 the elastic solution puts the largest shear stress on the central axis near \(z=0.48a\), with magnitude about \(0.31p_0\).

Worked example · Steel ball on a steel seat

Let \(R=10.0\) mm, \(F=120\) N and \(E=210\) GPa, \(\nu=0.30\) for both bodies. Their combined modulus is \(210/[2(1-0.3^2)]=115.4\) GPa. The patch radius is 0.198 mm and the approach is \(a^2/R=3.93\,\mu\mathrm{m}\). The pressure averaged over the patch is 971 MPa, and \(p_0=1.46\) GPa. The maximum shear is about 452 MPa near a depth of 0.095 mm.

The 120 N force alone is a poor guide to stress at the contact. These are elastic predictions. Judging service life also requires material condition, lubrication, repeated loading and the rest of the bearing geometry. Compare how force and geometry determine nominal stress in a beam.

Transfer problem · A pin with two shear planes

A 12 kN load passes through a central 6 mm thick lug between two 4 mm thick clevis plates. Initially the pin diameter is 8 mm. Assume symmetric load sharing and negligible pin bending for this preliminary check. A cut through both shear planes gives mean pin stress \(\tau=F/[2(\pi d^2/4)]\). Nominal bearing pressure uses the projected pin area \(td\) in each plate, with the central lug carrying \(F\) and the outer plates \(F/2\) each.

For the 8 mm pin the mean shear is 119 MPa. Nominal bearing pressure is \(12000/(6\times8)=250\) MPa in the central lug, with dimensions here in millimetres, and \(6000/(4\times8)=188\) MPa in each outer plate. Suppose the exercise specifies limits of 125 MPa in mean shear and 200 MPa in nominal bearing. The central lug controls the diameter. Increasing \(d\) to 10 mm gives 76.4 MPa shear, 200 MPa in the central lug and 150 MPa outside. This meets the two stated nominal checks at the limiting diameter. Edge tear-out, net-section failure and pin bending would require additional dimensions and material data.

Exercises

1. Increase the contact preload

Increase the sphere load eightfold, maintaining geometry and elastic properties. Predict the factors of change in contact radius, peak pressure, approach and incremental stiffness. Calculate the new approach.

Show the load scalings

From \(a\propto F^{1/3}\), the radius doubles. The patch area quadruples, so \(p_0\propto F/a^2\) also doubles. The approach rises fourfold to about 15.7 µm, and the tangent stiffness doubles. A load path based on a geometrically fixed patch would overestimate the pressure increase.

2. Widen the contact or change the material?

At the original 120 N, compare doubling the sphere radius and halving the reduced modulus. Which choice reduces the peak pressure more, and which produces less movement?

Show the design comparison

Both changes double \(R/E^*\), increasing \(a\) by \(2^{1/3}=1.260\) and reducing \(p_0\) to \(2^{-2/3}=0.630\) times its former value. Since \(\delta=a^2/R\), doubling \(R\) gives \(2^{-1/3}\delta=3.12\) µm, whereas halving \(E^*\) gives \(2^{2/3}\delta=6.24\) µm. The larger sphere gives the same pressure reduction with a stiffer response at fixed load.

Experiment · Change imposed approach and modulus

The example brings a 10 mm sphere towards a finite block. It prescribes the approach and measures the resulting force. Use the first mesh labelled 7 cells per reference radius for the comparisons below. Its baseline has youngs_modulus=210e9, poisson_ratio=0.30 and a final approach of 25 µm. The computed contact radius comes from a pressure-weighted moment of the contact patch. A force integral over the quarter model is multiplied by four in the supplied script.

  1. Multiply the array approaches by 0.5, keeping material properties and mesh unchanged. The final approach is now 12.5 µm. Relative to baseline the half-space prediction gives a radius factor \(\sqrt{0.5}=0.707\) and a force factor \(0.5^{3/2}=0.354\). Reuse the same mesh to see how closely this finite-body calculation follows these ratios.
