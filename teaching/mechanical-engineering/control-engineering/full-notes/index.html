Chapter 1 · From a balance law to a measured plant

Predict a transient from conservation of energy, then use measurements to decide whether a first-order model is credible.

Choose variables that describe stored energy

A heater warms a well-stirred tank. A sensor measures only the liquid temperature, and a controller will eventually change the heating power. Before designing the controller, ask what information fixes the future of the plant when the power is given. If the tank, its contents and the thermometer can be represented by one uniform temperature, a single initial temperature suffices. A slow thermometer or a heavy tank wall may require its own initial condition. This is a physical way of deciding how many states to put in a model.

Let \(C\) in J/K be the combined heat capacity, \(H\) in W/K the thermal conductance to the room, \(T_a\) the ambient temperature and \(u\) the delivered heater power. Over an interval \(dt\) the heater adds \(u\,dt\), the room receives \(H(T-T_a)\,dt\), and storage increases by \(C\,dT\). Taking the limit of a short interval gives

\[C\dot T=u-H(T-T_a).\]

The signs can be checked without solving anything. Set \(u=0\) while \(T>T_a\). Stored energy must decrease. At equilibrium \(\dot T=0\), so a constant 20 W input and \(H=5\) W/K sustain a 4 K excess over the surroundings. This check will later reveal a faulty actuator calibration even if the transient looks smooth.

Separate gain, time scale and initial condition

Choose an operating point \((u_0,T_0)\) in equilibrium, keep the ambient temperature fixed and write \(v=u-u_0\), \(y=T-T_0\). Subtracting the equilibrium balance cancels the constant terms. Dividing by \(H\) gives \(\tau\dot y+y=Kv\), where \(K=1/H\) is the steady gain and \(\tau=C/H\) is a time. An input step of amplitude \(v_s\) moves the equilibrium to \(Kv_s\). Subtract this new equilibrium to leave a homogeneous decay equation. Integrating from zero time yields

\[y(t)=Kv_s+\big[y(0)-Kv_s\big]e^{-t/\tau}.\]

After one time constant, 36.8% of the initial distance to the new value remains. Reaching a specified fraction of the final temperature and reaching a tolerance relative to the final change are equivalent only if the output is initially zero in the chosen coordinates. Working in temperature deviations makes the convention explicit.

Recover a model from a step experiment

Start from equilibrium and increase the power by 30 W. The final rise is 6 K. At 20 s the rise is 2.36 K. The gain is \(6/30=0.20\) K/W, so \(H=5.0\) W/K. The remaining fraction is \((6-2.36)/6=0.607\). Taking logarithms gives \(\tau=-20/\ln(0.607)\simeq40\) s, hence \(C\simeq200\) J/K. At 60 s the predicted rise is \(6(1-e^{-1.5})=4.66\) K. A measurement at 60 s tests a prediction that was not used in the fit.

The same experiment can expose a missing state. Plot the logarithm of the remaining fraction against time. A single time constant gives a straight line with slope \(-1/\tau\). If the early trace has a shoulder but the late trace is straight, the sensor or a second thermal mass might be responsible. In the last few samples, the temperature difference in the logarithm may be comparable to measurement noise. Use those samples to estimate the final temperature rather than relying on their individual log slopes.

Transfer the argument to a rotating shaft

With shaft inertia \(J\), viscous drag \(b\) and driving torque \(u\), conservation of angular momentum gives \(J\dot\omega=u-b\omega\). A suspended flywheel with \(J=0.12\) kg·m² and \(b=0.030\) N·m·s/rad has a 4.0 s time constant. A 0.30 N·m step from rest eventually gives 10 rad/s. The equilibrium gain and transient have the same mathematical form as the thermal problem. Their units and the physical meaning of a disturbance differ. A load torque enters the shaft equation directly. A change in ambient temperature enters the tank balance through \(H\). This distinction matters once the loop must reject disturbances.

Chapter 2 · Transfer functions and connections between systems

Build a model of a sensor-actuator chain from individual balances and determine which responses a measurement can reveal.

Eliminate a state in the transform domain

Chapter 1 gave a differential equation and an initial condition for a single energy store. Cascading several components leads to simultaneous equations. The Laplace transform changes the time derivative into \(sY(s)-y(0)\). Applied to \(\tau\dot y+y=Kv\), this gives \((\tau s+1)Y=KV+\tau y(0)\). With zero initial deviation the ratio \(G(s)=Y/V=K/(\tau s+1)\) describes the forced response. For nonzero initial state the extra term predicts free decay. This is why an experimental transfer function should identify a particular operating point and initialisation procedure.

The pole at \(s=-1/\tau\) locates the decaying exponential. The DC gain is \(G(0)=K\). The high-frequency magnitude of this model tends to zero because the energy store cannot change its state instantaneously in response to bounded power. A transfer function can thus be assessed from dimensions, a static balance and a short-time thought experiment before any curve fitting.

Connect the sensor and plant

A sensor may have its own lag. Let the actual plant output be \(y\) and the indicated value \(z\). With plant \(G_p=1/(4s+1)\) and sensor \(G_s=1/(s+1)\), the individual relations \(Y=G_p U\) and \(Z=G_s Y\) give \(Z/U=1/[(4s+1)(s+1)]\). For a unit step divide by \(s\); partial fractions or substitution in the differential equation gives

\[z(t)=1-\frac43e^{-t/4}+\frac13e^{-t}.\]

Check \(z(0)=0\), \(z(\infty)=1\) and \(\dot z(0)=0\). The actual plant slope begins at \(1/4\) per second, but the indicated output starts with a horizontal tangent. At 4 s the plant has reached 0.632 and the indicator only 0.516. A designer who assigns the entire delay to the plant may demand an unnecessarily powerful actuator. A designer who ignores the sensor will underestimate the phase lag in feedback.

Close a loop algebraically

Let \(C(s)\) be a controller and use the indicated output in negative feedback, so \(U=C(R-G_sY)\). Substitute this in \(Y=G_pU\) and collect all terms multiplying \(Y\). The result is \(Y/R=G_pC/(1+G_pCG_s)\). The same denominator occurs in other responses, but the numerator depends on where the signal enters. A disturbance \(D\) added at the plant input gives \(Y/D=G_p/(1+G_pCG_s)\) with zero reference. Measurement noise added to the feedback signal gives \(Y/N=-G_pC/(1+G_pCG_s)\). Forcing every disturbance to enter at the reference would predict the wrong amplitudes.

Recognise an invisible mode

A rational expression may simplify by cancellation. Consider a cascade of an integrator-like unstable stage \(1/(s-1)\) and a downstream stage \((s-1)/(s+2)\). The algebraic transfer from external input to final output is \(1/(s+2)\) at zero initial conditions. The state between stages can still grow as \(e^t\). A small mismatch in either factor also leaves an unstable component in the observed output. Physically realising two factors gives internal variables that still need checking even when their product has a well-behaved step response.

For a stable example, the measured lag above can be estimated with a first-order fit from late observations alone. The resulting single pole describes the slow tail but loses the zero initial slope. Choosing the level of model detail therefore requires stating the quantity of interest: a long-time energy estimate and a rapid feedback correction need different bandwidths. The next chapter uses the true characteristic polynomial to determine stability before assessing accuracy of tracking.

Problem sheet 1 · Modelling a plant and interpreting its signals

Use balances, local linearisation and block diagrams to decide which parameters a commissioning test actually measures.

1. A step on a heated plate

A plate initially in equilibrium at 22 °C receives a further 40 W. Its final temperature is 32 °C and it reaches 28.32 °C after 50 s. (a) Estimate the incremental static gain and time constant. (b) Recover heat capacity and heat-loss conductance in a one-capacity model. (c) Describe a second experiment that could expose a temperature-dependent loss.

Recover the physical coefficients

Write \(C\dot\theta+G\theta=\Delta P\) about the initial equilibrium. The 10 K final increment gives \(K=0.25\) K/W and \(G=4\) W/K. The recorded increment is 63.2% of the final value, giving \(\tau=50\) s and \(C=\tau G=200\) J/K. Repeat a small equal step about a higher initial temperature with the same airflow. A change in incremental gain indicates a changed local loss slope; a second slow exponential could instead indicate another stored-energy state. One measurement at 50 s cannot distinguish these hypotheses.

2. A tank drained by gravity

An open tank of cross-section \(A=0.50\) m² discharges \(q_\mathrm{out}=c\sqrt H\) with \(c=0.020\) \(\mathrm{m}^{5/2}/\mathrm{s}\) and level \(H\) in metres. (a) Find the inflow at a 1 m operating level. (b) Derive the incremental transfer function and predict the rise for a sustained additional 0.002 m³/s. (c) Check the new equilibrium exactly and identify the sign of the approximation error.

Linearise the outflow at the operating level

The balance is \(A\dot H=q-c\sqrt H\). At \(H_0=1\) m, \(q_0=0.020\) m³/s. The slope of the outflow is 0.010 m²/s, so \(\delta H/\delta q=1/(0.50s+0.010)\) in the Laplace domain. Its time constant is 50 s and its final incremental prediction is 0.20 m. The exact new level is \((0.022/0.020)^2=1.21\) m. The local model underestimates the rise by 0.01 m because the outflow curve becomes flatter as the level rises.

3. A slow sensor on a fast drive

The transfer functions from torque to speed and from speed to the recorded signal are \(P=2/(1+0.5s)\) and \(H=1/(1+2s)\). (a) Determine the transfer from torque to the recording and its DC gain. (b) For a unit step derive the recording in time. (c) Would the dominant time visible in this record be a good plant-only identification?

Distinguish dynamics by tracing the signal

The recording has transfer \(PH=2/[(1+0.5s)(1+2s)]\) and DC gain 2. Partial fractions for a unit step give \(y_m=2[1-\tfrac43 e^{-t/2}+\tfrac13e^{-2t}]\). Its value and slope are zero at switch-on, whereas physical speed has initial slope 4 in the chosen units. The late transient is dominated by the sensor's 2 s time constant. An independently calibrated sensor model or a faster sensor separates its effect from the 0.5 s plant response.

Chapter 3 · Closed-loop poles, damping and performance

Translate a time-domain requirement into acceptable poles and find when additional gain destabilises a higher-order plant.

Relate polynomial coefficients to observed motion

For a normalised second-order system, the free response satisfies \(\ddot y+2\zeta\omega_n\dot y+\omega_n^2y=0\). Substitution of \(e^{st}\) gives \(s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\). The real part sets the decay rate. For \(0<\zeta<1\), the imaginary part \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\) sets the time between successive extrema. Doubling \(\omega_n\) at fixed \(\zeta\) halves all characteristic times but leaves the fraction of overshoot unchanged.

Assume a unit DC gain, a constant numerator and zero initial displacement and velocity. The step response is \(y=1-e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}[\cos(\omega_dt)+(\zeta\omega_n/\omega_d)\sin(\omega_dt)]\). Differentiate the bracket together with its envelope. The cosine terms cancel and the remaining derivative is proportional to \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_dt)\). The first peak occurs at \(t_p=\pi/\omega_d\). Substituting that time gives the fractional overshoot

\[M_p=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right).\]

Design for a specified peak

Suppose a positioning axis may overshoot a 10 mm command by at most 1 mm. Solving the previous expression for the limiting damping ratio gives \(\zeta=-\ln M_p/\sqrt{\pi^2+(\ln M_p)^2}=0.591\). At \(\omega_n=5\) rad/s the corresponding poles are approximately \(-2.96\pm4.03i\) per second and the first peak occurs at 0.779 s. A nominal oscillatory second-order system with this damping just meets the 1 mm bound. A zero near the dominant poles, a saturated drive or another slow pole changes the transient, so the result should be checked against the full model after the poles are chosen.

A settling specification differs from a peak specification. The absolute normalised error stays below the envelope \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). To guarantee a 2% settling band using this bound, solve \(t\ge-\ln[0.02\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}]/(\zeta\omega_n)\). This conservative time for the example is about 1.40 s. The commonly used \(4/(\zeta\omega_n)\) is a convenient estimate. The envelope bound spells out both the tolerance and its assumptions.

Follow a gain change on a third-order plant

A plant with two lags and an integrator has open-loop transfer \(K/[s(s+1)(s+2)]\) in units normalised to a common time base. Close unity negative feedback. The characteristic polynomial is \(s^3+3s^2+2s+K\). Its coefficients are all positive for \(K>0\), which rules out positive real roots but allows an unstable complex pair. Form the first column of the Routh array: \(1,\ 3,\ (6-K)/3,\ K\). All entries must have the same sign for strict stability. Thus \(0<K<6\).

At \(K=6\) the polynomial factors as \((s+3)(s^2+2)\). The step excites an undamped pair at angular frequency \(\sqrt2\), alongside a decaying component. At \(K=7\) the first-column signs change twice, so two roots have crossed into the right half-plane. The input-output gain alone did not reveal this upper bound. Additional lags allow feedback to arrive out of phase with motion. Chapter 4 reaches the same type of conclusion by examining sine-wave responses, which can often be measured even when coefficients of a polynomial are poorly known.

Chapter 4 · Frequency response, disturbance rejection and robustness

A loop that tracks slowly changing commands may amplify a disturbance at another frequency. Work from the sinusoidal response to a defensible choice of bandwidth.

Read a complex gain as an experiment

Drive a stable linear plant with a small sinusoid about its operating point. Once the transient dies away, the output is a sinusoid at the same angular frequency. Inserting \(u=Ue^{i\omega t}\) and \(y=Ye^{i\omega t}\) in the differential equation gives \(Y=P(i\omega)U\). The modulus gives the amplitude ratio and the argument gives the time shift as a fraction of a cycle. The units on a Bode plot matter: for a dimensional plant gain its magnitude in decibels presupposes a reference gain. The product around a feedback loop is dimensionless.

For \(P=K/(1+s\tau)\), the modulus falls to \(K/\sqrt{2}\) at \(\omega=1/\tau\), where its phase is \(-45^\circ\). Above this break, doubling frequency roughly halves the output amplitude. Adding a transport delay multiplies the frequency response by \(e^{-i\omega T}\); the magnitude is unchanged while phase falls by \(\omega T\) radians. A magnitude measurement alone cannot detect this mechanism.

Derive the sensitivity functions

Let \(u=C(r-y-n)\) for command \(r\) and sensor noise \(n\), with disturbance \(d\) added directly to the physical plant output \(y=Pu+d\). Substitution gives

\[S=(1+PC)^{-1},\quad T=PC(1+PC)^{-1},\qquad y=Tr+Sd-Tn .\]

The identity \(S+T=1\) holds for complex numbers at each frequency. A slow output disturbance is strongly rejected when the loop gain is large and far from \(-1\). Sensor noise at the same frequency is then nearly reproduced by the physical output with opposite sign. At high frequencies a deliberately small loop gain protects the output against measurement noise. If the disturbance enters at the actuator instead, its output transfer function is \(PS\). Mark its point of entry before reading any plot.

How much delay can a loop tolerate?

For a unity feedback loop take \(L(s)=4/[s(1+s)]\), with time in seconds. A unity-gain crossover satisfies \(\omega^2(1+\omega^2)=16\). The positive root gives \(\omega_c=\sqrt{(\sqrt{65}-1)/2}=1.88\) rad/s. At that frequency the phase is \(-90^\circ-\tan^{-1}1.88=-152.0^\circ\). A further \(28.0^\circ\) of lag would bring the Nyquist curve through \(-1\), where the closed-loop denominator vanishes. For pure delay alone the first corresponding delay is about \(0.489/1.88=0.260\) s. A 0.10 s delay consumes about \(10.8^\circ\) at crossover.

This estimate uses this plant's unique gain crossover and its initially stable closed loop. Multiple crossovers, unstable plant poles and poorly measured resonances require inspection of the entire Nyquist curve. Even before a pole crosses into instability, the distance to \(-1\) controls amplification: \(|S|=1/|1+L|\). For example \(L=-0.9+0.1i\) gives \(|S|=7.07\). A loop with low steady error may therefore magnify a narrow band of disturbance by a factor of seven.

Translate specifications into a measurement

Suppose a speed drive must reject load variations below 0.5 rad/s while its encoder contains noise above 20 rad/s. First locate the disturbance and noise on the block diagram. Specify maximum allowed output amplitude per unit amplitude at each point. Shape the gain, then inspect the two resulting transfer functions across the entire frequency range, including the transition between bands. Raising proportional gain alone shifts the crossover into regions of additional lag. A measured resonance there can set the achievable speed even when the motor model at low frequency is excellent.

Problem sheet 2 · Pole placement, gain and phase

Design a second-order transient, examine a hidden stability limit, and test a delay budget against a frequency response.

1. Select damping from a peak

A unity-static-gain second-order system has no zeros. Specify 10% overshoot and first peak at 1 s after a step. (a) Derive damping ratio and damped frequency. (b) Find the desired characteristic polynomial. (c) State which prediction fails if a numerator zero is added and the poles are held fixed.

Start with the logarithm of the overshoot

The positive logarithmic decrement to the first peak is \(-\ln(0.10)=2.303=\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\), giving \(\zeta=0.591\). The damped frequency is \(\omega_d=\pi\) rad/s, and the exponential decay rate is \(2.303\) s⁻¹. The poles are \(-2.303\pm3.142i\), so the monic polynomial is \(s^2+4.605s+15.17\). A zero changes the residues and the step shape. The poles still give the natural frequencies and decay rates, but first-peak time and overshoot need recalculation.

2. Three time constants and one gain

For \(L=g/[s(s+1)(s+3)]\) under unity negative feedback, (a) derive the closed-loop characteristic polynomial, (b) find the positive gain range for stability and the crossing frequency, and (c) compare the pole result with the open-loop frequency response at the crossing.

Locate the imaginary-axis pair

The polynomial is \(s^3+4s^2+3s+g\). The first column of the Routh array is \(1,4,(12-g)/4,g\); all signs agree for \(0\lt g\lt12\). At \(g=12\) factor the polynomial as \((s+4)(s^2+3)\). The crossing frequency is \(\sqrt3\) rad/s, with the third pole still at \(-4\). Substituting \(s=i\sqrt3\) in the open-loop denominator gives \(-12\), so \(L=-1\) at the same gain and frequency.

3. Delay on a positioning stage

With \(L=2/[s(1+s/2)]\), (a) find the gain crossover, (b) estimate the delay that would use all phase margin, and (c) evaluate the remaining margin after including 0.10 s delay. Count angles consistently.

Convert delay to a phase at crossover

Writing \(x=\omega_c^2\) gives \(x(1+x/4)=4\), so \(\omega_c=\sqrt{-2+2\sqrt5}=1.572\) rad/s. The phase margin is \(90^\circ-\arctan(1.572/2)=51.8^\circ\), or 0.905 rad. A pure delay leaves the crossover magnitude fixed, so the first crossing occurs at about \(0.905/1.572=0.576\) s. A 0.10 s delay costs 0.157 rad or 9.0°, leaving approximately 42.8°. This calculation assumes the given loop is otherwise accurately modelled.

Chapter 5 · States, reachability and estimation

Two mechanisms can produce the same output at one instant and different outputs an instant later. Determine what the actuator can change and what a sensor history can reveal.

From balances to independent states

A cart of mass \(m\) moves against a spring \(k\) and damper \(b\). Choose \(x=(q,v)^\mathsf{T}\), displacement and velocity. Newton's law gives \(\dot q=v,\ m\dot v=-kq-bv+u\). A state contains the initial information needed to propagate the motion with specified input. Measuring displacement at one instant supplies only half of that information. The state choice is not unique: replacing velocity by momentum changes the matrix entries, yet the predicted displacement history is the same.

\[\dot x=Ax+Bu,\quad y=Cx,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-b/m\end{pmatrix},\ B=\binom{0}{1/m},\ C=(1\;\;0).\]

With zero input the transition \(e^{At}\) propagates initial conditions. Integrating the effect of forces applied at each earlier time gives \(x(t)=e^{At}x(0)+\int_0^t e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds\). This decomposition explains why both the autonomous motion and the input direction enter control design.

Test an actuator and a sensor

Over a short time, the vectors \(e^{At}B=B+tAB+\cdots\) show the directions that a force can excite. In two dimensions \(B,AB\) suffice by the characteristic equation of \(A\). For the cart their columns have determinant \(-1/m^2\); a finite mass allows the actuator to change both position and velocity over time. This rank test assumes arbitrary inputs and sufficient time. Getting to a distant position in a very short time can still exceed an actuator limit.

With a known input, output and its first derivative contain \(Cx\) and \(CAx\). Here they are position and velocity, so the observability matrix has full rank. As a counterexample, consider two uncoupled tanks with equal decay rates, driven equally, and one sensor measuring the sum of levels. The difference evolves autonomously and never enters the sum. Recording a longer noiseless trace with that sensor cannot recover the initial difference. Moving a sensor or adding a distinct excitation has to break the symmetry responsible for the missing information.

Place poles and restore the reference scale

Take \(m=1,\ b=1,\ k=2\) in compatible SI units. Apply \(u=-f_q q-f_v v+n r\). The homogeneous characteristic polynomial is \(s^2+(1+f_v)s+(2+f_q)\). Desired poles at \(-2,-3\) give \(f_v=4,\ f_q=4\) by coefficient matching. At constant reference the velocity vanishes and \((2+f_q)q=nr\), so \(n=6\) gives \(q=r\) in the nominal model. A constant unmodelled force shifts this equilibrium. State feedback with a static reference multiplier alone supplies no mechanism to learn that force.

Reconstruct velocity without differentiating noisy measurements

An observer runs the model and uses the disagreement with the measured output to correct its state. Write \(\dot{\hat x}=A\hat x+Bu+H(y-C\hat x)\). In the ideal deterministic model the estimation error obeys \(\dot e=(A-HC)e\). Here its polynomial is \(s^2+(1+h_1)s+(h_1+2+h_2)\). Desired observer poles \(-4,-5\) give \(h_1=8,\ h_2=10\). Thus an initial state error decays even though the sensor supplies only displacement.

With the control law evaluated at \(\hat x\), the combined state and estimation-error dynamics are block triangular. The two sets of assigned poles therefore survive in the ideal linear model. Faster observer poles also give sensor noise larger correction gains. Compare simulated or measured velocity estimates during a quiet hold and during a sharp manoeuvre before accepting the faster design. A missing mode, a noisy estimate and the inability to measure an initial state exactly have distinct engineering implications.

For additional examples of state estimation and output feedback, see Åström and Murray’s Feedback Systems.

Chapter 6 · Sampling, saturation and commissioning

Carry a continuous-time design onto a sampled actuator, predict where its operating assumptions fail, and distinguish an observed final value from an equilibrium.

Derive the held-input model

A processor reads a sensor every \(h\) seconds and holds its most recent command until the next sample. Solve \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku_k\) exactly from \(kh\) to \((k+1)h\) with this constant input. The result is \(y_{k+1}=a y_k+K(1-a)u_k\), where \(a=e^{-h/\tau}\). This recursion is exact at the sampling instants for the assumed hold. Taking a large Euler step through the continuous closed-loop equation describes a different experiment. A real controller has only the sampled information available when it computes \(u_k\).

With proportional control \(u_k=K_p(r_k-y_k)\) the discrete pole is \(z=a-KK_p(1-a)\). Stability requires \(|z|\lt1\). A negative pole of modulus less than one alternates sign each update while decaying. For \(K=1,\ \tau=1\) s and \(K_p=3\), \(h=0.2\) s gives \(z=0.275\); \(h=0.8\) s gives \(z=-1.203\). The same nominal continuous design gives radically different sampled records. For positive gain the upper gain limit is \((1+a)/[K(1-a)]\). Additional computation and transport delays lower the available margin further.

Locate the sampling limit in a computed trace

Keep the plant integration at 40 RK4 substeps per hold. Predict which sample intervals give a monotone response, a decaying oscillation or a growing one. Compare the stored OneSimulation traces on either side of 0.69 s. Estimate the discrete pole from two successive output increments; their ratio removes the unknown equilibrium.

Why a saturated integrator remembers the past

Let \(v_k=K_p e_k+I_k\) be the requested command, and \(u_k\) its value clipped to the actuator range. An unconstrained integrator adds \(hK_i e_k\) to \(I_k\) at every sample. While an unreachable command holds a heater at its upper limit, positive error keeps accumulating; when the command becomes reachable again the stored integral may keep the heater fully on. This delayed recovery is called windup. Logging only actual heater power hides the growth of the internal demand. Log both signals and the integral state.

A back-calculation update feeds the clipping discrepancy back to the integrator:

\[I_{k+1}=I_k+h[K_i e_k+(u_k-v_k)/T_t].\]

When the request exceeds the upper stop, the second term is negative. At fixed error and fixed clipping level, this update attracts the requested command toward a value consistent with saturation, provided its discrete tracking dynamics are stable. For example, with \(h=0.1\) s, \(K_i=1\), \(e=2\), \(u=5\), \(v=8\), and \(T_t=0.5\) s, the integral increment is \(-0.4\), compared with \(+0.2\) without correction. Tuning \(T_t\) must account for the sample interval and actuator dynamics. Anti-windup cannot make an infeasible steady torque available.

Use a numerical result to distinguish two errors

Return to the OneSimulation tracking experiment. It models a continuous, unsaturated proportional loop and reports the error at a finite time. For a unit step from rest with loop gain \(g=KK_p\), the error after \(N\) closed-loop time constants is \(e_N=(1+g e^{-N})/(1+g)\). With \(g=8\) and \(N=6\), predict \(0.11331\). Its difference from the equilibrium \(1/9\) is transient motion. Halving the plant speed changes the physical time needed to reach the same dimensionless sample, while this sampled error stays fixed.

Test the interpretation by recording at three and six time constants and taking the ratio of the two deviations from equilibrium. Predict \(e^{-3}\) for the later deviation divided by the earlier. A persistent discrepancy warrants checking the recorded timestamps, the implementation of the step and whether a limit was active. To examine sampling or clipping, explicitly add these mechanisms to the model before comparing with the equations above. The logged linear-loop runs answer the finite-record question directly.

Problem sheet 3 · What a controller can learn and deliver

Use rank tests, an exact sampled model and a finite-time numerical observation to diagnose three different limits.

1. A pair of decaying modes

Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-2),\ B=(1,1)^\mathsf{T}\). (a) Test reachability. (b) Test observability for \(C=(1,1)\) and then for \(C=(1,0)\). (c) For zero input, recover the initial state from the first sensor when \(y(0)=3,\dot y(0)=-5\).

Use both direction and time history

Chapter 7 · Following poles as a controller gain changes

Use the geometry of the characteristic equation to discover which transient specifications one adjustable gain can meet.

A family of closed-loop systems

The earlier pole calculations chose a gain and found the poles. Reverse that question: if an engineer turns one gain dial continuously, which parts of the complex plane can the poles ever visit? For unity negative feedback write the open-loop transfer as \(K N(s)/D(s)\), with \(N,D\) coprime and \(K\ge0\). The closed-loop characteristic equation is \(D(s)+K N(s)=0\). At a trial complex point away from an open-loop zero, the required gain is \(-D(s)/N(s)\). The point is attainable only if this number is real and nonnegative. Its magnitude gives the gain; its phase supplies a separate feasibility test.

At \(K=0\) branches begin at open-loop poles. At very large gain, finite roots must approach zeros of \(N\). If the denominator has greater degree, the remaining roots go to infinity. Since a real-coefficient polynomial has conjugate roots, the locus is symmetric about the real axis. These observations put strong constraints on a sketch even before computing roots.

Derive a breakaway and read the transient

Take a positioning plant \(P=1/[s(s+4)]\) with proportional gain \(K\). The closed-loop poles are \(s=-2\pm\sqrt{4-K}\). They start at 0 and −4, approach each other along the real axis and meet at −2 when \(K=4\). Equivalently the real-axis gain \(K(s)=-s(s+4)\) has a stationary point at −2. Above the breakaway the complex pair moves vertically, so raising gain increases oscillation frequency while leaving the exponential envelope \(e^{-2t}\) unchanged.

The constant term gives \(\omega_n=\sqrt K\) and the linear coefficient gives \(\zeta=2/\sqrt K\). A specification of decay rate 3 s⁻¹ and damped frequency 4 rad/s would require poles \(-3\pm4i\). Since every complex pole here has real part −2, no choice of proportional gain meets both requirements. Failure to find such a gain numerically is a statement about controller structure.

Add a second degree of freedom

Feed back measured velocity as well as position: \(u=K_p(r-y)-K_v\dot y\). The closed-loop denominator becomes \(s^2+(4+K_v)s+K_p\); the reference numerator is just \(K_p\). For desired poles \(-3\pm4i\) the polynomial is \(s^2+6s+25\), hence \(K_v=2,\ K_p=25\). The derivative acts only on measured output, so a discontinuous reference does not directly request an impulse. Differentiating a noisy measurement can nevertheless demand excessive actuator effort. A separate velocity sensor or a filtered estimate changes the high-frequency dynamics and must be included in the design check.

Include the numerator when interpreting a pole plot

Moving a controller zero also reshapes the locus because it changes the angle of \(N/D\). A zero in the reference-to-output map changes step-response residues even if the poles coincide with a previous design. In particular a positive real zero imposes a severe tracking constraint. For the stable unit-DC transfer \(G=(1-s/z)/(1+s\tau)^2\) with \(z>0\), the unit-step initial slope is \(\lim_{s\to\infty}sG(s)=-1/(z\tau^2)\). Yet the final output is positive. The initial motion in the wrong direction follows directly from the zero. Increasing gain to make the initial transient faster cannot remove that geometry.

Chapter 8 · Uncertainty envelopes and robust stability

Turn a bounded discrepancy between plant and model into a frequency-by-frequency test of the feedback design.

What do several plant tests tell us?

The gain of a drive changes as it warms up, and a flexible mounting introduces poorly measured phase lag at high frequency. Let \(P_0\) be a nominal model. One useful description is multiplicative uncertainty, \(P=P_0(1+W\Delta)\), with stable dimensionless \(\Delta\) of gain at most one and a specified frequency weight \(W\). The magnitude of \(W\) bounds relative error. Estimate it from \(|P/P_0-1|\) across an ensemble of measurements, retaining phase in the complex ratio. A 20% amplitude difference and a 20° phase difference generally produce a larger relative error than either alone.

Where \(P_0\) is close to zero this relative description can be poorly conditioned. An additive uncertainty \(P=P_0+W_a\Delta\) can describe an absolute error floor there. A bound fitted only at sampled frequencies requires a reason to hold between samples, especially near flexible resonances. A robustness calculation is conditional on this modelling work.

Factor the perturbed characteristic equation

For the nominal controller \(C\), set \(L_0=P_0C\) and \(T_0=L_0/(1+L_0)\). Under multiplicative perturbation,

\[1+PC=(1+L_0)(1+W T_0\Delta).\]

Assume internal stability of the nominal interconnection and stable, proper uncertainty factors with no hidden unstable cancellation. If \(\sup_\omega|W(i\omega)T_0(i\omega)|<1\), the perturbation loop has gain less than one and cannot destabilise the feedback. One way to see the mechanism is the convergent inverse series \(1-G+G^2-\cdots\) for \(G=WT_0\Delta\) in the stable-system norm. The pointwise bound protects against all allowed dynamic phase choices. Failure of a sufficient test alone does not prove the particular measured plant unstable.

Work a frequency-domain budget

At one frequency a nominal loop has \(L_0=-0.8+0.4i\). Then \(|1+L_0|=\sqrt{0.2}\), \(|L_0|=\sqrt{0.8}\), and \(|T_0|=2\). Relative uncertainty below 50% at that frequency keeps \(|W T_0|<1\) there. A measured 60% bound crosses this threshold. Geometrically the perturbed loop samples a disk centred at \(L_0\) of radius \(|L_0 W|\). At 50% its rim touches \(-1\). Other frequencies still require examination, and a physically narrower family such as a single real gain may occupy far less than the full disk.

Add a performance requirement

A stable loop can still amplify an output disturbance unacceptably. For the uncertain plant the sensitivity is \(S=S_0/(1+WT_0\Delta)\). The triangle inequality gives \(|S|\le |S_0|/(1-|WT_0|)\) where the denominator is positive. If \(|S_0|=0.10\) and \(|WT_0|\le0.25\), disturbances can be amplified by at most 0.133 in this bound. Very near the robustness limit that bound becomes large. Shaping the nominal loop for a small uncertainty product near troublesome resonances can therefore protect both stability and useful performance. Record the sensor configuration, loading and frequency band over which the underlying plant measurements were made.

Chapter 9 · Optimal state feedback and the Riccati equation

Choose how heavily to penalise tracking error and actuator effort, then derive the feedback gain that minimises that stated cost.

Make the design objective explicit

Pole placement prescribes decay rates but says nothing about how much actuator energy a manoeuvre consumes. For a continuous linear system \(\dot x=Ax+Bu\) consider the regulator objective \(J=\int_0^\infty(x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru)\,dt\). Assume \(Q\) positive semidefinite and \(R\) positive definite. A large displacement penalty or a small control penalty generally encourages stronger corrective action. The ratio matters: multiplying both matrices by the same positive scalar leaves the best control unchanged. Scale states and inputs before comparing entries; a displacement in millimetres and a velocity in metres per second cannot share an arbitrary numerical weight without a unit convention.

Derive the continuous algebraic Riccati equation

For an infinite-horizon stationary problem propose a quadratic remaining cost \(V(x)=x^\mathsf{T}Px\). During a short interval \(dt\), optimality requires the minimum of instantaneous cost plus the change in remaining cost to vanish. In the limit this gives \(0=\min_u[x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru+2x^\mathsf{T}P(Ax+Bu)]\). Completing the square in \(u\) yields

\[u^*=-R^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}Px,\qquad A^\mathsf{T}P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P+Q=0.\]

The desired solution is the stabilising symmetric solution. Under standard stabilisability and detectability conditions it exists and yields finite nonnegative cost. Other algebraic roots need not make sense as stored future cost. Once a candidate is found, check the eigenvalues of \(A-BR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P\) and the residual of the matrix equation.

Solve the scalar unstable case

Let \(\dot x=x+u\) with \(Q=R=1\). The scalar equation is \(2P-P^2+1=0\), giving \(P=1\pm\sqrt2\). Only \(1+\sqrt2\) is positive and makes the closed-loop pole \(1-P=-\sqrt2\). From initial state \(x_0=2\), the theoretical minimum cost is \(4(1+\sqrt2)\simeq9.657\). Verify independently: insert \(x=2e^{-\sqrt2t}, u=-Px\) into the cost integral to obtain \(4(1+P^2)/(2\sqrt2)\), the same value. By contrast a stabilising gain of 2 gives state pole −1 and cost 10. Stabilising the state is necessary but still leaves a meaningful optimisation problem.

Distinguish control penalties from hard actuator limits

The optimal initial input for the scalar example has magnitude \(2P=4.83\). If the actuator is limited to 3, clipping the command invalidates the quadratic unconstrained solution during the clipped interval. Increasing \(R\) makes large inputs more expensive but generally supplies no absolute guarantee for arbitrary initial conditions. A concrete certificate is possible inside a stated region. In one dimension \(|u|\le u_{\max}\) is guaranteed by \(|x|\le u_{\max}/P\) under this gain, and the resulting stable exponential stays in that interval. Later we will impose hard constraints directly in a finite-horizon design.

The MIT notes on linear quadratic regulators extend this derivation to time-varying and finite-horizon problems.

Chapter 10 · Estimating hidden states from noisy measurements

Propagate both the state estimate and its uncertainty, and derive how much of an unexpected sensor reading to trust.

A prediction and an observation carry different information

Consider a sampled model \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k+w_k\), \(y_k=Cx_k+v_k\). Model disturbances \(w_k\) describe unmeasured forcing or model error, while \(v_k\) describes measurement error. Suppose they are independent, zero mean and white, with known covariances \(W,V\), and independent of the initial estimation error. Start each update with a predicted mean \(\hat x^-\) and covariance \(P^-\). The innovation \(y-C\hat x^-\) is the part of the reading that the model did not predict. Its covariance is \(S=CP^-C^\mathsf{T}+V\). Units are those of the sensor squared; a large innovation in volts is meaningful only relative to this scale.

Minimise posterior variance

Update the estimate by \(\hat x^+=\hat x^-+K(y-C\hat x^-)\). In the scalar direct-measurement case the squared error has expected value \(P^+=(1-K)^2P^-+K^2V\). Differentiating with respect to \(K\) yields \(K=P^-/(P^-+V)\). A precise sensor raises the gain; a precise prior prediction lowers it. The same covariance minimisation gives the matrix update

\[K=P^-C^\mathsf{T}S^{-1},\quad P^+=(I-KC)P^-(I-KC)^\mathsf{T}+KVK^\mathsf{T}.\]

The displayed covariance formula retains positive-semidefinite structure in computation better than a raw subtraction of nearly equal matrices. For the next prediction use \(\hat x^-_{k+1}=A\hat x^+_k+Bu_k\) and \(P^-_{k+1}=AP^+_kA^\mathsf{T}+W\). The control input affects the mean; assumed known exactly, it adds no direct covariance term. With Gaussian assumptions the recursion gives the conditional mean and covariance, and with just second moments it gives the optimal linear estimate under the stated model.

Recover velocity from a position reading

Suppose the prior covariance for position and velocity is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1.5\\1.5&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) in compatible metre and second units. A position sensor has variance 1. Then \(S=5\), \(K=(0.8,0.3)^\mathsf{T}\). If measured position exceeds prediction by 2 m, the updated position increases by 1.6 m and velocity by 0.6 m/s. The sensor did not measure velocity directly: the nonzero cross-covariance makes a position surprise informative about velocity. The posterior covariance is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.8&0.3\\0.3&0.55\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A robust implementation would obtain the gain by solving with \(S\) instead of constructing a matrix inverse.

Test innovations during commissioning

Under a correct linear Gaussian model the standardised innovations should have mean zero, appropriate variance and little serial correlation. A persistent offset points toward a biased sensor or missing force. A succession of innovations of the same sign during accelerations suggests unmodelled dynamics or too little process uncertainty. Simply enlarging \(V\) to suppress the innovations in the correction makes the filter trust the model more; it does not remove a systematic error. Compare held-out records that include manoeuvres and quiet intervals. When observer and regulator are combined, estimation and control share the quality of the underlying dynamic model.

Chapter 11 · Interacting actuators, pairing and decoupling

Predict how closing one loop changes the apparent gain of another, and identify a physically expensive actuator combination.

A second loop changes the experiment

Imagine two thermal zones with separate heaters and two temperature sensors. At steady state let \(y=G u\), with \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) K per unit power. Increasing heater 1 with heater 2 fixed raises both temperatures. Increasing heater 1 while actively holding temperature 2 fixed is a different experiment: heater 2 must back off. The effective gain in the first channel changes. This difference matters when a controller is tuned with its neighbour disconnected and then commissioned with both loops active.

For a general nonsingular two-by-two matrix hold \(y_2=0\). Solving \(g_{21}u_1+g_{22}u_2=0\) gives \(y_1/u_1=g_{11}-g_{12}g_{21}/g_{22}\). The ratio of the fixed-input gain to this fixed-output gain is \(g_{11}g_{22}/\det G\). More generally the relative gain array has entries \(\Lambda_{ij}=g_{ij}(G^{-1})_{ji}\). Its rows and columns each sum to one. Large magnitudes suggest cancellation. A negative relative gain indicates that the paired steady response changes sign when the other output is perfectly held, an important warning for independent-loop tuning.

Compare candidate pairings

For the two-zone matrix above the diagonal relative gains are \(4/3\) and the off-diagonal gains are \(-1/3\). The direct pairing has fixed-input gain 2 and fixed-other-output gain 1.5. The cross-pairing changes sign. These steady results do not include transport delays or transient coupling. If the direct heater path is very slow but the cross path changes quickly, a frequency-dependent model is needed around the intended control bandwidth.

Diagonalise a symmetric plant by physical combinations

Commanding both heaters together excites the common direction \((1,1)\) with gain 3; commanding them oppositely excites \((1,-1)\) with gain 1. For the desired temperature increment \((1,-1)\), exactly equal and opposite input increments \((1,-1)\) are required. Negative input here is a reduction from a positive bias; a heater already at zero cannot supply it. Inspecting a transformed system without retaining the original actuator limits can make an impossible target seem easy.

Now bring the gains closer together: \(G=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.98\\0.98&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). The common gain is 1.98, while the differential gain is just 0.02. A unit differential target requires differential input of magnitude 50 in each actuator. Static inversion \(u=G^{-1}v\) algebraically decouples the two commands but amplifies measurement, calibration and modelling errors along that weak direction. If both temperature sensors move together during every available test, the weak direction is also particularly hard to identify.

Choose a practical validation test

First perturb each actuator separately about the operating bias and measure both outputs. Repeat at different loads and frequencies. Next excite the predicted weakest input combination within safe operating limits and check whether the differential output emerges above the noise. A design can prioritise the common temperature and relax allowable imbalance when the plant is inherently hard to split. A constrained controller can express this trade directly by penalising both output errors while respecting individual actuator bounds.

Chapter 12 · Finite-horizon control with real actuator constraints

Build a small predictive controller from a sampled model and determine when the requested target is physically reachable.

Predict a sequence before applying the first action

For \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\) with measured or estimated \(x_k\), successive substitutions give \(x_{k+2}=A^2x_k+ABu_k+Bu_{k+1}\). The initial state sets the free response; the planned input sequence contributes linearly. With quadratic tracking and effort penalties, linear dynamics and linear bounds on states or inputs, planning becomes a convex quadratic programme. At each update apply only the first planned command, acquire a new measurement and solve again. Receding the horizon provides feedback when disturbances make actual motion depart from the earlier prediction.

Solve a two-step constrained problem

Take the dimensionless integrator \(x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k\), \(x_k=2\), and target zero. Minimise \(x_{k+1}^2+x_{k+2}^2+u_k^2+u_{k+1}^2\). With \(u=u_k,v=u_{k+1}\), the objective is \((2+u)^2+(2+u+v)^2+u^2+v^2\). Its stationary equations are \(3u+v=-4,\ u+2v=-2\). The unconstrained plan \(u=-1.2,\ v=-0.4\) reaches states 0.8 and 0.4. If both input magnitudes are limited to one, the first command is infeasible. Fixing \(u=-1\) and reoptimising the second gives \(v=-0.5\), reaching 1 and 0.5. The derivative with respect to \(u\) points toward smaller \(u\) at this boundary, confirming the active limit.

After applying −1, suppose the new state is exactly 1. The next two-step unconstrained plan scales the original one by one half, giving first action −0.6. The earlier second action was −0.5. This change is consistent even without disturbances, since the new optimisation includes a further future step. A terminal cost approximating the remaining cost of control can reduce this artificial dependence on the planning horizon.

Feasible commands and feasible trajectories differ

A command bound always has feasible choices if zero lies within it. Adding a hard state constraint, such as a position stop, may make all predicted trajectories infeasible from the current state. Before asking for a time-optimal move, compute elementary reachability bounds. With \(|u|\le1\), a scalar integrator can move at most \(N\) units in \(N\) samples. A terminal constraint demanding zero from state 3 in two samples is impossible. Allowing slack in a comfort-related temperature band may be defensible; a physical actuator limit must still be enforced. Report which constraints were active and what performance was sacrificed.

Close the engineering loop

For a practical comparison, first establish a verified baseline with the OneSimulation proportional-loop record. Reconstruct its pole and finite-record bias from the model parameters. This record witnesses how an unconstrained linear loop behaves; it does not itself exercise a predictive optimiser. Now design a separate held-input or constrained test whose command reaches a known bound, record measured timestamps and actuator signals, and compare with a by-hand prediction of the first active limit. The useful performance claims differ: accurate steady tracking, stable sampled dynamics, adequate estimator uncertainty and feasibility under limits each require an observation capable of testing them.

Applied study

Design feedback for a tracking requirement and examine how loop gain changes speed, offset and stability.

Compare the experimental runs

Turn a machine balance into a plant model

A speed controller drives a motor against viscous drag. The shaft has inertia \(J\), drag coefficient \(b\), angular speed \(\omega\) and commanded torque \(u\). With a constant opposing load torque \(d\), the balance is \(J\dot\omega=u-b\omega-d\). Without feedback a step in load changes the steady speed by \(-d/b\). An engineer who can measure speed can command more torque as soon as the speed falls.

Divide by \(b\). With output \(y=\omega\), time constant \(\tau=J/b\) and static gain \(K=1/b\), the unloaded plant becomes \(\tau\dot y+y=Ku\). A heating element with finite heat capacity and heat loss has the same first-order form under constant material properties. Transfer functions or step-response measurements can therefore guide controller design before the full machine is available.

Choose the loop gain from a tracking requirement

A proportional controller applies \(u=K_p(r-y)\) for a reference \(r\). Substitution gives \(\tau\dot y+(1+KK_p)y=KK_pr\). If \(1+KK_p>0\), the closed loop settles with time constant \(\tau_{\rm cl}=\tau/(1+KK_p)\). Following a step from rest,

\[y(t)=\frac{KK_p}{1+KK_p}r\,(1-e^{-t/\tau_{\rm cl}}),\qquad e_\infty=\frac{r}{1+KK_p}.\]

The steady error supplies the command that balances the drag. For a normalised model with \(K=2\), \(\tau=20\) s, \(r=1\) and \(K_p=4\), the loop gain is 8. The output tends to \(8/9=0.8889\) with time constant 2.222 s. Keeping the steady error within 5% of the setpoint requires \(1/(1+2K_p)\le0.05\), hence \(K_p\ge9.5\). At the instant of a unit step this higher gain asks for 9.5 units of actuator effort. A drive limited to 5 units initially saturates, so the linear prediction for the rise time must be revised.

Reject a load disturbance with integral action

For the motor with a constant load, proportional control settles at \(y=(K_pr-d)/(b+K_p)\). Adding \(K_i\int (r-y)\,dt\) to the command lets the controller supply constant torque even at zero error. If the loop settles and the actuator has sufficient reserve, a bounded steady integrator state requires \(r-y=0\).

The extra state changes stability and damping. For the normalised plant and a PI controller, the closed-loop characteristic polynomial is \(\tau s^2+(1+KK_p)s+KK_i\). At \(K=2\), \(\tau=20\) s, \(K_p=4\) and \(K_i=1\) per second, its roots are \(-0.225\pm0.222i\) per second. Both decay, but a disturbance can now excite an oscillation. A gain that removes steady error can change the transient response substantially.

Problem class

A motor picks up a load

Take \(b=0.50\) N·m·s/rad, \(K_p=2.0\) N·m·s/rad and reference speed 10 rad/s. A 1 N·m load is added after the motor has settled. Find the two steady speeds and the torque an integral controller would need to restore 10 rad/s.

Balance torque before and after the load step

Before the load, \(y=20/2.5=8.0\) rad/s. After it, \(y=(20-1)/2.5=7.6\) rad/s. At the desired speed the drag is 5 N·m, so the restored steady state needs a total of 6 N·m from the motor. At zero error the integral term supplies that torque.

Design for critical damping

Keep the normalised plant and \(K_p=4\). Find the integral gain for a repeated pole. Predict the effect of doubling this integral gain on the pole locations.

Find the poles from the characteristic polynomial

Critical damping requires \((1+KK_p)^2-4\tau KK_i=0\), giving \(K_i=81/160=0.50625\) per second. The pole is \(s=-9/40=-0.225\) per second. Doubling \(K_i\) leaves the real part unchanged and gives an imaginary part of magnitude 0.225 per second. The poles now form a complex pair.
