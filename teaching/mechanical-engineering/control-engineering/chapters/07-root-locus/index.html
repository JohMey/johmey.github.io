A family of closed-loop systems

The earlier pole calculations chose a gain and found the poles. Reverse that question: if an engineer turns one gain dial continuously, which parts of the complex plane can the poles ever visit? For unity negative feedback write the open-loop transfer as \(K N(s)/D(s)\), with \(N,D\) coprime and \(K\ge0\). The closed-loop characteristic equation is \(D(s)+K N(s)=0\). At a trial complex point away from an open-loop zero, the required gain is \(-D(s)/N(s)\). The point is attainable only if this number is real and nonnegative. Its magnitude gives the gain; its phase supplies a separate feasibility test.

At \(K=0\) branches begin at open-loop poles. At very large gain, finite roots must approach zeros of \(N\). If the denominator has greater degree, the remaining roots go to infinity. Since a real-coefficient polynomial has conjugate roots, the locus is symmetric about the real axis. These observations put strong constraints on a sketch even before computing roots.

Derive a breakaway and read the transient

Take a positioning plant \(P=1/[s(s+4)]\) with proportional gain \(K\). The closed-loop poles are \(s=-2\pm\sqrt{4-K}\). They start at 0 and −4, approach each other along the real axis and meet at −2 when \(K=4\). Equivalently the real-axis gain \(K(s)=-s(s+4)\) has a stationary point at −2. Above the breakaway the complex pair moves vertically, so raising gain increases oscillation frequency while leaving the exponential envelope \(e^{-2t}\) unchanged.

The constant term gives \(\omega_n=\sqrt K\) and the linear coefficient gives \(\zeta=2/\sqrt K\). A specification of decay rate 3 s⁻¹ and damped frequency 4 rad/s would require poles \(-3\pm4i\). Since every complex pole here has real part −2, no choice of proportional gain meets both requirements. Failure to find such a gain numerically is a statement about controller structure.

Add a second degree of freedom

Feed back measured velocity as well as position: \(u=K_p(r-y)-K_v\dot y\). The closed-loop denominator becomes \(s^2+(4+K_v)s+K_p\); the reference numerator is just \(K_p\). For desired poles \(-3\pm4i\) the polynomial is \(s^2+6s+25\), hence \(K_v=2,\ K_p=25\). The derivative acts only on measured output, so a discontinuous reference does not directly request an impulse. Differentiating a noisy measurement can nevertheless demand excessive actuator effort. A separate velocity sensor or a filtered estimate changes the high-frequency dynamics and must be included in the design check.

Include the numerator when interpreting a pole plot

Moving a controller zero also reshapes the locus because it changes the angle of \(N/D\). A zero in the reference-to-output map changes step-response residues even if the poles coincide with a previous design. In particular a positive real zero imposes a severe tracking constraint. For the stable unit-DC transfer \(G=(1-s/z)/(1+s\tau)^2\) with \(z>0\), the unit-step initial slope is \(\lim_{s\to\infty}sG(s)=-1/(z\tau^2)\). Yet the final output is positive. The initial motion in the wrong direction follows directly from the zero. Increasing gain to make the initial transient faster cannot remove that geometry.
