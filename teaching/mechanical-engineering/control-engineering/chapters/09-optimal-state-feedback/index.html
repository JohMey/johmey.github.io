Optimal state feedback and the Riccati equation

Choose how heavily to penalise tracking error and actuator effort, then derive the feedback gain that minimises that stated cost.

Make the design objective explicit

Pole placement prescribes decay rates but says nothing about how much actuator energy a manoeuvre consumes. For a continuous linear system \(\dot x=Ax+Bu\) consider the regulator objective \(J=\int_0^\infty(x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru)\,dt\). Assume \(Q\) positive semidefinite and \(R\) positive definite. A large displacement penalty or a small control penalty generally encourages stronger corrective action. The ratio matters: multiplying both matrices by the same positive scalar leaves the best control unchanged. Scale states and inputs before comparing entries; a displacement in millimetres and a velocity in metres per second cannot share an arbitrary numerical weight without a unit convention.

Derive the continuous algebraic Riccati equation

For an infinite-horizon stationary problem propose a quadratic remaining cost \(V(x)=x^\mathsf{T}Px\). During a short interval \(dt\), optimality requires the minimum of instantaneous cost plus the change in remaining cost to vanish. In the limit this gives \(0=\min_u[x^\mathsf{T}Qx+u^\mathsf{T}Ru+2x^\mathsf{T}P(Ax+Bu)]\). Completing the square in \(u\) yields

\[u^*=-R^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}Px,\qquad A^\mathsf{T}P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P+Q=0.\]

The desired solution is the stabilising symmetric solution. Under standard stabilisability and detectability conditions it exists and yields finite nonnegative cost. Other algebraic roots need not make sense as stored future cost. Once a candidate is found, check the eigenvalues of \(A-BR^{-1}B^\mathsf{T}P\) and the residual of the matrix equation.

Solve the scalar unstable case

Let \(\dot x=x+u\) with \(Q=R=1\). The scalar equation is \(2P-P^2+1=0\), giving \(P=1\pm\sqrt2\). Only \(1+\sqrt2\) is positive and makes the closed-loop pole \(1-P=-\sqrt2\). From initial state \(x_0=2\), the theoretical minimum cost is \(4(1+\sqrt2)\simeq9.657\). Verify independently: insert \(x=2e^{-\sqrt2t}, u=-Px\) into the cost integral to obtain \(4(1+P^2)/(2\sqrt2)\), the same value. By contrast a stabilising gain of 2 gives state pole −1 and cost 10. Stabilising the state is necessary but still leaves a meaningful optimisation problem.

Distinguish control penalties from hard actuator limits

The optimal initial input for the scalar example has magnitude \(2P=4.83\). If the actuator is limited to 3, clipping the command invalidates the quadratic unconstrained solution during the clipped interval. Increasing \(R\) makes large inputs more expensive but generally supplies no absolute guarantee for arbitrary initial conditions. A concrete certificate is possible inside a stated region. In one dimension \(|u|\le u_{\max}\) is guaranteed by \(|x|\le u_{\max}/P\) under this gain, and the resulting stable exponential stays in that interval. Later we will impose hard constraints directly in a finite-horizon design.

The MIT notes on linear quadratic regulators extend this derivation to time-varying and finite-horizon problems.
