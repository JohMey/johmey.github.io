Translate a mass offset into a periodic bearing load

A rotor can have its total mass centre on the shaft axis and still shake its housing. The reason is spatial: two equal and opposite centrifugal force vectors in separate transverse planes form a rotating couple. First describe one small excess mass \(m\) at radial offset \(e\) and clock angle \(\phi\). At constant angular speed \(\omega\), its required radial acceleration has magnitude \(e\omega^2\). The equal and opposite reaction transmitted to the rotating assembly has magnitude \(me\omega^2\).

Represent its radial unbalance by a complex number \(U=me(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)\). This is shorthand for two transverse components at a reference shaft orientation, not a complex physical force. Multiplication by \(e^{i\omega t}\) rotates all vectors together. Since both \(\omega^2\) and this rotation are common to all masses, cancellation can be carried out using \(U\) alone.

\[\sum U_j=0,\qquad \sum z_jU_j=0.\]

Here \(z\) is axial location from a chosen reference plane. The second condition cancels the rotating transverse moment; its vector direction includes a common quarter-turn from the cross product that drops out of the zero condition. If the first condition holds, shifting the axial origin leaves the second condition unchanged.

Split one known unbalance between two planes

A rigid rotor has an 18 g excess mass at radius 40 mm, angle 0°, and axial location 120 mm between two correction planes at \(z_A=0\) and \(z_B=300\,\mathrm{mm}\). Its unbalance is \(720\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) along the reference direction. Moment balance gives \(300 U_B=-120(720)\), hence \(U_B=-288\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). Force balance then gives \(U_A=-432\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\). Both corrections point at 180° because the original mass lies between the planes.

At correction radius 60 mm these vectors require added masses 4.80 g in plane B and 7.20 g in plane A. If material is removed instead, the equivalent removed masses lie at the original 0° clock position. At 1800 rpm the original force amplitude was \(0.000720(188.5)^2=25.6\,\mathrm N\). Doubling speed quadruples that force, yet the same mass corrections still balance the assumed rigid rotor.

Identify what a single-plane test can miss

Consider instead two unbalances \(+400\) and \(-400\,\mathrm{g\,mm}\) in planes 200 mm apart. Their resultant force is zero at every speed. On ideal low-friction supports there is no preferred heavy side in a static test. Their couple magnitude at 1800 rpm is \(0.000400(0.200)(188.5)^2=2.84\,\mathrm{N\,m}\), sufficient to generate equal opposing reactions at bearings. Measurements in two support planes can distinguish this pattern from a net shaking force. Near a flexible rotor’s bending critical speed, shaft deflection changes the mass distribution and response phase; the rigid-body correction formulas then require a modal or measured influence-coefficient model.

Interpret correction accuracy

An angular placement error also leaves a residual vector. A correction of the correct magnitude set one degree away leaves a residual approximately \(U\pi/180\) for small angle, since the tip of the vector follows a circle. At large operating speed even this residual produces a force scaling with the square of speed.
