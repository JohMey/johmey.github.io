Impulse response and finite-duration loading

A short event and a long response

A latch strikes a mounted instrument for only a few milliseconds, but its motion persists after contact has ended. Peak force alone gives an incomplete description of that motion. Duration, impulse and the system’s natural time scale all matter. Define impulse \(J=\int F(t)\,dt\), with units N·s. Integrating \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F\) over an ideal instantaneous event leaves \(m[\dot x(0^+)-\dot x(0^-)]=J\). The position remains continuous since no finite mass can jump a finite distance in zero time under a finite impulse.

For an initially resting underdamped oscillator, the subsequent response follows from the two initial conditions \(x(0^+)=0\) and \(\dot x(0^+)=J/m\). With \(\alpha=c/(2m)\) and \(\omega_d=\sqrt{k/m-\alpha^2}\), the displacement is \(x=Je^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega_dt)/(m\omega_d)\) for \(t>0\). The response to a unit impulse is the same expression with \(J=1\). Its units are displacement per unit impulse.

Build a solution from elementary impulses

Divide a general forcing record into short intervals at times \(s\). Each interval contributes impulse \(F(s)\,ds\) and creates a response delayed by \(s\). Adding all contributions in a linear time-invariant system with zero initial state produces the convolution

\[x(t)=\int_0^t F(s)\,\frac{e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\sin[\omega_d(t-s)]}{m\omega_d}\,ds.\]

This construction makes causality explicit: later parts of the force record cannot influence the current response. Nonzero initial conditions add the free solution. In a multi-mode system each mode has its own impulse response, and the load’s location determines the force projection into it. Replacing the structure by its first mode may describe long-wave displacement well while missing a short, local acceleration peak.

Work a pulse of known duration

Neglect damping and apply constant force \(F_0\) for \(0<t<t_p\). During the pulse integration gives \(x=(F_0/k)[1-\cos(\omega_n t)]\) and \(\dot x=F_0\omega_n\sin(\omega_n t)/k\). After unloading the mass oscillates freely. Compute its amplitude from the end displacement and velocity:

\[A=\sqrt{x(t_p)^2+\big[\dot x(t_p)/\omega_n\big]^2}=\frac{2F_0}{k}\left|\sin(\omega_n t_p/2)\right|.\]

Let \(m=4.0\) kg, \(k=1600\) N/m, \(F_0=20\) N and \(t_p=0.050\) s. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and the impulse is 1.0 N·s. The formula predicts \(A=25\sin(0.50)=12.0\) mm after unloading. Replacing the whole pulse by an instantaneous impulse gives \(A_{\rm imp}=J/(m\omega_n)=12.5\) mm. Some restoring force developed during the real pulse, so the impulse approximation is slightly high.

Find the criterion and its limitations

Divide the finite-pulse amplitude by the impulse approximation to obtain \(|\sin z/z|\) with \(z=\omega_nt_p/2\). Its small-\(z\) expansion is \(1-z^2/6+\cdots\). At fixed impulse, a sufficiently short pulse therefore gives about the same low-mode amplitude regardless of duration. A shorter event has a broader frequency content, potentially increasing the importance of higher modes.

A pulse lasting exactly one natural period gives zero residual amplitude in the ideal undamped oscillator, although the displacement during the pulse peaks at \(2F_0/k\). Checking only the post-event motion would miss that maximum. In experiments record the force and response during contact as well as after it. Damping, uncertain pulse shape or nearby modes make exact cancellation unlikely, but the example shows why pulse duration must accompany a quoted peak force.

Compare pulses of equal impulse

A force transducer may report identical total impulse for a sharp tap and a broader pulse. For an undamped mode, the amplitude after the force has ended is the modulus of its frequency-weighted impulse divided by \(m\omega_n\): \(A=|\int F(t)e^{-i\omega_nt}dt|/(m\omega_n)\). This follows by expanding the sine kernel of the convolution into two quadratures. For a rectangular pulse the ratio to the ideal instantaneous-impulse amplitude is \(|\sin(\omega_nt_p/2)/(\omega_nt_p/2)|\). At a duration equal to half the natural period that factor is \(2/\pi=0.637\). Two force records with equal signed area can therefore have visibly different post-event amplitudes at one sensor if their durations differ. A short force pulse can consequently calibrate a low-frequency mode while underexciting a faster one in the same structure.
