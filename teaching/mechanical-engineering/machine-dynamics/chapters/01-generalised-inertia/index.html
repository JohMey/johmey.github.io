Coordinates, energy and equivalent inertia

Follow the motion through a mechanism

A motor accelerating a load feels inertia from components that turn at different speeds. Simply adding their physical moments of inertia would treat all of those speeds as equal. Instead, choose one independent coordinate and express every kinetic energy in terms of it. No-slip belts, rigid gears and inextensible cables give useful constraints when their compliance and backlash are negligible over the motion of interest.

Consider a drum of radius \(R\) winding a cable carrying a vertically guided mass \(m\). Choose the drum angle \(\theta\) positive when the mass rises. If the effective winding radius is constant, displacement, velocity and acceleration satisfy \(x=R\theta\), \(\dot x=R\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot x=R\ddot\theta\). These equalities are kinematic. A torque relation requires force balance or work in addition. When the cable builds successive layers on a spool, the effective radius varies with angle and the constant-ratio assumption must be revisited.

Reflect mass and forces to a common shaft

Let \(J_d\) be the drum inertia. Its rotational kinetic energy and the load’s translational kinetic energy sum to \(K=(J_d+mR^2)\dot\theta^2/2\). The equivalent inertia seen at the drum is \(J_{\rm eq}=J_d+mR^2\). A small positive angle raises the mass through \(R\,d\theta\), changing its potential energy by \(mgR\,d\theta\). Thus gravity contributes a resisting torque \(mgR\). A positive applied torque \(T_d\) contributes power \(T_d\dot\theta\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12J_{\rm eq}\dot\theta^2+mgR\theta\right)=T_d\dot\theta,\qquad J_{\rm eq}\ddot\theta=T_d-mgR.\]

The second expression also holds at zero speed by force balance. It separates steady effort, needed to hold or raise the load against gravity, from the extra effort needed to accelerate the two inertias. With the motor disconnected, an ideal light cable does not make the mass accelerate quite at \(g\), since the drum must gain rotational kinetic energy too.

Calculate a motor torque

A drum with \(R=0.10\) m and \(J_d=0.15\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) lifts 25 kg. A reduction gear gives \(\dot\theta_m=4\dot\theta\). The rotor has inertia \(J_m=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Refer the train to the motor coordinate. The drum-plus-load inertia becomes \((0.15+25(0.10)^2)/4^2=0.025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), so the total at the motor is 0.045 kg·m². Gravity contributes \(25(9.81)(0.10)/4=6.13\) N·m at that shaft.

Demand upward acceleration 0.80 m/s². The drum must accelerate at 8.0 rad/s² and the motor at 32 rad/s². The motor supplies \(6.13+0.045(32)=7.57\) N·m. A separate check at load speed 1.0 m/s gives motor speed 40 rad/s. Its 303 W then divide into about 245 W raising the weight and 57.6 W increasing kinetic energy. Ideal gearing changes torque and angular speed inversely while preserving transmitted power.

Identify which approximation controls the answer

At constant lifting speed the inertial torque vanishes. At constant angular acceleration, doubling the transmission ratio decreases reflected load inertia by four but also doubles the motor acceleration for the same load motion. The rotor’s own inertia remains at the motor and eventually dominates. A gear ratio therefore cannot reduce every part of acceleration torque indefinitely. Bearings, cables and gears also dissipate energy or store it elastically. Include their effects when a measured transient shows a repeatable lag, oscillation or loss that an ideal rigid constraint cannot explain.

Is the largest reduction always preferable?

For a prescribed drum acceleration \(\alpha_d\) and reduction \(n=\omega_m/\omega_d\), collect gravity and load-side acceleration into the drum demand \(T_d=mgR+(J_d+mR^2)\alpha_d\). The motor torque becomes \(T_m=T_d/n+J_mn\alpha_d\). The first term falls as reduction rises, while rotor acceleration grows. Differentiating with respect to \(n\) gives an ideal minimum at \(n=\sqrt{T_d/(J_m\alpha_d)}\). For the stated lift \(T_d=27.73\) N m and \(\alpha_d=8\) rad/s², giving \(n=13.2\) approximately. This torque minimum assumes the motor can achieve the associated speed. At a drum speed of 10 rad/s it would turn at 132 rad/s, over three times its speed with the original reduction. Shaft-speed and voltage limits can therefore select a different practical ratio.
