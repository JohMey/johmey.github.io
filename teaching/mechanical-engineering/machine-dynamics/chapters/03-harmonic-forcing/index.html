Harmonic forcing, phase and dissipated power

Predict the steady vibration under a rotating force and relate the phase lag to the power absorbed by damping.

Extract one sinusoidal component

A slightly eccentric rotating part exerts a periodic force on its housing. Measure the housing displacement from static equilibrium and first represent a single transverse force component by \(F_0\cos\omega t\). Linear response then satisfies \(m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t\). Its total solution contains the free transient plus a steady periodic part. Wait several decay times before comparing measured peak amplitudes with a harmonic prediction.

Write the steady part as \(\mathrm{Re}\{Xe^{i\omega t}\}\), taking the real force phasor \(F_0\) as phase reference. Each differentiation contributes \(i\omega\). The displacement phasor therefore satisfies

\[(k-m\omega^2+i c\omega)X=F_0.\]

The factor in parentheses is dynamic stiffness in N/m. At low speed the phasor is close to \(F_0/k\). At high speed the inertia term dominates and displacement is almost opposite the applied force. A phase measurement can distinguish those regimes even if their displacement amplitudes happen to be equal.

Read magnitude and phase separately

Introduce \(r=\omega/\omega_n\), \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_n)\). Take the magnitude and argument of the dynamic stiffness to obtain \(|X|/(F_0/k)=[(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2]^{-1/2}\). The displacement lags by \(\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(2\zeta r,1-r^2)\). At \(r=1\) elastic and inertia forces cancel in phase and the remaining quadrature force is viscous. The displacement lags by 90 degrees and has amplitude \(F_0/(c\omega_n)\).

For constant force amplitude, maximum displacement occurs at \(r=\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}\) if \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2\). This follows by differentiating the squared denominator with respect to \(r\). Thus the frequency of greatest displacement need not coincide exactly with the undamped natural frequency. A rotating imbalance also changes force amplitude with speed, so its peak cannot be inferred from a constant-force plot alone.

Check a resonant measurement by energy

Let \(m=10\) kg, \(k=4000\) N/m, \(c=40\) N·s/m and \(F_0=8.0\) N. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and \(\zeta=0.10\). At resonance the predicted displacement amplitude is \(8/[40(20)]=10.0\) mm. The velocity amplitude is 0.200 m/s. The time-average input power of a sinusoidal force and velocity is half the product of their peak magnitudes times the cosine of their phase difference. Since velocity is in phase with force at this frequency, the average is 0.800 W.

Independently, the damper dissipates \(\overline P=c\,\overline{\dot x^2}=c\omega^2|X|^2/2=0.800\) W. The spring returns the energy it stores each cycle and the mass has no net change in kinetic energy. If force and displacement peaks are simply multiplied and reported as watts, the dimensions and the missing phase both reveal the error.

Apply the result to a rotor

A rotating eccentric mass \(m_e\) displaced a radius \(e\) generates force amplitude \(m_e e\omega^2\) when its circular motion is prescribed. With \(m_e e=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m}\) and \(\omega=20\) rad/s, this is the 8.0 N used above. At 40 rad/s the applied force grows to 32 N. The dynamic stiffness magnitude becomes \(\sqrt{(4000-10(40)^2)^2+(40(40))^2}=12.1\) kN/m, giving about 2.64 mm displacement. The force grew fourfold while the response fell because the system moved away from resonance. A speed sweep therefore needs both the changing excitation and the changing dynamic stiffness.

Estimate damping from a frequency sweep

A useful second identification uses the vibration velocity at constant force amplitude. At resonance the velocity amplitude is \(F_0/c\). At the two frequencies where mean dissipated power has fallen to half its maximum, the velocity amplitude is \(F_0/(\sqrt2 c)\). Inverting the velocity response gives \(|k/\omega-m\omega|=c\). Solving the two sign cases and subtracting gives the bandwidth \(\omega_2-\omega_1=c/m\) for this viscous single-mode system. In the numerical example the difference is 4 rad/s; the predicted limits are about 18.10 and 22.10 rad/s. This result applies to the power or velocity response at constant force amplitude. A sweep driven by a growing rotor imbalance would also require correcting the measured amplitudes for their changing excitation.
