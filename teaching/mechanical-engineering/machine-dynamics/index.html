Applied study

Move from a single oscillator to the natural frequencies and mode shapes of a machine on a flexible frame.

Can a motor support resonate at running speed?

A compact motor sits on the horizontal arm of a steel L-frame. After a disturbance it can move horizontally, bounce vertically and rock. A spring scale measures how far the mount yields under static load. To predict the frequencies of those three motions, we also need to know where the mass sits.

Start with one undamped coordinate \(q\), measured from equilibrium. A spring with compliance \(c\) moves \(cP\) under force \(P\), so its restoring force is \(-q/c\). Newton’s law gives \(m\ddot q+q/c=0\) and \(f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{mc})\). Gravity sets the static equilibrium. For small vibrations about it, constant gravity drops out of the linear equation.

Find the frame’s vertical compliance

The frame has a clamped column of height \(H\) and a horizontal arm of length \(L\), rigidly joined. Both have area \(A\), bending second moment \(I\) and modulus \(E\). Under a vertical tip force \(P\), the arm bends with moment \(P(L-x)\). The column carries axial force \(P\) and constant bending moment \(PL\). Neglect shear deformation and store the bending and axial energies. A slowly applied load performs work \(P^2c_{zz}/2\), so

\[c_{zz}=\int_0^L\frac{(L-x)^2}{EI}\,dx+\int_0^H\frac{L^2}{EI}\,dz+\int_0^H\frac{1}{EA}\,dz=\frac{L^3/3+L^2H}{EI}+\frac{H}{EA}.\]

Take \(H=2.0\) m, \(L=1.5\) m, \(A=0.0050\,\mathrm{m^2}\), \(I=8.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^4}\) and \(E=210\) GPa. This gives \(c_{zz}=3.35\times10^{-6}\) m/N. A 1 kN vertical force displaces the tip by 3.35 mm. The column-bending term alone contributes 2.68 mm. Calling the arm a cantilever with a perfectly fixed root would miss the dominant compliance.

Translation and rocking share the restoring energy

If only vertical inertia were retained, a 200 kg motor would give \(f=6.15\) Hz. In fact the column sways and the arm rotates when the motor moves vertically. In a light-frame approximation collect the tip coordinates in \(\mathbf q=(u_x,u_z,\theta)\), measured at the machine’s centre of mass. Apply a horizontal force, a vertical force and an end moment separately to obtain the three columns of the flexibility matrix \(\mathbf C\). Its off-diagonal entries measure the movement in one coordinate caused by a load in another. For linear elastic members reciprocity makes this matrix symmetric.

The stiffness matrix is \(\mathbf K=\mathbf C^{-1}\). With motor mass \(m\) and rotary inertia \(J\), let \(\mathbf M=\mathrm{diag}(m,m,J)\). Substituting harmonic motion into \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf K\mathbf q=0\) gives \(\mathbf K\boldsymbol\phi=\omega^2\mathbf M\boldsymbol\phi\). A mode shape \(\boldsymbol\phi\) describes a ratio of motions. The frequency \(f=\omega/(2\pi)\) is independent of the scale used to draw that shape.

For \(m=200\) kg and \(J=60\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), the three light-frame frequencies are 5.048, 15.449 and 44.645 Hz. If the steel frame’s distributed mass is included they fall to 4.701, 13.550 and 42.41 Hz. The first light-frame mode corresponds to about 303 rpm for an excitation that repeats once per revolution. Whether it produces a large vibration also depends on damping and on the projection of the applied force onto that mode. Explore how inertia affects the rotation of a rigid body.

Transfer problem · Isolate a machine from floor vibration

A 50 kg instrument on a spring and viscous damper sits on a floor moving as \(y(t)\). The mount extends by \(x-y\), where \(x\) is the instrument’s absolute displacement. Force balance gives \(m\ddot{x}+c(\dot{x}-\dot{y})+k(x-y)=0\). For sinusoidal floor motion the ratio of instrument to floor amplitude follows by replacing each derivative by \(i\omega\):

\[\frac{|X|}{|Y|}=\sqrt{\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}},\quad r=\omega/\omega_n,\quad \omega_n=\sqrt{k/m},\quad \zeta=\frac{c}{2m\omega_n}.\]

Choose \(k=20\,000\) N/m and \(c=200\) N·s/m. Then \(\omega_n=20\) rad/s and \(\zeta=0.10\). At 40 rad/s, \(r=2\) and the ratio is \(\sqrt{1.16/9.16}=0.356\). A 1.0 mm floor oscillation gives 0.356 mm of instrument motion. At 20 rad/s the same mount amplifies motion by \(\sqrt{1.04/0.04}=5.10\). A mount selected for stable operation at one speed can amplify vibration as a machine runs up through resonance.

Exercises

1. Infer a frequency from measured sag

A light support with one vertical coordinate deflects 4.0 mm under a machine’s weight. Infer its natural frequency directly from the sag. Compare it with the forcing frequency of a once-per-revolution imbalance at 450 rpm.

Show the estimate

From \(mg=k\delta\), \(k/m=g/\delta\). Hence \(f=(2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{9.81/0.0040}=7.88\) Hz. The imbalance frequency is \(450/60=7.50\) Hz. The forcing is about 5% below the estimated frequency. Estimating a response amplitude would additionally require the forcing magnitude and damping.

2. Add a geometrically identical payload

For the light L-frame double both \(m\) and \(J\) while holding the support stiffness fixed. Predict the change in all three frequencies and the first critical speed. Does the same factor apply to a frame whose own mass stays fixed?

Show the modal scaling

The entire tip mass matrix doubles. The same eigenvectors then solve the equation with each \(\omega^2\) halved. The three frequencies become 3.570, 10.924 and 31.569 Hz. The first once-per-revolution critical speed is about 214 rpm. With distributed frame mass only part of the assembled mass matrix doubles, so there is no common frequency scaling factor.
