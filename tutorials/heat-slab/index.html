Heat conduction in a slab: one model, every method

One physics declaration, Fourier conduction in a steel slab whose faces are held at two temperatures, is solved with every applicable combination of discretization family (finite differences, finite volumes, continuous, discontinuous and Nitsche finite elements), time integrator, nonlinear solver and linear solver. Each run is graded against the exact Fourier series; combinations the library refuses are reported as such. 232 runs on the compiled OneSimulation runtime: 199 ran as declared, 33 failed as the mathematics predicts and are shown hatched with their stabilized counterpart where one exists, none was refused by the library.

A steel slab of thickness \(L=20\,\)mm starts at \(T_0=300\,\)K. At \(t=0\) its left face is raised to \(T_1=400\,\)K and held there; the right face stays at \(T_0\). Fourier's law gives

\[\rho c_p\,\partial_t T=\partial_x\big(k\,\partial_x T\big),\qquad T(0,t)=T_1,\quad T(L,t)=T_0, \quad T(x,0)=T_0,\]

with \(k=45\,\)W/(m K), \(\rho=7850\,\)kg/m³ and \(c_p=460\,\)J/(kg K), so the diffusivity is \(\alpha=k/(\rho c_p)\approx1.25\cdot10^{-5}\,\)m²/s. The heat equation is declared once. Everything numerical is an argument of Numerics: the discretization family, its diffusion operator and boundary treatment, the time integrator and, for implicit steps, the nonlinear and the linear solver.

Reference. Separating the steady line \(T_s(x)=T_1+(T_0-T_1)\,x/L\) leaves a sine series (Carslaw and Jaeger, Conduction of Heat in Solids, §3.3):

\[T(x,t)=T_s(x)+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2(T_0-T_1)}{n\pi}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\, \exp\!\Big(-\frac{n^2\pi^2\alpha t}{L^2}\Big).\]

Grading. At \(t=3\,\)s (Fourier number \(\alpha t/L^2\approx0.093\)) every run is evaluated at the cell centres \(x_i\): the mean absolute error \(\sum_i|T_h(x_i)-T(x_i)|\,\Delta x/L\) and the largest deviation; the wall time covers validation, binding and time stepping.

The physics, declared once

Nothing in this cell names a numerical method.

thickness, hot, cold, end_time = 0.02, 400.0, 300.0, 3.0
cells, coarse_cells, time_cells = 200, 100, 50
steel = IsotropicSolid(
    density=7850.0,
    specific_heat_capacity=460.0,
    thermal_conductivity=45.0,
    youngs_modulus=2.1e11,
    poisson_ratio=0.3,
    thermal_expansion_coefficient=1.2e-5,
)
diffusivity = steel.thermal_conductivity / (steel.density * steel.specific_heat_capacity)
slab = Line(start=(0.0, 0.0, 0.0), end=(thickness, 0.0, 0.0))
heat = HeatConduction(domain=slab.line, material=steel)
problem = Problem(
    name="Heated slab",
    geometry=slab,
    physics=(heat,),
    boundary_conditions=(
        FixedTemperature(physics=heat, boundary=slab.boundaries.start, value=hot),
        FixedTemperature(physics=heat, boundary=slab.boundaries.end, value=cold),
    ),
    initial_conditions=(InitialValue(heat.temperature, cold),),
)
meshes = {
    n: StructuredLineMesher(cells=n).generate(slab) for n in (cells, coarse_cells, time_cells)
}
centres = {n: mesh.cell_centers for n, mesh in meshes.items()}
print(
    f"{meshes[cells].cell_count} cells of width {1e3 * thickness / cells:.2f} mm "
    f"(mesh check: {meshes[coarse_cells].cell_count} cells; time-integrator cut: "
    f"{meshes[time_cells].cell_count} cells), diffusivity {diffusivity:.4e} m²/s, "
    f"Fourier number {diffusivity * end_time / thickness**2:.4f}"
)

The exact solution

The series is summed with 2000 terms at every cell centre and on a fine plotting grid.

The method catalogue

Finite differences with central, fourth- and sixth-order polynomial Laplacians; finite volumes with the two-point flux and with the corrected central flux of the native cell balance; continuous Lagrange elements of degree 1 to 3 with strong boundary values, symmetric and nonsymmetric Nitsche boundary terms and continuous interior penalty; discontinuous elements of degree 1 to 3 with the symmetric interior-penalty (SIPG) flux at two penalty levels. The finite-difference heat flux needs a first derivative, so each stencil is paired with the gradient of the same order.

Time integrators: the explicit one-step and multistep methods, implicit Runge-Kutta, Adams-Moulton and BDF methods, Rosenbrock methods and the adaptive integrators. Implicit stages take a Newton solver with a sparse direct solve; the linearly implicit Rosenbrock stages take the sparse direct solve alone. Explicit methods advance at their stable step (safety factor 0.5), implicit ones at \(\Delta t=0.05\,\)s.

Schemes that must fail, and their stabilized counterparts

The diffusion operator has a real, negative spectrum reaching \(-\lambda_{\max}\sim-12\alpha/h^2\). Adams-Moulton 3 and 4 are not A-stable: their stability interval on the negative axis ends at \(\lambda\Delta t\approx6\) and \(3\), while \(\lambda_{\max}\Delta t\) is several hundred at \(\Delta t=0.05\,\)s. Leapfrog has no damping: every decaying mode is amplified at any step. A fixed-point (Picard) iteration of an implicit step contracts only for \(\lambda_{\max}\Delta t<1\). BDF5 and BDF6 are stable on the negative axis but not monotone: at large \(\lambda\Delta t\) their first steps after the jump overshoot. Crank-Nicolson is A-stable but not L-stable: the stiffest modes of the initial jump at the hot face are multiplied by almost \(-1\) in every step, and on the high-order and Nitsche elements the resulting overshoot leaves \([T_0,T_1]\). Each of these must fail; its stabilized counterpart, shown behind a grey hatching, is the same method at a step it is stable or damping for, or leapfrog with the Robert-Asselin-Williams filter. A counterpart runs only where the declared scheme actually failed.

The test matrix

Three cuts through the space of combinations:

1. Space × time integrator — every spatial scheme on 200 cells with four implicit integrators (backward Euler, Crank-Nicolson, BDF2, SDIRK2) at \(\Delta t=0.05\,\)s. 2. Time integrator × space — every integrator on three spatial schemes (central differences, two-point volumes and CG P1). An explicit method on a diffusion operator must take \(\Delta t\propto h^2\), so this cut uses 50 cells; the spatial accuracy is the subject of the first cut. Higher-degree and discontinuous elements are left out of this cut: degree and interior penalty raise \(\lambda_{\max}\), and an explicit step would have to be five to twenty times smaller; they run with the four implicit integrators of the first cut. 3. Nonlinear × linear solver — backward Euler on central differences with 50 cells (the fixed-point counterpart needs \(\lambda_{\max}\Delta t<1\), which is affordable on this grid).

The representative schemes are repeated on 100 cells for the mesh check.

Run everything

One loop, the same problem object every time. A combination the library refuses is recorded with its validation message. A run whose temperature leaves the interval \([T_0, T_1]\) violates the maximum principle of the heat equation and is recorded as failed, as is a solver that does not converge. A failed run with a stabilized counterpart appends that counterpart to the list, so the same loop runs it next.

Space × time integrator

The mean temperature error of every spatial scheme with four implicit integrators. Backward Euler is first order in time; at \(\Delta t=0.05\,\)s its time error dominates every spatial error, while Crank-Nicolson, BDF2 and SDIRK2 reveal the spatial ranking.

Profiles of representative combinations

Temperature through the slab against the series, and a close-up of the hot face where the initial jump between \(T_0\) and \(T_1\) is smoothed out.

Time integrator × space

Explicit integrators at their stable step, implicit integrators at \(\Delta t=0.05\,\)s, on 50 cells.

Nonlinear × linear solver

Backward Euler on central differences with 50 cells. The heat equation is linear, so every converging Newton variant reproduces the same discrete solution in one iteration per step; the wall time differs. The step matrix \(I+\Delta t\,K\) of the symmetric central stencil is symmetric positive definite, but this owner does not declare it so: conjugate gradients and the symmetric algebraic multigrid are refused. That is a gap of the library, shown as "rejected".

Mesh check: 200 against 100 cells

BDF2 at \(\Delta t=0.05\,\)s on half as many cells. Second-order schemes should reduce the spatial error about fourfold; once it falls below the time error of the step, the ratio levels off.

What the library refused, and what failed

A refused combination is caught by validation or binding before any time step; a failed one started and left the physical range or did not converge.

Methods that do not apply to this problem

Riemann solvers, reconstructions and slope limiters act on convective fluxes; pure conduction has none, so finite volumes take only a diffusive face flux. SUPG, GLS and graph viscosity stabilize convection and have nothing to act on here. Staggered Verlet needs a second-order system and IMEX splits a stiff from a non-stiff part, which a single diffusion operator does not have.

Going further

Replace a FixedTemperature by HeatFlux or ConvectiveHeatFlux and the matrix code stays as it is; add a column by adding one object to a dictionary.
