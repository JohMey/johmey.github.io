A diffracting Gaussian beam: one model, every method

One physics declaration, the paraxial envelope equation with a Gaussian input beam, is solved on 400 cells with every applicable combination of discretization family, field representation, time integrator, nonlinear solver and linear solver. Each run is graded against the exact Gaussian beam: its complex amplitude and its width w(z). 250 runs on the compiled OneSimulation runtime: 170 ran as declared, 28 failed as the mathematics predicts and are shown hatched with their stabilized counterpart where one exists, 18 have no stable explicit step at this resolution and are marked as such, none was refused by the library.

A monochromatic beam of wavelength \(\lambda=1\,\mu\)m enters a homogeneous medium with a Gaussian transverse profile of waist \(w_0=20\,\mu\)m. In the frame that travels with the group velocity \(v_g\), the slowly varying envelope \(A(x,t)\) obeys the paraxial (Schrödinger-type) equation

\[\partial_t A=\frac{i\,v_g}{2k}\,\partial_x^2 A,\qquad z=v_g t,\]

with one transverse coordinate \(x\) and the wavenumber \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). The operator is purely dispersive: every Fourier mode keeps its modulus and only turns its phase. The equation is declared once; everything numerical is an argument of Numerics: the discretization family (finite volumes, finite differences, continuous and discontinuous finite elements), the representation of the envelope (one complex field, or a real and an imaginary field), the time integrator and, for implicit steps, the nonlinear and the linear solver.

Reference. The exact solution for \(A(x,0)=E_0\,e^{-x^2/w_0^2}\) (Kogelnik and Li, Appl. Opt. 5, 1550, 1966, reduced to one transverse dimension) is

\[A(x,z)=\frac{E_0}{\sqrt{1+i z/z_R}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{x^2}{w_0^2\,(1+i z/z_R)}\Big),\qquad z_R=\frac{k w_0^2}{2},\qquad w(z)=w_0\sqrt{1+(z/z_R)^2}.\]

The beam is followed to \(z=2z_R\), where its width has grown by \(\sqrt5\) and its peak amplitude has fallen by \(5^{-1/4}\). The transverse window \(|x|\le 12\,w_0\) holds the beam with amplitude below \(10^{-12}\) at its edges, so the zero envelope prescribed there does not disturb it.

Grading. At \(z=2z_R\) every run is evaluated at the 400 cell centres \(x_i\): the relative \(L_2\) error of the complex amplitude, \(\|A_h-A\|_2/\|A\|_2\), and the error of the beam width from the second moment, \(w_h^2=4\sum x_i^2|A_h|^2/\sum|A_h|^2\). The wall time covers validation, binding and time stepping.

The physics, declared once

The same beam in both field representations. Nothing in this cell names a numerical method.

wavelength, waist, amplitude, group_speed = 1.0e-6, 20.0e-6, 1.0e3, 2.99792458e8
wavenumber = 2 * np.pi / wavelength
rayleigh = wavenumber * waist**2 / 2
distance = 2 * rayleigh
end_time, cells, coarse_cells = distance / group_speed, 400, 200
window = Line(start=(-12 * waist, 0.0, 0.0), end=(12 * waist, 0.0, 0.0))
medium = dict(
    domain=window.line,
    wavenumber=wavenumber,
    group_speed=group_speed,
    permittivity=8.8541878128e-12,
)
envelopes = {
    "complex envelope": ComplexParaxialEnvelope(**medium),
    "real + imaginary pair": ParaxialEnvelope(**medium),
}
beams = {
    "complex envelope": Problem(
        name="Gaussian beam, complex envelope",
        geometry=window,
        physics=(envelopes["complex envelope"],),
        boundary_conditions=(
            PrescribedValueBoundary(
                physics=envelopes["complex envelope"],
                boundary=window.boundaries.all,
                value={"envelope": 0j},
            ),
        ),
        initial_conditions=(
            InitialValue(
                envelopes["complex envelope"].envelope,
                lambda x: amplitude * np.exp(-((x / waist) ** 2)) + 0j,
            ),
        ),
    ),
    "real + imaginary pair": Problem(
        name="Gaussian beam, real and imaginary fields",
        geometry=window,
        physics=(envelopes["real + imaginary pair"],),
        boundary_conditions=(
            PrescribedValueBoundary(
                physics=envelopes["real + imaginary pair"],
                boundary=window.boundaries.all,
                value={"real": 0.0, "imaginary": 0.0},
            ),
        ),
        initial_conditions=(
            InitialValue(
                envelopes["real + imaginary pair"].real,
                lambda x: amplitude * np.exp(-((x / waist) ** 2)),
            ),
            InitialValue(envelopes["real + imaginary pair"].imaginary, 0.0),
        ),
    ),
}
meshes = {n: StructuredLineMesher(cells=n).generate(window) for n in (cells, coarse_cells)}
centres = {n: mesh.cell_centers[:, 0] for n, mesh in meshes.items()}
print(
    f"{meshes[cells].cell_count} cells of width {24 * waist / cells * 1e6:.2f} µm "
    f"(coarse check: {meshes[coarse_cells].cell_count} cells); Rayleigh length "
    f"{rayleigh * 1e3:.3f} mm, propagation to z = {distance * 1e3:.3f} mm (t = {end_time:.3e} s)"
)

The exact beam

The complex amplitude at \(z=2z_R\) at the cell centres and on a fine grid for the plots, and the exact width.

The method catalogue

Finite volumes with the two-point flux; finite differences with the central and the 4th- and 6th-order polynomial Laplacians; continuous finite elements of degree 1 to 3 with the Galerkin diffusion form and the prescribed envelope imposed weakly by Nitsche's method (symmetric, incomplete and nonsymmetric variants), and with a Galerkin least-squares term; discontinuous finite elements of degree 1 to 3 with the symmetric interior penalty.

Explicit step. The paraxial operator has an imaginary spectrum, so an explicit step must lie inside the part of the time method's stability region that covers a segment of the imaginary axis. Every spatial scheme is given the same rule, SpectralEnclosureStability(enclosure="imaginary_axis"): it estimates the spectral radius of the executed spatial operator (Arnoldi iteration on its residual) and returns the largest step for which the selected method's amplification stays below one on that segment. Implicit methods are not limited by it; their step is \(\Delta t=t_{end}/100\).

Time integrators: explicit one-step and multistep methods, the leapfrog scheme with and without the Robert-Asselin-Williams filter, implicit Runge-Kutta, Adams-Moulton and BDF methods, Rosenbrock methods and the adaptive integrators. Implicit stages take a Newton solver with a coloured finite-difference Jacobian and a sparse direct solve; the linearly implicit Rosenbrock stages take the sparse direct solve alone.

The fixed-step Gear BDF methods of order 3 to 6 and Adams-Moulton 3 and 4 build their first history values with explicit RK4 steps at the full step size. On the stiff transverse spectrum of the finite elements that start-up is unstable, so these methods must fail at Δt = t_end/100. The stabilized counterpart of a Gear BDF method is the same order with backward-Euler start-up (BDF(order=k), one ingredient changed); Adams-Moulton 3 and 4 (whose stability regions are also bounded) and the fixed-point iteration (which contracts only while Δt times the spectral radius stays below one) are repeated at Δt = t_end/3200. Counterparts are shown behind the hatching. On the discontinuous elements, whose penalty modes make the spectral radius far larger, Adams-Moulton 3 and 4 stay unstable even at that step.

Krylov solvers without a preconditioner may stall on the stage system; their counterpart adds an ILU preconditioner (one ingredient).

The test matrix

Three cuts through the space of combinations:

1. Space × representation and time — every spatial scheme with the complex envelope and with the real/imaginary pair, each advanced implicitly with Crank-Nicolson at \(\Delta t=t_{end}/100\) and explicitly with classical RK4 at its stable step. 2. Time integrator × space — every integrator on the 4th-order finite differences and on continuous and discontinuous P1 elements, complex envelope. 3. Nonlinear × linear solver — Crank-Nicolson on the 4th-order finite differences, for the complex envelope and for the real/imaginary pair.

Explicit steps of finite elements of degree 2 and 3, and of the GLS-stabilized elements, are limited by the Nitsche and interior-penalty boundary modes, whose eigenvalues grow like \(p^4/h^2\): at 400 cells they need tens of thousands of steps. The library runs them (the stability rule gives the step), but they are outside this cut and marked with a dash.

A few representative combinations are repeated on 200 cells for the mesh check.

Run everything

A stabilized counterpart is run only where the declared combination did not run. One loop over the two problem objects. A combination the library refuses is recorded with its validation message. Two kinds of failure are expected: an explicit method whose stability region contains no segment of the imaginary axis has no stable step at all (the stability rule says so before the first step), and a method that is not A-stable amplifies the high transverse modes at a large implicit step (the amplitude grows instead of keeping its modulus).

Time integrator × space

Explicit integrators at their stable step, implicit integrators at \(\Delta t=t_{end}/100\).

Nonlinear × linear solver

Crank-Nicolson on the 4th-order finite differences, once with the native complex envelope and once with the real/imaginary pair. The problem is linear, so every converging solver reproduces the same discrete solution; the wall time differs.

The implicit stage of the native complex field is solved on real coordinates (each complex value as a real/imaginary pair), so every solver pair of the real/imaginary representation also runs on the complex envelope: the automatic linear-solver selection picks the sparse direct solve of that real system, JFNK with a sparse direct inner solve probes the stage on the paired coordinates, and Aitken acceleration measures the complex fixed-point update in the same coordinates.

Mesh check: 400 against 200 cells

The representative combinations on half as many cells. The amplitude error of a second-order scheme falls by about four, that of the 4th-order differences by about sixteen, as long as the time error stays below the spatial one.

What the library refused, and what failed

A refused combination is caught by validation or binding before any time step. "No stable step" is the stability rule's verdict before the first step; a failed run started and amplified its high modes.

Methods that do not apply to paraxial diffraction

Forward Euler, Heun, SSP-RK2, the explicit midpoint rule, the embedded Heun-Euler pair and Adams-Bashforth 2 have stability regions that touch the imaginary axis only at the origin: on a purely dispersive operator every mode is amplified at every step size, so no stable step exists and the stability rule refuses them before the first step ("no stable step" in the matrix). No single added ingredient changes that without changing the method or the physics (a damping term).

Other methods in the library are not in the matrix because the paraxial equation lacks what they are built for: Riemann solvers, reconstructions and limiters are for convective fluxes, and the envelope has none; Runge-Kutta-Chebyshev and -Legendre super-steps are built for the negative real axis of parabolic operators; conjugate gradients and algebraic multigrid need a symmetric positive definite operator, while the complex paraxial stage matrix is symmetric but not Hermitian.

Going further

Add an absorbing medium (absorption=) or a refractive-index perturbation (refractive_index_perturbation=) to the two envelope declarations: the exact beam is multiplied by \(e^{-\alpha z}\) or \(e^{ik\Delta n z}\), and the matrix code stays as it is.
