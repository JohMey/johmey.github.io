What it computes

OneSimulation builds the two-body equations of motion as a signal-flow graph of integrator and gravity blocks and integrates them with the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method at four fixed step sizes.

What it checks

The period matches Kepler's third law (37 921.96 s) to the printed digit, the radius stays on the Kepler ellipse within 2e-8, and the energy drift after three revolutions falls from 6e-5 to 2e-9 between 120 s and 15 s steps (largest error order 4.3).

The notebook

The complete notebook, cell by cell. Run it to produce the figures and the comparison printout.

A spacecraft leaves a low Earth parking orbit on a geostationary transfer orbit: at the perigee, 250 km above the surface, it has just the speed that carries it out to the geostationary radius of 42 164 km. Only the Earth's central gravity acts, so the motion stays in the orbital plane and obeys

\[\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\,\mathbf r,\qquad \mathbf r=(x,y),\quad r=|\mathbf r|,\]

with the gravitational parameter of the Earth \(\mu=GM=3.986\,004\times10^{14}\) m³/s². OneSimulation assembles the equations as a signal-flow graph: four integrator blocks hold the position and the velocity, two blocks evaluate the gravitational acceleration, and the graph is integrated with the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method at a fixed step.

Reference. Kepler's laws for the two-body problem (H. D. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 3rd ed., Butterworth-Heinemann 2014, ch. 2). With the perigee and apogee radii \(r_p\), \(r_a\), the semi-major axis and the eccentricity are \(a=(r_p+r_a)/2\), \(e=(r_a-r_p)/(r_a+r_p)\), and

\[r(\theta)=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos\theta},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}=-\frac{\mu}{2a},\qquad h=|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}.\]

The vis-viva equation gives the perigee speed \(v_p=\sqrt{\mu\,(2/r_p-1/a)}\).

import numpy as np
from onesim.numerics.time import DEFAULT_TIME_INTEGRATOR_REGISTRY
from onesim.physics.systems import ControlSignalFlowProvider, SignalBlock
from onesim.results.sampling import crossing_times
from onesim.visualization import plots, theme

theme.set()

Orbit

The spacecraft starts at the perigee on the \(x\) axis with the vis-viva speed in the \(y\) direction and is followed for three revolutions.

mu, earth_radius = 3.986004418e14, 6.378137e6
perigee, apogee = earth_radius + 250e3, 42.164e6
semi_major, eccentricity = 0.5 * (perigee + apogee), (apogee - perigee) / (
    apogee + perigee
)
period = 2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(semi_major**3 / mu)
perigee_speed = np.sqrt(mu * (2 / perigee - 1 / semi_major))
energy_exact, momentum_exact = -mu / (2 * semi_major), np.sqrt(
    mu * semi_major * (1 - eccentricity**2)
)
print(
    f"a = {semi_major / 1e3:.1f} km, e = {eccentricity:.5f}, period T = {period:.1f} s ({period / 3600:.3f} h), "
    f"perigee speed {perigee_speed:.2f} m/s"
)

Signal-flow graph and step study

The same graph is integrated with steps of 120, 60, 30 and 15 s; the 15 s run is the result shown below. Near the perigee the spacecraft covers about 150 km per step.

steps = (120.0, 60.0, 30.0, 15.0)
runs = {
    dt: ControlSignalFlowProvider(
        blocks=(
            SignalBlock(
                "x", "integrator", inputs=("vx",), parameters={"initial_value": perigee}
            ),
            SignalBlock("y", "integrator", inputs=("vy",)),
            SignalBlock("vx", "integrator", inputs=("ax",)),
            SignalBlock(
                "vy",
                "integrator",
                inputs=("ay",),
                parameters={"initial_value": perigee_speed},
            ),
            SignalBlock(
                "ax",
                "custom",
                inputs=("x", "y"),
                custom_evaluator=lambda s, t, p: -mu * s[0] / np.hypot(*s) ** 3,
                custom_derivative=lambda *a: (),
            ),
            SignalBlock(
                "ay",
                "custom",
                inputs=("x", "y"),
                custom_evaluator=lambda s, t, p: -mu * s[1] / np.hypot(*s) ** 3,
                custom_derivative=lambda *a: (),
            ),
        ),
        final_time=3 * period,
        time_step=dt,
        time_integrator=DEFAULT_TIME_INTEGRATOR_REGISTRY.resolve_id("classical_rk4"),
    ).execute()
    for dt in steps
}
orbit = {
    dt: {k: np.asarray(run.signals[k]) for k in ("x", "y", "vx", "vy")}
    for dt, run in runs.items()
}
energy = {
    dt: 0.5 * (s["vx"] ** 2 + s["vy"] ** 2) - mu / np.hypot(s["x"], s["y"])
    for dt, s in orbit.items()
}
momentum = {dt: s["x"] * s["vy"] - s["y"] * s["vx"] for dt, s in orbit.items()}
times, s = np.asarray(runs[15.0].times), orbit[15.0]
radius, anomaly = np.hypot(s["x"], s["y"]), np.arctan2(s["y"], s["x"])

Comparison with Kepler's laws

The period is the time between successive perigee passages, where \(y\) crosses zero upwards; the orbit radius is compared with the conic section at the same true anomaly \(\theta\); energy and angular momentum must stay at their initial values.

ellipse = (
    lambda theta: semi_major
    * (1 - eccentricity**2)
    / (1 + eccentricity * np.cos(theta))
)
passages = np.asarray(
    crossing_times(times, s["y"], 0.0, direction="rising", after=0.5 * period)
)
print(
    f"perigee passages after {', '.join(f'{t:.1f}' for t in passages)} s;  "
    f"period {np.mean(np.diff(np.r_[0.0, passages])):.2f} s, Kepler {period:.2f} s"
)
print(
    f"apogee radius {radius.max() / 1e3:.2f} km (exact {apogee / 1e3:.2f} km),  "
    f"largest deviation from the ellipse {np.abs(radius / ellipse(anomaly) - 1).max():.1e}"
)
print(
    f"specific energy {energy[15.0][0] / 1e6:.4f} MJ/kg (exact {energy_exact / 1e6:.4f}),  "
    f"angular momentum {momentum[15.0][0] / 1e6:.2f} km²/s (exact {momentum_exact / 1e6:.2f})"
)
print(
    "  step [s]   energy drift after 3 revolutions   largest energy error   angular-momentum drift"
)
for dt in steps:
    print(
        f"{dt:9.0f} {abs(energy[dt][-1] / energy_exact - 1):24.2e} {np.abs(energy[dt] / energy_exact - 1).max():22.2e} "
        f"{abs(momentum[dt][-1] / momentum_exact - 1):24.2e}"
    )
drift = [abs(energy[dt][-1] / energy_exact - 1) for dt in steps]
largest = [np.abs(energy[dt] / energy_exact - 1).max() for dt in steps]
print(
    f"observed order between the two finest steps: {np.log2(largest[-2] / largest[-1]):.2f} for the largest energy error, "
    f"{np.log2(drift[-2] / drift[-1]):.2f} for the drift after whole revolutions"
)

The classical Runge-Kutta method has global order four, and so does the largest energy error along the orbit. Nearly all of the error is made during the fast perigee passages, and right at the perigee part of it cancels, so the drift evaluated after whole revolutions falls even faster.

Figure

The computed orbit over the Kepler ellipse with the Earth to scale; the distance from the Earth's centre over time (dotted verticals: the perigee passages); the relative drift of energy and angular momentum; and the energy drift against the step size.

theta = np.linspace(0.0, 2 * np.pi, 721)
fig = plots.figure(2, 2, size=(14, 10.5))
plots.line(
    y={
        "computed orbit": (s["x"][::4] / 1e6, s["y"][::4] / 1e6),
        "Earth": (
            earth_radius * np.cos(theta) / 1e6,
            earth_radius * np.sin(theta) / 1e6,
        ),
    },
    reference={
        "Kepler ellipse": (
            ellipse(theta) * np.cos(theta) / 1e6,
            ellipse(theta) * np.sin(theta) / 1e6,
        )
    },
    xlabel="x [1000 km]",
    ylabel="y [1000 km]",
    title="Geostationary transfer orbit",
    legend="upper left",
    ax=fig[0],

plots.line(

    {"computed r(t)": radius / 1e6},
    hlines={"apogee r_a": apogee / 1e6, "perigee r_p": perigee / 1e6},
    vlines=passages / 3600,
    xlabel="time [h]",
    ylabel="distance from the Earth's centre [1000 km]",
    title="Radius over three revolutions",
    legend="center right",
    ax=fig[1],
)
plots.line(
    times / 3600,
    {
        "energy ε/ε0 - 1": energy[15.0] / energy_exact - 1,

    },
    xlabel="time [h]",
    ylabel="relative drift",
    title="Conservation laws, step 15 s",
    legend="center",
    ax=fig[2],
)
plots.convergence(
    np.array(steps),
    {
        "largest energy error": np.array(largest),
        "drift after 3 revolutions": np.array(drift),
    },
    order=(4, 5),
    xlabel="time step [s]",
    ylabel="relative energy drift",
    title="Energy drift against the step size",
    ax=fig[3],
)
plots.show()

Going further

Add a third block pair for the \(J_2\) oblateness acceleration and watch the line of apsides rotate; or a thrust block that fires at apogee to circularise the orbit at the geostationary radius (the classical Hohmann manoeuvre, \(\Delta v\approx1.47\) km/s). With a symplectic integrator instead of Runge-Kutta the energy error stays bounded instead of drifting, a property that matters for very long propagations.
